Sand Creek

The Sand Creek Massacre: Treaty Disputes, Colorado Warfare, and the Destruction of a Peace-Seeking Village, 1851–1865

Chapter Opening

In March 1863, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, and other Plains leaders met President Abraham Lincoln in Washington. Twenty months later, a Colorado cavalry force attacked a village of Cheyenne and Arapaho families who believed they had arranged safety with the United States Army.

The Washington delegation of 1863 belonged to a long tradition of Indigenous diplomacy. Leaders from the Southern Plains traveled east to see the officials who claimed authority over their homelands, to hear the government's intentions, and to see for themselves the size of the society pressing toward them. Among the Cheyenne who made the journey was Lean Bear, who received a peace medal bearing the president's image. Lean Bear's later death, on May 16, 1864, at the hands of Colorado troops, was an early episode in the chain of events this chapter traces. It is a measure of how quickly diplomacy collapsed on the Colorado plains that a man who had been received in Washington was shot while approaching soldiers.

The central event of the chapter occurred on November 29, 1864, on Big Sandy Creek in southeastern Colorado Territory, a place usually called Sand Creek. At dawn, Colorado volunteer soldiers under Colonel John M. Chivington attacked an encampment of Cheyenne and Arapaho people. The National Park Service's current interpretation describes roughly 675 soldiers attacking a village of about 750 people and killing an estimated 230, more than half of them women and children. Other estimates, treated in Section 74.12, are higher and lower. Survivors and several of the soldiers present described a village in which leaders had been told they could expect protection, in which a United States flag and a white flag were displayed, and in which families were shot as they fled. Official condemnation followed in 1865, but no one was convicted for what occurred.

This chapter asks how the expansion of the United States into Cheyenne and Arapaho homelands turned treaty disputes and regional warfare into the massacre of a village whose leaders had been seeking peace. The question requires attention to the violence itself, and also to the decisions that made it possible: the treaties of 1851 and 1861 and the disputes over their meaning; the gold rush that brought thousands of settlers to the Front Range; the creation of Colorado Territory; the political ambitions of territorial officials; the Civil War, which created volunteer regiments and diverted regular troops; the contradictory proclamations and orders of 1864; and the diplomatic choices of Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders who pursued peace under hard conditions.

The chapter does not treat Sand Creek as the work of a single man. Chivington's responsibility is central and well documented, but he acted within a structure that included Governor John Evans, who was also the territorial superintendent of Indian affairs, department commanders, the officers at Fort Lyon, newspaper editors who demanded military action, and a settler public frightened by the killing of the Hungate family. The chapter equally resists treating Cheyenne and Arapaho people as a single actor. The Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho were distinct nations with different political institutions. Within them, peace chiefs, military societies, band leaders, and families made different judgments about treaties and war. Those differences matter to the history of 1864, and they matter to the historical record, because later accounts sometimes flattened them.

The evidence for Sand Creek is unusually rich and unusually contested. It includes treaty texts, official correspondence, military reports, the proclamations of Governor Evans, the transcript of the Camp Weld conference, letters from Captain Silas Soule and Lieutenant Joseph Cramer, testimony before a congressional committee and a military commission, newspaper coverage, and accounts by Cheyenne and Arapaho survivors and their descendants, including the later recollections of George Bent. Each kind of evidence has limits, and the chapter shows how historians weigh them against one another.

This chapter follows the preceding account of the Dakota War of 1862 in Chapter 73. It does not retell that war, but it takes into account that its news raised fears among Colorado officials that Plains nations might unite against settlements. It also stops short of the later wars of the Plains, which are the subject of Chapters 75 through 81, and it treats the later death of Black Kettle at the Washita in 1868 only as an epilogue.

Four conclusions guide the chapter. First, the Cheyenne and Arapaho were independent political societies with their own institutions, territorial rights, and diplomatic traditions, all increasingly threatened by American expansion. Second, conflicting federal and territorial policies, disputed treaties, military escalation, and failed negotiations produced the conditions for the massacre. Third, the attack of November 29 was the massacre of a peace-seeking village, and later investigations exposed grave misconduct without producing effective criminal accountability. Fourth, Sand Creek devastated Cheyenne and Arapaho communities and their peace leadership, but did not extinguish their sovereignty, their diplomacy, or their determination to preserve the historical record.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political, economic, and ceremonial organization of the Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho before and during the 1850s, including the roles of peace chiefs, military societies, and bands.
  2. Compare the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 with the Fort Wise Treaty of 1861 and explain why ratification by the United States did not settle Indigenous consent.
  3. Explain how the Pike's Peak gold rush and the creation of Colorado Territory changed land use, political demands, and military expectations on the central Plains.
  4. Analyze how the Civil War produced volunteer regiments and altered the relationship among regular officers, territorial authorities, and Indian agents.
  5. Reconstruct the events of spring and summer 1864, including the killing of Lean Bear and the Hungate family, while distinguishing evidence from attribution of responsibility.
  6. Evaluate Governor John Evans's proclamations and the contradiction between offers of protection and authorizations of attack.
  7. Interpret Black Kettle's and Left Hand's diplomacy as an active political strategy rather than as submission.
  8. Assess the Camp Weld conference as a documentary episode in which participants left with incompatible understandings.
  9. Reconstruct the attack of November 29, 1864, and explain why historians classify it as a massacre rather than a battle.
  10. Evaluate soldiers' letters, Indigenous survivor testimony, official reports, and congressional records as historical evidence, identifying provenance and limitations.
  11. Distinguish official condemnation from legal accountability, and explain the investigations of 1865 and the Little Arkansas Treaty's unfulfilled compensation provisions.
  12. Explain how the survival of Cheyenne and Arapaho political communities and the work of descendants shaped the historical memory of Sand Creek.

Historical Context

By the mid-nineteenth century, the central and southern Plains between the Platte and Arkansas rivers were the homeland of the Cheyenne and Arapaho, who shared the region with Kiowa, Comanche, and Plains Apache allies and with enemies including the Pawnee, Ute, and, at times, the Shoshone and Crow. Their economy rested on bison, horses, and trade. The movement of horses across the Plains, traced in Chapter 49, had reshaped Plains politics generations earlier, and the power of the Comanche, examined in Chapter 50, shaped the Southern Plains that the Cheyenne and Arapaho entered and, after 1840, shared in peace. Trade with American merchants, especially at Bent's Fort on the Arkansas River, linked bison robes to manufactured goods and tied several families, including that of the trader William Bent, to Cheyenne society by marriage.

In the 1840s and 1850s, American migration across the Plains changed this setting. Emigrant trains following the Platte and Santa Fe routes consumed grass, wood, and game and carried epidemic disease. Cholera in 1849 caused severe losses among Plains peoples. The federal government responded with treaty councils that sought to secure safe passage along the roads by assigning territories to nations. The treaty tradition and its limits are a recurring theme in this volume; compare the settlements discussed in Chapters 73 and 76 and the broader treatment of diplomacy in Chapter 55. Treaties assumed that nations had defined boundaries and that a few chiefs could bind everyone, assumptions at odds with Cheyenne and Arapaho political practice.

The gold rush of 1858–1859 and the creation of Colorado Territory in 1861 introduced a new settler society into the heart of that homeland. Within a few years, Denver and the mining towns of the Front Range created demand for roads, food, grazing land, and protection. At the same moment the Civil War drew regular troops from the West and brought volunteer regiments into being under territorial control. These pressures created a political economy in which military action against Indigenous nations could appeal to settlers, contractors, newspaper publishers, and ambitious officials.

Students should keep in mind that the federal government itself spoke with several voices in these years. Indian agents and the superintendent were responsible for annuities and treaty relations. The regular army commanders of the Department of Kansas and District of Colorado were responsible for security. Colorado's governor held the superintendency of Indian affairs and also promoted territorial interests. Volunteer officers answered to the army but also to local public opinion. The chapter follows how these overlapping authorities, which could and did contradict one another, left Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders unable to rely on any single assurance.

Section 74.1

The Cheyenne and Arapaho Peoples of the Southern Plains

The Cheyenne and Arapaho spoke related Algonquian languages, but they were distinct peoples with distinct institutions. Cheyenne tradition and historical research place their ancestors in the woodlands and prairies east of the Missouri River, from which they moved westward over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, some villages practicing farming along the Missouri before the horse economy drew most families onto the open Plains. Arapaho history similarly involves a westward movement and a gradual shift toward mounted bison hunting. How far back particular movements can be dated, and by what route, remains a matter of scholarly inference combining oral tradition, linguistics, and archaeology, as discussed in Chapter 1. What is secure is that by the early nineteenth century both peoples were mounted nations with extensive territories, strong trading relationships, and long-standing alliances with each other.

In the 1830s, the Cheyenne divided into northern and southern groupings, a separation shaped by the establishment of Bent's Fort on the upper Arkansas River, which drew many families south to trade, and by the great distances involved in moving as a single people. The Arapaho likewise comprised northern and southern divisions. The division was never absolute: families visited, intermarried, and joined one another's camps, and the northern and southern groupings coordinated in war and ceremony throughout the 1860s. The Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho, the focus of this chapter, ranged between the Platte and the Arkansas, wintering in sheltered river valleys and following the bison herds in summer. In 1840, the Cheyenne and Arapaho made a lasting peace with the Kiowa and Comanche, creating a regional alliance that shaped relations across the Southern Plains for a generation.

Cheyenne government combined a council of chiefs with military societies, institutions that did different work. The central council, usually called the Council of Forty-Four, consisted of chiefs drawn from the ten traditional bands, with additional senior chiefs. Council chiefs were expected to be peacemakers, to place the welfare of the people ahead of personal quarrels, and to settle disputes within the camp. Their authority rested on persuasion and reputation rather than on coercive power. Men's military societies, among them the Dog Soldiers, the Kit Fox, the Elk, and the Bowstring societies, organized war parties, policed communal bison hunts, and enforced camp discipline. Societies were not the instruments of the council, and in moments of crisis their decisions could diverge sharply from those of peace chiefs. Chapter 74 returns repeatedly to this distinction, which is the key to understanding why no one Cheyenne leader could commit all Cheyenne people to peace or war.

Historical Vocabulary
Peace Chiefs, Military Societies, and Political Authority

Definition. A Cheyenne peace chief, or council chief, held a position on the council of chiefs and was expected to work for harmony within the people and peace with outsiders. A military society was a men's association that organized warfare and policed collective activities. Southern Arapaho political life combined band leaders with an age-graded system of men's societies and ceremonial responsibilities, so that authority was distributed differently than among the Cheyenne.

Historical context. American officials often looked for a single "head chief" who could sign treaties for a whole nation. Neither the Cheyenne nor the Arapaho operated this way. Consensus within bands and among leaders was essential, and a leader who agreed to terms in an official council did not necessarily command the support of others.

Why it matters. The distinction explains why the Fort Wise Treaty of 1861 was disputed, why some Cheyenne continued to fight while Black Kettle sought peace, and why American assumptions about who spoke for whom contributed to misunderstanding and violence.

Potential misunderstanding. The label "peace chief" does not mean a leader who was weak or powerless, and "military society" does not mean a group of outlaws. Both were legitimate institutions with defined responsibilities.

Cheyenne religious life centered on ceremonies and sacred objects held in trust for the whole people, including the Sacred Arrows and the Sacred Hat, which linked the people to their prophet and their understanding of their origins. Arapaho ceremonial life likewise centered on sacred bundles and on ceremonies in which the whole nation participated. Seasonal gatherings, especially the summer ceremonies, were times when scattered bands came together, when councils met, and when decisions that affected the whole nation could be discussed. These gatherings are important for understanding diplomacy, because a leader who negotiated away from them needed to bring the results back to the people.

The leaders who appear most often in the written record of 1864 were among many. Black Kettle, a Cheyenne council chief, had long experience in dealing with American traders and officials and was known as an advocate of peace. White Antelope, an older Cheyenne chief, had met American officials on several occasions. Lean Bear led a group of Cheyenne who favored accommodation with the United States. Among the Arapaho, Left Hand, who spoke English, and Little Raven were principal leaders of Southern Arapaho bands. None of them held authority over the whole of either nation, and the written record, which overwhelmingly reflects American attention to a handful of English-speaking and treaty-signing leaders, can obscure the many others who shaped decisions.

Women were central to the Cheyenne and Arapaho economies. Women owned and set up the lodges, processed hides, prepared meat and dried food for the winter, made clothing and containers, and organized the labor of the camp. Bison hunting, led by men, depended on this work, and the volume of robes that Cheyenne and Arapaho brought to Bent's Fort, and later to other posts, reflected women's labor as much as men's. The trade connected these economies to the American market in ways that benefited Cheyenne and Arapaho households while also making them vulnerable to changes in the bison herds and in American policy. The fundamental point is that, in the 1850s, both nations were independent political societies with their own laws, institutions, and diplomatic traditions, not communities waiting to be incorporated into someone else's territory.

Section 74.2

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 and the Fort Wise Treaty of 1861

In September 1851, thousands of people from Plains nations gathered at the mouth of Horse Creek, near Fort Laramie, for a council convened by the United States. Delegates of the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Assiniboine, Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara took part. The council's immediate purpose was to secure safe passage for emigrants on the Platte road and to define the territories of the participating nations so that the United States could treat them as distinct political entities. The resulting Fort Laramie Treaty assigned the Cheyenne and Arapaho a large territory between the North Platte and Arkansas rivers, extending from the Rocky Mountains eastward onto the Plains. In return for recognizing the roads and the right of the United States to build forts along them, the nations were promised annuities in goods. The Senate, in ratifying the treaty, shortened the period over which annuities were to be paid, a change that Indigenous signatories were not always clearly told about.

The 1851 treaty did not cede Indigenous land. It acknowledged territories and rights of passage, and it left Cheyenne and Arapaho nations in possession of a homeland that included the future sites of Denver and much of eastern Colorado. For Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders, it was a precedent: a treaty in which the United States recognized their territorial rights and committed to protecting them. For American officials, it was a convenience that secured the roads. Neither understanding would survive the pressure of the next decade.

By 1860, the Colorado gold rush (Section 74.3) had brought thousands of settlers across the territory the 1851 treaty had acknowledged as Indigenous. The government did not remove the newcomers; it sought a new agreement that would reduce the Cheyenne and Arapaho land base. In February 1861, at Fort Wise on the upper Arkansas River—then in Kansas Territory—U.S. commissioners Albert G. Boone and F. B. Culver met with a group of Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders. The resulting treaty, signed on February 18, 1861, ceded most of the territory recognized in 1851 and set aside a reserve in southeastern Colorado, described by the Sandy Fork (Sand Creek) at its eastern edge, the Arkansas and Purgatoire rivers, and the then northern boundary of New Mexico Territory. The treaty provided for the eventual allotment of land to individuals, for annuities, and for assistance in farming and the construction of mills and houses. Black Kettle, White Antelope, Lean Bear, and other Cheyenne chiefs signed, as did Little Raven, Left Hand, and other Arapaho leaders.

Many Cheyenne and Arapaho rejected the agreement. Those who signed had been persuaded by the argument that the buffalo country was already lost to settlers and that a secure reserve with annuities and agricultural help offered a future for their people. Others held that the chiefs who signed had no authority to surrender land belonging to the whole nation, and that those who objected had never agreed. The Dog Soldiers and other Cheyenne military societies, along with many Arapaho and Cheyenne who lived and hunted outside the reserve, continued to regard the Smoky Hill and Republican river country as theirs. Reports by American agents of the period indicate that several chiefs later said that they had not understood the extent of what they had ceded, though such statements, reported through intermediaries, need to be read with attention to who recorded them and why. The Senate ratified the Fort Wise Treaty on August 6, 1861, and it was proclaimed on December 5, 1861, giving it the force of law in the United States, while the legitimacy of the cession remained disputed by many of the people it affected.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Recognition, Cession, and Political Consent

Definition. A cession is the transfer of land from one political community to another by agreement. Ratification is the formal approval of a treaty by the constitutional authorities of a signatory, which in the United States means the Senate and the president. Political consent is the real acceptance of an agreement by the community it binds.

Historical context. In treaty-making with Plains nations, the United States generally treated a signature by a few recognized leaders and Senate ratification as sufficient to transfer land. Cheyenne and Arapaho political practice required broader consensus, and signatories could not bind bands that had not agreed.

Why it matters. The Fort Wise Treaty had legal force in United States law but lacked the broad acceptance within the Cheyenne and Arapaho nations on which a stable settlement would have depended. This gap between law and consent is central to the origins of the conflicts of 1864.

Potential misunderstanding. Treaty ratification should not be mistaken for evidence of Indigenous agreement. A treaty may be legally valid in one system and disputed in another, and historians can explain both without endorsing either.

Primary Source Window I

The Fort Wise Treaty, February 18, 1861

Creators: U.S. commissioners Albert G. Boone and F. B. Culver; Cheyenne and Arapaho signatories; interpreters and witnesses · Date: February 18, 1861; ratified August 6, 1861; proclaimed December 5, 1861 · Type: Treaty text, a formal bilateral legal instrument written in English

Context: The treaty was negotiated at Fort Wise, on the Arkansas River, in the months before the creation of Colorado Territory and the Civil War, at a time when federal officials sought to clear the way for settlement along the Front Range. The audience was both the signatories and the United States government that would ratify and enforce it.

Provenance: The treaty survives in the official Statutes at Large and in archival copies, and a published text is available in standard treaty compilations. The negotiating councils were conducted through interpreters, and no Cheyenne or Arapaho written record of the proceedings exists from 1861.

Paraphrase of key provisions: Article I records an extensive cession, and defines the retained reserve by reference to the mouth of the Sandy Fork of the Arkansas (Sand Creek), the Arkansas and Purgatoire rivers, and the former northern boundary of New Mexico Territory; Article II proposes forty-acre assignments to individuals, rather than an indefinite communal land base. Later articles provide for the survey and allotment of land to individuals, the use of annuities to support farming, and the protection of the reserve from intrusion. Other provisions deal with payments to named individuals and with the maintenance of friendly relations.

Analysis: The text shows the United States' objective: a smaller, bounded territory, assimilation through agriculture and allotment, and relief for settlers from competing claims. The names of signatories show that the government dealt with prominent leaders who were willing to negotiate. The text, however, does not record the consent of the bands who stayed away.

Limitations: The treaty is a legal text written in English and does not show what interpreters said, what chiefs understood, or what other leaders thought. Later disputes about its meaning, and about how many Cheyenne and Arapaho were represented at the signing, must be reconstructed from agent reports, later statements, and Indigenous tradition.

Perspective: The surviving record represents the perspective of the United States and a few Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders who agreed to sign. The perspectives of those who opposed the treaty, including many warriors and families, are underrepresented in the written record.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the treaty record, and what land does it describe?
  2. Who created it, and in what circumstances?
  3. What purposes shaped the United States' goals in negotiating it?
  4. What can historians learn from the signatory names and the territorial description?
  5. What cannot be established from the treaty text alone about Cheyenne and Arapaho understanding or consent?
Section 74.3

The Pike's Peak Gold Rush, Colorado Territory, and Settler Expansion

News of gold along Cherry Creek and the South Platte in 1858 brought thousands of prospectors to the Front Range in 1859. The gold deposits proved much less rich than advertisers claimed, and many early arrivals turned back, but lasting discoveries in the mountains west of Denver sustained a mining economy. Settlements at Auraria and Denver City grew quickly, and merchants, freighters, farmers, and ranchers followed. The new population did not merely pass through, as emigrants on the Platte road had. It established towns, claimed land, and demanded roads, mail routes, and security.

The settlements lay on ground that was central to Cheyenne and Arapaho life. The South Platte and its tributaries provided winter camps with shelter, wood, and water. Trails along the Platte and Arkansas rivers, and across the Smoky Hill and Republican country, were seasonal routes for people and horses and carried bison robes to market. Settlers' farms along the river bottoms occupied the places where Indigenous families had camped; their livestock grazed the same grass that Indigenous horse herds required; their wagon trains and mail routes cut through the buffalo ranges. Growing numbers of settlers also meant reduced game and growing conflict over timber and water.

Congress created Colorado Territory in February 1861, with Denver as its principal center. The first territorial governor, William Gilpin, raised volunteer troops and showed little regard for Indigenous land claims. In 1862, President Lincoln appointed John Evans as governor. Evans was a physician and railroad promoter who had helped found Northwestern University and who saw the territory's future in agriculture, mining, and the construction of a railroad linking Denver to the East. As governor he also served as ex officio superintendent of Indian affairs, an arrangement that placed the officer responsible for Indian relations at the head of a government whose economic interest favored removal of Indigenous people from land that settlers wanted. Evans's later claim that the Cheyenne and Arapaho obstructed the territory's development reflected this political economy.

The relationship between economic expansion and demands for military protection ran through all of territorial politics. Merchants and freighters wanted safe transportation, and mine owners wanted protection for workers and supplies. Contractors sold goods and forage to the army. Newspapers, particularly the Rocky Mountain News published in Denver, amplified fears of attack and urged military action. Territorial politicians understood that a threatening frontier could advance statehood and influence in Washington. None of these interests required a massacre, and many settlers preferred peace. The point is that the structures of the territory's political economy rewarded a hard line and made peace costly for officials who sought it.

The existence of American settlements did not establish uncontested ownership of surrounding Indigenous homelands. Under the 1851 treaty, the United States had recognized Cheyenne and Arapaho rights across the region that Colorado's settlers were occupying, and the Fort Wise Treaty, whatever its legal force, assigned the Cheyenne and Arapaho a reserve in southeastern Colorado and left unresolved the status of other lands where many continued to live and hunt. Settlers claimed land under American law, but the territorial government rested on an assumption, not a settled legal fact, that Indigenous claims would be extinguished.

Section 74.4

The Civil War, Colorado Military Politics, and the Escalation of Frontier Conflict

The Civil War shaped the Colorado frontier in several ways. Regular army troops were withdrawn eastward, leaving the Plains garrisons thinly staffed, and territorial governments raised volunteer regiments to fill the gap. Volunteer soldiers enlisted for defined terms and served under officers drawn from local communities. Their discipline was uneven, their relationship to the regular chain of command was often strained, and they were exposed to local politics in ways that regular troops were not. The First Colorado Volunteers, organized under Governor Gilpin in 1861, were initially intended for service against Confederate forces.

In early 1862, a Confederate army marched up the Rio Grande from Texas into New Mexico Territory. Colorado volunteers, including the First Colorado under Colonel John P. Slough and Major John M. Chivington, marched south and helped defeat the Confederate advance at the Battle of Glorieta Pass in late March. Chivington, who had been a Methodist minister and an outspoken opponent of slavery, led a detachment that destroyed the Confederate supply train, a stroke that proved decisive. The episode made Chivington a hero in Colorado and gave the territory's volunteers a reputation for effectiveness. After the war's early victories, Chivington commanded the Military District of Colorado, reporting to General Samuel Curtis, the commander of the Department of Kansas.

The war also fueled fear. Colorado's population included many people from the South and border states, and rumors of Confederate plots, including rumors that Confederate agents were encouraging Plains nations to attack, circulated through the territory. The Dakota War of 1862, discussed in Chapter 73, heightened these anxieties. Territorial officials pointed to Minnesota as proof of what a coordinated Indigenous rising could do, and some warned that the Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota might be planning one. There is little evidence that such a plan existed. The concern, nevertheless, shaped how officials interpreted every incident, and it made it easier to treat clashes as part of a general conspiracy.

By 1864, several distinct authorities claimed responsibility for the area. Regular army officers, such as the commanders at Fort Lyon, answered to the department commander and ultimately to the War Department. The governor, as superintendent of Indian affairs, was responsible for relations with Indigenous nations but held no command over troops. Federal Indian agents distributed annuities and reported to the Indian Office. Volunteer officers answered to the army but depended on the political support of their communities. Civilians demanded protection and sometimes organized independently. The chapters that follow show how these authorities, each with its own interests, produced contradictory instructions at the very moment when clarity was most needed.

The end of the Confederate threat in the Southwest altered the mission of the Colorado volunteers without reducing their numbers. Units established to fight the Confederacy came to be used against Indigenous nations whose lands they were now policing. Contractors, merchants, and officers had a stake in keeping the troops active. This did not make a campaign against the Cheyenne and Arapaho inevitable, but it meant that the territory had an armed force with a reputation to maintain, a commander who was politically ambitious, and a population that was ready to approve of aggressive action. In 1864, the combination proved lethal.

Section 74.5

The Colorado War of 1864: Violence, Raiding, and the Killing of Lean Bear

The delegation to Washington in March 1863 was meant to reassure Plains leaders and to impress them with American power. Lean Bear and other Cheyenne chiefs, together with Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, and Apache representatives, met Lincoln and other officials, and the Cheyenne leaders returned with presents and with medals, which were supposed to identify them as friends of the government. Lean Bear brought his medal home. The trip is evidence that at least some Cheyenne leaders believed that direct relations with the highest authorities of the United States could secure peace.

In the spring of 1864, that belief was tested. The immediate sources of conflict were disputes over stolen livestock, tensions on the roads, and the actions of Colorado troops operating on the Plains. In April, a lieutenant of the First Colorado Cavalry pursuing missing stock clashed with Cheyenne in the area of the South Platte. Later in the spring, Major Jacob Downing, a Colorado officer, attacked a Cheyenne camp, burning lodges and killing people, in an operation that reflected the aggressive posture of Colorado commanders. Reports of the period attribute to Colonel Chivington orders that Cheyenne were to be attacked wherever found; the precise wording of such orders, as recorded in later testimony, should be treated as reported rather than established verbatim. Army leaders in the region, including General Curtis, signaled that they expected firmness. The Dog Soldiers and other Cheyenne societies, for their part, responded to attacks with raids on livestock and travelers.

On May 16, 1864, a column under Lieutenant George S. Eayre of the First Colorado Cavalry met a large party of Cheyenne on the Plains in the area of Ash Creek, in present-day Kansas. Lean Bear, who was with the party, rode forward to meet the soldiers, wearing the medal he had received in Washington and carrying the papers that testified to his visit. Accounts by Cheyenne witnesses, and by traders who later reported what they were told, agree that the soldiers shot Lean Bear as he approached, and that fighting followed in which several others were killed. Eayre's detachment was forced to withdraw. Different accounts of the number of casualties and the exact sequence survive, and historians treat the details of the clash as less certain than the central fact: that Colorado soldiers killed a prominent Cheyenne peace chief who had approached them openly, with a symbol of friendship from the president.

The killing of Lean Bear had a profound effect. It removed a leader committed to diplomacy, and it demonstrated to many Cheyenne that carrying a presidential medal or meeting a president did not guarantee safety. The result was a strengthening of those who argued that the Colorado troops were enemies and that the Cheyenne should fight. Warriors responded with attacks on ranches, stage stations, and wagon trains along the Platte and Smoky Hill routes during the summer, taking livestock and killing travelers. The extent of the violence on the roads, the number of the dead, and the identities of those responsible are documented unevenly. What is clear is that a cycle of attack and reprisal had begun, involving specific bands and societies, not the whole of the Cheyenne and Arapaho nations.

On June 11, 1864, Nathan Hungate, his wife Ellen, and their two daughters were killed at their ranch on Running Creek, about thirty miles southeast of Denver. Their bodies were brought to Denver and displayed publicly, and the display deepened settlers' fear and fueled demands for military action. Officials of the period attributed the killings to Cheyenne or Arapaho warriors. The identity of the killers has never been established by trial or by conclusive evidence, and later accounts differ about which people were involved. Even if members of a Cheyenne or Arapaho party were responsible, it does not follow that any peace chief authorized the attack or that all Cheyenne and Arapaho communities were party to it. The episode illustrates how a real tragedy, publicly displayed and politically used, could be turned into a justification for collective punishment.

A key interpretive point for students is that the war of 1864 was not a war between two unified sides. Some Cheyenne bands, among them the Dog Soldiers, were actively fighting. Others, including the people around Black Kettle, were seeking peace and avoiding the roads. Many Arapaho, under leaders such as Left Hand and Little Raven, were trying to stay out of the fighting altogether. Colorado officials, however, often treated Indigenous people as one hostile population, and their orders, as the next sections show, failed to distinguish those who had committed acts of war from those who had not.

Section 74.6

Governor John Evans, the 1864 Proclamations, and the Politics of Extermination

Governor John Evans's actions in 1864 are central to understanding how the situation deteriorated. In the spring, Evans wrote to federal officials about the danger of an Indigenous war and sought additional military support. In the summer, he moved toward a formal policy of dividing Indigenous people into friendly and hostile groups. On June 27, 1864, he issued a proclamation addressed to the friendly Indigenous people of the Plains, directing them to go to designated places of safety, where they would be provided with food and protection by United States authorities. In general terms, Arapaho and Cheyenne of the Arkansas were to go to Fort Lyon, and those of the South Platte to Camp Collins. The proclamation also warned that those who did not do so would be treated as hostile.

That proclamation was in practice a promise of protection to anyone who responded, and many Indigenous leaders understood it that way. It was, however, difficult to implement. Messengers had to carry the notice across a large region, few of them reached the Cheyenne and Arapaho most likely to respond, and officers had little guidance and few supplies for the people who might arrive. The proclamation also did not specify what would happen to people who came in while fighting continued elsewhere.

On August 11, 1864, Evans issued a second proclamation. It authorized citizens of the territory to go in pursuit of hostile Indigenous people, to kill and destroy them, and to take and hold whatever property they captured. The document referred to those who had committed hostilities and those who had not responded to the June invitation. Ordinary people on the Plains had no practical means of making these distinctions, and the proclamation therefore exposed approaching Indigenous communities to attack by armed civilians. Taken together, the two proclamations showed the contradiction at the heart of territorial policy: communities were told to come in and be safe, and then the population was authorized to attack those who had not.

Evans's role must be assessed precisely. He did not command the troops at Sand Creek and did not order the attack on the village. He did, however, create the political framework that made it possible. He issued the August proclamation. He publicly argued for military action. He supported the creation of the Third Colorado Cavalry, a regiment of one-hundred-day volunteers raised in the late summer and fall of 1864, at a time when his own correspondence and public statements portrayed the territory as endangered. He later attended the Camp Weld conference, where, as the next sections show, he told the Cheyenne and Arapaho delegates that the matter was now in military hands. Historians continue to debate the weight of his responsibility. Some emphasize his political ambitions, his hostility toward Indigenous land claims, and his public statements. Others stress that he faced real violence on the roads and genuine pressure from settlers. Any adequate account must distinguish what Evans said and intended, what his orders accomplished in practice, and the direct responsibility of the field commanders who planned and carried out the attack.

Newspapers amplified the climate of fear. The Rocky Mountain News, edited by William Byers, regularly published demands for severe measures against Indigenous people, often in language that treated them as a single enemy. Public meetings, resolutions, and editorial campaigns pressed officials to act. Settlers' fears were not imaginary, since people had been killed on the roads and ranches, but the language of extermination that appeared in some publications went beyond a demand for security. It reflected a broader belief, widespread in territorial society, that Indigenous peoples were an obstacle to be removed. The Third Colorado Cavalry, recruited in this atmosphere on enlistments of one hundred days, was assembled largely to fight Indigenous people, and its volunteers were eager to take action before their terms expired.

Students should keep in mind three levels of responsibility. The first is policy: the proclamations and public statements of an official who set the framework. The second is the effect of specific orders and the conflicting instructions that left Indigenous communities exposed. The third is the direct responsibility of the officers who planned, ordered, and carried out the killing. Evans and Chivington stood at different places along this spectrum, and the chapter's later sections show that the evidence supports serious criticism of both, while the direct command decision to attack the village belonged to Chivington and the officers under him.

Section 74.7

Black Kettle, Left Hand, and the Pursuit of Peace

As the violence of the summer escalated, Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders weighed their options. Black Kettle and other peace chiefs, supported by trusted traders and interpreters, concluded that continued fighting would be disastrous. The bison herds were depleted in places, trade was disrupted by the war, and Cheyenne and Arapaho families faced hunger. Winter was coming. Seeking peace was not an admission of defeat but a strategic judgment about how best to protect the people, and it was consistent with the role of the peace chief in Cheyenne political tradition.

In late August 1864, Black Kettle and other leaders sent a message to the commander at Fort Lyon. According to accounts that survive in military correspondence and later recollections, the message was written in English by or with the help of George Bent, who had been educated in St. Louis and was able to write, and was delivered to Major Edward W. Wynkoop. It indicated that the chiefs wished to make peace and were prepared to return captives who had been taken during the fighting. The text that survives should be treated as an English-language record of what interpreters and writers set down, not as a verbatim record of Cheyenne speech. Its significance is that it documents an Indigenous diplomatic initiative, one that Wynkoop, an officer with sympathy for the Cheyenne and Arapaho, took seriously.

Wynkoop assembled a force of about 125 soldiers and marched to the Smoky Hill River, where, in early September, he met with Black Kettle and other leaders at a large Cheyenne and Arapaho gathering that also included many Dog Soldiers. In councils there, Wynkoop heard views both for and against peace. Some warriors were angry and suspicious, and Wynkoop's small force was in a vulnerable position. The leaders who favored peace persuaded the others to allow negotiations and delivered several captives to Wynkoop. Wynkoop then offered to escort a group of leaders to Denver to meet with Governor Evans and Colonel Chivington. Black Kettle, White Antelope, Bull Bear, and the Arapaho leader Neva and others agreed, accepting considerable personal risk. Not all Cheyenne agreed to the arrangement, and the Dog Soldiers and others remained outside the process.

It is important to understand the choices made by Indigenous leaders on their own terms. Black Kettle was not acting on behalf of all Cheyenne, and he did not claim to. A peace chief's authority depended on persuasion, and he could advise bands that trusted him but could not compel the military societies. Left Hand and other Arapaho leaders acted within different institutions, and their decisions were made through their own councils and band relationships. Their choice to seek protection near American posts reflected the practical needs of families facing hunger and the threat of attack, but it also reflected a calculation about American power and a long tradition of using diplomacy to protect the people. Diplomacy was an active political strategy, not evidence of submission.

Peace-seeking communities also confronted practical problems. Fort Lyon was a military post that could not feed large numbers of people on its own supplies, and the officers there had no clear authority to promise food, annuities, or protection beyond the immediate area. The disruption of trade and the loss of game added to hunger among those who stayed away from the roads. Wynkoop, who sympathized with the leaders, faced pressure from superiors, and his personal commitments were limited by the orders he received. The structure of the arrangement meant that Black Kettle and his followers depended on the goodwill of particular officers at a time when authority over them was divided and contested.

Section 74.8

The Camp Weld Conference of September 28, 1864, and the Failure of Peace

On September 28, 1864, Black Kettle, White Antelope, Bull Bear, and the Arapaho leader Neva, with other Cheyenne and Arapaho representatives, met at Camp Weld, near Denver, with Governor Evans, Colonel Chivington, Major Wynkoop, and other officials. A record of the proceedings was made by an officer present and survives. It is the closest thing to a transcript of a negotiation between these parties, though it is an English-language record produced through interpreters and was later subject to dispute.

According to the record, the Indigenous delegates said that they wished for peace and had come at Wynkoop's invitation. They stated that they had not responded to Evans's June proclamation because they had not received it in time, and that they were prepared to return captives and to accept the government's terms in order to protect their people. Evans responded by expressing displeasure at their delay, by noting that the war was widespread and that other Indigenous groups were involved, and by stating that he had no authority to make peace, which had passed to the military authorities. Chivington said, in effect, that he was not the person to make peace, that the military had the authority in the territory, and that the delegates should make arrangements with Wynkoop, the commander at Fort Lyon. The record indicates that the leaders were told to return to the vicinity of Fort Lyon and to place themselves under Wynkoop's protection while the question was settled, and the delegates returned to their people with the understanding that, so long as they remained near the post and kept the peace, they would not be attacked.

Participants' accounts of what was settled diverged. The Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders, and Wynkoop, understood the meeting as providing a path toward peace and protection, conditional on their staying near the fort. Evans, in reports to Washington and in later testimony, insisted that he had not made peace and that nothing in the discussion bound the military to refrain from further operations. Chivington, for his part, later testified that he had made no promise. It is possible to read the record in ways that support each of these positions: nothing in the record is an explicit treaty, and nothing in it explicitly tells the Cheyenne and Arapaho that they were at war with the army. The ambiguity was dangerous precisely because Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders, who had no way to verify the internal chain of command, acted on the most reasonable reading of what they had been told.

Division IIIThe Sand Creek Massacre, November 29, 1864
Section 74.9

Fort Lyon, Changing Military Orders, and the Movement to Sand Creek

In the weeks after Camp Weld, Cheyenne and Arapaho families responded to the invitation to come near Fort Lyon in different ways. A group of Arapaho under Left Hand and some Cheyenne under Black Kettle moved toward the post and camped nearby, relying on Wynkoop's assurances and on the understanding reached at Denver. Others, including many Cheyenne, stayed on the Smoky Hill and Republican rivers, where the Dog Soldiers and others continued to resist. The Cheyenne and Arapaho did not move as a single population. The families who approached the fort were a portion of their nations, and they were the families that had accepted the path toward peace.

Wynkoop's removal and replacement by Major Scott J. Anthony in early November changed the situation. Anthony, who had been critical of Wynkoop's approach, found Indigenous families camped near the fort, seeking food and protection. He did not honor the assurances Wynkoop had given. According to later testimony, he demanded that the people give up their arms and told some leaders that he could not feed them or negotiate peace without higher authority, and that they should move away from the post and wait. The restrictions on camping near the fort, and the limited provisions available, led the families to look for food and grass elsewhere.

Black Kettle's community and a smaller group of Arapaho under Left Hand moved to a campsite on Big Sandy Creek, usually called Sand Creek, about forty miles to the northeast of the fort. The campsite lay on or just within the eastern, Sand Creek boundary of the tract defined by the disputed 1861 Fort Wise Treaty, according to National Park Service and National Archives historical mapping; the site’s precise relation to a line described in nineteenth-century terms should not be made more exact than the record permits. Its residents were in any case not made hostile merely by camping there, and they had approached the Fort Lyon authorities to seek protection. The creek offered shelter, water, and grazing for horses, and the bison herds were within reach. Black Kettle's decision to camp there reflected the expectation that the community would be safe so long as it remained peaceful and that officers at Fort Lyon knew where it was and had been told to leave it alone.

It is important to distinguish the families at Sand Creek from the broader populations of their nations. The village at Sand Creek included lodges of Black Kettle's Cheyenne, other Cheyenne families, and a smaller number of Arapaho. Many men were away hunting. Many other Cheyenne and Arapaho were encamped elsewhere, including on the Smoky Hill and Republican rivers, and some of those camps contained the warriors who had been fighting in the summer. The village that was later attacked was therefore not a gathering of the warriors that Colorado officials sought. It was a camp of people who had followed the path that officers had set out for them.

While the families settled into winter camp, Colorado officers prepared for an expedition. The Third Colorado Cavalry, whose members had enlisted for only one hundred days, faced the end of their terms without having fought a decisive engagement. Their commander, Colonel George L. Shoup, and Colonel Chivington, who was preparing to take part in a campaign, were under pressure from the public and the press to deliver results. By late November, Chivington had gathered his forces and was moving toward Fort Lyon. Whether he planned from the outset to attack Black Kettle's village, or whether the plan formed as he learned where the Cheyenne were camped, is not entirely clear in the surviving evidence. What is clear is that, by the time he arrived at Fort Lyon on November 28, he had decided to attack the camp.

Reservation geography also helps explain a mismatch between paper boundaries and actual land use. Article I of the 1861 Fort Wise Treaty used rivers, territorial lines, and stream mouths rather than the surveyed parcels that settlers were beginning to claim. Sand Creek marked an edge of the intended reservation, while Cheyenne and Arapaho seasonal movement continued across a much wider area. Government promises of agricultural instruction, mills, protection from trespass, and food assistance were unevenly implemented; the Fort Lyon agency struggled with leadership disputes and inadequate supplies. The people who moved close to the fort therefore faced the dilemma of accepting a confined and poorly supported reserve while the bison, forage, and fuel needed for daily survival often lay elsewhere. Moving to a traditional creek campsite was a practical response to that dilemma, not in itself an act of warfare.

Section 74.10

The Sand Creek Village: Families, Peace Chiefs, and Expectations of Protection

The camp at Sand Creek was a residential encampment in preparation for winter. According to the National Park Service's interpretation, which draws on both documentary and Indigenous sources, it held approximately 750 people, most of them Cheyenne, with a smaller number of Arapaho. The lodges, set up according to Cheyenne custom, housed families of several generations. Many of the people present were women, children, and elders. Horses were grazed nearby, and the families had gathered meat and other supplies for the winter, though the shortage of food that had pushed some of them toward the fort was not fully resolved. It was not a fortified position, and it had no military defenses.

Leadership in the camp rested with the council chiefs and band leaders. Black Kettle was the most prominent Cheyenne leader present, and White Antelope, an older chief who had been a signer of the Fort Wise Treaty, was also there. Among the Arapaho, Left Hand's group had camped with the Cheyenne. Other families were present for their own reasons, some because they had come from the fort, others because relatives were there. Some Cheyenne who had been involved in the fighting during the summer may also have been in or near the camp, but the evidence indicates that the village as a whole was dominated by families who had accepted the peace initiative. How many men of fighting age were in camp at the time of the attack is disputed, but most accounts suggest that the majority were away on a hunt or at other camps.

The people at Sand Creek expected to be safe. They had been told by Wynkoop, and, in their understanding, by Evans and Chivington at Camp Weld, that if they remained peaceful and stayed near the army's post, they would not be attacked. Anthony had also encouraged them to remain where they were. Several accounts, including those of survivors and of soldiers who later testified, describe Black Kettle raising an American flag on a lodge pole, together with a white flag, when the attack began, as a signal that the village was at peace. The evidence for the flags comes from multiple witnesses, including soldiers who were present, and it is consistent with the expectation of protection that the leaders had acted upon. The flag was not a gesture of hope in a vacuum but a signal to a government to whose officers the leaders had been talking for months.

Everyday Life and Social History

Camp, Family, and Winter on the Southern Plains

A Cheyenne or Arapaho winter camp was a community built from kinship. Households were organized around extended families, and the lodge, owned and managed by the women of the household, was the center of domestic life. Women tanned hides, prepared and dried meat, made and repaired lodges and clothing, and managed the food stores that would carry families through the cold months. Men hunted, cared for horse herds, and, as members of societies, took on responsibilities for camp security and, when necessary, for war. Children were raised by parents, grandparents, and other relatives, and elders were the keepers of history, ceremony, and advice.

In 1864, the war and the disruption of trade had made winter preparation harder. The families who camped at Sand Creek had less food and fewer goods than they would have wished, and the people who had come from the fort had been warned that the post could not feed them. Their decision to camp on the creek reflected the practical need for grass and firewood as much as the political arrangement they believed they had made. The loss of lodges, food, and horses on November 29, described later in the chapter, therefore fell on families already under strain and entering the harshest season of the year without shelter.

Because the written record of the camp was produced largely by soldiers and officials, historians reconstruct the village's daily life from Indigenous testimony, from the accounts of traders and interpreters who lived among the Cheyenne, and from archaeological work at the site, which has recovered objects associated with the camp. These sources show a community in which the ordinary business of living, caring for children, and preparing for winter was underway on the morning of the attack.

Students should be careful not to romanticize the village or to treat it as simply passive. The camp was not isolated from the war; some of its members had relatives who were fighting, and some young men of the camp may have taken part in earlier raids. The question that matters for the historical classification of the event is not whether any individual in the camp had ever fought, but how the camp as a whole was organized, what it signaled to the army, and what was done to it. The evidence on all three points, from the flags and the location to the composition of the dead, supports the conclusion that the attack targeted a community that was seeking protection and that most of those killed were noncombatants.

Section 74.11

Chivington's March and the Dawn Attack of November 29, 1864

Chivington arrived at Fort Lyon on the morning of November 28, 1864, with a column that included the Third Colorado Cavalry under Colonel Shoup and a battalion of the First Colorado Cavalry, joined at the post by troops of the garrison. The officers at Fort Lyon were informed of the expedition's purpose, and some of them objected. Captain Silas Soule and Lieutenant Joseph Cramer, among others, warned that the Cheyenne and Arapaho at Sand Creek were under the protection of the fort and that an attack would be a violation of promises made by Wynkoop and, in their view, by the government. Chivington, according to witnesses who later testified, dismissed the objections. Guards were placed around the post to prevent anyone from leaving and warning the village, and Chivington made known his intention to attack.

The force that marched from Fort Lyon on the evening of November 28 numbered approximately 675 men, according to the current National Park Service interpretation, drawn from the Third Colorado Cavalry, companies of the First Colorado Cavalry, and attached units. It also included four mountain howitzers. Robert Bent, the son of the trader William Bent and a Cheyenne woman, was compelled to serve as a guide, and other civilians and interpreters accompanied the column. Several of the soldiers later said that they were told the attack would be on a hostile camp and that no prisoners were to be taken, though accounts of what orders were given differ in detail. The night march covered about forty miles over rough ground, and the column reached the vicinity of Sand Creek around dawn on November 29.

The column approached from the south and east and deployed on the open ground along the creek. Some troops moved to cut off the village's horse herd, which was grazing nearby, and others took positions on the bluffs and along the stream to bring the village under fire. Artillery was placed in position, and the soldiers opened fire on the camp. The village, according to survivors, was awakened by the sound of horses and gunfire. Black Kettle raised a flag over his lodge and urged people to stay calm and gather, in the belief that the soldiers would see that the camp was peaceful. White Antelope walked toward the troops, signaling that the village was friendly, and was killed.

The initial minutes were chaotic. Families ran from the lodges, many of them heading up the creek bed, which ran as a wide, sandy channel with a little water. Some men seized their weapons and tried to hold the soldiers back, to allow women and children to escape. A number of warriors made a stand, protected by the banks of the creek, and fought a delaying action that allowed many to flee. The soldiers pressed forward, firing on fleeing people, and the howitzers shelled the area where people had taken shelter. The initial attack on the village was followed by a prolonged pursuit along the creek, which continued for several hours.

Chivington's later report described the operation as a great victory over a large hostile force, claiming that several hundred warriors had been killed and that the village was a stronghold of the people responsible for the summer's raids. That characterization cannot be sustained against the evidence. The village was not a military camp: it was a residential encampment of families who had sought protection. The composition of the dead, the display of flags, the testimony of soldiers who were present, and the accounts of the survivors all contradict the claim of a conventional victory. The attacking force was also much larger and better armed than the camp's defenders. The result was a massacre, a conclusion reached by the federal investigations of 1865 and endorsed by the National Park Service and virtually all modern historians.

Some of the soldiers' conduct that morning shows that the attack was not universally accepted within the force. Soule and Cramer ordered their companies to hold their fire. Some soldiers refused to take part in the killing, and others, in the confusion, fired at their own men. The command's discipline broke down as soldiers pursued individuals along the creek, and officers lost control of the force. These details matter because they show that the outcome was the result of choices made by commanders and by individual soldiers, not an unavoidable consequence of a military operation.

Section 74.12

Killing at Sand Creek: Women, Children, Elders, and Survivors

The killing at Sand Creek took place in several phases. In the first, soldiers fired on the village as people left their lodges. White Antelope, an elder and a signer of the Fort Wise Treaty, was killed early, as he approached the troops. Accounts attribute to him a death song, which Cheyenne witnesses recalled, but the wording passed through several retellings and should be treated as tradition rather than as a verified record. Other council chiefs, including War Bonnet and Standing in the Water, were killed, and One Eye, who had served as an intermediary with the army, also died. Left Hand, according to Arapaho and other accounts, refused to fight and was wounded, and he died of his injuries after the massacre. Black Kettle escaped. According to accounts, he left the village on foot, helping his wife, who had been wounded multiple times and who survived.

In the second phase, families fled up Sand Creek and dug pits in the sandy bed and along the banks, using whatever tools they had, to protect themselves from fire. Many of those who took refuge there were women and children, with some men and elders. Soldiers surrounded the area and fired from the banks. Howitzers were brought up to shell the pits, and the fire continued for hours. Some people in the pits returned fire, and a number of soldiers were killed or wounded, a fact that Chivington later pointed to as evidence of a battle. Survivor accounts and the testimony of soldiers describe parents sheltering children and individuals attempting to surrender and being shot. Children were also killed during the pursuit. The detailed experiences of individual people, which come from later and varied sources, should be presented with attention to provenance and not as established fact when they cannot be confirmed.

A third phase was the pursuit of people who fled across the plains. Some escaped and walked through the night and the following days, in cold weather and without adequate clothing or food, to reach other Cheyenne and Arapaho camps on the Smoky Hill and Republican rivers. Many were wounded, and many did not survive the journey. Other survivors were hidden or protected by those who found them, including relatives who came to the aid of the wounded. The distinction between those who died in the initial attack and those killed in the pursuit and in the later hours of the fighting is difficult to establish exactly, because the testimony varies and because people continued to die of wounds and exposure in the days that followed.

Estimates of the number of dead vary widely, and the differences have an explanation. Chivington reported that several hundred warriors had been killed, a figure that served his claim of a battle and that is not supported by the evidence. Other participants and observers gave lower estimates. Some Army officers and soldiers who testified in 1865 placed the number killed at about 100 to 200, with a majority being women and children. Cheyenne and Arapaho accounts, which counted the missing and the dead among families, gave higher figures in some cases. The estimate in the National Park Service's current interpretation, about 230 killed, with more than half women and children, rests on a comprehensive reevaluation of these sources and on consultation with descendants. Older published works have given lower numbers. The disagreement reflects the difficulty of counting the dead in a chaotic event where bodies were scattered over a large area, where many were not recovered, and where the sources are written by people with very different interests. Historians generally agree that the dead were predominantly noncombatants, that the number was in the low hundreds, and that no single figure can be given without qualification.

The army suffered casualties as well, though the numbers were small compared with those of the village. The National Park Service reports that records document at least sixteen soldiers killed or later dying of their wounds and as many as seventy-eight wounded; its review also notes that some casualties may have resulted from fire among the attackers. These figures are not independently established for each company, and they should not be used to infer that the encampment was a prepared hostile military force. Chivington and his supporters stressed these losses to argue that a real battle had been fought. The evidence shows that some warriors fought effectively to cover the flight of families and that some soldiers died. It does not alter the conclusion that the village was a camp of families seeking peace, and that the great majority of the people killed were not fighting.

Historical Vocabulary
Massacre, Military Responsibility, and War Crimes

Definition. A massacre, in historians' usage, is the killing of large numbers of people who are unarmed, defenseless, or not engaged in fighting, typically in a one-sided attack. A battle is an armed engagement between organized forces. A noncombatant is a person not taking part in fighting, including women, children, and the elderly.

Historical context. Nineteenth-century armies operated under customary standards of conduct, and officers were expected to refrain from killing noncombatants and those who had surrendered. These customs were not enforced as modern international criminal law, and the term "war crime" in its modern legal sense was not available to the officers or courts of 1864. Contemporaries nevertheless used the language of murder and atrocity to condemn Sand Creek, including soldiers who were present and investigators in 1865.

Why it matters. The classification of Sand Creek as a massacre rests on the evidence of who was attacked, how, and with what knowledge of the village's status. The word is an analytical conclusion supported by evidence, not merely a moral judgment.

Potential misunderstanding. Calling the event a massacre does not mean that no Indigenous person fought or that no soldier died. It means that the attack's character was the killing of a largely noncombatant community.

They have insisted that the massacre be understood from the standpoint of the victims and that the site and the record be protected.

Section 74.13

The Aftermath of the Attack: Mutilation, Looting, and the Destruction of the Village

After organized resistance had largely ended, many soldiers remained on the field, and the evidence shows that numerous atrocities occurred. Soldiers who testified in 1865, officers who wrote letters in December 1864, and Cheyenne and Arapaho survivors all describe the mutilation of bodies. Men, women, and children were among those mutilated, and body parts were taken as trophies. Some soldiers later displayed such trophies in Denver, where they were exhibited publicly and celebrated in some quarters. This chapter records these facts because they are central to the evidentiary case for classifying Sand Creek as a massacre and to the investigators' condemnation, but it does not reproduce graphic details beyond what is needed to establish what happened and who was responsible.

The soldiers also destroyed and looted the village. They burned lodges, took or destroyed the winter food supplies, and seized personal possessions, including robes, blankets, clothing, and ceremonial items, many of which were later carried back to Denver as souvenirs. They captured the camp's pony herd, a loss of immense significance in a horse-based economy. For surviving families who made their way to other camps, the loss of lodges, food, clothing, and horses meant exposure and hunger during a Plains winter. The material destruction is therefore not a secondary effect but part of the human catastrophe: survivors who had lived through the attack faced a winter in which the means of survival had been burned or taken.

The evidence supports distinctions among different levels of responsibility. Individual soldiers committed or took part in mutilations and killings of noncombatants. Officers who were present, including Chivington, either ordered, permitted, or failed to prevent these acts. The testimony of soldiers who objected shows that the conduct was not demanded by military necessity and that some officers refused to join in it. Responsibility also extended up the chain of command to those who authorized the expedition and, in the political sphere, to those whose rhetoric and policies had fostered a climate in which such action could be proposed. At the same time, not every soldier in the column took part in the atrocities, and some refused. A historical account should neither exculpate the force as a whole nor treat each individual as equally responsible.

The expedition returned to Denver in December, and its reception shows how divided Colorado opinion was. The Third Colorado Cavalry and Chivington were welcomed in Denver, and the Rocky Mountain News and other papers celebrated the operation as a victory. Soldiers paraded trophies, and some theaters and public spaces featured displays. Chivington and his men were widely praised. But the celebration was not unanimous. As letters from Soule, Cramer, and others began to circulate, and as stories reached Washington and eastern newspapers, a growing number of people expressed disbelief, then outrage. The public presentation of Sand Creek as a victory was soon challenged by evidence from within the army itself.

The material destruction mattered as much to survivors as the immediate physical losses. A lodge contained bedding, tools, dried provisions, clothing, and objects used in ordinary family and ceremonial life. Horses allowed a family to move its shelter, gather fuel, and pursue bison; the seizure of herds deprived surviving relatives of both transportation and wealth. Families who escaped into the winter landscape could not readily return to retrieve supplies from a destroyed or occupied camp. This explains why the later Little Arkansas negotiations concerned property as well as life: survivors were being asked to begin again without the material basis for seasonal movement and subsistence. The treaty promised compensation, but an official commitment in 1865 did not repair the household losses incurred in November 1864.

Section 74.14

Silas Soule, Joseph Cramer, and the Soldiers Who Refused to Participate

Captain Silas Soule commanded a company of the First Colorado Cavalry, and Lieutenant Joseph Cramer was an officer in another company. Soule, who had been a Kansas abolitionist before the war, had been at Camp Weld in September and had taken part in Wynkoop's dealings with the Cheyenne and Arapaho. Both men were at Fort Lyon on the night of November 28, and both objected, as noted in Section 74.11, to the planned attack. When Chivington ordered the column to march, they went with it, as officers under orders. At Sand Creek, Soule and Cramer ordered their men not to fire on the village, and their companies are described in testimony as having held back from the killing.

Their positions within the command structure were difficult. Both were junior officers in a chain of command headed by a colonel who was also the district commander. Open refusal to march might have led to court-martial, and they went forward while declining to order their men into the attack. In the weeks after the massacre, both wrote letters to Wynkoop, who had been the commander at Fort Lyon and who had gone east after his removal. These letters described what they had seen in detail and expressed their disgust. In February and March 1865, Soule testified before a military commission convened in Denver to investigate the events, and Cramer also gave evidence. Soule's testimony, given in a hostile local atmosphere, was an act of courage, and it put him at personal risk.

Soule's courage was costly. He married in early April 1865, and on April 23, 1865, he was shot and killed on a Denver street. Charles Squires (also rendered Squier in some accounts), a former Colorado volunteer, was identified as an assailant; he was arrested but escaped confinement before trial. He was never convicted. Soule's friends and later historians have suggested that his testimony against Chivington was the reason for his killing, and the timing is suggestive. The evidence does not, however, establish who, if anyone, ordered or encouraged the murder, and this chapter makes no claim on that point. What is documented is that a key witness against Chivington was killed in the streets of Denver within weeks of testifying, and that the killing was never successfully prosecuted.

The example of Soule and Cramer is important for historical reasoning about command responsibility. The presence of men within the column who refused to take part shows that alternatives existed, and that the participation of other soldiers resulted from choices. It also undercuts any later argument that the whole force acted under compulsion or that the character of the village was unclear. Officers who were on the scene concluded, at the time, that it was a peaceful camp.

The letters of Soule and Cramer also illustrate why firsthand evidence should be read closely rather than merely praised as brave dissent. The writers had military positions and access to officers, but they were not neutral outside observers: they were participants in the expedition, accountable for their companies and interested in distinguishing their conduct from that of other soldiers. Their testimony is especially strong on orders heard, actions witnessed, and the behavior of the units near them; it is less complete on events beyond their lines of sight. Historians compare their accounts with statements by Robert Bent, Wynkoop, Chivington, and Indigenous survivors, asking which details converge despite very different interests. This method is important because the original army narrative presented Sand Creek as a battle, and the counter-record emerged from overlapping testimony rather than from a single definitive eyewitness.

Section 74.15

Cheyenne and Arapaho Survivor Testimony and the Reconstruction of Sand Creek

Any account of Sand Creek that relied only on American military and official sources would reproduce a particular perspective. The people who lived through the attack gave their own testimony, and descendants preserved it. Some survivors spoke to government commissions and to journalists in the years after the massacre. Others transmitted their memories within families and communities, and Cheyenne and Arapaho historians, in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, have worked with these traditions and with archival records to reconstruct what happened. These accounts are historical evidence in their own right and are indispensable for the experience of the people who were in the village.

One of the most important individual sources is George Bent. Bent, the son of William Bent and Owl Woman, grew up between Cheyenne and American worlds, was educated in St. Louis, and was present in the village at Sand Creek, where he was wounded. In later life, he lived among the Cheyenne and corresponded with the writer and historian George E. Hyde, providing extensive recollections of Cheyenne history and of the massacre. Those letters and notes, preserved in archival collections and used by later scholars, are a major source for the reconstruction of Sand Creek, though they require careful reading. Bent wrote decades after the event, with a view to a particular audience, and his accounts were shaped by Hyde's questions and editorial presence as well as by his own memory. Other Cheyenne and Arapaho witnesses, including women and men whose accounts were recorded by interviewers, add detail on the experience of fleeing, of hiding in the creek bed, and of the aftermath.

Historians use several methods to reconstruct the event from such sources. They compare accounts against one another and against documentary evidence, looking for points of corroboration, and they pay attention to provenance: who recorded the testimony, when, in what language, and for what purpose. They evaluate how traditions were transmitted within families and communities and how they were shaped by repeated telling. They also rely on archaeological and landscape research at the site, which has helped locate the village and clarify the geography of the attack. When independent sources, including Indigenous testimony and the letters of officers like Soule and Cramer, agree on key points, such as the display of flags, the presence of women and children, and the killing of fleeing people, historians treat those points as well established.

Section 74.16

Congressional Investigations, Military Inquiries, and the Failure of Accountability

As accounts of Sand Creek reached the East in early 1865, several investigations were launched. The Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War, a congressional committee that had been examining military affairs, took testimony in Washington from witnesses including officers who had been at Sand Creek and others who had knowledge of events on the Plains. A separate Joint Special Committee on the Condition of the Indian Tribes, which examined the broader state of Indian affairs, also took up the massacre and reported later. The army convened a military commission in Denver, which heard testimony from soldiers, officers, interpreters, and others over a period of months. The commission's work took place in a community where many people supported Chivington, and witnesses sometimes gave evidence under pressure.

The investigations examined the village's status, the flags, the orders given, the killing of noncombatants, and the post-massacre atrocities. The testimony of Soule, Cramer, Wynkoop, and other witnesses contradicted Chivington's claim of a battle, and the reports condemned the conduct of the attacking force in strong terms. The congressional committee characterized the operation as a massacre and held Chivington responsible; the military commission likewise concluded that the attack had been unjustified and that serious atrocities had been committed. In their criticisms of the operation, the investigators also faulted Colorado officials, and the findings contributed to a broader reassessment of Indian policy in Washington.

Official condemnation, however, was not accountability in a legal sense. Chivington had left military service before the inquiries concluded; his regiment's term ended in early 1865, and he was mustered out, which placed him beyond the reach of a court-martial. No criminal prosecution followed in civilian courts, and Chivington was never convicted of any offense related to Sand Creek. The soldiers who took part likewise faced no trial. Colorado civil authorities showed no inclination to prosecute, and federal authorities lacked a clear mechanism to try discharged volunteers for acts committed against Indigenous people. Chivington lived for decades afterward, remaining a figure in Colorado public life for a time and dying in 1894.

John Evans, who testified before the congressional committee, defended himself by arguing that he had not known of the planned attack and that he had not made peace at Camp Weld. The committee was critical of his testimony, and Evans was removed from office in August 1865, when President Andrew Johnson ended his term as territorial governor. His removal was a political consequence, not a legal one. Later, the question of Evans's responsibility has been revisited. At the time of the massacre's 150th anniversary, committees at institutions connected to Evans, including Northwestern University, conducted studies of his role, and the Northwestern University committee reported in 2014 that no known evidence showed Evans planned the massacre or knew of it in advance, but that his failures as superintendent of Indian affairs, within a defective federal policy, contributed to the circumstances permitting the attack. Other historians and the University of Denver have placed differing emphases on the extent of his political and moral responsibility. The distinction between prior knowledge and systemic responsibility is essential.

The distinction between documented responsibility and actual legal consequences is the central lesson of this section. The investigations of 1865 produced a documentary record in which federal bodies condemned an operation by United States troops, and that record has been valuable to later historians and to Cheyenne and Arapaho descendants. Yet the system that produced it lacked the capacity or will to impose penalties. The result was a pattern in which acts of violence against Indigenous people could be officially censured while the perpetrators were left unpunished, and the weakness of the legal response fed the distrust with which many Cheyenne and Arapaho regarded promises made by the United States.

Historical Debate

Battle or Massacre? Assigning Responsibility for Sand Creek

The question: How should historians classify the events of November 29, 1864, and how should responsibility be distributed among Chivington, other officers, Governor Evans, and the broader structure of territorial policy?

Contemporary defense — a battle against a hostile village: Chivington and his supporters argued that the camp contained warriors who had raided settlements, that stolen property was found in the village, that the soldiers faced armed resistance, and that Indigenous people were at war with the territory. They presented the operation as a legitimate military action.
Documentary and scholarly assessment — a massacre of a peace-seeking village: Federal investigators in 1865 and virtually all modern historians conclude that the village was a residential camp whose leaders had sought protection, that flags were displayed, that most of the dead were women, children, and elders, and that atrocities followed. They regard resistance by some warriors and the presence of some stolen property as inadequate to describe the event as a battle.
Debates about the distribution of responsibility: Scholars differ about the relative weight of Chivington's personal decisions, the political framework created by Evans, the role of army commanders such as Curtis, and the effect of public pressure. They also differ about how best to categorize the event in broader frameworks, including whether it should be analyzed as part of a pattern of violence against Indigenous peoples that some historians describe in terms of genocide, and others, while condemning it, prefer to place within the history of massacre and settler-colonial warfare.

Evidence: The classification debate turns on evidence about the composition of the village and of the dead, the flags, the testimony of soldiers and survivors, and the orders given. Disagreement about responsibility concerns causation and interpretation more than the basic facts. The disputes about wider categories involve terminology and definitions.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern casualty estimates (evidence), the weight of Evans's role (causation and interpretation), and the appropriate conceptual label for the event (terminology). The core factual conclusion that Sand Creek was a massacre is not seriously contested among historians.

Why it matters: The way historians classify and explain Sand Creek affects how it is remembered, how responsibility is assigned, and how the relationship between settler expansion and Indigenous dispossession is understood. A framework that treats the massacre as the product of one man's brutality can obscure the political structures that enabled it.

Division VSand Creek's Consequences and the Persistence of Cheyenne and Arapaho Sovereignty
Section 74.17

The Destruction of Peace Leadership and the Transformation of Indigenous Politics

Sand Creek struck at the institutions through which Cheyenne and Arapaho communities managed relations with the United States. Several council chiefs were killed, among them White Antelope, War Bonnet, Standing in the Water, and One Eye, and Left Hand, an Arapaho leader committed to accommodation, was fatally wounded. The Council of Forty-Four was a body whose authority depended on the presence and reputation of its members, and the loss of so many chiefs at once disrupted its capacity to deliberate and to speak for the people. Many families also lost relatives, shelter, supplies, and horses, and the kinship networks through which work, ceremony, and support were organized were torn apart.

The massacre also destroyed trust. The leaders who had argued that diplomacy could protect the people had done so on the basis of assurances from American officers, and those assurances had proved worthless. Survivors and other Cheyenne and Arapaho drew different conclusions from this. Many, particularly among the military societies, concluded that the United States could not be trusted and that armed resistance was the only means of defending the people and their territory. Others, including Black Kettle, continued to seek a negotiated settlement, believing that further war would bring even greater losses, and that the survival of the people depended on securing a peace that the government would respect.

These responses did not follow a single pattern. Within the Cheyenne nation, some bands moved north to join the Northern Cheyenne and Lakota along the Powder River, where they could continue to fight and where distance from American forts offered some protection. Others remained in the south and sought terms. The Dog Soldiers, whose members had already resisted the Fort Wise Treaty, became more prominent. Among the Arapaho, some, including followers of Little Raven, who had not been at Sand Creek, continued to look for a settlement, while others moved north to join their relatives. Black Kettle, who had survived the massacre, faced both the grief of losses within his own family and criticism from some Cheyenne who held that his policy had led to disaster. His standing was diminished in some quarters, though many people continued to follow him.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Cheyenne and Arapaho Political Organization Before and After 1864

What Changed

  • The Council of Forty-Four and Arapaho band leadership lost many experienced leaders, weakening the institutions most committed to negotiation
  • The relative influence of military societies, especially the Dog Soldiers, grew in the Cheyenne nation after 1864
  • Many survivors moved north or scattered, altering the distribution of Cheyenne and Arapaho families across the Plains
  • Trust in American assurances declined sharply, and diplomacy was conducted under the shadow of the massacre

What Stayed the Same

  • Decision-making remained consensual and distributed, with no single leader able to speak for the whole nation
  • Ceremonial life, kinship, and the sacred objects held in trust for the people continued, and the council continued to meet
  • Peace leaders continued to use diplomacy as a political strategy, as the 1865 treaty negotiations show
  • Cheyenne and Arapaho nations maintained a claim to territory and to political self-determination

Pattern and timing: The change was abrupt in its immediate effects, since leaders were killed in a single morning, but its political consequences unfolded unevenly over the following years. Those who lost relatives and leaders experienced it most directly, while ceremonial institutions and nationhood showed greater continuity. Why it matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents the conclusion that Sand Creek ended Cheyenne and Arapaho political life, and also avoids understating the damage it inflicted.

Political reconstruction was not uniform across the bands. The Cheyenne Council of Forty-Four suffered grievous losses, but its surviving members and other leaders retained relationships of kinship, ceremonial obligation, and regional diplomacy. Military societies did not suddenly acquire uncontested national authority, nor did all families reject further negotiations with the United States. Among the Arapaho, leaders who had sought to avoid fighting confronted the death or injury of relatives and the insecurity of their settlements; they too weighed migration, temporary alliances, and further diplomacy. The most important consequence was a loss of confidence in the reliability of American protection, not the destruction of Indigenous capacity to make collective decisions. Later treaties must be interpreted against this background of interrupted leadership, displacement, and persistent attempts to preserve communities.

Section 74.18

Retaliation and the Expansion of Plains Warfare, 1864–1865

In the winter of 1864–1865, survivors of Sand Creek reached other Cheyenne and Arapaho camps on the Smoky Hill and Republican rivers, bringing news of the massacre. Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota leaders met in councils and decided to respond. The sacred pipe was circulated among the camps, and war parties formed to strike at the roads, stage stations, and settlements of the South Platte valley, which had been the base from which the attack on Sand Creek had been launched. The decisions were made by particular leaders and military societies in councils, not by entire nations. Some Cheyenne and Arapaho took part, while others, including those who still hoped for peace, did not.

The most prominent early action was the attack on Julesburg, a stage station and the nearby military post of Camp Rankin, on the South Platte in the northeastern corner of Colorado Territory. In early January 1865, a large force of Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota warriors approached the post. In an initial engagement on January 7, a small party was used to draw soldiers out of the fort, and the warriors killed a number of soldiers and civilians who followed. The attackers then plundered the stage station and supplies stored there. On February 2, 1865, a second major assault destroyed much of the old Julesburg station and nearby facilities; accounts should distinguish this event from the January 7 encounter and the broader series of winter raids. The campaign included attacks on other stations, the cutting of telegraph lines, and the disruption of the overland road, which carried mail and freight to Denver and the mining districts.

The effects were significant. The Platte road and telegraph line, vital to Denver's economy, were cut for periods, prices rose, and the territory felt isolated. Federal and territorial officials, who had contended that Sand Creek had been a decisive blow, found instead that the warfare was spreading. The raids also showed that Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota leaders could coordinate across nations to respond. These were decisions by identifiable leaders and societies, drawing on relationships that predated the massacre, and they were assessed by the participants as reasonable responses to a betrayal.

Historians distinguish between retaliation for Sand Creek and the continuing struggles over territorial control that preceded it. The Dog Soldiers and others had been fighting in the summer of 1864, and conflict over the roads and the Smoky Hill country had deep roots in the treaties of 1851 and 1861. Sand Creek intensified and broadened the conflict and gave it a powerful moral charge, but it did not create it from nothing. Equally, not every attack in 1865 should be read as vengeance for the massacre: some were continuations of earlier struggles over livestock, trade, and land.

The wider Plains wars that followed Sand Creek, including the conflicts along the Bozeman Trail, the later campaigns against the Southern Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, and Comanche, and the broader reservation consolidation, are the subject of subsequent chapters, among them Chapters 75 and 77 on Red Cloud's War and the Great Sioux War, and Chapter 84 on the reservation order. This chapter treats the 1865 retaliation only as the immediate consequence of the massacre and as context for the diplomacy that followed.

Cause and Effect

The Road to Sand Creek and Its Consequences

Long-term causes

Growing American migration across the Plains; treaties that assumed a few chiefs could bind whole nations; the gold rush and settler demand for land, roads, and protection; a territorial political economy that rewarded a hard line.

Short-term causes

Disputed 1861 cession; Civil War volunteer regiments looking for a mission; fear after the Dakota War; killings on the roads in 1864, including Lean Bear and the Hungate family; contradictory proclamations and orders.

Immediate trigger and context

The replacement of Wynkoop by Anthony; the approaching end of the Third Colorado's one-hundred-day enlistments; Chivington's decision to attack a camp that believed itself under protection.

Event

The dawn attack of November 29, 1864, on the Sand Creek village by about 675 soldiers, killing an estimated 230 people, most of them noncombatants.

Immediate effects

Loss of peace chiefs and families; destruction of lodges, food, and horses; retaliatory raids in winter 1864–1865; investigations and public controversy.

Long-term consequences

Deep distrust of United States promises; renewed treaty-making that left compensation unpaid; a shifting balance among Cheyenne and Arapaho political institutions; a lasting tradition of memory and commemoration.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Sand Creek. Territorial ambition, treaty disputes, wartime institutions, public fear, and individual command decisions interacted, and at several points alternatives existed, as the conduct of Wynkoop, Soule, and Cramer shows. The massacre was not inevitable, but it was made possible by structures that left a peace-seeking village exposed.

Section 74.19

The Little Arkansas Treaty of 1865: Reparations, Land Loss, and Renewed Diplomacy

The federal government, disturbed by the reports from Sand Creek and by the spreading warfare, sent commissioners to negotiate with Plains nations in the autumn of 1865. Meetings were held near the mouth of the Little Arkansas River, in present-day Kansas. Federal commissioners included John B. Sanborn and William S. Harney, joined by the frontiersman Kit Carson, the trader William Bent, and other officials. Cheyenne and Arapaho representatives, among them Black Kettle and Little Raven, took part. Many Cheyenne, particularly the Dog Soldiers, stayed away, and not all Cheyenne bands were represented.

The treaty signed on October 14, 1865, set aside new territory for the Cheyenne and Arapaho south of the Arkansas River, in an area that included parts of present-day Kansas and Indian Territory, and extinguished claims to the lands in Colorado that had been described in the Fort Wise Treaty. It also sought to restore peace and to provide for annuities and for the right of way for American roads. In these provisions, the treaty continued the pattern of cession in exchange for promises that had characterized the earlier agreements. The Indigenous negotiators accepted a loss of territory in a context of pressure, loss of leaders, and fear of further violence, and they were not able to bring all of their people into the agreement.

The treaty is unusual in acknowledging Sand Creek. It referred to the wrongs done to the people by the soldiers of the United States, and in Article VI it made provision for land grants and compensation to people who had lost relatives or property in the massacre. Article VI provided 320-acre patents to four named chiefs—Black Kettle, Seven Bulls, Little Robe, and Black White Man—and 160-acre grants to members of affected bands made widows or who had lost a parent, with restrictions on alienation. It separately promised compensation for property lost or destroyed, in securities, animals, goods, provisions, or other articles as selected by the Secretary of the Interior. These are the provisions of the ratified treaty text; they are not equivalent to proof that the promised benefits reached survivors.

Primary Source Window V

The 1865 Federal Investigations and Article VI of the Little Arkansas Treaty

Creators: Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War; the military commission at Denver; federal treaty commissioners and Cheyenne and Arapaho signatories · Date: investigative reports and testimony, 1865; treaty signed October 14, 1865, ratified May 1866, proclaimed February 1867 · Type: Congressional and military investigative records; treaty text

Context: The investigations were launched after accounts of Sand Creek reached the East, and they were intended to determine what had happened and who was responsible. The treaty was negotiated in the autumn, as the government sought to end the warfare and to respond to public criticism of the massacre.

Provenance: The investigative testimony and reports were printed in federal documents and are preserved in the National Archives. The treaty text is available in standard treaty collections, including the digital edition of Indian treaties at Oklahoma State University. The ratification and proclamation dates should be distinguished from the date of signature.

Paraphrase of content: The investigative records contain testimony about the village's status, the flags, the conduct of the attack, and the atrocities that followed, together with findings condemning Chivington and the attacking force. Article VI condemns the Sand Creek attacks and provides 320 acres to each of four named chiefs, 160 acres to those made widows or who lost a parent, and separate compensation for destroyed or seized property; Article V, by contrast, provides a different category of 640-acre grants to named people related by blood to Cheyenne and Arapaho families and must not be conflated with Article VI reparations.

Analysis: The investigative records are the most direct evidence of how federal authorities assessed the event in 1865, and the treaty shows the government's formal acknowledgment of wrongdoing. Together they illustrate the contrast between official acknowledgment and delivery: the National Park Service reports that the promised reparations were never paid.

Limitations: The investigations were shaped by political circumstances, took testimony largely from soldiers and officials, and did not result in prosecution. The treaty text records promises, not their fulfillment, and it reflects negotiation under duress by representatives who could not speak for all Cheyenne and Arapaho.

Perspective: Both sources reflect federal perspectives, though they include testimony and signatures from Indigenous participants. The experience of those who never received compensation is documented in later petitions and tribal histories.

The distinction between the treaty's formal promises and its implementation is essential. The National Park Service reports that the reparations promised in 1865 were never paid. The intended Article VI grants were not fulfilled in the manner promised, although the treaty also included distinct Article V land grants and the implementation history of those should not be casually combined with the unpaid Sand Creek reparations. The Senate ratified the treaty on May 22, 1866, subject to changes reflected in the published text—including a proviso in Article II concerning the location of a future reservation outside Kansas—and President Andrew Johnson proclaimed it on February 2, 1867; the sequence of signature in October 1865, ratification in May 1866, and proclamation in February 1867 shows how long Cheyenne and Arapaho signatories waited for the agreement to take effect, and how much could change in the interval. The process by which the amendments were presented to Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders is a subject for specialized study.

The treaty of 1865 did not end the conflict. The Dog Soldiers and others who had not signed continued to resist, and American expansion on the Plains continued. In October 1867, federal commissioners met Southern Plains nations again at Medicine Lodge Creek in Kansas, and the Cheyenne and Arapaho signed a further treaty that set out a new reservation. That agreement and the consolidation of the reservation system belong to later chapters. As an epilogue, in November 1868, Black Kettle was killed with his wife when Lieutenant Colonel George A. Custer's Seventh Cavalry attacked his camp on the Washita River, a reminder that the aftermath of Sand Creek did not bring peace.

The text of the Little Arkansas agreement requires unusually exact reading because several land provisions appear close together. Article V named people related to Cheyenne and Arapaho families for separate 640-acre grants; these were not the massacre reparations. Article VI explicitly repudiated the conduct at Sand Creek and identified four chiefs for 320-acre patents, plus 160-acre allotments for certain widowed or bereaved members of the affected bands, along with compensation for losses of property. Even the latter provisions were conditioned by federal control over land selection and the form of payment. Article II, meanwhile, described a new reservation and, as ratified, allowed presidential designation of another location outside Kansas with tribal assent. A student should therefore ask whether the treaty restored territorial security or reorganized dispossession while promising narrowly defined redress. Neither the striking language of governmental condemnation nor the precision of an acreage figure establishes actual delivery.

Section 74.20

Remembering Sand Creek: Sovereignty, Historical Truth, and the Legacy of Massacre

Sand Creek exposed the consequences of settler expansion, contradictory peace policies, military violence, and the failure of federal accountability. A treaty that lacked broad consent, a territorial government with an interest in land, a volunteer army looking for a mission, and officials who issued contradictory orders combined to leave a peace-seeking community exposed. The massacre was carried out by identifiable commanders and soldiers, and it was enabled by a system in which those responsible were not held to account. The pattern of official condemnation without punishment recurs elsewhere in this volume, and Chapter 90 returns to a similar episode at Wounded Knee in 1890.

The Cheyenne and Arapaho nations survived. Despite the loss of leaders and families, they continued to govern themselves, to practice their ceremonies, and to pursue their claims. Black Kettle's and Little Raven's people, and the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho who share the history, remain political communities today. Chapter 118 and the later chapters on sovereignty examine the legal and political forms through which those communities exercise self-government. The massacre transformed the relationship between these nations and the United States by deepening distrust, but it did not eliminate their political identity. Military destruction is not the same as the elimination of Indigenous sovereignty.

Descendants preserved the memory of Sand Creek through oral tradition, family history, and annual commemoration. In the late twentieth century, Cheyenne and Arapaho descendants and tribal governments worked to locate the massacre site, which had been uncertain because of the nature of the landscape and the passage of time, and to protect it. Their work, with that of historians and archaeologists, led to the identification of the site and to federal action. Congress authorized Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site in 2000, and, after land was acquired and agreements were completed, it was formally established in 2007. Tribal consultation shaped the site's interpretation, and the National Park Service's current account draws on Indigenous testimony as well as documentary sources. The history of the effort to locate and protect the site is the subject of Ari Kelman's study of memory, listed in the bibliography below.

Memory of Sand Creek has remained contested. For many years, Colorado's public commemorations of Chivington and Evans, in place names, monuments, and institutions, reflected an understanding of the massacre as a heroic episode or ignored it. On December 3, 2014, Governor John Hickenlooper formally apologized to Cheyenne and Arapaho descendants on behalf of Colorado; universities connected to Evans also commissioned investigations of his role. Chapter 132 examines such struggles over monuments and historical memory more broadly. The preservation of survivor testimony, the protection of the site, and the insistence of descendants that the history be told truthfully are part of a continuing assertion of sovereignty over the meaning of the past.

The history of Sand Creek ends, therefore, not with disappearance but with persistence. The massacre devastated communities and their peace leadership, and its aftermath, from the unpaid reparations of 1865 to the further treaties and wars that followed, reshaped Cheyenne and Arapaho life. Yet the nations survived, their diplomats continued to negotiate, and their descendants preserved the historical record. Sand Creek transformed Cheyenne and Arapaho relations with the United States, but it did not extinguish their sovereignty or their determination to preserve historical memory.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the expansion of the United States into Cheyenne and Arapaho homelands turned treaty disputes and regional warfare into the Sand Creek Massacre of November 29, 1864. It has argued that the massacre was the product of identifiable decisions within a structure of territorial ambition, contradictory policy, and military escalation, and that it was an attack on a village whose leaders had sought peace.

The Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho were independent political societies. Cheyenne government combined the Council of Forty-Four, whose chiefs were peacemakers, with military societies that organized warfare and policed the camp; Arapaho institutions differed. No single leader could commit either nation, a fact that American treaty-makers repeatedly ignored.

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 recognized extensive Cheyenne and Arapaho territory. The Fort Wise Treaty of 1861 reduced it to a small reserve, was signed by some leaders, ratified by the United States in August 1861, and proclaimed in December, but was disputed by many Cheyenne and Arapaho. The gap between legal ratification and political consent was a root of later conflict.

The Pike's Peak gold rush, the founding of Denver, and the creation of Colorado Territory placed a settler society in the middle of Indigenous homelands and generated demands for roads, land, and protection. Governor John Evans, who also served as superintendent of Indian affairs, headed a territorial government whose economic interests favored a hard line.

The Civil War created volunteer regiments and diverted regular troops. The victory at Glorieta Pass raised Chivington's reputation, fear after the Dakota War spread through the territory, and divided authority among regular officers, the governor, agents, and volunteers produced contradictory instructions.

In 1864, attacks on Cheyenne communities, the killing of Lean Bear on May 16, retaliatory raids, and the killing of the Hungate family on June 11 led to open war between particular bands and Colorado forces. Evans's proclamations of June 27 and August 11 invited peace-seeking people to come in and then authorized attacks on those classified as hostile.

Black Kettle, Left Hand, and others pursued peace as an active strategy, negotiating with Major Wynkoop and attending the Camp Weld conference on September 28. Participants left with incompatible understandings, and the replacement of Wynkoop by Major Anthony at Fort Lyon left the families near the post exposed.

On November 29, about 675 soldiers under Chivington attacked the Sand Creek village. The National Park Service estimates about 230 deaths, more than half women and children; other estimates differ and no single figure is exact. Atrocities followed the killing. Soule and Cramer refused to participate and recorded what they saw, and Cheyenne and Arapaho survivors preserved their own testimony.

Federal investigations in 1865 condemned the attack, but Chivington left service before a court-martial and was never convicted. Evans was removed in August 1865, Soule was killed in April, and the Little Arkansas Treaty of October 1865 acknowledged the massacre and promised reparations that were never paid. Cheyenne and Arapaho nations, devastated but not destroyed, continued to negotiate, resist, and remember, and their descendants helped bring about the creation of Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site.

Chapter Reflection

Sand Creek matters because it shows, in one morning, how settler expansion, disputed law, and military power could converge on a community that had done what officials asked of it. It also matters because the record of what happened was produced by people who refused to accept the official account, including soldiers who risked their careers and lives and survivors who preserved testimony across generations.

The chapter reveals processes that recur across this volume: treaties that claimed more consent than they had, volunteer forces whose purposes outran their mandate, officials who deplored violence in reports while enabling it by their orders, and investigations that condemned without punishing. These patterns shaped the reservation order examined in Chapter 84 and the wars of the Plains that followed in Chapters 75 through 81.

The chapter also shows continuity. Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders kept negotiating, ceremonial life continued, and families rebuilt. The unpaid reparations of 1865 became part of a long record of claims, and the memory of the massacre became part of how descendants understand their nations' history and rights.

For the larger textbook, Sand Creek is a reminder that the history of Indigenous North America cannot be told as a story of disappearance. The massacre devastated communities, but the nations it attacked remain, and the work of preserving and interpreting the site continues the exercise of sovereignty over history that this book has traced from its first chapter.
