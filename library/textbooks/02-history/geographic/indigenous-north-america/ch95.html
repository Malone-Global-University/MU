Reform Movements and the Crisis of Assimilation

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

By the 1920s, the policies that the United States and Canada had built to absorb Indigenous peoples into settler society were being challenged on every front: in council houses and Congress, in newspapers and courtrooms, in mission schools and medical reports. The challenge did not originate with sympathetic officials. It grew out of decades of Indigenous insistence that land, children, religion, and government were not the government's to take.

In November 1922, representatives of the Pueblo communities of New Mexico gathered in council to confront a bill then moving through the United States Senate. Introduced by Senator Holm O. Bursum, the proposal purported to settle the long-running disputes between Pueblo villages and non-Indigenous settlers who occupied or claimed land within Pueblo grants. The Pueblo leaders who assembled understood the measure as a threat to land that their communities had cultivated, irrigated, and governed for generations. Their decision to act collectively, and to carry their case to a national audience through public appeals and delegations, is one of the most important episodes of Indigenous political advocacy in the early twentieth century. It also illustrates the subject of this chapter: the point at which a policy system that had seemed securely entrenched began to lose its authority, because the people it was supposed to transform refused to accept its premises.

The policy system in question is the one traced in Chapters 85 through 88: the combination of allotment, administrative guardianship, compulsory schooling, and cultural suppression that the United States and Canada pursued from the late nineteenth century onward. Officials in both countries presented these measures as protective and civilizing. Land would be divided into individual holdings so that Indigenous owners could become self-supporting farmers. Children would be educated in distant institutions so that they could enter the wider economy. Federal agents would hold property in trust so that vulnerable owners were not defrauded. Ceremonies and political institutions regarded as obstacles to progress would be discouraged or prohibited. This chapter asks how those assumptions came under sustained pressure, who exerted that pressure, and with what results.

The answer requires attention to several kinds of actors whose interests overlapped but were never identical. Indigenous intellectuals and organizers, some of them products of the very schools they criticized, built national organizations and newspapers. Community councils and village governments defended land and ceremony in ways that rarely attracted the attention of national audiences. Non-Indigenous reform associations, churches, women's clubs, lawyers, journalists, and professional researchers entered the field as allies, patrons, and sometimes competitors. In Canada, First Nations leaders pursued land claims and political organization under legal restrictions that the government tightened in response. Each group defined the problem differently. Some wanted a more honest and efficient administration. Some wanted the coercive institutions dismantled. Some demanded that Indigenous governments and land rights be recognized as permanent features of the continent's political order.

The chapter's chronology runs principally from about 1900 to 1933, with essential precedents from the 1880s. Its culmination is the survey published in 1928 as The Problem of Indian Administration, usually called the Meriam Report. The report is among the most important documents of the period, but it should not be treated as the beginning of reform. Its authors drew on conditions that Indigenous critics had been describing for years, and one member of its survey staff, Henry Roe Cloud, was himself an Indigenous educator with a long record of institutional work. The report documented, with a thoroughness that earlier critics could not command, how far the results of federal administration departed from its stated purposes. It did not, however, resolve the question that Indigenous activists had been raising: whether a reformed federal bureaucracy could ever be an adequate substitute for Indigenous authority over Indigenous lands, children, and institutions.

Historians draw on an unusually varied evidentiary record for this period. Federal and Canadian departmental files, annual reports, and parliamentary committee proceedings preserve the official perspective and, in petitions and correspondence, a great deal of Indigenous testimony filtered through bureaucratic channels. Indigenous-edited periodicals, including the journals of the Society of American Indians and Carlos Montezuma's newsletter Wassaja, offer direct political argument. Reform organizations such as the Indian Rights Association and the American Indian Defense Association produced pamphlets, investigative reports, and correspondence that survive in institutional archives. Scientific and social surveys, medical reports, and legal records add systematic data. Each source type has limits that the chapter notes where they matter. Reformers' publications, for instance, were written to persuade, and official reports were written to defend or reform an administration.

A caution is needed about the word "reform." In the early twentieth century it described very different programs. For some advocates it meant eliminating corruption and raising standards within the existing system. For others it meant ending the system altogether and treating Indigenous people as individuals under ordinary law. For others it meant the restoration of collective land rights, community institutions, and political authority that the system had been designed to dismantle. Treating these as a single movement would obscure the real disagreements among participants. This chapter therefore distinguishes among administrative reform, the abolition of coercive institutions, and the restoration of Indigenous governmental authority, and it asks throughout which of these a given campaign, organization, or report actually advanced.

The chapter also marks a boundary. It does not narrate the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, which belongs to Chapter 97, or provide a full survey of the condition of Indigenous communities on the eve of the New Deal, the subject of Chapter 96. First Nations politics between the wars receives comprehensive treatment in Chapter 98; here, Canadian developments are examined for what they reveal about resistance to assimilationist administration. What this chapter establishes is that by 1933 confidence in the existing policy system had badly eroded, and that this erosion was the product of long Indigenous struggle as well as official investigation. It also establishes that a crisis of confidence in a policy is not the same thing as the creation of a different system, a distinction that governs the chapter's conclusion.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the crisis of assimilation is better understood as the result of decades of Indigenous opposition than as a sudden official discovery of policy failure.
  2. Distinguish among reform programs aimed at improving administration, abolishing coercive institutions, and restoring Indigenous governmental authority.
  3. Analyze the policy agenda, organizing methods, and internal disagreements of the Society of American Indians and the National Council of American Indians.
  4. Evaluate Carlos Montezuma's campaign against the Office of Indian Affairs and explain how his program differed from those of other Indigenous reformers.
  5. Explain how allotment, trust restrictions, and guardianship contributed to land loss, and distinguish opposition to compulsory land division from rejection of individual property as such.
  6. Trace the Pueblo campaign against the Bursum Bill and the passage of the Pueblo Lands Act of 1924, assessing both as examples of reform producing partial and unequal results.
  7. Assess the evidentiary value and limits of investigative reports such as Oklahoma's Poor Rich Indians.
  8. Compare reform strategies concerning schooling, religious freedom, and cultural suppression in the United States and Canada.
  9. Describe Peter Henderson Bryce's investigations of residential-school health and explain why official knowledge of conditions did not produce effective change.
  10. Interpret the 1927 Indian Act amendment on fundraising for land claims as an exercise of administrative power over Indigenous political organization.
  11. Analyze the Meriam Report's findings and recommendations, including its limits as an instrument of Indigenous self-determination.
  12. Evaluate why a crisis of confidence in assimilationist policy by 1933 did not by itself create a fundamentally different system of Indigenous governance.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who identified the failures of assimilationist policy first, and how did their criticisms differ from those of later official investigators?
  2. What were the differences between demanding a better administration, demanding the end of an administration, and demanding the restoration of Indigenous government?
  3. How did Indigenous organizations operate with limited funds, geographically scattered memberships, and unequal access to political power?
  4. Why could the same legislation be regarded as reform by some observers and as continued dispossession by others?
  5. How did alliances between Indigenous communities and non-Indigenous reform organizations both strengthen and complicate Indigenous leadership?
  6. What kinds of evidence were most persuasive to legislators and the public, and what kinds of Indigenous knowledge did official investigations overlook?
  7. Why did Canadian authorities respond to land-claims organizing by restricting its financing rather than by addressing its substance?
  8. In what respects did the Meriam Report challenge assimilationist assumptions, and in what respects did it preserve them?
  9. How does the distinction between a crisis of policy and a change of system help explain the situation in 1933?
  10. Whose reform stories are best preserved in the historical record, and whose local campaigns are harder to recover?

Historical Context

Students entering this chapter should bring with them the policy history established in Chapters 85 through 88. In the United States, the General Allotment Act of 1887 (the Dawes Act) authorized the division of reservation land into individual allotments, with surplus land opened to non-Indigenous purchase. Allotted land was ordinarily held in trust for a period, during which the federal government controlled its sale and lease. In Canada, the Indian Act, first consolidated in 1876 and repeatedly amended, defined legal "Indian" status, regulated reserve land, authorized the enfranchisement of individuals who gave up status, and vested extensive authority in the Department of Indian Affairs. Both countries operated systems of boarding and residential schools, many of them managed in partnership with churches, whose stated purpose was the transformation of Indigenous children into members of settler society.

Both systems rested on a theory of guardianship. Indigenous people were treated as wards whose property, education, and legal capacity were to be supervised until they were judged capable of managing their own affairs. This theory had a practical effect: it concentrated enormous discretion in the hands of agents, superintendents, and departmental officials, and it created a vocabulary of protection that could be invoked to justify measures Indigenous communities opposed. It also gave non-Indigenous reformers a standard against which to measure the government's performance. If the government claimed to be a guardian, critics could ask whether it was in fact protecting the people and property in its care.

Reform of Indian policy was not new in 1900. Since the 1880s, organizations such as the Indian Rights Association, founded in 1882, and annual gatherings such as the Lake Mohonk Conference had brought non-Indigenous reformers together to press for allotment, schooling, and citizenship. These reformers were frequently the architects of assimilationist policy rather than its critics. What changed over the following decades was the kind of criticism that became audible. As the consequences of allotment accumulated, as medical and economic data on Indigenous communities became available, and as Indigenous people trained in schools and professions began to speak publicly in their own voices, the terms of the debate shifted. The question was no longer whether Indigenous people should be assimilated but whether the methods being used were succeeding, whether they were just, and who should decide.

The wider political environment also changed. The Progressive Era produced a broader culture of investigation and administrative professionalization, with new expectations that public institutions be studied, measured, and made efficient. The First World War, examined in Chapter 94, brought Indigenous military service and wartime mobilization that complicated arguments about dependence and citizenship. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924, treated in Chapter 93, extended United States citizenship to Indigenous people born in the country who were not already citizens, but it did not end federal guardianship or settle the legal status of Indigenous governments. In Canada, the postwar years brought political organizing among veterans and First Nations leaders and intensified official efforts to tighten administrative control. Indigenous economic circumstances, discussed in Chapter 91, and the emergence of Indigenous intellectuals and organizations, discussed in Chapter 92, supplied both the grievances and the personnel of reform.

Finally, students should keep in view the limits of the sources. Much of the documentary record of reform was generated by non-Indigenous institutions, and many Indigenous actors appear in it through translated or mediated statements. Local campaigns, especially those led by women and community councils, often left thin archives. The chapter therefore proceeds with attention to how historians recover Indigenous leadership from records that were not always created to preserve it.

Division I · Sections 95.1–95.4 The Emergence of Organized Indigenous Reform Criticism of assimilation, national advocacy, and Indigenous-directed organizations
Section 95.1

The Crisis of Assimilation: Federal Policy and Indigenous Opposition, 1880–1920

The assimilation policies of the late nineteenth century rested on a set of linked assumptions. The first was that Indigenous societies were at an earlier stage of a single path of human development and could be moved along it by deliberate intervention. The second was that the decisive agents of change were private property and formal education: an Indigenous family settled on its own farm, with children schooled in English and in the habits of wage and household labor, would shed collective ownership, kin-based obligations, and ceremonial life. The third was that the transition required supervision. Until Indigenous people demonstrated "competency," the government would hold their land in trust, manage their funds, and direct their institutions. Allotment, boarding and residential schooling, and administrative guardianship were therefore treated as parts of a single program.

Officials justified the program in the language of protection and preparation. Allotment would shield Indigenous families from the pressure of collective land sales and would prepare them for citizenship. Schools would equip children for a changing economy. Trusteeship would prevent exploitation. Yet each measure also restricted Indigenous control. Allotment transferred decisions about land from councils and kin groups to federal officials and individual owners acting under federal supervision, and the "surplus" left after allotment was sold to outsiders. Schooling removed children from the authority of families and communities. Guardianship placed Indigenous property, income, and legal standing under the discretion of agents. Prohibitions on ceremonies and dances, and the displacement or overriding of Indigenous governments, extended the same logic to religion and politics. The central contradiction of the system, which later critics seized upon, was that a policy justified as preparation for independence required extensive and continuing dependence on the very officials who administered it.

Opposition existed from the beginning. Indigenous communities petitioned Congress and the Canadian Parliament, sent delegations to Washington and Ottawa, refused to cooperate with enrollment and allotment surveys, withheld children from schools or removed them, and continued to meet in councils whose decisions agents had declared void. Defense of land took many forms. Some communities used treaties and prior agreements as legal arguments, some retained lawyers when they could afford them, and some relied on political pressure through sympathetic members of Congress. Resistance to the suppression of ceremonies was often quiet, with communities modifying the timing, location, and public character of rituals rather than abandoning them. Chapters 85 through 89 describe these developments in different contexts. For present purposes the important point is that Indigenous criticism of assimilation did not need to be invented by twentieth-century reformers. It was already a continuing feature of political life, though it was often unrecorded or recorded only in the files of the officials it was directed against.

What changed between 1880 and 1920 was the character and visibility of that criticism. The first generation of boarding-school students became adults, and some became lawyers, physicians, clergy, teachers, writers, and clerks, with the literacy, contacts, and professional standing to address national audiences. The consequences of allotment became visible in reduced land bases and in the growth of leasing and sale. Public and congressional attention grew as investigators reported on economic and health conditions. The fragmentary organization of earlier decades gave way to national and regional groups that pooled resources, published newspapers, and lobbied legislators directly.

For analytical clarity, this chapter distinguishes three broad orientations within the movement for change. The first sought administrative reform: better-qualified personnel, honest accounting, adequate funding, humane schools, improved health services, and an end to corruption and political patronage in the Indian Service. Reformers of this kind generally accepted the premise that the government would continue to administer Indigenous affairs and that assimilation remained the long-term goal. The second sought the abolition of coercive institutions: an end to guardianship, to the compulsory features of schooling, and to the special bureaucracy itself, so that Indigenous people would stand before the law as individuals. The third sought the restoration of Indigenous governmental authority: recognition of collective land rights, community control of education and religion, and the continuing legitimacy of Indigenous governments and treaty relationships.

These orientations were not always mutually exclusive, and individuals sometimes moved among them. A reformer who demanded that a corrupt agent be removed was practicing administrative reform; a petition against the sale of tribal lands, if it asserted the right of a nation to decide, belonged to the third category. But the distinctions mattered, because they led to different political alliances and different judgments of success. A reform that improved the quality of federal administration could leave intact the structure of dependence that critics in the second and third categories wanted to dismantle. Keeping these categories in view will allow students to evaluate each campaign, organization, and report in the chapter by what it actually sought and achieved rather than by the single label "reform."

Historical Vocabulary
Federal Paternalism

Definition. A system of government in which authority over a group's property, education, and legal affairs is held by officials who claim to act in that group's interest while limiting the group's own decision-making.

Historical context. Paternalism was the operating principle of United States and Canadian Indian administration in this period. Officials described Indigenous people as wards, dependents, or children of the state, and used this language to justify trust restrictions, control over funds, compulsory schooling, and restrictions on ceremony. Critics, Indigenous and non-Indigenous, increasingly used the same term to describe and condemn the system.

Why it matters. The concept explains why many Indigenous reformers objected not only to specific abuses but to the structure of guardianship itself. It also explains why some reformers who attacked corruption nonetheless defended administrative supervision.

Potential misunderstanding. Paternalism does not require ill will. Many officials and missionaries sincerely believed that they were protecting Indigenous people. Historians evaluate the system by its effects and structure of authority, not only by the intentions of those who administered it.

Section 95.2

The Society of American Indians and the Development of National Reform Politics

The Society of American Indians (SAI) was founded in 1911 at a meeting in Columbus, Ohio, associated with Ohio State University. Its founders were Indigenous professionals and intellectuals from many nations, including educators, physicians, clergy, writers, and government employees, along with a number of non-Indigenous associate members who were permitted to take part but not to hold the highest offices. Chapter 92 introduced its leading figures and intellectual world. This section examines the Society as a political organization: what it proposed, how it worked, and where it divided.

The Society's agenda reflected the priorities of a membership that was educated, often Christian, and committed to the idea that Indigenous people could and should speak for themselves. It pressed for improved education, for the opening of federal courts to Indigenous claims, for the extension of citizenship, and for reform of the Office of Indian Affairs. It sought to counter stereotypes in popular culture and to present Indigenous people as capable participants in modern life. Its methods were those of Progressive Era voluntary associations: annual conferences, published proceedings, committees, petitions, public addresses, and correspondence with members of Congress and officials of the Office of Indian Affairs. Its periodical publications, first the Quarterly Journal and later the American Indian Magazine, circulated its arguments and gave members a forum. Gertrude Bonnin, who served as the Society's secretary and edited the magazine for a time, was one of its most active organizers.

The Society was also a site of disagreement, and these disagreements were as historically significant as its achievements. Members differed over citizenship. Some regarded immediate citizenship as the proper remedy for dependence; others worried that citizenship on federal terms might undermine treaty rights and communal property, or leave guardianship intact in practice. They differed over the Office of Indian Affairs: some wished to reform the Bureau from within and cooperate with sympathetic officials, while others, notably Carlos Montezuma, regarded the institution as irreformable and called for its abolition. They differed over education, with some defending the opportunity that schooling provided and others criticizing its coercive and unequal features. They differed over religion, and especially over the use of peyote in ceremony, an issue examined in Section 95.12. And they differed over the future of reservations, which some viewed as zones of enforced dependence and others as the necessary base of Indigenous communities.

These disagreements reflected genuine differences in life experience as well as ideology. Members employed by the federal government had different incentives from those who stood outside it. Members from nations with treaty land bases weighed the risks of dissolving reservations differently from those from nations that had already lost much of their land. The Society's leadership was drawn disproportionately from educated, often Christian, often relatively mobile individuals, and it struggled to build deep roots in reservation communities. Its finances were precarious, dependent on dues and contributions from members who often had modest incomes. Because it had no electoral base or large membership, it relied on persuasion and on its standing as an Indigenous voice, a form of authority that officials sometimes welcomed and sometimes ignored.

The Society's annual meetings continued until 1923, and by then the organization had weakened through internal conflict, financial strain, and the departure or death of key figures. Its significance should not be measured only by what it accomplished legislatively. It established that national intertribal organization was possible, trained a cohort of activists, circulated Indigenous arguments in print, and modeled forms of political engagement that its successors, including the National Council of American Indians, would carry forward. It also demonstrated the limits of an organization led by a professional elite whose members did not always agree on the fundamental question of how far assimilation should go.

Section 95.3

Carlos Montezuma, Wassaja, and the Campaign against Federal Paternalism

Carlos Montezuma, who bore the Yavapai name Wassaja, was one of the most uncompromising critics of the Office of Indian Affairs. Born around the mid-1860s, he was separated from his family in childhood and raised outside Yavapai society, and he became a physician, practicing in Chicago and for a period serving as a physician at a federal Indian school. Chapter 92 sketched his life. His political significance lies in the program he developed during the 1910s and early 1920s and in the journalism through which he promoted it.

In April 1916 Montezuma began publishing Wassaja, a newsletter issued from Chicago that he wrote and edited largely himself. Over the following years it carried editorials, reprinted documents, and commentary attacking the Indian Bureau, federal guardianship, the reservation system, and what he regarded as the economic and moral dependence they produced. Its identifiable issues allow historians to trace his arguments in his own words and to see how he used the form of a newsletter to build a continuing case. Where the Society of American Indians relied on conferences and committees, Montezuma used the press as an instrument of sustained administrative criticism, and in doing so established Indigenous journalism as an enduring tool of political critique.

Montezuma's argument was radical in its logic. He contended that the Bureau had a stake in perpetuating Indigenous dependence, since its employees' positions depended on a population defined as needing supervision. He denounced guardianship as a form of bondage, criticized the reservations as institutions that confined people and obstructed their economic opportunity, and argued that individual liberty, citizenship, and equal treatment before the law were the proper remedies. He condemned corruption and the cost of the administrative apparatus. His preferred solution was the dissolution of the Bureau and the integration of Indigenous people into the ordinary institutions of the country. He supported specific communities when he judged them wronged, and he took part in efforts to defend the interests of Yavapai people at Fort McDowell in Arizona, a case in which land and water were at stake.

His position differed sharply from those of other reformers. Fellow members of the Society of American Indians who favored working with the Bureau regarded his demand for abolition as impractical or dangerous, worrying that removing federal protections without alternative safeguards would expose communities to rapid dispossession. Reformers who defended tribal institutions and collective landholding viewed his emphasis on individual liberty as insufficiently attentive to the collective rights of Indigenous nations. Montezuma, for his part, regarded those who sought to improve the Bureau as accomplices in its survival. These disagreements were not simply personal. They reflected competing diagnoses of the problem. Was the cause of Indigenous poverty and powerlessness the Bureau and its guardianship, or the loss of land and institutional capacity that guardianship had facilitated? Montezuma emphasized the former; communal-rights advocates emphasized the latter.

Montezuma died in January 1923, and the newsletter did not long outlast its founder's active direction. His legacy is complex. His attacks on the Bureau contributed to the climate in which official administration came to be questioned. His individualist program, however, could be turned to purposes he might not have endorsed, since arguments for removing federal supervision were later used by advocates of policies that stripped Indigenous nations of recognized status, a development treated in Chapter 104. Students should therefore avoid reading Montezuma as a straightforward precursor of later self-determination. He sought liberty from paternalism, but he did not generally seek the restoration of collective Indigenous government, and that distinction is central to the comparative analysis in Section 95.16.

Section 95.4

Indigenous Political Networks, Women's Leadership, and New Reform Organizations

Reform politics operated through networks that extended well beyond the Society of American Indians. A single campaign might draw on petitions from a reservation council, a letter-writing effort by a church or club, legal assistance from an attorney in a distant city, and lobbying by an Indigenous activist in Washington. These networks were built on correspondence, personal acquaintance, and shared institutional ties, including school alumni connections, church affiliations, and membership in clubs. They were often unstable, dependent on a handful of individuals, and constrained by distance and cost. But they allowed communities that were geographically dispersed and politically unequal to coordinate pressure on federal institutions.

Women played central roles in this activity, though their contributions are less fully recorded than those of male officeholders. Indigenous women served as writers, secretaries, investigators, and organizers, and non-Indigenous women's organizations, notably the General Federation of Women's Clubs, became important participants in Indian policy debates in the 1920s. Gertrude Bonnin (Zitkala-Ša), a Yankton Dakota writer, musician, and activist, was the most prominent Indigenous woman in national reform politics. She worked with the Society of American Indians, wrote and spoke widely, and in the early 1920s became associated with the General Federation of Women's Clubs' Indian welfare work. Women's organizations provided publicity, fundraising, and a political constituency that cut across the lines of existing reform groups, and they brought pressure to bear through channels, such as club newsletters and petitions, that male-dominated institutions often neglected.

In 1926 Zitkala-Ša and her husband, Raymond T. Bonnin, established the National Council of American Indians (NCAI) in Washington. It should not be confused with the National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, a different organization examined in Chapter 107. The 1926 council was conceived as an Indigenous-led body to coordinate action across tribal communities, to lobby Congress, and to support legal and investigative work on behalf of Indigenous people. Gertrude Bonnin served as its president for most of its existence. Its program emphasized citizenship rights, protection of property, opposition to exploitation of Indigenous resources, and the right of Indigenous communities to be heard in matters affecting them. It sought to link local grievances to national advocacy and to give those grievances a continuing institutional voice in the capital.

The organization's accomplishments were real but modest by comparison with its ambitions. It conducted investigations, gathered testimony, wrote to officials and legislators, and made use of the Bonnins' connections in reform and women's organizations. Its limitations, however, were also characteristic of the period. It operated with scarce funding, depended on the energy of a small group, and lacked the ability to compel federal attention. Its members had competing interests: communities with treaty rights, communities seeking compensation for claims, and individuals concerned with citizenship did not always agree on priorities. It faced unequal political access in a capital where non-Indigenous organizations with larger budgets and established congressional relationships often had the advantage. The history of the NCAI demonstrates both the possibility of Indigenous-directed national organization and the structural obstacles that organizations without resources or electoral leverage encountered.

Section 95.5

Allotment under Attack: Land Loss, Guardianship, and Failed Economic Policy

Chapter 86 explained how allotment worked. This section examines how criticism of it developed. The General Allotment Act of 1887 and related legislation divided reservation land into individual parcels, assigned them to enrolled members, and opened the remaining "surplus" to non-Indigenous purchase. Allottees held their land in trust for a period, ordinarily twenty-five years, during which it could not be sold or taxed without federal permission. The theory was that individual ownership and farming would foster self-sufficiency and citizenship. In practice, the system produced a decline in the Indigenous land base on a scale that even many of its original supporters came to regard as a failure. Estimates of the total loss from allotment and related policies are large and vary by method of calculation; the important point for this chapter is that the effects were widely visible by the 1910s and 1920s.

Several mechanisms drove the loss. The sale of surplus lands removed the portion of reservations that had not been assigned to individuals. The Burke Act of 1906 allowed the Secretary of the Interior to issue fee patents, ending trust protection, to individuals judged competent, and later administrative campaigns accelerated such determinations, with the result that many owners could sell, mortgage, or lose land to taxation. Leasing arrangements, which the government encouraged when allottees were deemed unable or unwilling to farm, often placed land under the control of non-Indigenous lessees. Inheritance fragmented parcels into ever smaller undivided interests, since trust land passed to multiple heirs and became increasingly difficult to use or manage. In some regions, irrigation costs and the inadequacy of credit and equipment made farming allotments impracticable. The combined result was that land intended to support independent Indigenous farmers frequently supported them poorly or passed out of their hands.

Criticism came from several directions. Some Indigenous leaders objected to the compulsory division of collectively held lands and to the process of enrollment that determined who received allotments. Others objected to the way the policy was applied: to forced competency determinations, to the sale of land over owners' objections, and to the neglect of allottees who needed assistance. Non-Indigenous reformers, including some who had supported allotment, began to document its failures. By the 1920s, researchers and investigators were assembling evidence that allotment had not produced the prosperous Indigenous farming communities that its proponents had promised. It is important to distinguish these criticisms. Indigenous political leaders did not all reject private ownership of land. Many Indigenous families valued secure individual holdings, and some leaders, like Montezuma, saw individual property rights as essential to liberty. What many contested was the compulsory division and alienation of collectively held lands, the loss of community control over land use, and the government's discretion over what owners could do with their own property.

The contradictions of federal trusteeship lay at the center of the critique. The government claimed to hold Indigenous land in trust to protect it. Yet the same government administered the policies that facilitated transfer outside Indigenous communities: it approved leases, issued patents, determined competency, and managed the sale of inherited land. A trustee who is also the instrument of the beneficiary's dispossession is in an obviously conflicted position, and critics increasingly pointed this out. The problem was not simply that individual officials were dishonest, though some were. It was that the structure of the system placed decisions about Indigenous property in the hands of officials who answered to Congress and to political and economic pressures from non-Indigenous constituencies rather than to Indigenous owners.

This analysis helps explain why proposals to reform allotment took different forms. Some reformers wanted to slow or halt the issuance of fee patents and to protect trust status more consistently. Others wanted to end further allotment and restore tribal ownership of unallotted lands. Others demanded compensation for lost land or the return of surplus lands. Still others, wary of federal supervision, wanted owners to have greater freedom to manage their property. These alternatives anticipated the debates of the early 1930s and are examined in the Meriam Report's treatment of allotment in Section 95.18. The significance of the critique, however, is not merely that it prepared the ground for later policy. It demonstrated that the central instrument of assimilation had produced results at odds with its purposes, and that Indigenous people and their allies were prepared to say so publicly.

Section 95.6

Pueblo Resistance to the Bursum Bill, 1922–1923

The Pueblo communities of New Mexico occupy a distinctive place in the legal history of Indigenous land. Their villages, with long traditions of irrigated farming and community government, had been recognized under Spanish and Mexican law as holding land grants, and the United States, after the annexation of New Mexico in 1848, treated the Pueblos in a manner that left their legal status uncertain for decades. Pueblo people were not placed on reservations in the way that many other nations were, and they held their lands under community grants rather than through allotment. In an 1876 decision, United States v. Joseph, the Supreme Court held that the Pueblos were not "Indians" in the sense of the federal statutes protecting Indigenous land from alienation, which had the effect of leaving non-Indigenous settlers' claims on Pueblo land legally secure in many cases. Over the following decades, Hispanic and Anglo-American settlers occupied or purchased land within Pueblo grants, and disputes over title multiplied.

In 1913, in United States v. Sandoval, the Supreme Court reversed course. It concluded that the Pueblos were Indigenous communities under federal guardianship and that Congress could legitimately protect their land. The decision cast doubt on the titles of non-Indigenous occupants, whose acquisitions might be held void, and it set the stage for conflict over how the resulting uncertainty should be resolved. Non-Indigenous settlers, who had lived on and improved land for years, regarded their claims as legitimate and feared dispossession. Pueblo communities regarded the encroachments as invasions of land to which they held long-established rights. Legislation was needed, and the question was whose interests it would serve.

Senator Holm O. Bursum of New Mexico introduced a bill in 1922 that purported to settle these disputes. Its supporters presented it as a practical compromise that would resolve title uncertainty. Critics argued that it would in practice confirm many non-Indigenous claims, place a burden on Pueblo communities to prove their title, and apply state-law doctrines of possession in ways that favored claimants who had occupied land over time. They also objected that it would reduce the protection that Pueblos had under federal law and potentially expose additional land and water rights to loss, since the Pueblos' agricultural base depended on irrigated lands along the river valleys where non-Indigenous settlement was concentrated. The measure, in short, threatened to turn a legal victory in Sandoval into a defeat in the legislative arena.

The Pueblo response was collective. In November 1922, leaders from the Pueblos gathered in council and agreed to oppose the bill together, an act of political coordination that crossed the linguistic and institutional differences among the villages. They issued a public appeal addressed to the people of the United States, prepared petitions, and sent delegations to present their case. The appeal, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, framed the issue as one of fundamental justice, linking the defense of land to the survival of Pueblo communities and their ways of life. A delegation of Pueblo leaders traveled to eastern cities in 1923 to speak to audiences and officials, bringing the dispute before a national public that had largely been unaware of it. The form of the campaign, a coordinated council of governments acting on behalf of their communities, was itself an assertion of continuing Indigenous political authority.

Non-Indigenous allies took part in substantial numbers. John Collier, who had come to New Mexico in 1920 and been drawn to Pueblo life, became a leading publicist and lobbyist for the Pueblo cause and, with allies, helped form the American Indian Defense Association in 1923. Stella Atwood of the General Federation of Women's Clubs mobilized the Federation's membership; artists and writers in Santa Fe and Taos contributed publicity; and organizations including the Indian Rights Association and others in the national reform community took positions. These allies brought valuable resources: access to newspapers, to national organizations, to lawyers, and to politicians. But the historical record of the campaign shows that it was driven by Pueblo decisions. The Pueblo councils determined the strategy, made the public appeals, and organized the delegations. A narrative that credits the outcome principally to Collier or other outside advocates misrepresents the sequence of events and diminishes Pueblo political agency. A more accurate account treats the campaign as a collaboration in which Pueblo leadership defined the objective and non-Indigenous allies amplified it, while recognizing that those allies also had their own agendas, which did not always coincide with the preferences of Pueblo communities.

The campaign succeeded in blocking the bill in the form that Bursum had proposed. That outcome was significant, and it demonstrated that organized Indigenous opposition could defeat legislation that had seemed likely to pass. But the underlying problem of competing claims remained to be settled, and the compromise that followed, examined in Section 95.7, preserved important elements of the loss that Pueblo leaders had sought to prevent.

The original November 5, 1922 Santo Domingo council was not an abstract gathering of reform professionals. Delegates from Pueblo communities considered the proposal together and agreed on a public appeal that could be carried beyond New Mexico. An independently published scholarly study identifies the meeting, the collective deliberation, and the appeal's subsequent circulation. That evidence is sufficient to document the campaign without pretending that every surviving transcription is a photographic facsimile of the document approved at the council. The appropriate source-study method is to identify the appeal by its historical title, distinguish the Pueblo delegates' collective position from the language supplied by later editors, and verify any quotation against a clearly identified transcription before publication.

Section 95.7

The Pueblo Lands Act of 1924: Reform, Compromise, and Its Limitations

After the defeat of the Bursum Bill, Congress turned to alternative legislation. The result was the Pueblo Lands Act of June 1924, which established a procedure for resolving the competing claims. The Act created a Pueblo Lands Board to investigate and determine, village by village, which non-Indigenous claims within Pueblo grants should be recognized and which should be rejected. Claimants who could demonstrate possession for specified periods, under conditions the Act defined, could have their titles confirmed; claims that failed were to be extinguished. The Act also provided for compensation: where the Board recognized non-Indigenous title to land that had belonged to a Pueblo, the Pueblo was to receive payment for the loss, and where the Board rejected claims, the claimant could in some circumstances be compensated for improvements.

Many reformers regarded the Act as a substantial improvement over the Bursum proposal. It placed the determination of claims in a federal body rather than leaving it to state courts and local procedures, it removed some of the features of the earlier bill that had been most objectionable to Pueblo advocates, and it recognized the principle that Pueblo land was federally protected. Pueblo leaders and their allies had succeeded in changing the character of the legislation. Their campaign had demonstrated that Indigenous political mobilization could alter the content of federal law, an achievement of lasting significance.

Yet the Act's operation shows the limits of the reform. The Board's decisions confirmed many non-Indigenous claims, including claims to valuable irrigated land, and the compensation awarded to Pueblos for lost land was frequently regarded as inadequate by Pueblo communities and their advocates. Disputes continued over valuation and over the lands the Board had treated as lost. The Department of the Interior's own historical review documents how the Act ultimately enabled non-Indigenous claims to valuable Pueblo lands and produced compensation controversies that required additional congressional action in the following decade. In effect, the Act resolved the legal uncertainty by transferring much of the contested land out of Pueblo hands, while providing a form of payment that did not restore the loss of water, irrigated fields, and the ability to use them.

The Act therefore illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout the history of Indigenous reform: political victory could produce partial benefit while preserving structural inequalities. Pueblo advocacy blocked a worse alternative and secured a federal procedure. But the procedure was designed by Congress, administered by a federal board, and constructed around a framework that treated the claims of non-Indigenous occupants as legitimate subjects of accommodation. A historian evaluating the Act must hold both facts in view: it was a measurable improvement over the proposal that preceded it, and it was a settlement that cost Pueblo communities land and water they had sought to keep. Assessing its operation, rather than assuming that its enactment resolved the controversy, is essential to understanding what the reform did and did not accomplish.

A legal settlement of possession does not, by itself, settle a community's relationship to its landscape. Irrigation depended on the location and reliability of canals, diversion structures, and water access as well as on the number of acres formally retained. The taking of a relatively small tract at the edge of a village could impair access to adjoining fields or to a route that connected homes and agricultural land. For that reason a simple ledger of acreage and compensation cannot capture all the losses that might follow from the confirmation of outside titles. The Pueblo controversy must be read as a dispute over the integrity of interconnected land and water systems, not merely an ordinary contest between individual real-estate claimants.

The legislative record also makes it possible to separate the statute's stated purpose, its procedural machinery, and the evidence of implementation. Section 6 of the 1924 law made compensation possible for losses attributable to the United States' failure to bring timely actions to recover Pueblo property. Later congressional action responded to the inadequacy of board awards: the legislation of May 31, 1933 authorized additional compensation. That later measure is useful here only as an indicator that the 1924 settlement had not adequately repaired earlier losses. It did not retroactively restore every field, watercourse, or aspect of local jurisdiction. Students should compare the text of the 1924 Act with Interior Department histories and Pueblo community accounts before deciding whether it constitutes a success or an incomplete remedy.

From the Bursum Bill to the Pueblo Lands Act

Long-term causes

Spanish and Mexican land grants; the legal ambiguity of Pueblo status after 1848; the 1876 Joseph decision; decades of non-Indigenous settlement and purchase within Pueblo grants.

Short-term causes

The 1913 Sandoval ruling, which cast doubt on non-Indigenous titles; pressure from settlers and New Mexico politicians for a legislative resolution; limited federal attention to Pueblo interests.

Immediate trigger and event

Introduction of the Bursum Bill in 1922; the formation of a Pueblo council and public appeal; a national campaign culminating in the bill's defeat and the passage of the Pueblo Lands Act in 1924.

Immediate effects

Creation of the Pueblo Lands Board; confirmation of many non-Indigenous claims; compensation to Pueblos for lost lands; a precedent for Indigenous national advocacy.

Long-term consequences: Continuing disputes over valuation and compensation; later congressional action to address shortfalls; a reinforced alliance between Pueblo leaders and national reform organizations; and a model of collective Indigenous political action studied in later struggles.

Historical complexity: The outcome cannot be attributed to any single factor. Pueblo political organization, the judicial history of Sandoval, national publicity, congressional politics in New Mexico, and the position of non-Indigenous settlers all contributed to a compromise that no party fully controlled.

Section 95.8

Oklahoma's Poor Rich Indians (1924): Guardianship, Oil Wealth, and Legalized Exploitation

In 1924 the Indian Rights Association published a report titled Oklahoma's Poor Rich Indians, prepared by Gertrude Bonnin (Zitkala-Ša), Charles H. Fabens, and Matthew K. Sniffen. Bonnin served as the representative of the Indian Welfare Committee of the General Federation of Women’s Clubs; Fabens, an attorney, represented the American Indian Defense Association; and Sniffen was secretary of the Indian Rights Association, which published their report. The associations were cooperating investigators, not three authors employed by a single organization. The report examined conditions in Oklahoma, where members of the Five Tribes and other Indigenous landowners held allotments that, in many cases, included land with oil and other mineral value. Its title captured the contradiction at the center of the investigation: people whose land generated considerable wealth were, in many cases, impoverished, and their property was passing into other hands.

The mechanisms of exploitation that the investigators described centered on guardianship. Under Oklahoma law and federal supervision, minors and certain adults, particularly those classified as incompetent or holding restricted allotments, could be placed under guardians appointed by county courts. Guardians controlled the property and income of their wards, and fees and expenses were paid from the estates. The report documented, through case examples and testimony, arrangements in which guardians, attorneys, and others took substantial portions of the wealth of Indigenous owners, in some cases through excessive fees, mismanagement, or collusion. It argued that the system placed vulnerable owners at the mercy of local interests whose incentives were to maximize their own gain, and that existing safeguards, including federal supervision, were inadequate or ineffective. The report's subtitle and rhetoric characterized the arrangement as graft that operated within the law.

The investigators' method combined field visits, interviews, examination of court records and guardianship files, and the gathering of individual case histories. This approach had a particular political value: it replaced general assertions with detailed evidence that could be examined and cited. Detailed investigative reports of this kind allowed reformers to present Congress and the public with specific names, amounts, and circumstances, and to argue that the problems arose from systemic features of guardianship rather than isolated misconduct.

Historians nonetheless approach the report with care. As an advocacy document produced by reform organizations, it was designed to persuade, and its rhetoric was deliberately forceful. Its detailed cases illustrate patterns, but illustrative cases are not a statistical measure of how widespread abuse was, and individual allegations require corroboration from court records, government files, or other independent evidence before they can be treated as established fact. The report's strength lies in the cumulative weight of its documentation and in its identification of structural problems; its limits lie in the selectivity inherent in an investigation designed to expose abuse. Students should therefore distinguish the documented allegations and illustrative cases presented in the report from conclusions about the scale and distribution of exploitation that would require systematic evidence beyond the report itself.

The report also reveals the role of Indigenous women in exposing corruption. Bonnin's involvement connected the investigation to her wider work in Indigenous organizations and women's clubs and gave the report a measure of Indigenous authorship in a field where non-Indigenous reformers often dominated. Her participation illustrates how Indigenous women used investigative research and publication as political tools, translating local observations into evidence suitable for congressional hearings and press attention. The report contributed to a growing body of exposure that, together with the Meriam survey, undermined the argument that federal trusteeship was functioning as a protective institution.

The report's organizational history is revealing. The Indian Rights Association had initiated the inquiry into Oklahoma probate practices; the General Federation of Women's Clubs supplied Bonnin to the investigation; and the American Indian Defense Association selected Fabens. Their cooperation joined distinct bodies with overlapping but not identical reform programs. Bonnin brought Indigenous political experience and networks developed through women's organizations, while the participating associations could distribute a compact report to audiences familiar with legal reform and legislative hearings. Its circulation was therefore part of the campaign, not an accidental consequence of discovering wrongdoing. Yet the collaboration also raises a question about representation: when an Indigenous person's account was translated into a lawyer's case summary, who chose which grievance appeared and how it was described?

Section 95.9

Criticism of Boarding Schools and the Demand for Educational Reform

Chapters 87 and 88 examined federal Indian schooling and the experiences of children and families within it. This section is concerned with a different question: how organized criticism of the system developed and what it demanded. By the 1910s and 1920s, the boarding-school model that Richard Henry Pratt had popularized at Carlisle, which closed in 1918, had spread into a network of federal, mission, and contract institutions. Criticism of this network came from Indigenous parents and community leaders, from teachers and physicians within the Indian Service, from reform associations, and from researchers who began to measure conditions in schools.

The objections were numerous. Schools were chronically underfunded, and food, clothing, and medical care were frequently inadequate. Overcrowding encouraged the spread of tuberculosis, trachoma, influenza, and other diseases, as medical investigators on both sides of the border documented. Children were required or pressured to attend and were sometimes taken from families by compulsion. Discipline could be harsh and coercive. Because schools relied on student labor to operate farms, kitchens, laundries, and workshops, the line between instruction and unpaid work was often blurred, and the vocational training offered was frequently limited to trades with few prospects, particularly for girls, whose training emphasized domestic service. The suppression of Indigenous languages and the prohibition of ceremonial and cultural practices were central features of the system and a continuing source of grievance for families.

Distinguishing the experiences of individual children, covered in Chapters 87 and 88, from the organized efforts to change school policy is important. Children and families experienced schooling in intensely personal ways, including loss, hardship, achievement, and resistance. Organized criticism translated some of these experiences into arguments about institutions and policy. Reformers collected data on mortality and health, compared school budgets with the costs of adequate food and medical care, and presented findings to Congress and the public. Indigenous parents and leaders made their own representations, sometimes through petitions, sometimes through refusal to send children, and sometimes through requests for local day schools that would keep children within their communities.

Reformers did not agree on what should replace or improve the system. One position held that federal boarding schools should be maintained but improved through better funding, qualified staff, adequate nutrition, health care, and a curriculum suited to students' needs. Another argued that education should shift from distant boarding schools to day schools located in communities, preserving family ties and reducing the health risks of congregate living. A third favored integration into public schools where Indigenous children could attend with their non-Indigenous neighbors, and proposed that states or localities take over responsibilities for Indigenous education, a position that raised questions about funding and about the quality of local public schooling. A fourth, grounded in Indigenous demands for control, held that communities themselves should direct the education of their children, with curricula that respected Indigenous languages and cultures. The Meriam Report, discussed in Section 95.18, incorporated elements of the first three positions, and it did not endorse community control in the sense that Indigenous nations later demanded.

The debate over schooling thus raised the wider question of Indigenous authority over children and learning. If the state claimed the right to compel attendance, determine curriculum, and regulate language, then education was an instrument of the guardianship system rather than a service to Indigenous communities. Criticism of schooling consequently overlapped with criticism of administration: reforming the schools without altering who controlled them would not address the objection that the schools were being operated for purposes communities had not chosen.

Historical Vocabulary
Administrative Reform

Definition. A program of change that seeks to improve the performance, honesty, and efficiency of existing institutions without altering the underlying structure of authority.

Historical context. In Indian affairs, administrative reform meant measures such as hiring qualified personnel, replacing patronage with merit-based appointments, increasing appropriations, improving school food and medical care, and auditing the handling of Indigenous funds. Its advocates included many reformers within and outside the Indian Service and was the dominant strategy of Progressive Era investigators.

Why it matters. Administrative reform could produce real improvements in material conditions, but it left unresolved the question of who held authority. Indigenous critics often argued that better administration of a paternalistic system was still paternalism.

Potential misunderstanding. Administrative reform is not necessarily conservative or insincere. In many cases it addressed urgent problems of health and survival. Its limits lay in its relationship to Indigenous political authority, not in an absence of good intention.

Section 95.10

Henry Roe Cloud, Elizabeth Bender Cloud, and Indigenous Educational Leadership

Henry Roe Cloud, a Ho-Chunk (Winnebago) educator, and Elizabeth Bender Cloud, an Ojibwe educator, offer an example of Indigenous-led institutional reform within a system dominated by government and religious bodies. Roe Cloud was educated at mission and preparatory schools and at Yale University, where he earned his undergraduate degree in 1910 and pursued further study. He took the name Roe from Walter C. Roe, a missionary who had supported his schooling. His educational experience convinced him that Indigenous students could succeed in rigorous academic programs and that existing institutions underestimated them.

In 1915 Roe Cloud founded the Roe Indian Institute in Wichita, Kansas, a college-preparatory school for Indigenous boys renamed the American Indian Institute around 1920–1921. Its objectives were to prepare students academically for higher education and professional life, to cultivate leadership and a sense of responsibility to their communities, and to demonstrate that Indigenous young people were capable of advanced study. The Institute was a small institution, supported by private donations and church connections rather than by federal appropriations, and it depended on fundraising among supporters. Elizabeth Bender Cloud was a partner in the work, and the Institute's history is inseparable from the couple's shared commitment to educational leadership. The Presbyterian Historical Society maintains a documented collection of the Institute's records, which allows historians to study its administration, students, finances, and correspondence.

The Institute's approach contrasted with that of many federal schools. It emphasized academic preparation over narrow vocational training, aimed to send graduates on to colleges and professional training, and expressed a belief in the importance of Indigenous leadership. At the same time, it operated within a missionary and Christian framework and within an educational environment that expected students to adopt the values and habits of the wider society. The Clouds worked pragmatically, seeking opportunities for students while navigating the limits imposed by funding, denominational relationships, and the broader assimilationist climate. Their work should therefore be understood neither as simple resistance to assimilation nor as simple acceptance of it. It was an effort to create Indigenous professional and intellectual capacity under constrained conditions.

Roe Cloud's later role in the Meriam survey, which scholarly research establishes, brought this educational experience into the federal investigation of Indian administration, as discussed in Section 95.17. His participation underscores a theme of this chapter: Indigenous educators and professionals were not merely subjects of reform but contributors to it, and their institutional experience informed the critique of federal schooling.

The archival history provides a clearer institutional chronology than a general label such as 'alternative school.' The Institute opened in Wichita in 1915; according to the Presbyterian Historical Society, Harry Coons became its first graduate in 1919, and the school adopted the name American Indian Institute around 1920 or 1921. Beginning in 1927 Roe Cloud negotiated the transfer of its administration, property, and financial responsibility to the Presbyterian Church's Board of National Missions. This sequence matters because the original institution was established through Indigenous educational initiative but survived by working with non-Indigenous patrons. Its changing organizational arrangements are evidence both of institutional ambition and of the difficulty of maintaining financial independence.

Funding and governance should be examined together. A private school must provide buildings, teachers, lodging, food, and oversight before it can deliver a college-preparatory curriculum. Dependence on donations made institutional planning vulnerable to fluctuations in enthusiasm, and church connections could bring resources while also setting expectations about religion and conduct. The Clouds did not simply choose between traditional education and assimilation: they negotiated with actual institutions that controlled money and recognition. Students should therefore avoid assuming that an Indigenous-run school possessed complete autonomy, just as they should avoid assuming that Christian affiliation erased the agency of its founders and students. The central issue is which decisions the Clouds could make for themselves and which required approval or assistance from outside sponsors.

The Institute's surviving records offer several ways to reconstruct what reform meant on the ground. Letters about admissions and placements can show which students were sought and how administrators understood educational promise. Financial records can reveal how funding constrained the school, while curricula and alumni accounts can illuminate the gap between aspirations and graduates' opportunities. None of these alone establishes that every student experienced the institution identically. A full history would also ask students and families why they selected the Institute, what alternatives they had, and how they judged the benefits and costs of its regime. In that evidentiary sense, the school becomes more than a success story: it is a working example of the choices made by Indigenous educators within unequal systems.

Everyday Life and Human Experience

Building a School: The Roe Indian Institute as a Documented Institution

The ordinary work of establishing a small school is easy to overlook beside the larger political campaigns of the 1920s, yet institutional records preserve evidence of it. The records of the Roe Indian Institute held by the Presbyterian Historical Society, together with published accounts and correspondence from the period, indicate that the Institute's founders spent considerable effort on the practical tasks of a school operating without federal funding: soliciting donations, corresponding with sponsors and denominational officials, recruiting students from Indigenous communities, and arranging for the instruction, housing, and care of those students.

The records also show that the school's existence depended on a web of relationships that crossed lines of nation, region, and institution. Students came from different Indigenous nations; support came from non-Indigenous churchgoers and organizations who were often attracted by the project's missionary and educational purposes; and the Clouds had to represent the school to audiences who held varied assumptions about Indigenous capacity. Historians reconstructing this experience must rely on institutional documents and contemporary accounts, which preserve the perspective of administrators and supporters more fully than that of individual students. Because those documents seldom record students' own thoughts and feelings in detail, this feature does not attempt to describe them. What the records allow is an understanding of how an Indigenous-led institution functioned as a practical and financial undertaking, and of how its leaders sought to build educational opportunity within the constraints of the period.

Section 95.11

Peter Henderson Bryce, Residential-School Health, and Canadian Administrative Failure

In Canada, the most important early exposure of conditions in residential schools came from a government physician. Peter Henderson Bryce served as chief medical officer for the Department of the Interior and, by extension, for Indian Affairs. In 1907 he was commissioned to inspect schools in western Canada and to report on the health of pupils. The report he submitted documented widespread tuberculosis and other disease and reported high mortality among pupils. In his later 1922 account of the 1907 survey of thirty-five schools, Bryce wrote that approximately 24 percent of the pupils in the historical school returns were known to have died over the period the returns covered, with much higher cumulative losses documented for particular institutions. This was not an annual mortality rate, a census of all Canadian residential schools, or a claim that every death occurred on school premises. Historians have discussed the methods and limits of his data, but the central finding, that conditions in the schools were making children gravely ill and killing many of them, is well supported by the surrounding medical and archival record.

Bryce attributed the problem substantially to the physical conditions of the schools: poor ventilation, overcrowding, inadequate sanitation, and the admission of children who were already ill, which spread infection among the pupils. He recommended improvements to facilities and to medical inspection and care. The Department's response fell far short of the reforms he proposed. Bryce stated in his 1922 pamphlet that the 1907 report had been published separately but that its recommendations had not been published; in his account, the necessary changes were not carried out on a scale adequate to address the conditions. Publication of selected findings must therefore be distinguished from disclosure of the recommendations and from effective action on them. Bryce's own career within the civil service later became strained, and he left the service in 1921, after which he published a more public indictment.

That indictment was The Story of a National Crime, published in 1922 as an appeal to the Canadian public. Written after his departure from the civil service, it reviewed his earlier investigations, argued that the government had known of the dangers and failed to act, and criticized the administration of Indian Affairs under Duncan Campbell Scott. The pamphlet is available as a complete historical text and is the source for the account in the Primary Source Window below. Its publication brought the question of school mortality into public debate, though it did not produce immediate reform.

Why did government knowledge of these problems not produce effective change? Several factors operated together. The department regarded the schools as essential instruments of its assimilation policy and was reluctant to endanger them. Churches that ran many of the schools and the government shared responsibility, and each had interests in minimizing costs. Funding arrangements, including per-capita grants that tied payments to enrollment, created incentives to keep schools full and to admit and retain children regardless of health. The department's priorities placed assimilationist goals ahead of health concerns, and its administrative culture tended to treat criticism as a threat. Parliament and the public, meanwhile, were largely uninformed or unresponsive. The failure was thus institutional and political, not merely a lapse of information.

A further distinction is essential. Bryce was a non-Indigenous medical critic whose investigations and public writing exposed conditions from within the framework of the government service. His report and pamphlet are significant evidence. But they should not be read as the beginning of opposition to the schools. First Nations families and communities had long objected to compulsory schooling, to the removal of children, and to the conditions in the schools, and they had expressed these objections through petitions, refusals, and attempts to retain control of their children. Bryce's work added a medical and statistical voice to criticism that Indigenous people had been making in their own terms, and it is important to preserve this distinction to avoid crediting an outside observer with the discovery of what communities already knew.

The arithmetic of Bryce's historical returns illustrates why denominators matter in institutional history. A cumulative statement that roughly one in four pupils known to have attended the surveyed institutions had died concerns a sequence of enrollments over years; it cannot be compared directly to one year's mortality rate for the total school population. Nor does it establish the exact number of children who died inside school buildings. Some deaths took place after pupils returned home, which Bryce regarded as relevant to the question of illness contracted or worsened during attendance. His sample and surviving records do not license a single national percentage. Correctly framed, the figures are grave evidence of extended health failures, and their limitations are a reason to investigate additional records, not to discount the suffering they reveal.

Section 95.12

Indigenous Religious Freedom, the Native American Church, and Opposition to Cultural Suppression

The defense of Indigenous religious life was an important dimension of resistance to assimilation, since ceremony was both a source of community cohesion and a target of administrative and missionary hostility. Chapter 89 treated the Ghost Dance and other religious movements; this section addresses the legal and political defense of religious practice in the early twentieth century and avoids repeating that earlier history.

In the United States, federal regulations, supported by missionary opposition, had long discouraged or prohibited various Indigenous ceremonies, and agents exercised wide discretion in restricting dances and gatherings. In the early 1920s the Commissioner of Indian Affairs issued circulars discouraging certain dances, and these policies prompted organized opposition from Indigenous communities and reformers, who argued that they infringed on religious liberty. A particular focus of conflict was the ceremonial use of peyote, a cactus used in religious ceremonies by a growing number of Indigenous people, especially on the southern Plains and in Oklahoma. Missionaries, officials, and some Indigenous critics condemned peyote use as harmful or heathen, and state and federal efforts were made to restrict it.

In October 1918, adherents organized the Native American Church in Oklahoma, incorporating under state law. The incorporation is well documented. By adopting the form of a recognizable legal religious institution, with articles of incorporation, officers, and a statement of purposes, members sought to defend practices important to them under the protections that American law extended to organized religion. The strategy illustrates how Indigenous communities adapted legal forms of the settler state to protect their own religious life, without abandoning the substance of their practices. The church spread across many communities in subsequent decades, and its legal status remained contested.

Indigenous activists disagreed over peyote, religion, and the meaning of modernization. Not every Indigenous reformer supported the Native American Church. Some, including members of the Society of American Indians, opposed peyote use, regarding it as harmful or as an obstacle to progress, and some of these critics were committed Christians or advocates of social reform. Others defended it as a legitimate religion and a vehicle of community cohesion and moral discipline. These divisions show that Indigenous reform was not a monolithic movement and that debates over religion cut across other political alignments. An activist who opposed assimilationist administration might nevertheless oppose peyote; another who defended Indigenous ceremony might be critical of other aspects of reform.

In Canada, the state's suppression of ceremony operated through the Indian Act. An 1884 amendment prohibited the potlatch, a central institution of governance, property, and social order among Northwest Coast peoples, and later amendments in the 1890s and the early twentieth century extended prohibitions to the Sun Dance and other ceremonies and to certain forms of dress and performance. Enforcement was uneven, but prosecutions occurred, including a well-known set of arrests in the early 1920s connected to a large potlatch on the British Columbia coast, and communities continued their practices in modified or concealed forms. The prohibitions illustrate the connection between religious suppression and the broader assimilationist program, and they remained in force until the revision of the Indian Act in 1951.

These cases from the United States and Canada show similar mechanisms operating through different legal systems: the identification of Indigenous ceremony as an obstacle to civilization, the use of law and administrative regulation to restrict it, and persistent Indigenous efforts to preserve it through adaptation, negotiation, and sometimes concealment. The longer history of religious freedom law for Indigenous peoples extends beyond this chapter's period, but the defense of ceremony in the 1910s and 1920s established arguments and institutions that later movements would build upon.

The aftermath of the December 1921 Village Island potlatch demonstrates the coercive force of these laws. The gathering associated with Dan Cranmer brought Kwakwaka'wakw participants to a major ceremonial event; prosecutions followed in 1922, and families faced pressure to surrender masks, regalia, and other ceremonial belongings. Officials often depicted such seizures as the voluntary relinquishment of objects in exchange for relief from punishment, but that language conceals the legal and economic pressure under which the choices were made. Ceremonial property was not simply display material: its possession and use helped sustain rights, memory, and obligations among families and communities. The resulting losses therefore extended beyond objects to the circumstances under which cultural and political knowledge could be transmitted.

Section 95.13

First Nations Land-Claims Movements and Organized Political Advocacy in Canada

In Canada, the same decades saw the expansion of organized First Nations political advocacy, much of it centered on land. The issue was especially acute in British Columbia, where, unlike in much of the rest of the country, most Indigenous nations had not signed treaties extinguishing their title. The provincial government had long refused to recognize Indigenous title and had assigned reserves that were small relative to the territories Indigenous nations had occupied. First Nations leaders insisted that title remained unsurrendered and sought recognition and a process for resolving the question. The federal government, which had constitutional responsibility for "Indians and lands reserved for Indians," generally deferred to the province's position on land and proposed adjustments to reserves rather than recognition of title.

Political organization developed around this impasse. In the early twentieth century, the Nisga'a pursued a petition to the imperial government in London, and Indigenous leaders from various nations worked to coordinate their efforts. In June 1916 the Allied Indian Tribes of British Columbia was formed at a conference on a Squamish reserve, bringing together coastal and interior nations. Its leaders included Peter Kelly, a Haida clergyman, and Andrew Paull, a Squamish organizer, and it employed legal counsel to prepare arguments for submission to Canadian authorities. The coalition's strategies included petitions, correspondence with federal officials, delegations to Ottawa, and attempts to obtain judicial consideration of the title question through a reference to the courts. The reserve commission that had examined BC reserves in the 1910s, known by the names of its commissioners, adjusted reserve boundaries but did not address title, and the Allied Tribes sought a different kind of settlement.

The emphasis here is on political strategy and its difficulties. Coalition formation across nations with different languages, economies, and interests required sustained effort, and unity could not be assumed. Legal argument depended on counsel and money. Fundraising meant collecting contributions from communities with limited resources. Delegations to Ottawa required travel across a continent. Maintaining an organization across large and often remote territories was costly and fragile. Those obstacles were compounded by the opposition of federal and provincial authorities and, as Section 95.14 explains, by legislation that targeted the means by which claims could be financed.

Elsewhere, other efforts at national or regional organization emerged. The League of Indians of Canada, established in 1919 by Frederick Ogilvie Loft, a Mohawk from the Six Nations of the Grand River and a First World War veteran, sought to unite First Nations across the country in a common organization, with particular strength in the prairie provinces. It held conferences, lobbied for changes in the Indian Act, and advocated for First Nations interests, but it struggled with limited funds, departmental hostility, and the difficulty of coordinating a national organization across vast distances and diverse communities. In the early 1920s, Six Nations political leaders also pursued their claims to self-government internationally, an episode associated with Deskaheh (Levi General), a Cayuga chief, that belongs to the broader story of interwar First Nations politics examined in Chapter 98. These movements shared a common structure: they challenged the premise of assimilationist administration that Indigenous people were wards without political standing, and they were met with administrative measures designed to limit their capacity to organize.

The coalition itself had an identifiable organizational beginning. At a conference on a Squamish reserve in June 1916, representatives of many British Columbia nations established the Allied Indian Tribes of British Columbia; Peter Kelly was elected chairman and James Teit secretary. Andrew Paull, a Squamish organizer and advocate, was also important to the alliance. Their efforts connected the Nisga'a and Interior Tribes' earlier petitions with broader regional mobilization. Naming these people and the year of organization matters: it prevents later national advocacy from obscuring the practical labor of building the coalition. It also shows that the Allied Tribes was not merely a designation assigned retrospectively to loosely related protests; it was a political organization through which Indigenous representatives sought a common platform.

Section 95.14

The 1927 Indian Act Amendment: Restricting Political Organization and Land Claims

In 1927 the Parliament of Canada revised the Indian Act and added what became section 141. The provision made it an offence to solicit or receive from First Nations people any payment or contribution for the purpose of raising funds to prosecute a claim on behalf of a band or tribe, unless the Superintendent General of Indian Affairs had given consent in writing. In practical terms, it required First Nations claimants to obtain the permission of the very department whose policies they were contesting before they could raise money to challenge them.

The provision's background lay in the federal response to British Columbia's title claims. In 1926 and 1927 a Special Joint Committee of the Senate and House of Commons reviewed the Allied Tribes' petition and arguments, hearing from representatives of the coalition and from government officials. The committee concluded that the claim of Indigenous title had not been established, recommended that the matter be regarded as closed, and proposed a payment to British Columbia Indians as a measure of benefit, which the government framed as a discretionary provision rather than recognition of title. The financing restriction actually received royal assent on March 31, 1927, shortly before the committee issued its final report. The order of events matters: federal officials were moving simultaneously toward rejection of the title claim and restriction of the resources used to pursue further advocacy, but the final committee report cannot be identified as the prior cause of a statute already enacted.

The restriction mattered because it targeted the financial basis of political action. Land-claims litigation and lobbying required lawyers, travel, research, and publication, all of which cost money that First Nations communities could assemble only through collective contributions. If raising such contributions required prior departmental consent, the Department of Indian Affairs held a veto over the process by which claims could be pursued. In the aftermath of the 1927 amendment, the Allied Tribes lost its capacity to function as before, and the province-wide organization dissolved. First Nations political organizing continued in other forms, including the groups that formed in subsequent decades, but the legal environment constrained it until the provision was repealed in 1951.

Precision about section 141 is important, because the provision is often described in ways that exaggerate or oversimplify it. It did not, on its face, ban all lawyers, all political meetings, or all court proceedings. Its statutory language concerned the solicitation or receipt of funds for the prosecution of claims, and it required departmental consent in writing. Its effect was nevertheless severe, because the practical ability to hire counsel and pursue claims depended on collecting money, and because the department that controlled consent was the department against which many claims were directed. Historians examine both the text of the provision and its administrative interpretation to understand how it operated and how it was applied, and they distinguish its precise scope from later generalizations about it.

The original amending statute provides an unusually precise test of the chronology. Chapter 32 of the Statutes of Canada for 1926–27 received royal assent on March 31, 1927. Its section 6 inserted a provision numbered 149A into the then-current Indian Act. In the later 1927 consolidation that restriction appears as section 141, the designation by which historians usually refer to it. The amendment therefore preceded the special joint committee's final report on the Allied Tribes rather than following it. Both are products of the same campaign over British Columbia title claims, but the committee's final judgment cannot be described as a completed event that caused the earlier royal assent. Separating those steps avoids turning political association into an impossible chronology.

The text is narrower than some shorthand accounts but still far-reaching. It punished a person who solicited or received money or a promised contribution from an Indian for the prosecution of a claim made on behalf of the person's tribe or band, unless the Superintendent General had given written consent. The stated sanctions were a fifty-to-two-hundred-dollar fine or imprisonment for a term of up to two months on summary conviction. The provision did not expressly outlaw every consultation with a lawyer or every meeting held by a First Nation. Yet the cost of collecting evidence, communicating across communities, and retaining professional assistance made fundraising integral to organized legal action. By placing that activity under the supervising department's permission, Parliament made the practical exercise of a right depend on executive discretion.

Subsequent departmental interpretation further enlarged the restriction's potential reach. In correspondence examined by legal historians, federal officials treated preparatory work for litigation as potentially within the meaning of pursuing a claim and expected applicants to disclose the substance of proposed cases before permission was considered. That arrangement could provide officials with advance knowledge of challenges directed against their own department. Recent archival research finds that formal prosecutions were relatively uncommon; nevertheless surveillance, investigation, and intimidation could discourage organizing without a successful conviction. It would be inaccurate to infer from a small prosecution count that the provision was harmless, just as it would be inaccurate to claim that all Indigenous political activity was formally criminalized. The combination of statutory text, administrative practice, and community response supplies the better historical explanation.

Section 95.15

Duncan Campbell Scott, Compulsory Schooling, and the Limits of Canadian Reform

Duncan Campbell Scott, a career civil servant and published poet, served as Deputy Superintendent General of Indian Affairs from 1913 to 1932, the period spanning most of the events of this chapter. Under his leadership the department pursued assimilationist goals vigorously. Scott regarded the long-term objective of policy as the absorption of First Nations people into the general population, so that the special legal status and the department itself would eventually be unnecessary, and he supported measures that he believed would hasten that outcome, including compulsory schooling and the enfranchisement of individuals who gave up legal status.

In 1920 Parliament amended the Indian Act to strengthen compulsory attendance. The amended provisions required First Nations children, within specified ages, to attend school, and they authorized the department to compel attendance and to commit children to schools. It is important to state the scope of these provisions precisely: the amended school-attendance provisions concerned day, industrial, or boarding schools, and did not establish a requirement that every First Nations child attend a residential school specifically. In practice, however, the department's emphasis on residential institutions, and the scarcity of day schools in many regions, meant that compulsion frequently resulted in residential placement. The same legislative session also introduced provisions allowing enfranchisement without the individual's consent, a measure that provoked protest and was repealed within a few years, an episode that shows the political limits of the department's ambitions.

The department also continued and expanded restrictions on ceremony and political activity. The prohibitions on the potlatch and the Sun Dance, discussed in Section 95.12, remained in effect, and amendments in the 1910s extended controls on dress and performance. Administrative resistance to demands for greater First Nations control was consistent. Scott and his department generally treated First Nations political organizations with suspicion, regarded delegations and petitions as the work of agitators, and used the powers of the Indian Act to restrict independent organization, as the 1927 amendment shows. The department's language of protection and civilization persisted, but its administrative practice rested on compulsion.

Official rhetoric of protection and civilization was used to justify expanded coercive powers. Statements of purpose emphasized the welfare of Indigenous children and communities, while the mechanisms of enforcement, including truant officers, committal provisions, and the authority to withhold benefits, expanded the state's capacity to override family and community decisions. Study of this language alongside administrative practice shows how the vocabulary of care could coexist with, and legitimate, extensive control.

Indigenous responses took many forms. Parents objected to the removal of children, communities petitioned for day schools and for improvements in conditions, individuals resisted or evaded compulsion, and First Nations organizations pressed for changes in policy. Churches, which operated many of the schools, had complex roles: they were partners of the government in the system and in some cases sources of internal criticism, though the institutional interests of denominations in maintaining their schools and funding often limited reform. Public critics, including Bryce and some journalists and clergy, raised alarms, but the department maintained its position. Parks Canada's updated historical assessment of Scott documents his assimilationist policies and administrative record, and recent scholarship has deepened understanding of how his career combined literary prominence with the direction of a coercive administrative system. Reform in Canada during this period was therefore limited, and its limits show that the existence of public criticism and documented evidence did not by itself alter a system supported by institutional interests, parliamentary indifference, and a settled assimilationist consensus.

Section 95.16

Competing Visions of Reform across Indigenous North America

Bringing the United States and Canadian cases together clarifies the range of programs competing for support in the 1920s. At one end stood reformers who accepted the assimilationist goal and sought to improve its administration. In the United States these included many officials, the Indian Rights Association in many of its campaigns, and some members of the Society of American Indians who believed that cooperation with federal authorities offered the best prospects for gradual progress. In Canada such views were represented by some church and public critics and by officials who favored modest improvements. Their emphasis was on efficiency, honesty, and adequate funding.

A second group favored full individual integration into settler political systems and the dismantling of special status and institutions. Montezuma is the leading example, and his arguments for abolishing the Bureau and treating Indigenous people as individuals under ordinary law were echoed in different forms by other critics who regarded guardianship as the problem. In Canada, individual enfranchisement served a similar function in official policy, though the government's version was coercive and Indigenous support for it was limited. The tension in this approach was that individual integration could be pursued as a liberation from paternalism or imposed as a mechanism of dispossession, depending on who controlled the terms.

A third group, which included many community leaders and organizations, demanded recognition of collective land rights and political sovereignty. The Pueblo council, the Allied Tribes, the Six Nations leaders who appealed beyond Canadian institutions, and community defenders of ceremonial life shared an insistence that Indigenous peoples were political communities with rights that predated and were not dependent on the settler state. For these advocates, the question was not how to improve guardianship or escape it as individuals but how to secure recognition of Indigenous governments, lands, and institutions.

Relationships between Indigenous communities and non-Indigenous reform organizations could be both productive and unequal. Non-Indigenous organizations provided funding, publicity, legal expertise, and access to legislators that Indigenous communities often lacked, and the Pueblo campaign shows how such partnerships could succeed. But the allies brought their own interests and assumptions. Some reformers believed they understood Indigenous needs better than Indigenous people did and sought to speak for them. Organizations competed for influence and credit, and their priorities, such as promoting a distinctive vision of preserving Indigenous culture, did not always match those of the communities they supported. Indigenous leaders frequently had to negotiate these relationships, accepting assistance while attempting to retain direction of their own campaigns.

Disagreements over education, communal property, religious freedom, and government authority cut across these categories. A reformer might support community schools but oppose peyote, or defend collective land while favoring citizenship, and individuals could change positions over time. Women organizers, including Zitkala-Ša and the many women active in clubs and local councils, were central to the movement, and many communities whose local campaigns received less national attention contributed through petitions, refusals, and the preservation of language and ceremony. The record of these contributions is less complete than that of prominent intellectuals and national organizations, and historians continue to recover it from local archives, oral histories, and community records.

This comparative framework provides the foundation for assessing the Meriam Report. The report, as Division V shows, emerged in an environment where each of these programs had adherents, and its findings and recommendations can be understood in relation to them. Where it endorsed administrative improvement, it aligned with the first group; where it criticized guardianship and allotment, it overlapped with arguments of the second and third; but it did not endorse the transfer of authority to Indigenous governments that the third group demanded.

Section 95.17

The Origins of the Meriam Investigation, 1926–1928

By the mid-1920s, pressure for an independent examination of federal Indian administration had accumulated from several sources. Reform organizations had published critical reports. Indigenous activists and the Pueblo campaign had drawn national attention to land loss. Congressional debate over guardianship, citizenship, and appropriations continued, and the Indian Service itself faced criticism over health conditions and the performance of its schools. Hubert Work, who served as Secretary of the Interior in the Coolidge administration, requested an independent review of the Indian Service. The reasons for his request combined genuine concern about conditions with a political interest in answering critics and in obtaining an authoritative assessment that might shape reform on the administration's terms. Historians continue to weigh these motives, and the available evidence does not allow a simple choice between them.

The survey was entrusted to the Institute for Government Research, a Washington research organization that specialized in studying public administration and that was later absorbed into the Brookings Institution. The Institute assembled a survey staff under the technical direction of Lewis Meriam, an experienced researcher in public administration. The staff included specialists in several fields, among them experts in economics, health, education, and family and community life. Henry Roe Cloud, the Ho-Chunk educator discussed in Section 95.10, was the Indigenous member of the survey staff, and his inclusion gave the investigation a connection to Indigenous educational experience, though a single Indigenous staff member could not represent the diversity of Indigenous communities or give them control over the inquiry.

In 1926 and 1927 the survey team visited numerous reservations, agencies, hospitals, and schools across the country. Its methods combined direct observation, interviews with Indigenous people and Indian Service employees, examination of records, and the application of professional standards drawn from public health, education, economics, and social work. The team compared conditions with the norms of other institutions and sought to identify gaps between the stated aims and actual performance of federal programs. The approach had strengths: it produced systematic information on a national scale, and it brought professional expertise to bear on questions of nutrition, disease, school management, and household economy. It also had limits. The visits were brief relative to the scale of the territory covered, the team's professional standards embodied assumptions about family, work, and progress that reflected the dominant culture, and the investigators relied heavily on their own judgment and on interviews that were shaped by the conditions of the visits.

A distinctive feature of the survey was its decision to examine administration, economic conditions, education, health, and family life together rather than in isolation. This approach allowed the authors to argue that the problems were interrelated: poor health undermined educational and economic achievement, inadequate economic resources contributed to poor health and school attendance difficulties, and the failure of administration affected all of them. It supported the report's argument that piecemeal reforms would be insufficient.

The relationship between earlier Indigenous criticism and the decision to undertake a systematic investigation is a matter of interpretation. The evidence suggests that the survey was a response to a climate of criticism that Indigenous organizations, reform associations, and the press had helped create, even though the formal request came from the Secretary of the Interior and the research was conducted by a non-Indigenous institution. To say that the Meriam survey grew out of this criticism is not to say that Indigenous activists commissioned or controlled it. The full report was submitted to the Secretary on February 21, 1928, and was subsequently published.

The survey's methods deserve the same scrutiny as its conclusions. A questionnaire administered by a researcher might elicit information about diet, attendance, wages, or disease that appeared comparable across field sites, but such questions could impose categories unfamiliar to the people answering them. Observers also saw institutions at particular moments; they did not necessarily witness an entire year of schooling or a complete cycle of economic activity. These methodological limits are not reasons to reject the report. They are reasons to put its observations beside school attendance registers, agency budgets, community testimony, and the earlier political petitions discussed in this chapter. Its distinctive achievement was to combine areas usually investigated separately, not to eliminate the need for other kinds of evidence.

Section 95.18

The Problem of Indian Administration (1928): Findings on Poverty, Education, and Health

The report published in 1928 as The Problem of Indian Administration was a long document, running to several hundred pages, organized by subject: general policy, health, education, family and community life, economic conditions, legal aspects, and missionary activities, among other topics. Its length and detail were part of its force. It presented its findings as the result of systematic investigation, and its tone was relatively restrained, which made its conclusions harder to dismiss as the work of partisan critics.

Its central finding was that the overwhelming majority of Indigenous people were poor, often extremely poor, and that their economic and social circumstances were not adjusted to the dominant economy. Incomes were low, much land was unproductive or leased to others, and many families lacked adequate housing, food, and sanitation. The report recognized that dispossession, inadequate resources, and deficient public administration contributed substantially to these conditions, but it also used paternalistic judgments about Indigenous work and family life that historians should not mistake for neutral descriptions of Indigenous capacity. It argued that the system had often treated individuals as objects of paternalistic management rather than as people capable of directing their own economic lives.

On health, the report documented widespread disease, high mortality, and inadequate medical services. It identified tuberculosis, trachoma, and other conditions as prevalent, found that hospitals and clinics were frequently understaffed and poorly equipped, and criticized the quality and training of medical personnel. It linked health to poverty and diet, noting that inadequate nutrition was a pervasive problem, including among schoolchildren. The report's treatment of health provided systematic evidence for what Indigenous communities and medical critics like Bryce, in the Canadian case, had long argued.

The report's critique of education was among its most important parts. The survey staff concluded that conditions in many federal boarding schools were grossly inadequate. It found that diets were insufficient, that facilities were overcrowded, that the work required of children was excessive and sometimes substituted for instruction, and that medical care was limited. It criticized the reliance on uniform institutional routines and on vocational training that did not prepare students for available employment. It questioned the practice of removing young children from their families to distant institutions and recommended greater use of day schools and community-based education, improved qualifications for teachers, and a curriculum adapted to students' lives and environments. In this the report departed from the older assumptions of the boarding-school model while retaining the broader aim of preparing Indigenous people for participation in the wider society.

On allotment and economic development, the report concluded that the allotment system had failed to produce the self-sufficient farming communities that its framers had envisioned and that it had contributed to the loss of land and to the fragmentation of holdings through inheritance. It recommended that further individual allotment proceed only with extreme conservatism and adequate educational support and that the government support more effective economic development, including assistance with agriculture, credit, and the use of Indigenous-controlled resources. It emphasized the need for qualified, trained personnel in the Indian Service, for adequate funding, and for a new orientation in administration, one oriented to education and the development of Indigenous capacity rather than to routine control. The report proposed that the federal government build a division to plan and develop programs, and that the service work cooperatively with Indigenous people.

Equally significant is what the report said about the purpose of Indian policy. It stated that the objective should be to prepare Indigenous people either to merge into the dominant society or to live within it at a standard of health and decency, and it acknowledged that those who wished to retain Indigenous ways of life should not be compelled to abandon them. That position was a notable softening of the stark assimilationism of earlier decades, but it was not a statement of Indigenous self-determination. The report assumed a continuing federal role, framed the issues as problems of administration, and relied on non-Indigenous professional expertise. Students should consult the original report rather than modern summaries when analyzing its wording, and should distinguish the survey's documented findings from the authors' recommendations and from the later uses to which the report was put.

The report's critique of administrative categories was especially important because the government often measured success by activities rather than outcomes. Maintaining an institution, spending an appropriation, or recording a child's attendance could be presented as progress without establishing that pupils were healthy or that their education opened viable opportunities. The survey instead asked how services worked in actual homes and schools. Its discussion of household nutrition, infant and adult health, schools, staff training, and work opportunities exposed the cumulative effect of underinvestment. When limited food, disease, and poor education reinforced one another, an isolated policy change might leave the central cycle intact. This was the logic behind its call for coordinated professional services rather than a single correction to school rules or agricultural policy.

The language of reform should not conceal the report's assumptions. Chapter IV, 'A General Policy for Indian Affairs,' explicitly allowed for two possible individual outcomes: joining the economic and social life of the prevailing white society, or retaining Indigenous ways while meeting a minimum standard of health and decency. It warned against forcing everyone into one cultural pattern, but it continued to frame public policy as work performed with or for Indigenous people by agencies possessing specialized expertise. The distinction between respecting an individual's choice and recognizing an Indigenous government's authority to make collective decisions is critical. Students can test this distinction directly against the published policy chapter, rather than simply crediting the report with either abandoning assimilation altogether or endorsing full tribal self-government.

Section 95.19

Historical Debate: Administrative Reform or Indigenous Self-Determination?

The Meriam Report forces a question that runs through this entire chapter: could a federal Indian administration, once improved, fundamentally resolve the crisis created by assimilationist policy? The report's authors thought that a better administration, staffed by trained professionals, adequately funded, and oriented toward education and economic development, could substantially improve conditions. Many Indigenous activists and communities, by contrast, argued that the problem lay in the structure of authority itself. This section examines the debate and the evidence that bears on it.

The distinction between a more effective paternalistic administration and government institutions accountable to Indigenous nations is central. The report criticized the failures of the existing administration in strong terms, but its remedies were administrative: better personnel, better planning, better coordination. It assumed that the federal government would continue to direct Indigenous affairs and that expert management could correct the deficiencies. Indigenous critics, including those who found its findings valuable, sought something different: recognition of Indigenous authority over land, education, and political institutions. The report's own endorsement of the freedom of individuals to retain Indigenous ways of life did not amount to a recognition of Indigenous governments as holders of political authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous Self-Determination

Definition. The principle that Indigenous nations and communities hold the authority to make decisions about their own governance, land, education, and cultural life, rather than having those decisions made by outside governments on their behalf.

Historical context. The phrase became a formal policy term in the United States in the 1960s and 1970s, treated in Chapter 114, but the underlying claim to Indigenous political authority was asserted much earlier, including by the Pueblo council, the Allied Tribes, and community defenders of ceremony. Using the term for the 1920s is a retrospective analytical convenience and not a description of how every participant spoke.

Why it matters. The concept allows historians to distinguish programs that aimed to improve how others governed Indigenous people from those that aimed to restore Indigenous governance.

Potential misunderstanding. Self-determination does not mean the rejection of all outside assistance or relationships; it concerns who holds decision-making authority.

Historical Debate

Could Assimilationist Administration Be Reformed, or Did Change Require Indigenous Control?

The question: Did the problems documented in the 1920s stem from remediable failures of administration, or from the structure of guardianship itself, such that meaningful change required restoring Indigenous control over political, economic, and cultural institutions?

Interpretation A — Administrative reform as a practical path: Some historians and contemporaries argue that improved funding, trained personnel, and better planning could and did relieve severe suffering and that insisting on structural change risked neglecting urgent needs. They note that the Meriam Report's evidence mobilized political support and that incremental improvements in health and education mattered to communities in crisis.
Interpretation B — Structural change as the necessary condition: Others argue that paternalism was the underlying problem and that reforms accepting federal tutelage left the source of dependence intact. They point to the pattern, seen in the Pueblo Lands Act and the Canadian experience, of reforms preserving structural inequalities, and to Indigenous leaders' consistent demands for authority over their own affairs.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the Meriam Report itself, on the administrative record of the Indian Service, on petitions and organizational statements from Indigenous communities, and on the later history of policy. Points of disagreement concern causation (whether poor administration or the structure of guardianship was the principal cause of conditions), interpretation (how to read the report's relation to assimilation), and counterfactual judgment (what administrative improvement could have achieved).

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians assess the 1920s: as a decade that prepared the ground for a new policy, or as one in which reform risked preserving old structures under a new name. It also affects how later policy is evaluated.

Student task: Construct the strongest case for each interpretation using evidence from this chapter. Then identify what kind of additional evidence, such as the actual outcomes of specific programs or the statements of Indigenous communities about the report, could help resolve the question. Note that the two interpretations are not mutually exclusive, since a reform could relieve distress and still leave authority unchanged.

Evaluating this debate requires attention to why Indigenous scholars and activists could regard some of the report's findings as valuable while seeking far greater authority. The report's documentation of poverty, disease, and educational failure corroborated what communities experienced and gave critics official evidence. At the same time, those who sought restoration of Indigenous government could not accept a framework in which Indigenous people remained the objects of administration. The two responses were consistent: evidence of failure was welcome, but the proper remedy was a matter of who decided. Recognizing this prevents two misreadings: the view that the report was irrelevant because it was paternalistic, and the view that it was a vindication of Indigenous demands because it criticized the existing system.

An administrative-reform argument begins from the immediacy of suffering. A child exposed to tuberculosis or served inadequate food needs functioning services regardless of the constitutional status of the supervising agency. Reformers who pressed for trained medical personnel, better budgets, and effective inspections could make measurable changes even if federal authority remained intact. Their case is strongest where the evidence concerns a clearly remediable deficiency, such as deficient sanitation or poor recordkeeping. Yet improvement in delivery does not answer who had the right to impose the institution in the first place. A boarding school might become safer without families gaining a meaningful choice about attendance, curriculum, or the institution's continued existence. The difference is between remedying a harm and transferring the authority that made the harm possible.

A sovereignty-centered argument begins from the contested foundation of federal power. Pueblo leaders defending communal grants, British Columbia claimants seeking a hearing on title, and communities protecting religious practice were not always asking for better management of institutions they accepted as legitimate. In many instances they challenged the state's authority to determine the future of their land or culture without consent. To describe those demands as mere requests for improved administration is to miss their political content. At the same time, neither land protection nor Indigenous government automatically supplied every needed medical service or schooling opportunity. The historical challenge is to ask how authority, resources, and accountability might be combined without substituting administrative expertise for Indigenous consent.

The Meriam survey itself permits a close comparison of these approaches. Its chapter on general policy recognized that some Indigenous people wished to retain their own culture and should not be forced into an unwanted pattern of life, a significant departure from more coercive prescriptions. But its examples were framed largely as choices for individual persons, and its preferred instruments remained educational work, health services, and professional administration. Indigenous political movements could welcome such recognition while still insisting that collective treaty rights and governmental authority were not merely cultural preferences. Students should identify which lines of the report concern conditions, which concern individual choice, and which, if any, establish that a tribal council should control the service being offered.

Finally, the apparent opposition between 'reform' and 'self-determination' should not become an automatic ranking of historical actors. The Society of American Indians included people with divergent goals; Carlos Montezuma's demand to abolish the Bureau could be emancipatory in one setting yet appear dangerous to nations facing the loss of trust protection in another. Non-Indigenous allies sometimes helped defeat harmful laws but also controlled resources or publicity. A responsible judgment therefore evaluates concrete proposals and their consequences, not the identity of their advocates alone. The stronger historical question is whether a particular reform increased Indigenous capacity to decide, protected rights already held, and supplied necessary services without building a new dependency.

Section 95.20

The Crisis of Assimilation by 1933: Reform, Resistance, and an Unfinished Transformation

By 1933, as the United States was in the depths of the Great Depression, federal Indian administration faced mounting criticism from multiple directions. Land-defense campaigns, Indigenous organizations, school reform initiatives, investigative reports, religious-freedom advocacy, and public criticism had collectively undermined confidence in established policy. The Meriam Report had given that criticism an authoritative form, and a Senate investigation of Indian affairs that began in 1928 continued to gather testimony and evidence of conditions. Congress and the administration had taken some incremental steps in response, including increases in appropriations and changes in some Indian Service practices, but the structure of guardianship and the framework of allotment remained largely in place.

The Depression added urgency. Economic collapse worsened conditions in many Indigenous communities that were already poor, reduced income from leasing and wage labor, and made federal fiscal restraint a political reality. At the same time, the economic crisis shook broader faith in established institutions and prepared a political environment more receptive to experimentation. The appointment of John Collier as Commissioner of Indian Affairs in 1933 by the incoming Roosevelt administration marked an important transition. Collier had been a leading critic of the Indian Bureau, an organizer of the American Indian Defense Association, and an ally of the Pueblos in the Bursum Bill campaign. His appointment signaled that the leadership of the Office of Indian Affairs would be in the hands of a figure committed to changing policy. This chapter does not recount the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 and the program that followed, which belong to Chapter 97.

Students should recognize that many Indigenous communities remained skeptical of reform proposals developed without adequate Indigenous participation. The history of the preceding decades gave them reason for caution: reforms had often been designed by outsiders, had been implemented through the same bureaucracy, and had sometimes preserved the dispossession they were intended to cure. Indigenous responses to the coming changes would vary, as they had varied in the 1920s, and would reflect differing assessments of the risks and opportunities of federal initiatives. Chapter 96 will assess Indigenous North America on the eve of the New Deal, and Chapter 97 will examine the Indian Reorganization Act and its implementation.

In Canada, the situation was different in important respects. Duncan Campbell Scott retired in 1932, but the legislative framework of the Indian Act, including the restrictions on claims-related fundraising and the system of residential schooling, remained in force. The crisis of confidence in assimilationist administration was less visible in official policy, though First Nations organizing continued and criticism persisted, as Chapter 98 will show. The contrast with the United States demonstrates that the crisis of assimilation was not a single continental event but unfolded unevenly, shaped by distinct political systems and by the different positions of Indigenous organizations within them.

The chapter closes with a distinction central to its argument: the difference between the crisis of an existing policy system and the creation of a genuinely different system. By 1933, the evidence that assimilationist administration had failed by its own standards was overwhelming, and confidence in it had eroded among reformers, officials, and parts of the public. That erosion was the result of long Indigenous struggle as well as of official investigation. But the erosion of confidence did not itself establish what would replace the old system, who would control the replacement, or whether Indigenous nations would hold authority in it. Those questions remained open, and the answers given in the following decade would be debated, contested, and partial. The transformation was unfinished.

The impending change of federal leadership did not supply a uniform answer to the crisis. John Collier's elevation in 1933 followed a decade of alliances with Pueblo leaders and criticism of allotment, yet Indigenous nations did not all share his assumptions or agree about the institutions he hoped to reshape. The Great Depression further increased pressures on household resources and public budgets at the very moment reform plans were becoming more ambitious. For the present chapter, the consequence is chronological restraint: Collier's appointment marks the opening of another debate, not proof that the problems documented by the Meriam Report had been solved. Subsequent chapters can test the reforms of the 1930s against the claims and experiences already established here.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Reform and the Assimilation System, 1900–1933

What Changed

  • Around 1900, criticism of assimilation came largely from individual and local resistance and from non-Indigenous reformers who supported its aims; by the 1910s, national Indigenous organizations and periodicals articulated criticism in their own voices
  • In the 1920s, investigative reports, coordinated land-defense campaigns, and legal strategies gave critics systematic evidence and political tools
  • By 1928, an official national survey acknowledged serious failures in poverty, health, schooling, and administration
  • By 1933, leadership of the Office of Indian Affairs had passed to a longtime critic of its policies

What Stayed the Same

  • The legal framework of federal guardianship, trust restrictions, and the authority of the Indian Service remained largely intact in the United States
  • In Canada, the Indian Act's controls on schooling, ceremony, and claims-related fundraising remained in force
  • Indigenous communities continued to assert land rights, community authority, and the legitimacy of their own institutions
  • Unequal access to money, lawyers, and political power continued to limit Indigenous organizations

When and how did change occur? Change was gradual in the accumulation of criticism, punctuated by episodes such as the 1922–1924 land campaign and the 1928 report, uneven between the United States and Canada, and contested within the Indigenous reform movement itself. Those who experienced change most directly were organizers and communities engaged in campaigns, while many families in distant reservations experienced greater continuity in daily administration.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that a decline in the legitimacy of a policy can precede, by years, the structural change that critics seek, and that the persistence of institutions does not mean the absence of change in the political environment around them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the crisis of assimilation in the early twentieth century was not the result of a sudden official discovery that federal and Canadian policies were failing. It grew out of decades of Indigenous opposition expressed through petitions, council decisions, land-defense campaigns, refusals, adaptation, and the continued exercise of political authority. What changed between 1880 and 1933 was the visibility of that opposition, the institutions through which it was organized, and the quantity of evidence available to document the gap between the stated aims of policy and its actual effects.

The chapter distinguished three orientations within the movement for change: administrative reform, the abolition of coercive institutions, and the restoration of Indigenous governmental authority. These orientations led to different alliances and different judgments of success, and they explain why campaigns that appear similar on the surface could rest on very different premises. Throughout, the chapter asked which of these goals a given organization, campaign, or report actually advanced.

Division I examined the emergence of organized Indigenous reform. The Society of American Indians, founded in 1911, created a national forum for Indigenous professionals and intellectuals but was divided over citizenship, federal administration, education, and religion, and it declined by the early 1920s. Carlos Montezuma used his newsletter Wassaja, begun in 1916, to attack the Office of Indian Affairs and federal guardianship, advocating individual liberty and the abolition of the Bureau, in contrast with reformers who defended tribal institutions and collective land. The National Council of American Indians, founded in 1926 by Zitkala-Ša and Raymond Bonnin, continued the project of Indigenous-led national advocacy under conditions of limited funding and unequal access to power.

Division II turned to land and economic exploitation. Criticism of allotment developed as its results became visible: the sale of surplus lands, the issuance of fee patents, leasing, and inheritance fragmentation reduced the Indigenous land base, and the trusteeship through which the government claimed to protect property was entangled in its alienation. The Pueblo campaign against the Bursum Bill in 1922–1923 showed collective Indigenous political leadership and defeated a measure that threatened Pueblo land, with non-Indigenous allies amplifying but not directing the effort. The Pueblo Lands Act of 1924 was a partial victory that nevertheless allowed many non-Indigenous claims to be confirmed. Oklahoma's Poor Rich Indians exposed how guardianship could be used to extract wealth from Indigenous owners.

Division III examined education, health, and cultural life. Criticism of boarding schools focused on underfunding, disease, compulsion, labor, and the suppression of language, and reformers disagreed about whether to improve federal schools, shift to day schools, integrate children into public schools, or establish community control. Henry Roe Cloud and Elizabeth Bender Cloud created an Indigenous-led institution emphasizing academic preparation. In Canada, Peter Henderson Bryce documented serious health failures in residential schools, and the government's failure to act revealed institutional rather than informational causes. The Native American Church, incorporated in 1918, and Canadian communities defying prohibitions on the potlatch and the Sun Dance illustrated the defense of ceremony and the disagreements it provoked among Indigenous people.

Division IV broadened the analysis to Canada and to the continent. First Nations land-claims organizing in British Columbia and national efforts such as the League of Indians of Canada confronted an administration that responded with restrictions rather than recognition. Section 141 of the Indian Act, added in 1927, required departmental consent for fundraising to pursue claims, and gave the department a veto over the financing of challenges to its own policies, though its statutory scope should not be exaggerated. Under Duncan Campbell Scott, the department intensified compulsory schooling and administrative control. A comparative framework of competing visions of reform, together with attention to the unequal partnerships between Indigenous communities and non-Indigenous organizations, provided the context for assessing the Meriam Report.

Division V examined the Meriam investigation. Secretary Hubert Work requested an independent review, which the Institute for Government Research conducted under Lewis Meriam with Henry Roe Cloud as the Indigenous member of the survey staff. Submitted on February 21, 1928, the report documented pervasive poverty, poor health, inadequate boarding-school conditions, weaknesses of personnel, and the failure of allotment, and it recommended halting further allotment, improving education and health services, and building planning capacity within the Indian Service. It softened assimilationist assumptions by acknowledging the right of individuals to retain Indigenous ways of life, but it framed the problem as one of administration.

The chapter's Historical Debate asked whether improved administration could resolve the crisis or whether meaningful change required Indigenous control over institutions. The distinction between a more effective paternalistic administration and institutions accountable to Indigenous nations explains why Indigenous leaders could value the report's findings while demanding far greater authority. The debate also shows why reforms such as the Pueblo Lands Act, which improved on earlier proposals while preserving structural inequalities, require assessment of their operation rather than of their enactment alone.

By 1933, confidence in assimilationist administration had eroded among reformers, officials, and parts of the public, and the appointment of John Collier as Commissioner of Indian Affairs signaled a change of leadership in the United States. In Canada, the legal framework of the Indian Act remained largely intact, and the crisis was less visible in official policy. The chapter emphasized that the erosion of confidence in a policy is distinct from the creation of a different system, and that many Indigenous communities remained skeptical of reforms developed without adequate Indigenous participation.

These developments set the agenda for the chapters that follow. Chapter 96 assesses the condition of Indigenous nations on the eve of the New Deal, Chapter 97 examines the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, and Chapter 98 treats First Nations politics between the wars. Students should carry forward two lessons from this chapter: that Indigenous-led reform preceded and shaped official investigation, and that the content of any reform must be judged by whether it altered the distribution of authority, not only by whether it improved conditions or acknowledged failure.

Key Terms

Assimilation
The policy goal of absorbing Indigenous people into the dominant society by changing their land tenure, education, religion, and political institutions.
Allotment
The division of communally held reservation land into individual parcels, authorized in the United States by the General Allotment Act of 1887, with unassigned land opened to non-Indigenous purchase.
Trust restrictions
Federal limits on the sale, taxation, or encumbrance of allotted land, imposed for a period during which the government nominally protected the owner's property.
Fee patent
A title document ending trust protection and allowing the owner to sell, mortgage, or lose land to taxation; issued more freely after the Burke Act of 1906.
Guardianship
The legal and administrative treatment of Indigenous people as wards whose property and affairs were supervised by officials or court-appointed guardians.
Federal paternalism
Governance in which officials claiming to act in a group's interest limit that group's own decision-making authority.
Administrative reform
Change aimed at improving the honesty, efficiency, and funding of existing institutions without altering the underlying structure of authority.
Indigenous self-determination
The principle that Indigenous nations hold authority over their own governance, land, education, and cultural life.
Office of Indian Affairs
The federal agency, also called the Indian Bureau or Indian Service, that administered federal Indian policy within the Department of the Interior.
Society of American Indians
A national intertribal reform organization founded in 1911 whose annual meetings continued until 1923.
National Council of American Indians
An Indigenous-led lobbying organization founded in 1926 by Zitkala-Ša and Raymond Bonnin, distinct from the National Congress of American Indians of 1944.
Wassaja
The newsletter published by Carlos Montezuma from 1916, which criticized the Indian Bureau and federal guardianship; also the Yavapai name by which he was known.
Bursum Bill
A 1922 Senate proposal to resolve Pueblo land disputes that Pueblo leaders opposed as threatening additional land loss.
Pueblo Lands Board
The body established by the Pueblo Lands Act of 1924 to determine non-Indigenous claims within Pueblo grants.
United States v. Sandoval (1913)
A Supreme Court decision holding that the Pueblos were Indigenous communities under federal guardianship, calling into question non-Indigenous titles on Pueblo land.
Boarding school
In the United States, an institution, federal or mission-operated, that housed Indigenous children away from their families for assimilationist education.
Residential school
In Canada, a church-operated, government-funded school system housing First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children for assimilationist education.
Enfranchisement
In Canadian law, the process by which an individual gave up or lost legal Indian status in exchange for citizenship rights, sometimes imposed against the individual's wishes.
Aboriginal title
The claim that Indigenous peoples hold rights to land arising from prior occupation, which in British Columbia had not been extinguished by treaty.
Allied Tribes of British Columbia
A coalition of First Nations that pursued recognition of Indigenous title through petitions and legal argument in the 1910s and 1920s.
League of Indians of Canada
A national First Nations organization founded in 1919 by Frederick Ogilvie Loft.
Section 141
A provision of the Canadian Indian Act added in 1927 that required written departmental consent for soliciting or receiving funds to prosecute claims on behalf of a band or tribe; repealed in 1951.
Native American Church
A religious institution incorporated in Oklahoma in October 1918 to protect ceremonial peyote use.
Potlatch
A ceremonial institution of governance, property, and social order among Northwest Coast peoples, prohibited under the Canadian Indian Act from 1884.
Meriam Report
The common name of The Problem of Indian Administration (1928), the survey of federal Indian administration directed by Lewis Meriam.
Institute for Government Research
The Washington research organization that conducted the Meriam survey and later became part of the Brookings Institution.
Special Joint Committee
The committee of the Canadian Senate and House of Commons that in 1926–1927 reviewed British Columbia land-title claims and rejected the Allied Tribes' arguments.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a common sequence in which policy failure is discovered by officials and then remedied by enlightened reform. The record examined here shows a different order of events. Indigenous communities, organizers, editors, and educators challenged assimilation first and persistently, and official investigation, when it came, drew on a climate they had helped create. Understanding this order of events restores Indigenous political agency to a period that is often described as one of passive dependence.

The chapter also reveals how reform could be both real and limited. The Pueblo campaign blocked a harmful bill and secured a federal process, yet the resulting law confirmed substantial non-Indigenous claims. The Meriam Report documented failures that officials could no longer deny, yet it framed solutions as better administration. In Canada, a government confronted with organized claims chose to restrict their financing. These cases show that political victories could coexist with structural continuity, and that the content of a reform depends on who controls its design and implementation.

These developments shaped what came after. The investigative evidence, organizational experience, and political relationships built in the 1910s and 1920s informed the leadership and debates of the 1930s, which later chapters examine, and the concern that reform should not substitute outside expertise for Indigenous authority anticipated disputes over the Indian Reorganization Act and later policy. The Canadian restrictions on organizing and the continued system of residential schooling help explain why the political landscape of First Nations in the following decades differed from that of the United States.

Within the larger textbook, the chapter illustrates a theme running from Volume I to Volume X: Indigenous nations were not only objects of imperial and national policy but political actors who contested its terms. The crisis of assimilation was not the end of a story but a turning point in an ongoing one, in which the question that Indigenous leaders had been asking, who has the authority to decide, remained open.
