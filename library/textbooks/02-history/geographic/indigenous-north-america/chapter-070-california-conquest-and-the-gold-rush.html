California Conquest and the Gold Rush

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

When gold was found at Coloma in January 1848, the American River valley was not uninhabited wilderness. It was Nisenan homeland, and Nisenan workers were present. The ensuing migration would remake the land and the lives of its Indigenous peoples through law, labor coercion, organized violence, and displacement.

The American River at Coloma flowed through Nisenan territory long before James W. Marshall found gold while working at John Sutter’s mill in January 1848. Nisenan workers were part of the enterprise. The historical event has often been remembered as the beginning of opportunity for migrants from across the world. For Indigenous families living in and around the foothills, it also marked the acceleration of land invasion, resource loss, coercive labor, and violence that would transform California within one generation.

California was neither vacant nor culturally uniform. From the coast and islands to the Central Valley, Sierra Nevada, and northern river systems, Indigenous peoples governed communities with distinctive languages, ecological knowledge, ceremonies, and economic practices. Some had already endured Spanish mission confinement or Mexican rancho exploitation; others faced relatively little sustained direct colonial administration before the Gold Rush. To understand what changed after 1848, the chapter begins with those histories instead of making settlers the only visible historical actors.

The U.S. military conquest and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo are treated in Chapter 69. Chapter 70 asks a different question: how did the arrival of newcomers, California's legal system, local and state financing of armed campaigns, and federal policies affect the people whose territories were being claimed? The answer is not supplied by the growth of mining towns alone. It requires attention to land, courts, employer power, military forces, forced relocation, and the collapse of access to food and family life.

The violence was documented at the time. Governor Peter H. Burnett anticipated a “war of extermination” in his January 1851 address; state records itemized expenditures for campaigns against Indigenous people; military correspondence and contemporary journalism reported attacks on villages. Indigenous survivors transmitted accounts of family loss and survival, while communities retained memories of sacred and residential places that officials often ignored. This chapter asks students to read these different records critically and to distinguish perpetrators' justifications from evidence of what occurred.

Historian Benjamin Madley reconstructs a decline in California's Indigenous population from perhaps 150,000 in 1846 to about 30,000 by 1873. Those are qualified historical estimates, not a direct count of 120,000 people killed by gunfire. Massacres, kidnapping, forced labor, disease, starvation, ecological damage, and displacement all contributed, often through interlocking causal processes. The chapter examines the evidence for genocide while distinguishing a historical analysis of intent and acts from simple demographic subtraction.

The history is regional as well as statewide. The attack on Pomo communities at Clear Lake followed years of abusive settler labor relationships; the Mariposa Battalion entered and damaged Ahwahneechee country in the Sierra; Tolowa and Wiyot communities were assaulted at ceremonial sites in northern California; and military-supervised removals pushed people toward Round Valley. These events arose under a broad settler order, yet they differed in participants, geography, and aftermath. Neither a single massacre nor an undifferentiated statewide story can stand in for all Indigenous California.

The chapter concludes with survival rather than disappearance. The communities attacked in the nineteenth century continued to maintain kinship, identities, languages, political institutions, and claims to homelands. Indigenous descendants have preserved historical evidence, challenged public narratives, and recovered sacred places. The central question is therefore how California's Gold Rush order produced enormous destruction, how that destruction should be explained and classified, and how Indigenous nations remained present despite the attempt to remake their country without them.

Historical Context

Spanish missions began in Alta California in 1769 and reshaped many coastal Indigenous communities through religious conversion, constrained labor, and high mortality. Mexican independence in 1821 and the secularization of missions during the 1830s transformed property relationships but seldom restored ancestral lands to their original inhabitants. Ranchos drew on Indigenous work, while communities in parts of the Sierra and northern California retained greater distance from direct colonial control. These varied experiences help explain why the Gold Rush affected different peoples in different ways.

The American conquest of California, recounted in Chapter 69, preceded statehood. Gold was discovered in January 1848, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed in February and took effect later that year, and California entered the United States in September 1850. A new settler order then combined migration, mining, ranching, discriminatory laws, and armed campaigns. Students should not confuse the formal transfer of Mexican territorial claims with Indigenous consent or imagine that every Indigenous community lost political agency when American officers occupied Californian towns.

Sources are asymmetrical. Official legislation and military accounting record what states claimed to do, while tribal history, survivor testimony, local landscape studies, and critical scholarship help establish what those actions meant for communities. Scholarly estimates must be attributed and uncertainty retained. The historical sources that follow are generally described in editorial paraphrase rather than as original-language or original-page collated quotations.

Section 70.1

Indigenous California on the Eve of American Conquest

Before American armies occupied coastal California and miners entered the Sierra foothills, the region contained one of the most varied assemblages of Indigenous societies in North America. The Nisenan, Maidu, Miwok, Yokuts, Pomo, Wintu, Wiyot, Yurok, Karuk, Hupa, Tolowa, Ohlone, Chumash, and numerous other peoples occupied homelands that were neither interchangeable nor conveniently confined within the boundaries of a future state. Languages belonged to several families and isolates; different systems of village leadership, territorial rights, trade, and ceremonial obligation governed community life. Even the word “tribelet,” used by some earlier anthropologists to describe certain small political communities, cannot substitute for the specific names and political traditions recognized by the people themselves. A credible account of conquest begins with the political existence of the people whose lands would become targets of migration.

California's environments supported sophisticated and diverse subsistence systems. Acorns supplied an important staple across many oak-woodland regions, but preparing them demanded careful gathering, storage, grinding, and leaching. Salmon and other fish sustained communities along the Klamath, Trinity, Eel, Sacramento, and coastal streams; shellfish and marine mammals supported coastal economies. Grass seeds, berries, roots, deer, and other resources contributed to regional diets. Skilled harvesting was ecological management, not passive dependence on untouched nature. Communities burned particular landscapes for basketry materials, food plants, forage, and travel, within local traditions and ecological conditions. Practices varied by place, season, and political community, and must not be transformed into a single California-wide system.

The northwest reveals why geographic specificity matters. Yurok communities maintained villages and ceremonial relationships along the lower Klamath and coast; Karuk communities lived upriver; Hupa communities maintained a distinct homeland along the Trinity. Fishing rights, wealth, ritual obligations, and diplomatic relationships structured exchanges and disputes. Around Humboldt Bay the Wiyot occupied villages associated with waterways, estuarine harvesting, and the World Renewal Ceremony. Along the coast to the north, Tolowa Dee-ni' homelands included ceremonial grounds later subjected to destructive settler attacks. These societies had established political geographies well before Europeans began charting the coast. A map that simply labels all of northern California “unsettled” reproduces the perspective of later colonizers.

Central California likewise contained diverse societies. Nisenan and other Maidu-speaking communities cultivated established relationships to the Feather and American river systems, while Miwok-speaking communities inhabited many Sierra foothill and valley landscapes. Pomo communities around Clear Lake and adjoining coastal regions possessed their own languages, villages, and distinctive technologies including finely worked baskets. Yokuts communities across the San Joaquin Valley and foothills depended on wetlands, rivers, grasslands, and oak resources. Farther south, Ohlone and Chumash communities had experienced centuries of European intervention more directly, especially through missions and ranchos. None of these differences made any people less Indigenous or less entitled to continuity in their homeland.

The years before 1848 were already marked by disruption. Epidemics, the mission system, Californio ranching, and expanding American commerce affected different regions to different degrees. Some people lived in mission or rancho labor systems; others had avoided sustained foreign occupation. Still others moved between farming, wage labor, hunting, gathering, and kin-based exchange. Such transitions were not simply stages of “assimilation.” People adapted under pressures that could include imprisonment, hunger, and violence, while preserving knowledge and relations of their own. The opening of goldfields in the Sierra thus struck a population whose historical experiences varied greatly, yet whose connections to land, livelihood, and community would all be jeopardized by the arrival of a new settler majority.

A further caution concerns population counts. Estimates for the Indigenous population before the American takeover vary widely because no comprehensive enumeration exists. Local histories, mission registers, archaeological evidence, and later demographic reconstruction capture different kinds of information. The chapter uses estimates carefully and does not use one statewide figure to flatten the variety of community histories. What can be said securely is that California was already inhabited, cultivated, governed, named, and understood by Indigenous peoples long before the Gold Rush; the catastrophe that followed was not a collision with a vacant landscape.

Section 70.2

From Spanish Missions and Mexican Ranchos to American Rule

The Gold Rush intensified older colonial processes rather than creating California's first experience of dispossession. Beginning with the Franciscan mission at San Diego in 1769, Spanish authorities established a network of missions along the coast and interior valleys. Conversion was intertwined with labor discipline and restrictions on movement. Baptized Indigenous people could be compelled to work within mission settlements and faced punishment when they attempted to leave. Disease and high mortality undermined families and communities; some people resisted, fled, negotiated, and retained ceremonial and kinship ties beyond mission boundaries. The system varied by time and mission, but describing it only as religious instruction would obscure the coercive organization of colonial society.

Mexico's independence in 1821 did not remove these inequalities. Secularization measures in the 1830s formally ended much of the missions' institutional landholding and reorganized their properties. In practice, large amounts of land passed into the hands of rancheros or other well-connected recipients, while the promised benefits for many Indigenous former mission residents were limited or denied. The ranchos required laborers for cattle, horses, hides, tallow, agriculture, and household service. Some Indigenous workers received wages or goods; others experienced debt, constraint, or force. Communities found ways to maintain households under the new conditions, but the transfer from mission to rancho seldom restored control over the land on which earlier villages had stood.

The geography of colonial intrusion was unequal. Along the southern and central coast, missions and ranchos directly transformed many local societies. In parts of northern California and the Sierra, Indigenous communities retained more of their political autonomy until American migrants approached their lands. This distinction is critical because the Gold Rush did not operate upon a uniform background. Some communities faced a new form of exploitation layered upon earlier coercive systems; others faced an abrupt incursion into landscapes where foreign government had previously been distant. Disease, violence, commercial pressure, and mobility crossed these boundaries, but they did not erase the differences.

John Augustus Sutter illustrates the continuity and change of labor arrangements. Sutter established New Helvetia near the confluence of the Sacramento and American rivers during the Mexican period, gathered livestock and agricultural resources, and drew upon Indigenous workers through arrangements that frequently involved intimidation and coercion. He was not a lone pioneer in empty country. Indigenous residents had existing agricultural, economic, and political lives in the surrounding region, while Sutter's enterprise inserted a private settler establishment into those networks. His sawmill at Coloma would become the site of the January 1848 gold discovery. Its labor force included Nisenan workers as well as non-Indigenous employees, a fact obscured by celebratory stories that place only two Euro-American men at the beginning of the Gold Rush.

The political transition itself occurred in stages. American naval and land operations established military positions during the U.S.–Mexico War, followed by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848 and California's admission to the Union in 1850. Chapter 69 addresses these military and diplomatic events. For Indigenous communities the essential issue was that a new government now claimed jurisdiction while settlers demanded access to land, labor, and resources without Indigenous consent. A treaty between Mexico and the United States did not represent a negotiated surrender of California Indigenous homelands by those living on them. The emerging American order also inherited a landscape of unequal property records and unresolved Indigenous title.

This continuity matters analytically. The mission regime and ranchos established precedents for constrained labor and settlement claims; the American period dramatically increased the volume of newcomers, the reach of racial law, and the military resources deployed against Indigenous people. The change after 1848 must be identified precisely rather than explained as though no earlier colonial histories existed. Indigenous Californians entered the Gold Rush neither isolated from the world nor devoid of their own political experience; they confronted a new state and settler society in ways shaped by decades, and sometimes generations, of previous struggle.

Section 70.3

Gold at Sutter’s Mill, 1848: Discovery in Nisenan Territory

On January 24, 1848, James W. Marshall found gold while examining the millrace at the sawmill being built for John Sutter along the South Fork of the American River at Coloma. The event became a foundational scene of California's American mythology. Yet the site was in Nisenan territory, not a place awaiting discovery by outsiders. Nisenan people worked in and around Sutter's establishment and were among the group associated with the find. The National Museum of the American Indian summarizes the discovery as involving Marshall and Nisenan workers. This is an essential correction to textbook narratives that identify Marshall alone while treating the surrounding Indigenous landscape as background.

The mill depended upon labor, water, timber, and existing pathways. In the world of settlers, water was a potential power source and gold-bearing gravel a prospecting opportunity; in the world of nearby Indigenous communities, the same river corridor was a homeland containing named places, food resources, and relationships of obligation. The same location could therefore hold fundamentally different meanings. A historical geography study should ask students to identify Coloma on a map of Nisenan lands and then consider what a standard map of “gold country” omits: older village territories, travel routes, food-gathering sites, and social relationships along the river.

The discovery initially circulated unevenly. News reached nearby settlements and San Francisco, where workers and merchants began leaving ordinary occupations to prospect. In 1848 migrants already living in California joined the search, and word moved to the Pacific coast of Latin America and across the continent. President James K. Polk's confirmation of the discovery in December 1848 amplified national interest; in 1849 a much larger migration followed. The sequence matters: the gold find itself was not the same event as the mass arrivals that transformed Indigenous livelihoods, and the most devastating results unfolded over years rather than on the day Marshall noticed gold.

For Nisenan communities and their neighbors, prospecting brought the sudden intrusion of armed men into streams, wooded valleys, and seasonal food landscapes. Mining could interfere with fisheries and water supplies and replace established access arrangements with self-declared miners' claims. Some Indigenous people exchanged food, provided labor, worked at mining sites, or attempted to maintain trade. Others faced threats, eviction, or violence when newcomers demanded exclusive control. Indigenous Californians were not merely victims appearing after the rush began; they were workers, guides, traders, and people with their own strategies, even as the rapidly increasing imbalance of force narrowed their choices.

Sutter's own ambition underscores the paradox. He expected a controlled agricultural and commercial estate supplied by Indigenous labor. The rush instead drew workers away and encouraged people to treat nearby lands and resources as opportunities independent of his direction. That disruption of one settler entrepreneur did not amount to freedom for the Indigenous laborers whose communities remained exposed. In later commemorations, accounts of Sutter and Marshall sometimes make settlers the only visible people. Recovering Nisenan history requires consulting tribal interpretation and careful readings of the evidence about labor, land, and who was present.

A source window for this section should therefore distinguish the 1848 gold artifact preserved by the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, the later accounts of discovery, and the National Museum of the American Indian's Indigenous-centered instructional account. The artifact establishes the physical event; neither a nugget nor Marshall's recollections alone can establish the political ownership of the surrounding valley. The historian must connect multiple kinds of evidence to explain how a gold discovery on Indigenous land became the center of an extraordinary settler invasion.

Section 70.4

The Gold Rush, Mass Migration, and the Seizure of Indigenous Homelands

Between 1848 and the early 1850s, California experienced a migration of extraordinary speed. Arrivals came by sea around Cape Horn or across the Isthmus of Panama, along overland routes from the United States, and from Mexico, Chile, China, Europe, and the Pacific. The composition of the migrant population changed over time, and its members did not all share one political ideology or form of conduct. Some came to mine, others to provide food, transportation, tools, lodging, or legal and commercial services. Nevertheless, the combined movement put enormous pressure on river corridors, agricultural valleys, port towns, and foothill homelands already inhabited by Indigenous communities.

The Gold Rush economy transformed places as much as populations. Miners diverted water through ditches and flumes, dug streambeds, felled timber, and established camps that became permanent settlements. Merchants and farmers supplied these camps, while roads and river shipping opened previously less accessible areas to sustained non-Indigenous entry. Each development altered relationships to land. A site useful to Indigenous families for harvesting, ceremony, fishing, or travel could be claimed by miners who recognized only the laws and customs of their own camps or the growing settler state. The injustice was not simply that people used land differently. It was that the authority to decide what would happen to it was taken from the communities who lived there.

Violence often emerged where miners, ranchers, or transport operators sought to control resources and labor. Accusations of theft or attacks on livestock could lead to a punitive expedition against an entire village, sometimes without credible inquiry into the circumstances. Newspapers printed allegations that treated Indigenous resistance to intrusion as a threat to public safety, while settler assemblies and official leaders called for force. Other individuals traded with local communities or opposed brutality. These differences must be acknowledged without hiding the cumulative result: a new political economy favored those who could obtain military support, legal recognition, or armed allies, and that was rarely an Indigenous village defending its established lands.

Indigenous responses were neither uniform nor passive. Some families moved away from mining zones, sometimes temporarily and sometimes permanently. People negotiated for access to work or food, adapted trading networks, and sought allies who could help protect particular places. Others resisted through armed defense when settlements were attacked. Still others found their villages caught between rival settler factions or military forces. Large numbers of women and children became vulnerable to kidnappers and exploitative employers who viewed Indigenous labor as available through coercion. These responses should be reconstructed locally: a Pomo community near Clear Lake, an Ahwahneechee community in Yosemite, and a Wiyot community at Humboldt Bay confronted related but different crises.

Economic change increased vulnerability to disease and hunger. A fishing site closed by mining could affect seasonal supplies; lost gathering grounds could undermine winter stores; the destruction of villages and seed-processing equipment could require exhausting movement to less productive lands. People weakened by deprivation were more vulnerable to illness and less able to recover after epidemics. The causal chains were not identical everywhere, and not every population decline resulted directly from a deliberate attack. But the structures of displacement amplified several sources of mortality at once, making it historically inadequate to attribute the catastrophe to “natural” disease alone.

The rush also transformed the emerging state's capacity. Migrants demanded laws and security forces that would protect property as they defined it. Courts, militias, county officials, and later federal administrative agencies gave these demands institutional form. Thus the movement of people from 1848 onward cannot be separated from the legislative order established in 1849–1850. The following division explains how property claims, racial exclusion, coerced labor, and failed treaty-making turned an explosive migration into a prolonged and organized assault on Indigenous lives and sovereignty.

Section 70.5

California Statehood and the Creation of a Racial Political Order, 1850

California's 1849 constitutional convention created a framework for admission as a state, and on September 9, 1850, Congress admitted California to the Union. This moment was not simply an institutional milestone. It defined political membership in racial terms. The new constitution restricted ordinary voting rights to white male citizens, with only a narrow mechanism through which the legislature could extend voting to certain Indigenous men. For most Indigenous Californians, the state being created over their homelands excluded them from meaningful participation in the institutions making decisions about their land, labor, and security. The language of freedom and self-government that accompanied American statehood thus coexisted with a sharply unequal political order.

Courts were another crucial site of exclusion. California legislation generally restricted Indigenous testimony in cases involving white persons, undermining victims' ability to obtain redress for kidnapping, assault, theft, or murder. In the notorious 1854 decision People v. Hall, the California Supreme Court extended the logic of racial testimony restrictions to Chinese witnesses. That case is often cited as an example of anti-Chinese racism, but it also exposes the reach and rationale of an existing system of Indigenous legal disability. A community's ability to seek justice depended on the court's willingness to hear its members and recognize their complaints. When a settler could commit an offense knowing that Indigenous witnesses might be excluded, the legal order helped make violence less costly to perpetrators.

State leadership contributed to the creation of this environment. In his address of January 6, 1851, Governor Peter H. Burnett forecast a continuing “war of extermination” between settlers and Indigenous people. The verified official transcript is evidence not merely that an isolated citizen harbored violent prejudice, but that the state's chief executive publicly described Indigenous destruction as a predictable political future. Burnett was not the sole architect of California policy, nor should the speech be mistaken for a formal written order to kill every Indigenous Californian. Its importance lies in the sanctioning language available to the governor at the very moment lawmakers, local volunteers, and military officers were deciding whether and how to finance operations against Indigenous communities.

Government responsibility operated on several levels. California legislators authorized funds and established rules; county officials organized local enforcement; volunteers and private citizens carried out raids; federal military units participated in some operations; and Congress later considered or paid reimbursements. These institutions were not interchangeable and did not act in identical ways. Some federal Indian agents protested violence, while other federal personnel helped administer confinement or forced relocation. Careful history needs a precise chain from official decision to military action and civilian consequence rather than a single undifferentiated accusation that “the government” did everything.

Political exclusion intensified the consequences of mass migration. Settlers could seek representation and demand state protection for property claims; Indigenous communities were often excluded from the same processes despite holding older claims to land. A local dispute about livestock, access to water, or a mining camp could become a public safety crisis as defined by settlers and then a reason to fund a punitive expedition. Racial language recast defense of an Indigenous village as hostility. Statehood gave the settler population greater capacity to institutionalize these assumptions, while Indigenous Californians faced an authority claiming legal jurisdiction without providing meaningful equal protection.

It is important not to collapse constitutional discrimination, Burnett's speech, the 1850 labor statute, and armed campaigns into one event. They were different mechanisms that reinforced one another. A constitution narrowed political voice; evidentiary restrictions obstructed justice; labor law facilitated coercion; public rhetoric normalized an expectation of destruction; and appropriations supported operations against communities labeled hostile. Taken together, they help explain why the period after 1850 saw not merely additional isolated violence but a more durable system that made dispossession and mass killing easier to organize and defend.

Section 70.6

The Act for the Government and Protection of Indians, 1850

On April 22, 1850, California enacted the Act for the Government and Protection of Indians. The title presented legislation as a measure for Indigenous welfare, but its provisions subordinated Indigenous people to settler officials and employers. The original act assigned authority to justices of the peace in matters involving Indigenous people, addressed their occupation of lands held by others, authorized a form of custody and labor control over Indigenous minors, and created punitive procedures for those designated as vagrants. These provisions operated in a society where many Indigenous people were unable to give legally effective testimony against white defendants. A document's declared purpose cannot substitute for a close reading of its actual powers and effects.

Section 2 nominally allowed Indigenous people living on property claimed by someone else to remain within limited circumstances, but it also placed decisions about their land use under local judicial supervision. The ability to set aside a small tract for an Indigenous community was not recognition of the full extent of its ancestral landholding. The starting assumption favored the property claim of the settler or proprietor, and disputes were heard by officials of the new state. This arrangement made Indigenous presence conditional within a system that had not generally obtained their consent to the transfer of title. The chapter must distinguish this form of circumscribed residence from secure communal ownership.

Section 3 addressed the custody and labor of Indigenous minors, allowing an adult who obtained a child to seek certification from a justice of the peace that the child had not been taken by compulsion. In practice the environment of violence, poverty, kidnapping, and unequal access to courts made meaningful consent profoundly doubtful in many cases. Section 20 authorized arrest of Indigenous people characterized as vagrants and their labor to be hired to bidders for limited terms. Such procedures did not always appear as formal slavery in statutory language, yet they enabled uncompensated or constrained work and could operate as an extractive labor system. Historians must identify the legal mechanisms precisely rather than simply use a label without demonstrating how they worked.

The statute changed over time. In 1860 California amended Section 3 to enlarge the categories of Indigenous people who could be bound under indenture, including adults, with potentially long terms that differed by age and sex. This expansion took place after a decade of documented exploitation. In 1863 the legislature repealed the indenture-related Section 3 as amended, but that did not abolish every coercive feature of the original statute or resolve the consequences of earlier indentures and forced labor. The remaining act was not repealed in its entirety until 1937. The difference among enactment, amendment, partial repeal, and complete repeal is a publication-critical point; treating 1863 as the end of the whole legal regime would be inaccurate.

The law should also be examined alongside other barriers to justice. When courts excluded the testimony of Indigenous witnesses against white individuals, the requirement that a magistrate judge whether a child was obtained without coercion was especially weak. Employers and captors might benefit from rules that purported to regulate abuses but placed the burden of proving them on those with minimal access to the courts. Some labor relationships proceeded outside formal law through threats, armed kidnapping, or private detention. The act therefore provided a framework that could legitimate coercion but was not the only means through which labor exploitation occurred.

A close source study should set the original act alongside the 1860 amendments and 1863 repeal. Students should ask who possessed legal power, whose statements courts could accept, how the law defined dependency, and how employers profited. They should also distinguish the original statutory words from summaries published much later. The Library of Congress identifies the 1850 law in the original printed California statutes; California State Library research provides the amendment history. Taken together, those documents show a legal system in which “protection” often functioned as control rather than enforceable security for Indigenous communities.

Section 70.7

Kidnapping, Indenture, and the Exploitation of Indigenous Labor

The Gold Rush increased demand for work while creating conditions under which Indigenous labor could be obtained through coercion. Mines and households needed workers to cook, clean, haul, gather fuel, tend livestock, and cultivate food. Agricultural estates and transport businesses also sought labor. Indigenous Californians sometimes entered paid work or traded services as strategies for survival, but this must not obscure the forced labor imposed upon many others. Employers could use the 1850 statute, the 1860 indenture amendment, debt claims, threats, or outright abduction. The legal category assigned to a person did not necessarily describe the person's actual freedom to leave an employer or reunite with family.

Children were particularly vulnerable. Kidnappers targeted young people because they could be compelled to work for years, separated from relatives, and raised in settler households where language and cultural practices were discouraged. Some captors sought a magistrate's certificate or indenture document, using the appearance of legal supervision to justify custody. Other transactions took place outside any lawful process. Indigenous parents facing raids, starvation, or the destruction of homes were rarely positioned to negotiate freely. A document that recorded nominal parental agreement might omit the threats that made resistance impossible. Historians need to compare such papers with accounts of raids, missing children, and later testimony by survivors.

Adult labor was also subject to coercion. Vagrancy provisions could turn the absence of recognized employment into an occasion for arrest and compelled service. Mining camps, ranches, and towns contained people who could be described as idle simply because their traditional work or seasonal movement did not fit settler legal categories. That classification allowed the state to exert force over individuals who were in fact living within their ancestral territory. The practice turned dispossession into a labor trap: the seizure of food resources increased poverty, and poverty could then be used as evidence that a person should be bound to work for someone else.

Violence against women and girls must be acknowledged without reducing them to passive figures. Sexual assault, domestic confinement, and separation from children appear in the larger history of kidnapping and coercive work, though records often disguise these abuses or omit victims' perspectives. Women also sustained food production, sought out missing relatives, escaped confinement, formed partnerships of mutual aid, and transmitted language and knowledge in changing circumstances. Men's experiences varied as well: some were forced into agricultural or mining labor; some attempted to obtain payment and protect kin; others resisted or moved to different regions. No one general pattern describes every individual, but the social scale of forced labor reached far beyond the formal workplace.

Newspapers provide especially difficult evidence. Accounts condemning kidnapping can document public awareness, but their authors could still use racist language or stereotype Indigenous people. Other articles defended the labor system as useful or portrayed kidnapped children as having been rescued. A printed account therefore must be read for its publication date, the interests of its editors, and the difference between an observed event and an allegation. The Smithsonian's Native Knowledge 360° source collection includes reports of apprenticing and kidnapping that are appropriate for comparing legal language with contemporary social practice.

The long-term consequences included broken kinship networks, economic dependency, loss of community members from ceremonial and agricultural work, and descendants who struggled to trace family origins. For an Indigenous nation organized through relationships among elders, children, villages, and land, the forced movement of children was not merely an individual labor abuse. It damaged the conditions under which a community reproduced language, identity, and political life. Students should retain the legal differences between slavery, indenture, coercive hiring, and kidnapping while explaining why they could combine within a destructive social order.

Section 70.8

The Eighteen Unratified Treaties of 1851–1852

Federal officials attempted to establish formal relationships with California Indigenous peoples through treaty negotiations in 1851–1852. Commissioners Redick McKee, George W. Barbour, and Oliver M. Wozencraft traveled to communities and negotiated eighteen agreements with leaders recorded in the documents as representing numerous nations, bands, and villages. These treaties offered areas reserved for Indigenous residence together with supplies, agricultural equipment, livestock, and other measures of support. The proposed arrangements were far from uniformly just; they sought major land cessions and sometimes rested on imperfect translation and limited understanding of who had authority to agree. Yet for participating communities they were also a possible means of securing recognized lands and protection against continuing settler intrusion.

The negotiating circumstances reflected the imbalance of power. Commissioners faced pressure to free valuable land for miners and settlers, while Indigenous representatives often confronted immediate threats to food, homes, and lives. Many communities were not represented at all. Treaties could employ the names of groups unfamiliar to outsiders, with later researchers struggling to identify every signatory, village, or claimed territory. The language of a “cession” might be expansive even when the people present had no authority to surrender the homes of absent neighbors. Historians therefore distinguish the existence of signed documents from the claim that all Indigenous Californians consented to an overarching settlement.

The federal government did not turn these negotiated texts into binding agreements. On July 8, 1852, the U.S. Senate refused its advice and consent to ratification of the eighteen treaties. The records were placed under an injunction of secrecy, which remained until January 18, 1905. This is a crucial sequence. The agreements were signed and submitted, but rejected; they never became ratified treaties. Concealment meant that the documentation was not readily available to communities and the wider public, while promised land protections failed to take legal effect. A later decision to publish the treaties did not retroactively ratify them or restore lost homes.

The consequences were severe. Indigenous communities that had negotiated or moved in anticipation of protection found that the federal government had not created enforceable reservations under those agreements. Settlers and California politicians had opposed the size and location of proposed lands, fearing that desirable fields, timber, or mineral resources might be withheld from non-Indigenous use. Meanwhile local violence continued, and federal agents were left to improvise other forms of reservation and administration. The resulting system should not be described as though the federal government had no contact or responsibility; it undertook a negotiated process and then refused to honor the agreements it had helped prepare.

The original documents remain useful despite nonratification. They record promised supplies, land arrangements, and the names of people who appeared as leaders. They also reveal the terms that federal commissioners considered acceptable in the early 1850s. A student working with a specific treaty should identify the date and place of signing, the Indigenous signatories, the land description, and the federal promises. The student must then pair that document with the Senate's rejection record, because treating the proposed terms as law would be a major historical error. The National Archives and the Oklahoma State University treaty database provide appropriate reference points, while the California Native American Heritage Commission explains the broader process.

The later history of California treaty claims demonstrates that rejected agreements did not disappear from political life. Indigenous communities and advocates continued to pursue legal and monetary redress in the twentieth century; Congress authorized claims work and enrollment rolls connected to the unratified treaties. Those later developments belong to a larger history of sovereignty and federal policy, but a brief notice here shows that the 1852 refusal produced enduring disputes. For Chapter 70 the central lesson is that legal promises, refusal of ratification, and secrecy formed an important part of the machinery of dispossession alongside raids, kidnapping, and private land seizure.

Section 70.9

State Militias, Vigilantes, and the Organization of Violence

Violence against California Indigenous communities after 1848 was committed through more than one mechanism. Armed settlers sometimes attacked villages on their own initiative; locally organized volunteer companies staged punitive expeditions; state officials authorized and reimbursed campaigns; and federal soldiers participated in particular operations. These categories overlap in practice but should not be confused in analysis. A volunteer who killed civilians could later receive payment through an approved claim, while a militia formally organized by state authority could act according to local settlers' demands. The institutional question is who ordered an operation, who supplied it, who carried it out, and who eventually paid for it.

An especially important piece of documentary evidence is the State of California's record of expenditures for military expeditions conducted during 1850–1852. The Smithsonian's Native Knowledge 360° project reproduces a California State Archives accounting that lists campaigns by regional designation and gives a total of $843,573.48 for specified expenditures. That is a historical sum stated in the document, not a universal total for the entire genocide and not an inflation-adjusted present-day figure. Other appropriations, authorizations, and federal reimbursements occurred in later years, so the total must not be treated as representing all public spending across the period. The record is nevertheless powerful evidence that violence could be financed through formal state administrative machinery.

The financial process mattered. Volunteers needed horses, arms, ammunition, rations, and transportation; officers or local officials compiled accounts for these costs; legislators or other authorities considered payment and reimbursement. Documentary language often described operations as defense of lives and property or suppression of “Indian hostilities.” Such official terms were political claims, not proof that the people attacked had initiated violence or that a campaign was justified. The historian must compare the reasons recorded in the authorization with the events preceding the expedition and the identity of those who were killed. In many cases the targets included women, children, elders, or people belonging to a different community from the alleged attackers.

Federal institutions also entered the record. Military detachments engaged in campaigns, while agents tried to create reservations or manage removal; different officials sometimes disagreed about the use of force. Congress appropriated money for some state expenses related to campaigns against Indigenous people, including legislation in 1861 to cover costs from several years in the 1850s. These acts of reimbursement are significant because they connect violence on the ground to fiscal decisions taken far from California. But they should be read with the precise years and amounts identified, not merged into an invented single ledger of federal spending.

Newspapers helped construct the conditions under which campaigns were acceptable. Reports of alleged livestock theft, road attacks, or settler deaths could be amplified into broad accusations against nearby Indigenous people. Editors could demand “protection” for miners while omitting the occupation of villages, destruction of crops, or prior attacks by settlers. Some journalists condemned massacres, and their accounts provide evidence that contemporaries recognized what was occurring. Comparing hostile and critical reporting reveals that racial extermination was not simply an unavoidable consensus. Officials and citizens made choices and articulated arguments about whether Indigenous lives should be protected or destroyed.

The result was a pattern in which civilian killings could be carried out under a language of public defense and then rewarded or excused through institutional channels. This does not mean every expedition had identical objectives, that all members committed the same acts, or that local conflicts lacked any Indigenous resistance. It means that state-building and the organization of violence were historically connected. Chapters on particular massacres must therefore identify perpetrators and events, while this section establishes the financial and political system that could make attacks repeatable over time. The distinction is fundamental to evaluating evidence of responsibility and intention in Section 70.19.

Section 70.10

The Clear Lake Massacre of 1850 and the Pomo People

Clear Lake in northern California was a Pomo homeland with villages, gathering places, water resources, and long-established relationships among communities. The settlement economy of the 1840s brought ranch operators who claimed land and demanded Indigenous labor. Andrew Kelsey and Charles Stone became notorious in historical accounts for exploiting and abusing Pomo workers and families. The details of their conduct are reconstructed from testimony and later accounts that require source criticism, but substantial evidence connects their regime to forced labor and physical mistreatment. When Pomo people killed the two men, settler authorities treated the deaths as grounds for punitive action rather than as events requiring full investigation of the preceding abuses.

In May 1850 a detachment of U.S. soldiers under Captain Nathaniel Lyon attacked Pomo people at Clear Lake in what became known as the Bloody Island massacre. The location has been described through several related place names and in changing accounts of the lake's geography. The soldiers fired on people gathered on or near an island, and the victims included noncombatants. Historical estimates of those killed vary widely. Some later commemorations and educational summaries present high figures, while other reconstructions give lower numbers. A responsible scholarly chapter should not declare an exact death total without explaining its source, the method used, and the limits of surviving documentation.

The massacre illustrates the danger of describing retaliatory state violence as a battle between equal armed forces. The immediate military operation involved government soldiers attacking people who were not organized as an army meeting them in conventional combat. The loss of relatives, family stores, tools, and access to safe homelands had consequences beyond those killed that day. Survivors faced the task of protecting children and elders while confronting the possibility that other settler or military forces might treat Pomo communities as collective enemies. A narrative that begins with Kelsey and Stone's deaths erases the labor regime and power imbalance that preceded the expedition.

Source comparison is essential. U.S. military accounts may record command, movement, ammunition, and claims about the engagement, but such reports reflect the perspective of the attacking organization. Pomo oral histories and tribal historical resources sustain memories of suffering, place, and community continuity that official records may not include. Later county histories can preserve details yet repeat settler assumptions about who was “hostile.” Students should identify whether an account was made immediately, decades afterward, or in modern commemoration, then ask which features are corroborated across different kinds of evidence. An uncertainty in the number killed does not make the fact of the massacre uncertain.

The episode also connects to the larger politics of California in 1850. The state had enacted the Act for the Government and Protection of Indians in April and was moving toward formal admission into the Union. The Clear Lake operation showed that federal military force could participate in violent displacement while state law was constructing a separate regime of Indigenous subordination. These were different institutions interacting within the same period of rapid territorial transformation. Any explanation limited to individual settler cruelty would miss the willingness of armed authorities to use severe violence on the surrounding Indigenous community.

Pomo peoples survived the massacre and continue to maintain historical, cultural, and political communities. Memorialization of Bloody Island gives the site a meaning different from the official language of an 1850 punitive expedition. The massacre should therefore be taught as an event with documented perpetrators and human consequences, but also as part of the continuing Pomo history of remembrance, land, and survival. The case demonstrates why the chapter uses tribal histories alongside military and settler records and why an Indigenous-centered account cannot end with the gunfire itself.

Section 70.11

The Mariposa War, 1850–1851, and Resistance in the Sierra Nevada

The Sierra Nevada foothills became an early center of Gold Rush conflict because mining camps, trading stations, and settler roads expanded into Indigenous homelands at extraordinary speed. Miwok, Ahwahneechee, and neighboring communities faced demands for land, food, and labor, and some people resisted attacks on their homes or property. Settler accounts commonly presented this resistance as inexplicable hostility toward miners. Such narratives fail to describe the destruction already experienced by communities around the mining settlements. The historian's task is to reconstruct the series of confrontations and political decisions that led to military organization, not simply accept the name “Mariposa War” as a transparent description of events.

James D. Savage, a trader and prominent figure in the region, became associated with the call for military action after attacks on settlements and trading posts. In early 1851 California authorities authorized a volunteer force known as the Mariposa Battalion. It contained companies under designated officers and operated as an armed force pursuing Indigenous communities through the Sierra foothills and Yosemite region. The men who composed it had differing motives, including fears about attacks, economic interests, and desires to open land for settlement. Yet the operation's actions had concrete consequences: villages and food supplies were destroyed, people were pursued and compelled to relocate, and communities lost control over sites essential to survival.

Yosemite Valley was the homeland of the Ahwahneechee and a place of extensive preexisting Indigenous knowledge. The battalion's entry in 1851 did not constitute the “discovery” of an uninhabited natural wonder. National Park Service historical interpretation describes the destruction of villages and food supplies and the forced movement of families. Tenaya, an important Ahwahneechee leader, appears in records of resistance and negotiation; those records often filter his perspective through military officers and settler writers. The local communities were not unified under a single commander with identical goals. Some representatives negotiated with federal commissioners; others resisted leaving a particular valley or returned after temporary displacement.

The Mariposa conflict intersected with the failed federal treaty process. Commissioners arriving to negotiate sought to persuade Indigenous leaders to accept designated reservations and reduce fighting. Their offers existed within circumstances of overwhelming pressure. An agreement might be understood by a settler official as consent to leave a homeland, while an Indigenous participant might regard it as a temporary attempt to obtain food, avoid killing, or protect relatives. Because the California treaties were later rejected, their promises could not furnish dependable legal security. Some communities who negotiated lost land without receiving the protections that might have justified the negotiation in their eyes.

The later memory of Yosemite illustrates another form of dispossession: the transformation of Indigenous history into a story of national wilderness. Tourists and writers celebrated the valley's scenery, sometimes portraying its former inhabitants as vanished or barely relevant. Yet descendants, tribal communities, and the National Park Service's own research document enduring cultural relationships to the area. An account of the Mariposa War should not turn Indigenous resistance into a brief obstacle on the path to scenic tourism. The destruction of villages and expulsion of residents were central to the creation of a landscape increasingly claimed as American property and later American heritage.

The episode should be compared with Clear Lake without merging the two. Both involved attacks justified as settler protection, but their geographical settings, named participants, and courses of resistance were different. In Mariposa, mining settlements and militarized entry into a mountain valley were especially important; at Clear Lake, ranch exploitation and a retaliatory federal attack formed the immediate chain. The shared mechanism is that the expansion of settler claims produced conflict and then armed forces redefined Indigenous defense of place as a problem to suppress. The continuing presence of Ahwahneechee and other communities challenges a narrative in which conquest completed Indigenous disappearance.

Section 70.12

The Northern California Massacres: Tolowa, Wiyot, and Other Communities

Northern California's coastal and river societies experienced a particularly destructive sequence of attacks during the 1850s and early 1860s. Tolowa Dee-ni', Wiyot, and neighboring peoples inhabited villages and ceremonial landscapes long before coastal ports and supply routes expanded with gold-related migration. Their economic lives included fishing, gathering, trade, and ceremonies linked to particular places. Settler incursions were not confined to encounters along mining creeks: they threatened villages where people had gathered for renewal observances, often when families were occupied with ceremonial activities rather than military defense. These attacks must be reconstructed with attention to Indigenous names and historical geography, not only the names imposed by newcomers.

In the northern coastal region, Tolowa Dee-ni' accounts describe campaigns against villages and ceremonial grounds, including the attack commonly dated to 1853 at Yontocket. The site was connected to renewal ceremonial life, and the massacre became one of several devastating attacks upon Tolowa communities during the decade. Contemporary newspapers and later written histories sometimes differ in dates, numbers of people killed, and how related events were counted. The Tolowa Dee-ni' Nation preserves historical descriptions of repeated village destruction and explains the continuing significance of particular sacred sites. A reliable textbook should not present a single casualty number as certain where sources and methods differ. It should, however, establish clearly that mass killing targeted Indigenous civilians and ceremonial life.

The better documented Wiyot massacre occurred on the night of February 25–26, 1860, at Tuluwat on Indian Island in Humboldt Bay, accompanied by attacks at other Wiyot settlements. Wiyot people had gathered for the World Renewal Ceremony, and many men were away gathering supplies when attackers arrived. Armed settlers killed sleeping women, children, elders, and others using hand weapons. The Wiyot Tribe identifies February 26 as the day associated with the massacre, though some historical accounts describe the attack as occurring in the early hours after February 25. Mortality estimates vary according to whether sources count Tuluwat alone or the broader coordinated attacks around Humboldt Bay. The larger history makes no sense if limited to one island detached from nearby communities.

These massacres were not unintended casualties of equal armed engagement. The attacks struck communities assembled for religious and social life; they destroyed families and undermined the transmission of knowledge. The Wiyot Tribe documents the severe population decline following settlement, disease, resource loss, slavery, displacement, and genocide. Survivors faced detention, inadequate provisions, and forced relocation, including movements to places such as Klamath and other reservations. The destruction affected not only those killed immediately but the continuity of ceremonies and the ability of remaining families to obtain food and maintain community institutions.

Survivor testimony and tribal history are indispensable here. Newspaper reports may confirm that violence occurred and sometimes condemn it, but the naming of communities, the meaning of ceremonial sites, and the longer history of survival require Indigenous sources. The National Museum of the American Indian includes accounts and images associated with survivors of the Indian Island massacre and other northern California attacks. The Wiyot Tribe's own history records both the massacre and later recovery of sacred land. Historical scholarship likewise emphasizes the capacity of descendants to sustain community life despite the attempted destruction of the social structures on which that life depended.

The sequence of Tolowa and Wiyot attacks demonstrates both a regional pattern and local variation. At different times and places settlers and volunteers attacked villages, sometimes drawing upon existing campaigns and sometimes acting outside formal military orders. State institutions and federal policies interacted with these operations, but no single official directed every attack. The historian should ask how settler fear, competition for land, organized violence, and the weak protection of Indigenous lives combined to make repeated massacres possible. The historical significance lies in the documented destruction of communities and the continuing Indigenous relationships to the places in which that destruction occurred.

Section 70.13

Indigenous Defense, Flight, and Resistance across California

California Indigenous peoples did not respond to expanding violence through one uniform policy. Different communities faced different levels of military pressure, possessed unequal access to food and allies, and pursued strategies conditioned by local history. Some defended villages or attacked armed intruders; others hid people and provisions, moved to distant relatives, sought negotiations, or entered temporary employment with settlers to obtain supplies. Resistance could consist of returning to a homeland after deportation, protecting ceremonial objects, preserving a language, or refusing to surrender the location of a community. An account limited to recorded battles would miss much of this history, while an account that describes only cultural persistence would obscure the armed resistance that also occurred.

In northern valleys and mountains, communities such as Yuki, Wailaki, Wintu, Yana, and Shasta faced campaigns and settler attacks linked to new roads, livestock ranching, and mineral development. The names cover distinctive peoples with different homelands and political structures; no statewide alliance unified their actions. When settler herds consumed gathering grounds or soldiers destroyed villages, local families could move toward steep mountain country or inaccessible valleys. Movement provided temporary refuge but could create new stresses on food supplies and relations with neighboring communities. Some people defended themselves with force, sometimes in response to the destruction of relatives or homes, and then faced indiscriminate retaliation against others with no demonstrated responsibility for the original clash.

In the Central Valley and Sierra, Miwok, Maidu, and neighboring communities combined avoidance, negotiation, and at times armed defense. Leaders had to weigh the immediate survival of children and elders against the loss of places that carried historical, ecological, and ceremonial importance. A treaty offer, ration promise, or temporary relocation could appear preferable to a military attack even if the long-term consequences were uncertain. The historian must not judge every decision from the comfortable knowledge of later outcomes. Indigenous individuals acted under coercion and with incomplete information, often trying to preserve the greatest possible part of community life when all available choices involved danger.

Local networks could offer assistance. Family and ceremonial relationships connected villages across ecological regions, and displaced individuals sometimes found shelter with kin or other Indigenous communities. Such assistance also had limits: receiving families might lack food or face their own military threats. The movement of refugees and the fragmentation of communities transformed local political relationships. People sometimes accepted a place on a reservation because it offered the possibility of rations or a reprieve from violence, while others feared that concentration would make them more vulnerable to disease, abuse, or further deportation. These different choices are evidence of agency under constraint, not proof that coercive policies were legitimate.

Written sources regularly misclassify these actions. A settler report might describe a band as “hostile” because it refused to leave an ancestral fishing site. A military officer might call the movement of children into forests “fleeing capture,” while a survivor later remembered the event as rescuing the community's young. Terms such as rebellion, raid, and negotiation require attention to who applied them and to what relationships of authority already existed. A nation defending its own land did not necessarily regard a settler as a lawful representative of an established government. Conversely, individuals within the same Indigenous community could disagree about the wisdom of resistance or the terms of peace.

The history of defense and flight is inseparable from later Indigenous survival. People who hid, crossed into neighboring regions, maintained kinship networks, and returned to ancestral places preserved the conditions of political continuity. Military operations sometimes destroyed villages or captured families, but they did not make Indigenous societies vanish. Section 70.18 will examine reconstruction, while the present section emphasizes the difficult decisions and active strategies by which people confronted violence as it was unfolding. Teaching these histories responsibly requires locally grounded evidence, not a generic account of statewide resistance or a triumphalist story in which survival erases loss.

Section 70.14

Federal Reservations, Forced Marches, and the Round Valley System

Federal officials responded to the crisis in California partly by creating reservations or administrative centers where Indigenous people were expected to reside under government supervision. These projects were often portrayed as humanitarian protection from settler violence, but they could become instruments of dispossession, confinement, and forced labor. The earliest reservation arrangements were established amid the failure of the 1851–1852 treaties, and they differed in location, legal standing, supplies, and management. Sites associated with Nome Cult and Round Valley, Mendocino, Klamath, and other areas were not interchangeable. The chapter must distinguish their individual histories rather than refer to one universal reservation system that worked the same way everywhere.

The basic contradiction was that removing people from homelands could be described as saving their lives. Federal agents and soldiers sometimes recognized that migrants and militias posed grave dangers; they nevertheless proposed concentration of Indigenous families in places selected for administrative convenience or settler demands. Some communities negotiated for food and land because the alternative was starvation or attack. But confinement could separate people from established fisheries, ceremonial grounds, and kinship obligations. Supplies often failed to arrive or were inadequate, administrators could be corrupt or ineffective, and disease spread among people concentrated under poor living conditions. Survivors might encounter further threats from settlers encroaching even upon designated reservation lands.

Round Valley, in the northern interior, became a central destination for coerced movement. The location, associated historically with the Nome Cult reservation and later reservation administration, was distant from the homelands of many people sent there. Routes to the valley could cross mountains and difficult seasonal terrain; captives might already have been weakened by hunger and prior attacks. The journey itself could become deadly. Even after arrival, the people deported were not all members of one nation, and the forced proximity of communities with different histories, languages, and territorial relationships created further difficulties. Such outcomes cannot be evaluated solely by a government report stating that a given number of people reached a reservation.

One especially well documented episode occurred in September 1863, when Indigenous people assembled near Chico were forced toward Round Valley under military escort. Historical reports describe 461 people beginning the march under guard and 277 reaching the destination. A contemporary officer's accounting attributed the difference to people left behind as too ill to continue, deaths on the route, and escapes. Those categories are not equivalent to a verified death toll for the entire episode: people abandoned by a column may have died later or survived, and other sources report the circumstances differently. The march is commemorated as California's Trail of Tears, a name expressing the suffering of those removed while identifying a history distinct from the southeastern removals treated in Chapter 66.

Families on these marches lost control over the pace of travel, provision of food, and care of the sick. Soldiers could decide whether an elderly person was capable of walking; people carrying children had to navigate long distances with limited resources. A commander might describe the movement as successful because a designated destination was reached, even when the route left vulnerable people behind. Historical methodology therefore requires comparing departure numbers, arrival numbers, military explanations, and the memories of families and communities. The distinction between the government's administrative definition of removal and survivors' experience is a core teaching point.

The reservation system should not be described as total or uniform captivity, nor as consistently protective. Some individuals escaped or returned, some negotiated space for community life, and others created lasting relationships in places to which they were forced. Many California Indigenous communities continued to live off-reservation or in small settlements, while others became part of new multi-community reservation populations. These patterns are central to the history of later identity and political organization. Yet the initial reservation policies must be assessed in the context of the violence and land seizures that made relocation appear to officials as the solution to a crisis their own society had helped create.

Section 70.15

Mining, Environmental Destruction, Hunger, and Disease

The environmental consequences of the Gold Rush extended far beyond the visible excavation of gold. Miners removed stream gravel, diverted water, cut timber, built ditches and dams, and later used powerful hydraulic techniques that accelerated the deposition of sediment and debris in waterways. These changes affected fisheries, wetlands, vegetation, and drinking water at different rates. Salmon and other fish depended upon suitable streams and river passages; damage to riverbeds, flow, and water quality could undermine food systems developed over generations. The timing should be kept precise: early placer mining expanded from 1848 onward, while large-scale hydraulic mining intensified after its development during the 1850s. The environmental story changed with technology and investment.

Other industries compounded mining's effects. Settler livestock grazed meadows and consumed plants or forage used by Indigenous communities; fenced farms and ranches blocked access to traditional gathering grounds; logging removed timber and altered watersheds; urban growth converted wetlands and river edges into property and transport infrastructure. Indigenous harvest practices often relied on movement among different ecological zones over the year. Losing access to even one seasonal gathering place could make a household vulnerable during the next season. The problem was not simply that the landscape changed, but that Indigenous people were increasingly prevented from adjusting their practices through customary use of established territories.

Food insecurity emerged through several mechanisms. A village destroyed by an armed expedition might lose stores of acorns, seeds, dried fish, or tools needed to prepare food. A population forced to leave a salmon river could not simply substitute an unfamiliar or already occupied place. A household whose children or productive adults had been kidnapped lost labor required for gathering and processing provisions. A reservation that promised rations but delivered them irregularly could create dependence without ensuring survival. These mechanisms interacted; none needs to be reduced to a single cause of starvation. Historians should be cautious about citing the mere presence of hungry people as evidence that Indigenous subsistence systems were inherently inadequate.

Disease was also consequential. Epidemics predated American annexation, and growing commercial connections brought new exposure and outbreaks. Crowded settlements, military confinement, labor camps, and forced marches could worsen vulnerability; malnutrition and stress often weakened people's ability to recover. Contemporary records sometimes attribute deaths to named illnesses, but the diagnostic language and coverage of reports vary. Where no reliable count survives, a textbook should say so rather than derive an exact epidemic death total from scattered narratives. The relationship between disease and violence is complex because people made vulnerable through removal, deprivation, or forced labor may have died of an illness that cannot be understood independently of those conditions.

Ecological history is not a substitute for political history. Mining sediment did not decide by itself that one community could be excluded from a fishery or that armed miners would seize a village. Those outcomes followed social institutions: property law, racial claims, military action, commercial investment, and the refusal to recognize Indigenous territorial authority. Similarly, the destruction of controlled burning regimes or the exclusion of Indigenous management practices could transform landscapes that settlers later described as deteriorated through Indigenous neglect. Scientific and historical evidence should be used to recover the older management systems and the consequences of disrupting them.

Students can examine the causal sequence through an everyday-life case: a family gathering food along a river before mining, then confronting a polluted stream, livestock competition, raids, or a relocation order. Which resources were lost first? Could relatives provide an alternative? What did the family do to retain its children, language, or customary labor? Those questions link large economic forces to human experience without reducing Indigenous people to numbers. They also explain why the demographic collapse evaluated in Section 70.16 cannot be interpreted only as fatalities from directly recorded massacres, and why environmental dispossession remains part of the history of state formation.

Section 70.16

Counting the Catastrophe: Demography, Mortality, and Historical Evidence

Historians have documented a catastrophic decline in California's Indigenous population during the first quarter century of U.S. rule, but the numbers require careful explanation. Benjamin Madley's reconstruction places the population at perhaps 150,000 in 1846 and no more than about 30,000 in 1873. Those figures are not counts made by a single reliable census office using consistent categories. They synthesize scattered estimates, government enumerations, demographic studies, records of violence, and other evidence. The magnitude of decline is central to the history, yet the exact numerical endpoints remain approximate. A scholarly chapter must preserve that uncertainty rather than convert a reconstructed estimate into an exact enumeration.

It is especially important to distinguish population decline from directly recorded killing. The difference between two estimates separated by decades cannot be labeled a documented number of murder victims. Some people died in massacres, smaller attacks, or individual homicides; others died through starvation, epidemic disease, forced labor, exposure, or the consequences of displacement and reservation confinement. Some left recorded Indigenous categories through movement or changes in enumeration; birth rates were also affected when households were disrupted. Sources vary by region and sometimes omit people beyond the reach of officials. Historians may estimate violent deaths through documented incidents, but the surviving record is incomplete and does not yield one universally accepted total.

Madley's work assembles evidence for more than three hundred massacres and mass killings and distinguishes organized lethal violence from other causes of mortality. Earlier demographers, including Sherburne F. Cook, used different available records and methods. Comparing such research is not a matter of selecting whichever figure is most dramatic. Scholars ask how incidents were defined, whether multiple sources described the same event, whether deaths during a forced march were recorded at departure or arrival, and whether a government report had incentives to minimize or exaggerate losses. An incident count is not automatically a person count, and a person count made from partial records is not a census of all casualties.

The documentation is unusually uneven. Some expeditions generated muster rolls, appropriations, and military correspondence, while private attacks may appear only in a newspaper or family memory. Officials might document their own expenses carefully and record Indigenous dead vaguely. Settler accounts could call an attack a “fight” even when witnesses described women and children as the principal victims. Tribal oral histories preserve place names, kin relationships, and forms of harm omitted by official papers, but recording dates and translation histories can complicate comparisons with written sources. Sound historical method draws on these distinct evidentiary forms without assuming that a government archive is automatically more complete or a survivor's memory automatically provides precise casualty arithmetic.

This distinction is important when evaluating a particular massacre. At Clear Lake or in the Tolowa region, published estimates may differ by large margins; historians should indicate what the evidence supports, not impose false precision. At Tuluwat, figures may refer only to the island or to multiple coordinated attacks around Humboldt Bay. During the 1863 removal toward Round Valley, the difference between people who departed and arrived included people abandoned, people who died, and those who escaped. These categories answer different questions. A good textbook should tell students the unit being measured and make clear when information remains missing.

Even qualified figures have interpretive force. A population falling from roughly 150,000 to around 30,000 within this period represents a devastation of unprecedented scale for many of California's Indigenous communities under the new regime. The scale of loss demands explanation in terms of specific political and social mechanisms, rather than fatalistic assertions that “contact” naturally produces disappearance. Yet the surviving population was not an undifferentiated remnant. Communities retained different histories, languages, affiliations, and places of refuge. Demographic evidence must therefore accompany the histories of governments, violence, families, and survival rather than replace them.

Section 70.17

Families under Conquest: Women, Children, Elders, and Everyday Survival

Conquest was experienced at the scale of households as well as armies and legislative bodies. A family needed safe access to water, food, clothing, and seasonal shelter; children needed the protection of adults and the opportunity to learn language and community obligations; elders carried knowledge of gathering places, medicines, histories, and ceremonial practice. Attacks on villages and forced movement disrupted all these relationships. When a woman lost a child to kidnapping, when an elder was left behind on a forced march, or when a family's food stores were destroyed, the consequences extended into the life of the community. A historical account centered only on adult male combatants would miss the extent of the catastrophe.

Women bore distinctive burdens. Some were subjected to sexual violence, compelled domestic service, or separation from children, and such experiences are often poorly recorded in official sources. Women also organized household survival by obtaining food, finding refuge, negotiating work arrangements, and sustaining relationships with kin. Traditional technologies of baskets, textiles, storage, and medicine could be carried, adapted, or taught under changed conditions. These practices were not automatically safe or uninterrupted. Their persistence required labor and decisions made amid fear, hunger, and the threat of further removal. Stories of endurance should therefore not be used to minimize the coercion that made endurance necessary.

Children were targets of labor exploitation because an employer could obtain years of service through capture or indenture. The loss of a child disrupted relationships among parents, grandparents, and communities responsible for teaching language, story, ecology, and ceremony. A child raised away from relatives might face barriers to recovering an original name or homeland. Some later reunited with families or maintained hidden connections, but surviving documents often identify them only through a settler employer's records. The history of coerced childhood labor therefore requires reconstructing both legal paperwork and the silences surrounding those excluded from the record.

One important source is the testimony of Sally Bell, a Sinkyone survivor of the massacre at Needle Rock. Her account was recorded much later and published in Gladys Ayer Nomland's 1935 study of Sinkyone materials. The National Museum of the American Indian presents the testimony alongside photographs and source questions. It describes the killing of members of her family and her own survival. The account must be identified with its recording and publication history rather than passed off as a document transcribed at the time of the attack. That chronological distance does not invalidate the history; it requires the historian to discuss memory, the circumstances of collection, and the very different kinds of information survivor testimony conveys.

Another testimony in the Smithsonian collection, associated with Wailaki survivor Lucy Young, illustrates how later documentation can preserve details of attacks and survival not captured in contemporary military reports. These accounts are not interchangeable: each relates to a specific community and event. The historian should avoid extracting a dramatic sentence and using it as if it represented every Indigenous Californian. Instead, students should ask where the survivor lived, who recorded the testimony, what the circumstances were, and which claims can be corroborated by other sources. First-person or remembered experience is particularly important for understanding fear, movement, and the preservation of life beyond the categories used by officers and officials.

Despite tremendous loss, households and community networks were continuously made and remade. People adopted orphaned relatives, preserved connections to ancestral places, adapted economies, and held ceremonies where possible. Elders taught young people how to recognize plants and maintain obligations even when settlement patterns changed. Some families sought safety in towns or reservations; others returned seasonally to their homelands. This everyday history is essential to the chapter's central argument: laws and violence attempted to transform Indigenous life, but Indigenous communities were living social institutions that responded with practical care, political action, and sustained knowledge.

Section 70.18

Indigenous Persistence: Community Reconstruction and Cultural Continuity

The history of California's Indigenous catastrophe must not end by treating survivors as remnants without future. Pomo, Nisenan, Maidu, Miwok, Wiyot, Yurok, Karuk, Hupa, Tolowa, and many other peoples maintained relationships to families and landscapes that settlers had attempted to appropriate. Some communities continued to occupy portions of ancestral territory; others moved to small rural settlements, rancherías, reservations, towns, or households linked through kinship. Displacement sometimes brought people of different nations into close contact, producing new relationships without erasing older identities. The persistence of community life was not guaranteed by American policy. It was the result of choices, work, resistance, and assistance among Indigenous people.

Continuity could be social, linguistic, ceremonial, and political. Elders and other knowledge holders preserved stories and place names, taught young people methods of gathering and preparing food, and sustained songs, ritual obligations, and relationships to particular sites. In some places ceremonies continued publicly; in others they were restricted or undertaken in less visible ways because of hostility from non-Indigenous authorities. Some people learned to use new economic opportunities while maintaining Indigenous identity, and others avoided settler institutions as far as possible. The continued presence of a community must not be measured solely by whether its landholding or political institutions looked identical to those of the year 1846.

The Wiyot experience offers a particularly clear example. The destruction of Tuluwat and other villages in 1860 inflicted staggering harm, but Wiyot descendants maintained their connection to Humboldt Bay and remembered the World Renewal Ceremony. The Wiyot Tribe's history records both the massacre and the long campaign to reclaim sacred land. The return of portions of Tuluwat to the Tribe, and the resumption of ceremony in modern times, belong to a later period; a brief reference here demonstrates that the massacre did not terminate Indigenous history. Modern land return does not undo the deaths, but it makes visible political and cultural continuity that older historical accounts too often ignored.

Pomo communities likewise preserved distinct identities after Clear Lake and other attacks. The consequences of displacement and coerced labor varied among bands and local communities. Some people lived on or near ancestral lands, others had to negotiate settlements or adapt to new wage economies, and many later pursued recognition or property rights. The same diversity appears among Maidu, Nisenan, and groups along the Klamath and Trinity. Broad statewide narratives are useful for explaining large-scale institutions, but they should be accompanied by specific community histories so that students can see which Indigenous people continued to govern, communicate, and survive in which places.

The word “reconstruction” is appropriate when it means the work of reestablishing the conditions of community, not when it implies that the old society vanished before a completely new one appeared. Kinship could extend across new settlements; remembered territories could remain central to identity after temporary exile; ceremonial obligations could continue even under confinement. At the same time, survival should not be romanticized. Languages were endangered, relatives lost, some institutions profoundly disrupted, and many people denied safe access to homelands. Indigenous perseverance is a historical achievement because the harms were real, not because adversity automatically produced strength.

Indigenous persistence also changes how students interpret nineteenth-century sources. An official who predicted disappearance might later be contradicted by a tribal government maintaining institutions and recovering historical documents. A settler account that called a place “abandoned” may have ignored seasonal use or families displaced only temporarily. A photograph of one survivor can be read within a history of descendants rather than as an image of a dying people. Section 70.19 will examine how historians use such evidence to judge the nature of the catastrophe. The present section establishes a necessary companion truth: the violence was devastating, and the nations subjected to it continued to exist.

Section 70.19

Was the California Catastrophe Genocide? Evidence, Responsibility, and Historical Interpretation

The term genocide requires historical argument, not just moral intensity. The United Nations Genocide Convention of 1948 defines genocide through specified acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such. The convention postdates the California Gold Rush by almost a century, so a modern historian applying its framework is not claiming that an 1850 official was prosecuted under a law that did not yet exist. Instead, the historian asks whether the documented actions, language, and institutional practices meet the analytical criteria. Population decline by itself, however catastrophic, does not establish the element of intent; neither does a single hateful statement independently explain every death.

Evidence of intent can be drawn from patterns and circumstances as well as words. California Governor Peter H. Burnett's January 1851 address anticipated a “war of extermination,” and that rhetoric must be read together with legislative appropriations, official authorization of military campaigns, the conduct of soldiers and volunteers, and attacks directed at noncombatants. Repeated destruction of villages, murder of women and children, and systems that facilitated kidnapping and forced labor are not reducible to accidents of migration. Historian Benjamin Madley identifies organized killing and state and federal participation, assembling a substantial documentary basis for describing the 1846–1873 catastrophe as genocide. Brendan C. Lindsay likewise analyzes the political culture and governmental structures that enabled exterminatory violence.

The evidence requires differentiation. Some killings were carried out by private settlers; some by state-recognized volunteers; some by U.S. soldiers. Some incidents involved armed clashes while others were attacks on sleeping families or ceremonial gatherings. State payments for expeditions do not alone prove that every soldier killed noncombatants; they do demonstrate institutional support for campaigns and require further examination of what those campaigns did. The 1850 law's labor provisions were not identical to massacre orders, but they enabled family separation and coercive conditions. Historians must trace specific decisions and consequences while explaining how violent acts could constitute parts of a larger policy environment.

Not all scholars have used the same conceptual frame. Gary Clayton Anderson has argued for a primary emphasis on ethnic cleansing or forced removal in parts of the wider American West; Madley responds that the California evidence also establishes genocidal intent and acts, and that removal and genocide need not be mutually exclusive. Settler colonialism describes a broader structure through which newcomers seek land and political control; it is not in itself a complete classification of each killing. The responsible approach is to assess competing interpretations against documentary evidence, their definition of terms, the regional and chronological limits of their analysis, and the experiences of Indigenous communities. It would be misleading either to claim no debate exists or to treat all interpretations as equally supported without examination.

Demographic evidence is an indispensable but distinct component. The estimated decline from roughly 150,000 people in 1846 to around 30,000 in 1873 reveals the scale of devastation. To explain intent, historians also examine government speeches, military correspondence, legislative acts, survivor testimony, and repeated patterns of violence. Not all of the population decline resulted from intentional killing: epidemic disease, starvation, forced labor, exposure, and disruption of reproduction contributed, sometimes as consequences of deliberate policies. A claim of genocide must be argued through the intersection of acts and intent, not through subtraction of population estimates alone. The coexistence of disease with organized killing does not disprove a genocide interpretation.

The historical conclusion should be evidence-based and clear. Strong scholarship documents targeted mass violence and significant institutional sanction, making genocide an important and well-supported explanation for much of the catastrophe. At the same time, precise numbers of victims, the intentions of particular local officials, and some massacre details remain uncertain. Recognizing those uncertainties strengthens the analysis by separating what can be demonstrated from what cannot. Indigenous survivors' accounts and tribal histories must be integrated as evidence about both destructive acts and the continued life of communities. Genocide analysis should not make the peoples affected disappear from the story at the moment scholars classify what was done to them.

Historical Vocabulary

Genocide, Ethnic Cleansing, and Settler Colonialism

Definition. Genocide is a legally and historically analyzed pattern of prohibited acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or part, a protected group as such. Ethnic cleansing emphasizes coercive removal of a population from a territory; settler colonialism refers to a wider structure of territorial replacement and rule.

Historical context. The 1948 United Nations Genocide Convention postdates the Gold Rush, but scholars use its categories analytically to examine documented killings, family separation, official language, and the organization of violence in California.

Why it matters. A documented population decline is insufficient by itself; historians examine intentional acts, their purposes, and patterns across institutions and places.

Potential misunderstanding. These terms are not automatic substitutes for one another. Ethnic cleansing, forced removal, and genocide can overlap, and a retrospective historical analysis is not a criminal conviction under an anachronistic statute.

Section 70.20

California after the Gold Rush: Indigenous Sovereignty, Survival, and Historical Legacy

By the early 1870s the Indigenous societies of California had undergone transformations of terrible scale. The discovery of gold was followed by settler migration, a new state constitution, coercive labor law, failed treaty negotiations, organized armed campaigns, repeated massacres, forced relocation, ecological disruption, hunger, and disease. The effects differed across regions and communities, but the general outcome was a major collapse in Indigenous population and the loss or fragmentation of control over many ancestral lands. No single event or institution explains the catastrophe. The chapter's purpose has been to show how identifiable decisions by settlers, officials, military leaders, and employers combined with wider economic forces to produce destruction.

The historical record also shows continuing Indigenous political authority. A community did not cease to be a people because California refused to recognize its land title or because its leaders had been excluded from a legislature. Relationships to homelands, shared history, kinship, ceremonies, languages, and political obligations continued, though sometimes under conditions of profound loss. Groups remained on ancestral lands where possible, established new settlements where necessary, and pursued recognition and protection over later decades. The modern existence of California tribal nations is not an argument that the losses were less severe than scholars describe; it is evidence that the project of eliminating Indigenous societies did not achieve complete erasure.

Later forms of redress and historical recovery grew from these continuities. Communities challenged land dispossession, sought recognition, documented genealogies, revitalized languages, and worked to restore access to sacred places. The long aftermath of the unratified treaties entered twentieth-century claims processes. These developments should be acknowledged but not expanded into an exhaustive account of modern law, which belongs elsewhere in the series. Their relevance here is methodological: evidence produced after 1873 can clarify names, family relationships, places, and memories that nineteenth-century official sources recorded incompletely or not at all.

Historical memory has changed as well. California's public history long celebrated miners and pioneers while treating Indigenous deaths as incidental or inevitable. Scholarship by Madley, Lindsay, Hurtado, and others has made governmental responsibility, coerced labor, and Indigenous survival harder to exclude. Tribal governments, families, historians, and museums have contributed essential evidence and challenged explanations that confuse settlement with civilizational progress. A serious textbook should neither replace one simplified morality tale with another nor revive the older assumption that only official papers are objective. It should make its evidentiary base visible, identify disputed points, and explain clearly why much of the violence has been characterized as genocide.

The chapter's final comparison should return to its opening landscapes. The American River at Coloma was not empty when gold was discovered. Clear Lake was Pomo homeland before federal troops attacked. Yosemite Valley was Ahwahneechee country before armed volunteers entered it. Tuluwat was a Wiyot ceremonial place before settlers murdered people gathered there. Round Valley became a site of forced concentration without erasing the identities of the communities brought to it. Each location reveals the difference between a territorial label on a settler map and the history of peoples who lived and maintained obligations there.

Chapter 69 explained how international war moved California into a new American jurisdiction. Chapter 70 has shown that the everyday making of that jurisdiction involved coerced labor, land seizure, violence, and the attempt to reorganize Indigenous society. The next chapter turns to the Diné and the Long Walk, a distinct history of military campaigns, incarceration, return, and political reconstruction. The shared continental theme is not that every Indigenous nation underwent the same experience, but that expanding states pursued control of territories already inhabited and governed by other peoples. Indigenous sovereignty and survival remain central to understanding the history beyond conquest.

Chapter Summary

California’s Gold Rush followed American conquest but did not begin in empty country. Indigenous nations had established homelands, political institutions, and food systems throughout the state. Gold was found at Sutter’s Mill in January 1848 in Nisenan territory, with Nisenan workers present. Rapid migration, mining, agriculture, and transportation networks expanded onto established Indigenous lands and disrupted access to livelihoods and sacred places.

Spanish missions and Mexican ranchos had already created coercive labor systems in parts of California. American statehood intensified legal inequality: the 1849 constitution restricted political participation by race, the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians enabled coercive labor and territorial interference, and many Indigenous witnesses were denied meaningful access to the courts. The 1860 amendment enlarged indenture powers; the repeal of Section 3 in 1863 was not a complete repeal of the statute, which survived until 1937.

Federal treaty commissioners negotiated eighteen agreements in 1851–1852, but the Senate rejected them on July 8, 1852, and imposed secrecy later removed in 1905. There was no binding reservation settlement under those treaties. Meanwhile state militia finance, federal participation, and private violence contributed to campaigns against Indigenous people. The Clear Lake attack on Pomo civilians, armed expeditions into Ahwahneechee territory, attacks on Tolowa ceremonial communities, and the 1860 Wiyot massacres reveal distinct regional histories within a larger violent pattern.

Reservations and forced marches created additional suffering. In September 1863 Indigenous people marched under military guard toward Round Valley; records distinguish those who arrived from the many left behind, dead, or escaped. Mining debris, altered rivers, grazing, and blocked gathering grounds compounded food shortages and exposure to disease. Benjamin Madley reconstructs a decline from perhaps 150,000 Indigenous Californians in 1846 to about 30,000 by 1873, but these figures are not a direct count of murder victims.

Genocide scholarship evaluates patterns of acts and intent, drawing upon governor’s statements, legislation, militia expenditures, documented massacres, and survivor testimony. Indigenous histories challenge older narratives of inevitable disappearance. Pomo, Wiyot, Nisenan, Maidu, and many other nations preserved ties to homelands, kinship, languages, ceremonies, and political identities despite grave losses. California’s history after 1848 must be understood as both catastrophic dispossession and continued Indigenous sovereignty and survival.

Key Terms

Essential Terms and Concepts

California Indian catastrophe
Historical description of intertwined killings, dispossession, unfree labor, deprivation, and demographic collapse under U.S. rule.
Clear Lake / Bloody Island
Site of the May 1850 U.S. military attack on Pomo civilians following abuses associated with Kelsey and Stone.
Coloma
Nisenan homeland location of the January 1848 gold discovery at Sutter’s Mill.
Commemoration
Public and community practices through which people remember events, victims, and survival.
Demographic reconstruction
Estimation of past populations from incomplete and differently produced historical records.
Ethnic cleansing
Expulsion or forced removal of an identified population from a territory, sometimes overlapping with genocide.
Genocide
Certain destructive acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a protected group as such.
Gold Rush
Large-scale migration and economic transformation triggered by gold finds beginning in 1848.
Indenture
A form of legally structured service that, under California law, could be used coercively against Indigenous adults and children.
Indian Act of 1850
California statute regulating Indigenous lives and enabling coercive labor practices; amended 1860, partly repealed 1863, fully repealed 1937.
Mariposa Battalion
State-authorized volunteer military force that operated in Sierra Nevada Indigenous homelands in 1851.
Mission secularization
Mexican-era transfer of many mission lands and institutions into non-mission control, with limited Indigenous restitution.
Nisenan
Indigenous people of the American River region, including land at and around Sutter’s Mill.
Reservation
Territory designated for Indigenous residence and government supervision, often not identical to ancestral homelands.
Round Valley
Northern California destination of forced movement, including the 1863 Chico-to-Round Valley march.
Settler colonialism
System of permanent settlement and political control that seeks to replace Indigenous authority over land.
State militia
Armed force organized or recognized under state authority, sometimes reimbursed through public funds.
Tuluwat
Wiyot ceremonial village and sacred place on Indian Island in Humboldt Bay, attacked in February 1860.
Unratified treaty
Signed proposed agreement lacking U.S. Senate consent and ratification; California’s eighteen 1851–1852 treaties were rejected.
Vagrancy provision
Law allowing officials to arrest a person classified as without recognized employment and compel labor.
World Renewal Ceremony
Wiyot and other northern Californian ceremonial practices renewing reciprocal relationships and world balance; culturally specific practices differ by nation.
Yontocket
Tolowa ceremonial and village site subjected to massacre during the 1850s.

Chapter Reflection

California’s Gold Rush was a transformative event in the history of settlement and wealth, but its history cannot be separated from Indigenous peoples whose land and work made the landscape possible and whose lives were subjected to an extraordinarily destructive political order. Studying the catastrophe responsibly requires tracing choices: who asserted a land claim, who wrote a law, who funded a campaign, who attacked, who benefited, and who attempted to survive.

Historical knowledge also requires attention to what records reveal and conceal. The evidence of massacres and governmental involvement is substantial, while exact casualty figures and the histories of some families remain incomplete. That limitation is a reason to specify sources, not a reason to ignore violence or to treat Indigenous testimony as secondary. The chapter’s conclusion is not Indigenous disappearance. The nations subjected to conquest continue to preserve communities, knowledge, and relationships to the places that the Gold Rush transformed.
