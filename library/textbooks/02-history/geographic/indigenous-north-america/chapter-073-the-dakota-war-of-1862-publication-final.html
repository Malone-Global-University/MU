The Dakota War of 1862

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On December 26, 1862, thirty-eight Dakota men were hanged together on a single scaffold at Mankato, Minnesota. They had been condemned by a military commission after six weeks of war, but the road to that scaffold began years earlier, in treaties that stripped the Dakota of most of their homeland and in a system of payments that failed the people it was meant to sustain.

The execution at Mankato is commonly described as the largest mass execution in the history of the United States. It took place in front of soldiers and a large crowd of Minnesota residents, in a town that had been placed under tight military control for the occasion. The men on the scaffold were not a random sample of the Dakota people. They had been selected from a much larger group of condemned prisoners through a process that involved a hurried military trial, a review by the President of the United States, and the political demands of a state still reeling from the deaths of hundreds of civilians. Behind them stood the thousands of Dakota men, women, and children who had never been tried for anything, and who were about to lose their homeland by act of Congress.

This chapter asks how the destruction of Dakota land rights, treaty failures, and the crisis of 1862 produced war in Minnesota, and how the United States transformed a six-week conflict into mass execution, collective punishment, and the forced exile of Dakota communities. The question has two halves, and both require careful treatment. The first concerns causes: why did a people who had signed treaties, farmed under federal supervision, and in many cases accepted Christian missions come to the edge of starvation, and why did some of their young men and leaders decide, in August 1862, that fighting was preferable to waiting? The second concerns consequences: why did a war fought by a portion of the Dakota population lead to the legal destruction of the treaty relationship with all of them?

The Dakota of Minnesota, whom nineteenth-century Americans usually called the Eastern Sioux or Santee, are the Mdewakanton, Wahpekute, Wahpeton, and Sisseton peoples. Their homeland, Mni Sota Makoce, was a country of rivers, lakes, prairies, and woodlands in which Dakota communities had hunted, fished, gathered wild rice, tapped maples, and cultivated gardens for many generations. In 1851 two treaties transferred most of that country to the United States. By 1858 a second round of agreements reduced what remained to a narrow strip along the south bank of the Minnesota River. By the summer of 1862, as the Civil War absorbed the attention and resources of the federal government, many Dakota families were hungry, in debt to traders, and waiting for delayed annuity payments that were already late.

The war that followed began on August 17, 1862, when four young Dakota men killed five settlers near Acton, and it spread across the Minnesota River Valley within days. Dakota fighters attacked the Lower Sioux Agency, ambushed soldiers at Redwood Ferry, and assaulted Fort Ridgely and the town of New Ulm. Hundreds of settlers, and a smaller number of soldiers, were killed. Many more fled, and several hundred people were taken captive. The Dakota were not united. Some joined the fighting, many did not, and a number of Dakota people risked their own safety to hide settlers, escort refugees, or protect captives. By late September the war was effectively over, and the people who had sought peace were in the same camp as those who had fought.

What followed was in some ways as consequential as the war itself. A five-officer military commission tried 392 Dakota men, sentenced 303 to death, and recommended executions that President Abraham Lincoln reduced, after review, to thirty-nine approved cases and, in the end, thirty-eight hangings. More than a thousand Dakota noncombatants were marched to a prison camp below Fort Snelling, where many died over the winter. In February and March 1863 Congress abrogated the Dakota treaties, declared annuity and land rights forfeited, and authorized the removal of the Dakota from Minnesota. The Ho-Chunk, who had taken no part in the war, were expelled through separate legislation. Dakota people were sent to Crow Creek in Dakota Territory and to a prison camp in Iowa, or fled to the northern Plains and Canada, while the State of Minnesota offered bounties for Dakota scalps.

The governing interpretation of this chapter distinguishes between the circumstances that produced the war and the decisions made by particular people within those circumstances. Broken treaties, corrupt administration, and hunger were central causes. They do not make the killing of civilians historically insignificant, and they do not turn individual decisions into inevitabilities. At the same time, the deaths of civilians cannot justify treating Dakota people who did not fight, or who actively opposed the war, as collectively guilty. The chapter holds both principles together, because the historical record requires both.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Treaty texts, federal correspondence, military reports, newspapers, trial transcripts, and legislative acts survive in quantity, but nearly all were produced by non-Dakota officials and observers with direct interests in the outcome. Dakota perspectives survive in letters, testimony given to military and civilian authorities, and recollections recorded years or decades afterward, most notably those gathered in the collection Through Dakota Eyes. Each kind of source carries its own limits, and each primary-source window in this chapter says so. The chapter also uses the names that appear in the historical record and in current scholarship: "U.S.–Dakota War," "Dakota War of 1862," and, in older literature, "Sioux Uprising" or "Minnesota Massacre," terms whose differing assumptions are discussed in Section 73.20 and in the Historical Debate feature.

Chapter 73 opens Volume VII, which turns from the removal and treaty era examined in Volume VI to the wars, reservations, and confinement of the later nineteenth century. It follows Chapter 72's account of Northwest treaties and wars, and it precedes the treatment of Sand Creek in Chapter 74 and the broader Plains conflicts that follow. Its responsibility is the Dakota treaty crisis, the six-week war, the trials and executions, the imprisonment, and the exile. It does not attempt to narrate the wider campaigns on the northern Plains. Above all, it insists on a conclusion that its final section develops: the Dakota War ended in military defeat and devastating collective punishment, but Dakota nations and Dakota people did not cease to exist in 1862 or 1863.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the four Dakota divisions, their homelands, and the forms of political authority that operated among them before the 1851 treaties.
  2. Explain how the treaties of Traverse des Sioux and Mendota were negotiated, how the Senate altered them, and why the Traders' Paper mattered to Dakota economic independence.
  3. Trace the reduction of the Dakota land base between 1851 and 1858 and explain its effects on hunting, farming, mobility, and food security.
  4. Analyze the interaction of annuity delays, trader credit, poor harvests, and administrative failure in producing the hunger of 1861–1862.
  5. Distinguish the structural causes of the war from the immediate events of August 17–18, 1862, and explain how Dakota leaders and soldiers reached the decision to fight.
  6. Assess the political divisions among the Dakota, including the Peace Party, and explain why the war cannot be described as the action of a unified Dakota nation.
  7. Reconstruct the principal military engagements of the war, including Redwood Ferry, Fort Ridgely, New Ulm, Birch Coulee, and Wood Lake.
  8. Evaluate the experiences of settler civilians, captives, and Dakota families, distinguishing documented deaths from estimates and armed engagements from attacks on noncombatants.
  9. Analyze the procedures of the military commission and explain the differences among the 392 men tried, the 303 sentenced to death, the 39 approved for execution, and the 38 executed.
  10. Interpret the Mankato executions and Lincoln's review within the political context of Minnesota, the Civil War, and federal Indian policy.
  11. Distinguish the February 16 and March 3, 1863 acts and the separate removal of the Ho-Chunk, and explain the difference among dispossession, confinement, exile, and exclusion.
  12. Evaluate the evidence for Dakota survival, return, and historical memory after 1863, and explain why exile did not extinguish Dakota sovereignty.

Historical Context

Dakota history in Minnesota extends far deeper than the treaty era. The Dakota are part of the larger Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires, whose western divisions, the Lakota and Nakota peoples, are examined in the chapters on the Plains. The Eastern Dakota, or Isanti, lived in the woodlands and prairie edges of what is now Minnesota, and for centuries they participated in the regional trade and diplomatic networks of the western Great Lakes, which are discussed in Chapters 27 and 45. When French traders arrived in the seventeenth century, they entered a Dakota world already marked by alliances, rivalries, and exchange, and the fur trade that followed, treated in Chapter 46, tied Dakota economies to European markets and to families of mixed Dakota and European ancestry who served as intermediaries.

American authority arrived in the early nineteenth century. In 1805 Lieutenant Zebulon Pike negotiated a small cession of land at the junction of the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers, a site the Dakota call Bdote, and the United States built Fort Snelling there in the 1820s. Treaties in the 1820s, 1830, and 1837 fixed boundaries, defined relations with neighboring Ojibwe peoples, and ceded additional land. By 1851 American settlement was moving rapidly northwest, and the federal government and the Minnesota Territory's leaders wanted access to the lands of the Minnesota River Valley. The two treaties of 1851, examined in Section 73.2, were the decisive transaction.

Minnesota became a state in May 1858, and the settler population grew quickly in the 1850s, including large numbers of German, Scandinavian, and other immigrants. New Ulm, founded in 1854, and other towns along the Minnesota River placed farms and businesses against the borders of the diminished Dakota reservation. When the Civil War began in 1861, Minnesota sent regiments to the Union army, Fort Snelling became a major training center, and federal attention and money were redirected from Indian affairs to the war effort. These pressures combined with a federal Indian administration that depended on political patronage, relied on traders, and structured Dakota subsistence around annual payments that could be delayed.

Students should keep two boundaries in mind. First, the war must not be read as a simple "outbreak" without history. The Dakota had been dispossessed through a sequence of treaties and administrative acts in which they were participants, but participants under heavy pressure and often with imperfect information. Second, the war did not by itself explain what happened afterward. The trials, executions, imprisonment, abrogation of treaties, and removal were deliberate acts by military and civilian authorities, and they are treated in this chapter as decisions with their own causes and consequences. Wider conflicts with Dakota, Lakota, and other Plains peoples after 1863 are the subject of later chapters, and this chapter provides only the background needed to understand them.

Section 73.1

Dakota Peoples, Homelands, and Political Life in Minnesota

The Dakota describe themselves as an oyate, a people or nation, and as part of the larger Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires, which also includes the Nakota and Lakota. The Dakota dialect was spoken by the four Eastern Dakota divisions of Minnesota: the Mdewakanton, the Wahpekute, the Wahpeton, and the Sisseton. Nineteenth-century Americans often called these peoples collectively the Santee or the Eastern Sioux, and "Sioux" is an exonym of the kind discussed in Chapter 1, derived from a term applied to them by neighbors. The Mdewakanton and Wahpekute lived mostly along the lower Minnesota and the Mississippi, the Wahpeton and Sisseton farther up the Minnesota River valley toward Lake Traverse. The four divisions were closely related by language, kinship, and ceremony, but each had its own villages, hunting grounds, leaders, and history.

Mni Sota Makoce, the Dakota homeland, is often glossed as the land where the waters reflect the clouds. Its center for the Dakota is Bdote, the meeting of the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers, which in Dakota teaching is a place of origin and of continuing spiritual significance. Around it lay a landscape that supported a varied economy. Dakota families fished the rivers and lakes, hunted deer and other game in woodlands and prairies, gathered wild rice and other plants, tapped maples for sugar, and grew corn, squash, and other crops in village gardens. Many communities moved through the year: to sugar camps in the spring, to summer villages with bark-covered houses, to wild rice lakes in late summer, and to winter hunting grounds, where portable lodges allowed families to follow game. Western divisions and neighbors hunted bison on the prairie, and the relations of the Dakota with the Lakota and Nakota, and with Ojibwe, Ho-Chunk, Ioway, and other peoples, involved trade, intermarriage, alliance, and periodic warfare.

Dakota political life was organized around kinship and consultation rather than around a single centralized government. Each division consisted of bands, and each band had headmen whose authority came from descent, reputation, and the consent of their followers. Councils met to consider major questions, and men's societies, sometimes called soldiers' lodges, took responsibility for enforcing discipline during communal hunts and for organizing military and ceremonial activity. A leader could advise and persuade, but his authority depended on the support of the people around him, and no individual could commit the entire oyate to a course of action. The practical significance of this structure appears repeatedly in this chapter: American negotiators looked for chiefs who could sign for entire peoples, while Dakota decisions about treaties and war emerged from debate among bands, councils, young men, and families.

The fur trade and the nineteenth-century American presence had already altered Dakota life before the treaties of 1851. Traders lived among the Dakota, many married Dakota women, and their children and relatives formed a mixed-ancestry community that served as interpreters, clerks, and intermediaries. Dakota hunters exchanged furs for guns, cloth, metal goods, and food, and credit relationships tied families to specific traders. Missionaries, including Presbyterian and Episcopal clergy, began working among the Dakota in the 1830s, and some Dakota people adopted Christianity while others did not. These influences produced a diverse social landscape in which ceremony, trade, gardening, and religious choice coexisted. The Dakota entered the era of American treaty-making not as a blank slate but as sovereign peoples with long experience of negotiation and with sophisticated strategies for dealing with outsiders.

This chapter therefore begins its history of the war with the Dakota as political communities. They possessed territory, laws, leaders, diplomatic traditions, and a system of production and exchange that sustained thousands of people. The treaties that followed were not events in an empty land, and the crisis of 1862 cannot be understood without recognizing what was taken, what was promised in return, and what the Dakota themselves understood themselves to be giving up.

Section 73.2

The Treaties of Traverse des Sioux and Mendota, 1851

By the early 1850s, Minnesota Territory was a region in which American settlers, land speculators, and territorial officials were eager to open the lands west of the Mississippi. The territorial governor, Alexander Ramsey, was also the superintendent of Indian affairs, and together with Commissioner of Indian Affairs Luke Lea he served as a treaty commissioner. The Dakota who gathered at Traverse des Sioux in the summer of 1851 were therefore negotiating with officials who were responsible for federal policy and also invested in territorial expansion. On July 23, 1851, representatives of the Sisseton and Wahpeton signed the Treaty of Traverse des Sioux. On August 5, representatives of the Mdewakanton and Wahpekute signed the Treaty of Mendota near Fort Snelling.

The two treaties ceded to the United States an enormous area. Commonly cited estimates put it at roughly twenty-four million acres, covering much of southern and western Minnesota and a portion of what is now Iowa and Dakota. In return the treaties promised a large total payment, most of which was to be held in trust by the federal government with interest paid to the Dakota as annual annuities, along with a smaller amount for immediate expenses, goods, schools, agricultural assistance, and a reservation along the upper Minnesota River. The structure of the payment mattered. The Dakota would not receive the principal; they would receive interest, in money and goods, at times and in forms determined by the United States, and from that interest the Dakota economy would henceforth be partly financed.

The negotiations took place under heavy pressure. Dakota leaders were not unanimous: some argued that land should not be sold, some feared that the Dakota would lose access to the resources on which they depended, and some, including leaders such as Mazasha (Red Iron) at Traverse des Sioux, voiced objections to the way the negotiation was conducted. American officials, for their part, emphasized that the Dakota's old hunting economy was declining, that settlement was inevitable, and that treaty money, schools, and farms would secure a future. The treaties were written in English and translated by interpreters, among them traders and mixed-ancestry intermediaries who had their own financial and personal interests. Under these circumstances what the Dakota understood as they signed is not fully recoverable from the surviving documents, and later Dakota statements suggest that understandings differed from the text.

The most controversial feature of the negotiations was what became known as the Traders' Paper. Traders, notably the firm headed by Henry Hastings Sibley and associates such as Alexander Faribault and others, held claims against Dakota families for goods advanced on credit over many years. At Traverse des Sioux, and in related arrangements at Mendota, Dakota leaders were asked to sign a document directing that large sums from the treaty funds be paid to traders and to certain mixed-ancestry relatives to satisfy these claims. The scale of the claims, the process by which they were verified, and the extent to which Dakota signers understood and consented to the arrangement were all disputed. Dakota leaders later said they had not been adequately informed. The handling of the payments later drew scrutiny from Congress. For the Dakota, the practical result was that a significant portion of the benefit of the treaties went to creditors, and that traders remained powerful figures in Dakota economic life.

The Senate ratified the treaties in 1852, but not in the form negotiated. It removed the provisions creating the reservation along the Minnesota River and substituted a payment for the lands intended as the reservation, together with authority for the President to select a new home for the Dakota. The Dakota leaders were then pressed to accept the amendment, and the President permitted them to remain on a strip along the Minnesota River for the time being. This is a point of accuracy: the negotiated reservation language did not survive unchanged. The legal basis for Dakota occupancy of the reservation was thus an executive permission rooted in an amended treaty, rather than the guaranteed homeland that Dakota negotiators had been told they were securing. The Lower (Redwood) and Upper (Yellow Medicine) Agencies were established in the following years, and the Dakota moved onto the reservation, where federal officials and missionaries encouraged farming, schooling, and a change in dress and housing.

The 1851 treaties must therefore be analyzed as both land transactions and a transformation in Dakota political economy. Before the treaties, Dakota subsistence relied on a mixture of hunting, fishing, gathering, gardening, and trade, over a large territory, with the fur trade supplying imported goods. After the treaties, Dakota families depended increasingly on annual payments, on goods issued by agents, on credit at traders' stores, and on farming within a bounded area. The change shifted power toward those who controlled the delivery of money and food, and it made the Dakota vulnerable to delays, corruption, and political decisions made far from the Minnesota River.

Primary Source Window I

The Treaty of Traverse des Sioux, Its Amended Terms, and the Traders' Paper

Creator: United States treaty commissioners and Dakota signatories; Senate amendments · Date: July 23, 1851; ratified with amendments, 1852 · Type: Treaty text and associated claims document, published in the standard treaty compilations

Context: The treaty was negotiated at Traverse des Sioux with Sisseton and Wahpeton representatives, in parallel with the Mendota treaty with the Mdewakanton and Wahpekute. The text was prepared by American officials in English, signed by Dakota headmen and by American witnesses, and then altered by the United States Senate before ratification. The Traders' Paper was a separate instrument directing payment of claimed debts to traders from the treaty funds.

Paraphrase of the source: In its ratified form the treaty provides for the cession of lands, the payment of a sum largely held in trust and distributed as interest-based annuities, goods, education, and agricultural assistance, and the removal of a reservation provision that Dakota negotiators had been told would secure a home along the Minnesota River. The Traders' Paper authorized deductions to satisfy trader claims and to pay certain relatives of mixed ancestry. This is a paraphrase of the documents' content, not a quotation.

Analysis: The documents show how land transfer, debt settlement, and federal management were combined into one transaction. They show that the Dakota economy was being redesigned around trust funds and annuities, and that the American signatories included people with direct financial stakes in the claims being paid.

Limitations: The English text does not record how the terms were explained in Dakota, who was present at each discussion, or how the Dakota understood the sequence of signing. The claims lists were prepared by creditors, and their accuracy is contested. The Senate's changes were made without Dakota participation.

Perspective: The source represents the perspective of the United States and its negotiators. Dakota perspectives appear indirectly, in later complaints and in the testimony of leaders who objected, and are not fully represented in the treaty text.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the treaty say, and which of its provisions changed between negotiation and ratification?
  2. Who created the treaty and the Traders' Paper, and what interests did the creators have?
  3. What purposes shaped the decision to hold most of the payment in trust and distribute only interest?
  4. What can historians learn about Dakota political economy from the structure of the payments?
  5. What cannot be established from the treaty text alone about Dakota understanding and consent?
Section 73.3

The Dakota Reservation, the 1858 Agreements, and the Loss of Northern Lands

Minnesota entered the Union on May 11, 1858, and statehood intensified pressure on the remaining Dakota lands. The reservation along the Minnesota River, a strip extending roughly ten miles on each side of the river, lay between rapidly growing white settlements, and settlers and officials argued that the northern portion should be opened. In the spring of 1858 delegations of Dakota leaders traveled to Washington, D.C. Little Crow and other leaders were among them. The Mdewakanton and Wahpekute representatives signed agreements in mid-June, and Sisseton and Wahpeton representatives signed shortly afterward.

The agreements ceded the Dakota lands north of the Minnesota River, a large portion of the reservation. The Dakota were left with a southern strip approximately ten miles wide along the south bank. The negotiations in Washington took place away from the communities that would be affected, under pressure to accept terms that officials presented as the best available, and once again with a role for traders who pressed claims against the money to be paid for the ceded land. The agreements also encouraged individual landholding and farming, in keeping with a federal policy that treated agriculture on individual farms as the path to "civilization." Congress did not always provide what was promised, and the Senate's handling of the agreements, and the later survey and allotment of land, were sources of confusion and complaint among the Dakota.

Dakota responses to the reservation economy were varied. Some families adopted aspects of government-promoted agriculture, cultivated fields of corn and potatoes, built log houses, wore American-style clothing, and sent children to mission schools. Others maintained more of their earlier practices, relying on hunting and gathering as far as the restricted territory allowed and participating in ceremonies that missionaries discouraged. Observers on the reservation used labels such as "farmer Indians" and "blanket Indians," and "cut-hairs" for men who adopted American dress. These labels mislead if read as a division between progressive and traditional communities. Many families combined strategies, kin ties crossed lines of dress and religion, and the Dakota who farmed were often those who most needed to protect their stake in the treaty system, and who were most exposed when it failed. Farmers also took part on different sides of the later war, which is why this chapter does not use agricultural adoption as a predictor of political choice.

The shrinking land base mattered in concrete ways. Before 1851 the Dakota could move across a wide territory in response to seasonal resources and the movement of game. By 1858 their land was a narrow band bordered by settlers, and hunting grounds beyond it were increasingly contested. Large-scale hunting required space, and a family's ability to supplement farm produce with game depended on its mobility. As settlement spread, game declined, and Dakota access to former hunting areas shrank. The loss also reduced political independence: with less land and fewer alternatives, Dakota communities depended more heavily on the federal agents and traders who controlled food and credit. The reservation was a place where subsistence was linked to administration, and a failure of administration could produce hunger.

The apparent precision of a surveyed reservation boundary should not obscure the uneven conditions of life within it. A family that retained a field near an agency might still need access to outlying marshes, winter hunting country, or relatives living farther away. When officials described the northern cession as a tract of acreage, Dakota people could understand it as the loss of routes connecting seasons, households, and obligations. Surveying and allotment also required decisions about where a family could live and who would be recognized by the agent as entitled to assistance. Thus a government boundary that looked administratively simple on a map could create overlapping local disputes about access and authority.

Economic change was not a clean passage from an independent hunting economy to dependency on farming. Dakota agricultural knowledge long preceded the federal program; women cultivated gardens while families combined several food sources. Federal agents nevertheless used farming, schooling, and Christian conversion as markers of compliance, encouraging their preferred households while sometimes stigmatizing others. These distinctions became politically dangerous after August 1862, when officials and settlers could misread an individual's residence, clothing, or religious affiliation as evidence of guilt or loyalty. A stronger historical analysis asks how policies distributed aid and status, not whether a family fitted an American classification.

Section 73.4

Annuities, Traders, Corruption, and Hunger before the War

By 1861 the Dakota reservation economy depended on a rhythm of payments and credit. Annuities, in money and goods, were due in the summer, and until they arrived many families relied on credit at traders' stores to buy food and supplies. Traders expected repayment from the annuity, and at the pay table claims were presented and deducted. A family might receive far less than its nominal share. This arrangement worked, in a fashion, only if the annuity was paid on time and in full, and if the traders were confident enough of repayment to extend credit.

The conditions of 1861 and 1862 undermined both assumptions. Crops failed or were damaged in 1861 in several areas, and a hard winter followed. Dakota families who had planted corn and potatoes found the harvest inadequate, and the limited land and declining game left little to fall back on. Administrative failures made matters worse. The Indian agency system was shaped by political patronage, and the change of administration in 1861 brought new appointees, including Thomas Galbraith, who became agent. Contemporary and later accounts accuse officials and contractors of corruption, including fraud in contracts and the diversion of goods, and the Dakota repeatedly complained about the quality and quantity of provisions. The extent of individual wrongdoing is difficult to document, but the structure of patronage, credit, and fraud provided abundant opportunity, and it eroded trust.

The summer of 1862 brought a crisis. The annuity payment, normally expected in June, was delayed. One reason was the federal government's difficulty, during wartime, in transferring gold payments to the frontier, and the delay was widely known on the reservation. Dakota families gathered at the agencies to wait, and many consumed what stores they had. At the Upper Agency in early August, a large crowd of Dakota soldiers confronted the agency staff, demanding that food stored in the warehouse be distributed. Soldiers from Fort Ridgely under Lieutenant Timothy Sheehan intervened, and after a tense standoff, the agent agreed to distribute some provisions, though the amounts were limited. At the Lower Agency, a council on August 15 brought Dakota leaders, including Little Crow, together with the agent and traders. The traders declined to extend further credit, pending the arrival of the annuity.

One statement attributed to a trader, Andrew Myrick, has become famous in popular accounts: that the hungry Dakota could eat grass. Students should treat it cautiously. It comes to modern readers through later accounts, recorded after the war by participants and witnesses, and the versions differ in wording and in who heard the remark. Myrick was killed at the Lower Agency on August 18, and the story that his body was found with grass in his mouth is also reported in later accounts. The remark and its aftermath became symbolic of Dakota grievances, and the symbolism may have shaped its retelling. What is better documented is the structural reality: traders refused credit, the annuity was late, food was in the warehouse, and the Dakota were hungry.

Missionaries, merchants, and some officials recognized the danger. Dakota leaders asked that food be distributed from the stores, and some non-Dakota people who knew the reservation warned that the delay was dangerous. These warnings did not change policy. The failure was not caused by a single decision, but by an overlapping set of conditions: the loss of land and game, the dependency created by the treaties, the allocation of payments to creditors, the wartime disruption of administration, and the reluctance of officials and traders to release food on credit or in advance. Hunger in 1862 was therefore a structural consequence of dispossession and policy failure, not merely an agricultural accident, and it set the conditions in which a small incident could become a war.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Annuities, the Traders' Paper, and Debt

Definition: An annuity was a regular payment, in money or goods, promised by treaty as compensation for ceded land. The Traders' Paper was the instrument by which claimed debts to traders were to be paid from treaty funds. Debt in this context refers to credit extended by traders for goods advanced to Dakota families against anticipated payments.

Historical context: Under the 1851 treaties, much of the payment was held in trust, and only the interest was distributed. Annuities arrived annually, usually in summer. In the interval, families depended on credit, and the claims of traders were presented at the pay table. Payments could be delayed, goods could be of poor quality, and deductions for debts could consume a large share.

Why it matters: The system tied Dakota food security to the timing and honesty of federal administration and to the willingness of traders to extend credit. It reduced Dakota independence by replacing a diverse subsistence base with a payment cycle controlled by others.

Potential misunderstanding: An annuity was not charity or welfare. It was payment owed under a treaty for the cession of land, and the Dakota understood it as such. Failure to deliver it was a breach of obligation, not the withdrawal of a gift.

Division II The Decision for War and the August 1862 Uprising
Section 73.5

The Civil War, Minnesota Politics, and the Crisis of Federal Authority

The Civil War reached Minnesota in several ways. The state's first regiment of volunteers was among the earliest to answer Lincoln's call in 1861, and by 1862 additional regiments were being organized and trained at Fort Snelling. When the federal government issued a new call for troops in the summer of 1862, Minnesota's governor, Alexander Ramsey, pressed recruitment and the state's men moved through Fort Snelling to the army. The war drew soldiers away from frontier posts, and it absorbed the attention of federal officials in Washington and of the army's western commanders. It also strained federal finances, and the difficulty of moving gold to the frontier was one reason offered for the delayed annuity.

Many Dakota people were aware of the war. Some accounts record that Dakota leaders believed the United States was weakened by the conflict and distracted from the frontier, and that the departure of young white men for the army made settlements more vulnerable. Such perceptions were not universal and are known partly from testimony recorded after the war, but they show that Dakota decision-making took place in a context of broader events. Mixed-ancestry men from the reservation area were also recruited into a volunteer company, the Renville Rangers, in 1862, which indicates that the war was reshaping the reservation community as well as the state.

The people who held authority in Minnesota in 1862 had different priorities. Ramsey, a Republican and a former treaty commissioner, sought to meet federal demands for troops and to manage the state's anxieties. Thomas Galbraith, the Dakota agent, was a political appointee of the Lincoln administration and was responsible for the distribution of annuities and goods. Henry Hastings Sibley, a prominent former trader, Democrat, and the state's first governor, held a military commission and would soon command the campaign against the Dakota. Traders and local merchants controlled credit. Missionaries, including Stephen Riggs and Thomas Williamson, and Episcopal Bishop Henry Whipple, who had urged reform of the Indian system, advocated on behalf of the Dakota, though in ways shaped by their own goals. Each of these people and groups had interests that diverged. Their divergent aims explain why the delayed annuity and the dispute over food could not be quickly resolved.

The Civil War therefore coincided with the Dakota crisis, intensified it, and shaped the responses to it, but it did not cause it. The treaty cessions, the reservation economy, the credit system, and the failures of administration predated 1861. What the war added was distraction, delay, and a heightened sense among settlers and officials that Minnesota was under threat on two fronts. When violence began, those perceptions would shape both the military response and the demands for retribution.

The war also exposed a problem of divided administrative responsibility. Agents held the local warehouses and kept records of issues and debts, but they did not control when federal funds would arrive; territorial and state politicians could demand action but could not independently honor the treaty payment schedule. Traders could advance provisions or refuse further credit. Each institution could point to another when hungry families demanded the food they had been promised. This does not remove responsibility from particular officials, whose choices remain open to investigation. It helps explain why the Dakota encountered not one negotiating partner but a chain of offices and private actors capable of delaying relief while continuing to insist on obedience.

The available evidence also requires care in assigning motive. Wartime testimony sometimes presents the American Civil War as an opportunity seized by Dakota fighters who believed federal power had weakened. Such statements may record a genuine calculation among particular leaders, but they do not replace the preceding decade of land loss and deteriorating conditions. Nor did the beginning of fighting automatically resolve the question of what the broader Dakota population wanted. The federal mobilization and the local war interacted, yet they followed different timelines and involved different actors.

Section 73.6

The Acton Killings, Little Crow, and the Decision to Fight

On Sunday, August 17, 1862, four young Dakota men, who had been hunting, came upon a farmstead near Acton in Meeker County. In circumstances that accounts describe differently, they killed five settlers. Later Dakota recollections, recorded decades afterward, offer several explanations of how a dispute began, including a dare that turned violent, and the surviving versions cannot be fully reconciled. Accounts name the men, in English translation, as Brown Wing, Killing Ghost, Breaking Up, and Runs Against Something When Crawling, and this chapter notes that the English renderings of Dakota names vary in the sources. What is not in dispute is that the four were young men, that they killed civilians, and that they returned to a Dakota village and reported what they had done.

What followed was a night of deliberation. The young men went to the village of Shakopee, a Mdewakanton leader, and the news spread. Dakota men gathered at Little Crow's house, and accounts describe an extended council in which the soldiers' lodge members, young men, and leaders debated what to do. The central argument was not about whether the killings had been justified. It concerned what the Americans would do in response. Many Dakota expected the United States to punish the entire community for the actions of a few, and some argued that, given that expectation, the Dakota should fight while the Americans were distracted by the Civil War. Others argued that war was hopeless, since the Americans were numerous and the Dakota few, and that the killers should be surrendered. The conversation turned on a combination of fear, anger over years of grievance, and the particular vulnerability created by hunger.

Taoyateduta, Little Crow, was by this time about fifty years old, a Mdewakanton leader from a family of prominent headmen. He had participated in the treaty negotiations of 1851 and 1858, had visited Washington, and had seen the scale of American population and power. His position among his people was not straightforward: he had been identified with the policies of accommodation, had adopted some features of farming and American dress, and, according to accounts, had recently lost a contest for the position of speaker among the Mdewakanton. Accounts recorded after the war, including Dakota recollections, state that he at first warned against war, argued that the Americans could not be defeated, and then, under pressure and, according to some tellings, in a moment of political calculation or conviction, agreed to lead. The details of his speech at the council are known only through retrospective testimony, and this chapter does not reproduce words attributed to him whose wording cannot be verified.

Two distinctions are essential. First, the four young men who killed settlers at Acton were four individuals. They did not represent the Dakota population generally, and the decisions that followed were made by particular men, in councils where views were divided. Second, the Acton killings precipitated the immediate decision, but they were not the cause of the war. The council's debate turned on years of dispossession, on the loss of land, and on the hunger of the summer. A small act of violence was the trigger, but the conditions that allowed it to become a war were created by treaty, policy, and administrative failure. A chapter that treated Acton as the cause would obscure the structure that produced it. A chapter that treated the structure as an excuse would obscure the fact that people made decisions that cost civilian lives.

Section 73.7

The Attack on the Lower Sioux Agency and Redwood Ferry, August 18, 1862

On the morning of August 18 the Dakota began organized attacks. Fighters struck the Lower Sioux Agency at Redwood, where they killed traders and government employees and seized goods and supplies from the warehouses and stores. The first targets included the traders, whose stores were among the most prominent symbols of the credit system, and the agency buildings, which held food and annuity goods. Some of the people at the agency were killed, others escaped across the river or into the country, and some were taken captive. Dakota people also warned and aided some of those who fled. John Other Day, a Christian Dakota, led a group of people, white and Dakota, from the Upper Agency to safety, and other Dakota helped individuals and families reach Fort Ridgely or the Minnesota settlements.

A military engagement followed at Redwood Ferry. Captain John Marsh, commanding at Fort Ridgely, set out with a detachment of soldiers, roughly forty-six men, to investigate reports of trouble at the agency. His force was ambushed at the ferry crossing by Dakota fighters. About half of the detachment, including Marsh, who drowned while trying to cross the river, were killed, and survivors made their way back to the fort. The engagement was an armed clash between soldiers and Dakota fighters, and it had a larger effect than its numbers suggest. It removed a significant part of the fort's garrison, demonstrated that the Dakota were organized for war, and encouraged other Dakota to join.

The Dakota did not agree on objectives. Little Crow, according to accounts, wanted to attack Fort Ridgely quickly, to remove the main military post in the valley. Others, including many of the young men, preferred to attack the settlements and the town of New Ulm, where they hoped to find supplies and strike at settlers. The result was a division of effort that affected the course of the war and weakened the Dakota's ability to seize the fort. Dakota fighters did not operate under a single chain of command. Leaders had different followers, and the groups that fought at Redwood Ferry, at the agencies, and in the countryside were not necessarily the same.

This section also marks a distinction the chapter maintains throughout: that between armed engagements and attacks on noncombatants. Redwood Ferry was a battle between soldiers and fighters. The killing of traders and agency employees at the agency, and the killing of farm families in the surrounding country on August 18 and the following days, involved civilians. Settlers in places such as Milford Township, west of New Ulm, were among those who died in large numbers. By the end of the day, American authority along part of the Minnesota River Valley had collapsed, refugees were streaming toward Fort Ridgely and New Ulm, and the war had begun.

The Redwood Ferry casualty narrative should be read beside the movements of the garrison. Captain John S. Marsh took approximately forty-six enlisted soldiers from Fort Ridgely toward the Lower Agency after receiving reports of violence. Lieutenant Timothy J. Sheehan and a separate detachment had been away from the post and were recalled. These are different bodies of soldiers, and merging their numbers produces misleading accounts of the forces available at the fort. After Marsh's death, the return of Sheehan's men mattered to the defense of Fort Ridgely. The sequence illuminates how a relatively small armed engagement could change the larger military situation by depleting one post and making reinforcement urgent.

Section 73.8

Dakota Political Divisions: War, Neutrality, Protection, and the Peace Party

It is a serious error to describe the war as a unified Dakota uprising. The decisions made in August 1862 by Dakota leaders, soldiers, families, and communities varied widely, and their variety is essential to understanding what happened afterward. Some men joined the fighting eagerly, some with reluctance, and some joined after initially opposing it. Many people did not fight. Some tried to avoid the conflict entirely, some acted as intermediaries, and some actively opposed the war and took risks to protect settlers and captives.

The Dakota who opposed the war acted from different motives. Some were Christian converts who rejected violence on principle. Others believed the war could not be won and would bring ruin. Some had relatives and friends among the settlers or among mixed-ancestry families. Others were leaders who judged that fighting would destroy the Dakota and sought to limit the damage. Among the figures whose actions illuminate these differences are Gabriel Renville, a leader of mixed Dakota and French ancestry among the upper bands; Paul Mazakutemani, a Christian Dakota leader; Taopi, a Dakota farmer who had adopted aspects of American agriculture and who protected captives; and Wabasha, a Mdewakanton leader whose role in the war is complex and whose later efforts to bring peace appear in the record. Other Dakota, whose names are less well known, hid families, carried messages, and provided food and shelter.

These differences do not map neatly onto categories of dress, religion, or agricultural adoption. Some farmers fought, and some men who retained older customs opposed the war. Family relationships, personal obligations, and local circumstances shaped choices. Gender also mattered. Women worked in households and camps, protected children, and in some cases sheltered captives and refugees, though their experiences were rarely recorded by officials and are best known through later Dakota testimony and the accounts of captives. The assumption that all Dakota people supported the war is false, and it is an assumption the American response encouraged.

As the fighting developed, the opponents of the war became more organized. This group, which came to be called the Dakota Peace Party, was not a formal political party but a loose association of leaders and families who worked to protect captives, communicated with the American commander, Sibley, and tried to bring the war to an end. They established a camp, sometimes called the friendly camp, where captives were sheltered, and they argued against continued fighting in councils. The Minnesota Historical Society specifically identifies the Peace Party's opposition to the fighting and its efforts to secure the release of captives. The party's existence meant that, when American soldiers eventually arrived, they would encounter a Dakota population divided between those who had fought and those who had worked against the war, and the question of how the army would treat that difference became central to what followed.

Cause and Effect

Land Loss, Annuity Failure, Hunger, Violence, Retaliation, and Dispossession

Long-term causes

The 1851 and 1858 cessions, the Senate's alteration of the 1851 treaties, the trust-and-annuity payment structure, the Traders' Paper, and the dependence of the reservation economy on credit and federal delivery.

Short-term causes

Crop failure in 1861, a hard winter, wartime disruption of federal administration, the late annuity of 1862, and the refusal of traders to extend further credit.

Immediate trigger

The killing of five settlers near Acton on August 17 by four young men, and the council that decided, under pressure and fear of collective retaliation, to seek war.

Event

Six weeks of war across the Minnesota River Valley, with attacks on agencies, forts, towns, and farms, the killing of civilians and soldiers, and captive-taking.

Immediate effects

Hundreds of settlers and soldiers killed, thousands of people displaced, a military expedition, trials, executions, and the imprisonment of Dakota noncombatants.

Long-term consequences

Abrogation of the treaties, forfeiture of land and annuities, removal from Minnesota, scattering of the Dakota across several jurisdictions, and a long struggle over return and memory.

Historical complexity: The war cannot be reduced to a single cause. Hunger and treaty violations created the conditions in which the decision for war was made, but individual choices, political divisions, and contingency shaped the outcome. The same complexity applies to the aftermath: the punishment of all Dakota was a choice made by officials and legislators, not an automatic consequence of the war.

Section 73.9

The Battles of Fort Ridgely, August 20 and 22, 1862

Fort Ridgely stood on a plateau above the Minnesota River, a few miles downriver from the Lower Agency. It was not a walled stockade but a collection of buildings around a parade ground, with ravines on several sides that offered attackers cover. Its military role was to support the agencies and to provide protection for settlers, and after the disaster at Redwood Ferry its garrison was small. In the days after August 18 the fort became a refuge for hundreds of civilians fleeing the valley, so that the defenders were responsible for a crowd of noncombatants as well as for the post.

The defense was organized by officers and noncommissioned men who took command in a chaotic situation. Lieutenant Timothy Sheehan, who arrived with additional soldiers, and Ordnance Sergeant John Jones, who directed the fort's artillery, are credited in the accounts with the key decisions. Reinforcements and volunteers helped to strengthen the garrison. The soldiers prepared defensive positions, used their cannon, and exploited the buildings for cover.

Dakota fighters attacked the fort on August 20, in a probing assault that was repelled. On August 22 a larger force, including leaders such as Little Crow, attacked again, and the fighting lasted for hours. Several factors worked against the attackers. The Dakota had few cannon and little ammunition in comparison with the garrison's artillery, which could break up charges across open ground. Dakota fighters in the ravines and buildings could be effective in skirmishing but struggled to cross the open space needed to take the fort. They did not operate under a unified command, the group that attacked on August 22 was divided in its aims, and weather and darkness affected both sides. The fort's defenders held, and the Dakota withdrew.

The defense of Fort Ridgely denied Dakota forces an important strategic position and preserved a base from which American reinforcements could operate. It also protected the civilians sheltering inside. Had the fort fallen, the way down the valley to the settlements of the lower river would have been more open. At the same time, the fighting made clear that the Dakota had not been defeated, and that reinforcements would be needed to restore control of the valley. For the Dakota, the failure to take the fort meant that the war would continue as a series of scattered engagements and raids rather than as a rapid campaign to seize the American position on the river.

The men identified as commanders served different functions. Lieutenant Timothy J. Sheehan returned after Marsh's death, assumed command from Lieutenant Thomas P. Gere, and coordinated the defense during the attacks. Ordnance Sergeant John W. Jones was the experienced artilleryman responsible for the fort's cannon and training soldiers to use them. His service was therefore operationally decisive without making him the commanding officer of the post. Contemporary reports by Sheehan and Jones and later historical reconstructions agree on the importance of artillery. Preserving this distinction between post command and the technical command of artillery makes their contributions intelligible without turning them into interchangeable heroes.

The fort also posed a strategic problem for Dakota forces that cannot be reduced to individual bravery. Its buildings and open approaches favored artillery once defenders could position their guns, while nearby ravines provided concealment but not an easy route across the parade ground. Meanwhile, civilians and refugees were taking shelter inside, reducing the freedom of defenders to abandon the post or maneuver. Failure to capture the fort meant that Dakota fighters still held initiative in parts of the countryside but had not removed the main military node in the valley. That distinction helps explain the continued fighting at New Ulm and farther west.

Section 73.10

The Battles of New Ulm and the Defense of Settler Communities

New Ulm, a town of German immigrants founded in 1854, was the largest settlement in the war zone, and its location near the Minnesota River made it a natural target and a natural refuge. On August 19, the day after the outbreak, Dakota fighters approached the town, and a first attack was repelled by defenders, townspeople and volunteers who had gathered from nearby communities, among them militia from St. Peter and other towns led by Judge Charles Flandrau and by local leaders such as Jacob Nix. Barricades were built in the streets, and the town's inhabitants, together with refugees from the countryside, crowded into the buildings of the town center.

The second battle, on August 23, was a much larger assault. Dakota fighters moved against the town from several directions and set fire to buildings, and the defenders fought from barricades and fortified houses. Much of the town was burned, and many defenders were killed or wounded. The inhabitants, with the support of the militia, ultimately held the central blocks, but the destruction of the buildings, the shortage of supplies, and the persistent threat led the town's leaders to decide on evacuation. On August 24 and 25 well over a thousand people, residents and refugees, left New Ulm under escort for Mankato, in long columns of wagons. New Ulm was abandoned for a time, and the loss of the town left the settlers of the surrounding country exposed.

The experiences of civilians in these battles deserve sustained attention. Families who had fled their farms found themselves besieged in the town. Women and children sheltered in cellars and buildings while fighting went on around them. The wounded needed care that the town could hardly provide, and the evacuation involved the loss of homes, property, and livelihood. The documented record distinguishes among categories: the dead in the battles, the wounded, the evacuees, and the estimated property losses. These are separate figures, and the surviving sources do not permit precise totals for all of them. Contemporary estimates of damage were made by interested parties, and later counts rely on incomplete records.

New Ulm should be understood as one element of a wider war rather than as the only significant settler community attacked. Farms, hamlets, and rural townships across a large area were struck during the same days, and the loss of settlers outside the towns was in total larger than the losses in the towns. The defense of New Ulm became an important part of the regional memory of the war in Minnesota and is commemorated in the town to this day, and students should read such commemorations as part of the history of how the war has been remembered, alongside the Dakota memorials discussed in Section 73.20.

Section 73.11

Warfare across the Minnesota Frontier: Lake Shetek, Refugees, and Civilian Loss

The great majority of settler deaths occurred outside the major engagements. In the first days of the war, attacks struck isolated farms and small communities across the Minnesota River Valley and beyond, including the townships near the agencies, areas of Renville, Brown, Redwood, McLeod, Meeker, and Kandiyohi counties, and settlements farther west. The attacks were carried out by separate groups of fighters, often small bands, moving across the countryside. Farm families were killed in their homes or in their fields, houses and barns were burned, and livestock and goods were taken. The numbers of civilians killed are uncertain. Estimates of settler deaths commonly range from several hundred to more than eight hundred, depending on how deaths are counted and on whether soldiers and civilians are separated, because many deaths occurred on remote farms and compilations were drawn from incomplete sources. This chapter uses a range rather than a single figure and treats the deaths of soldiers, settler civilians, Dakota fighters, and Dakota people who died in confinement or exile as separate categories.

Lake Shetek, in southwestern Minnesota, illustrates the pattern. Attackers, led by a man named White Lodge, whose followers were not under Little Crow's command, struck a settlement of families around the lake on August 20. A number of settlers were killed, and others escaped or were captured, and survivors endured days of flight, hunger, and hardship. The attack and its aftermath, which included the deaths of children, are among the most documented rural episodes of the war and formed part of the regional memory. Other places, including farms near Acton, Hutchinson, Forest City, and the area around the Upper Agency, saw violence or the fear of it. Fort Abercrombie, on the Red River in Dakota Territory, was also attacked in early September.

Settler women, children, and refugees bore much of the human cost. Families fleeing the valley moved by wagon, on foot, or by boat, abandoning farms and property and heading for towns and forts, often without adequate food or shelter. Several hundred people, many of them women and children, were taken captive, held for days or weeks, and moved with Dakota camps. Their experiences varied. Some were treated with consideration and protected by Dakota families, some were forced to work, some were killed, and many lived in fear of what might happen to them. Reports of sexual violence circulated widely in the state, and they became important in the later review of the trials. The surviving evidence of such assaults is difficult to assess, and Section 73.15 returns to it.

Dakota families also suffered. Those who took no part in the fighting, and those who did, lived through displacement, hunger, and fear of retaliation. As soldiers moved up the valley and settlers organized to defend themselves, Dakota communities faced the prospect of attack regardless of their role. Women and children traveled with the camps, and when the war ended they became the prisoners of the army.

Finally, the war's violence must be assigned to particular actors and not to an undifferentiated group. Dakota fighters did not all participate in the same operations. Different leaders commanded different groups, and some bands and individuals were involved in particular attacks while others were not. Responsibility for any specific killing must be established from evidence. The trials that followed would depend on testimony of uneven quality, and the failure to maintain this distinction was one of the central problems of the military justice that came after.

Experiencing the War: Six Groups, Six Understandings

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical syntheses of patterns documented in the historical record. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

Dakota fighters and the soldiers' lodges: Many understood the war as a response to years of dispossession, hunger, and broken promises, and to the failure of leaders and officials to solve the crisis. They feared that the Americans would punish the whole community for the killings at Acton, and some believed the Civil War offered an opportunity. Their actions included attacks on civilians, for which individuals bore responsibility.
Dakota peace advocates: Leaders and families who opposed the war believed it could not be won and would destroy the Dakota. They protected settlers and captives, urged an end to fighting, and communicated with the American commander. They were, in effect, working against some of their own relatives and neighbors, and they exposed themselves to danger from both sides.
Settler civilians: Families in the valley experienced the war as sudden violence against homes and communities. Many lost relatives, farms, and property, and many fled. Their fear and anger were genuine and informed demands for severe punishment, which drew on broad categories and not on the specific individuals responsible.
Captives: White and mixed-ancestry captives, many of them women and children, lived at the mercy of those who held them. Some were protected and treated humanely, some were mistreated, and some died. Their later accounts, written for the public, are valuable testimony and also shaped by the circumstances of publication.
Federal and state officers: Officials in Minnesota and in the army sought to restore order, protect settlers, and respond to public demands. Many equated the actions of the fighters with the guilt of the whole Dakota population, and some officials, including the governor, called publicly for the Dakota to be expelled or destroyed.
Dakota women and families: Women and children experienced the war through displacement, hunger, flight, and fear, and in many cases through the protection of captives or the care of the wounded. Their testimony, recorded later, shows that they had little control over the decisions that shaped their lives but carried a large share of the consequences.
Section 73.12

Birch Coulee, the Dakota Peace Party, and the Struggle over the War's Direction

While Dakota forces fought in the valley, Colonel Henry Sibley assembled an expedition at Fort Ridgely. His force of volunteer soldiers, many of them recently recruited and poorly equipped, moved slowly, and Sibley has been criticized, then and since, for delay. He faced real difficulties of supply and organization, and in the meantime the war continued. On August 31 and September 1, Sibley sent a burial party under Major Joseph R. Brown, a long-time resident of the area who had Dakota family ties, and Captain Hiram Grant to bury the dead near the Lower Agency. Brown's party, a detachment of well under two hundred men, camped on the night of September 1 at a place called Birch Coulee.

At dawn on September 2, Dakota fighters, including leaders such as Big Eagle and Gray Bird, attacked the camp. The soldiers took shelter behind wagons and dead horses and dug shallow pits, and they were pinned down for some thirty hours without adequate water as the Dakota kept up the fire. Casualties among the soldiers were heavy: about thirteen men were killed and more than fifty wounded in one Minnesota Historical Society summary (other historical summaries give nearly fifty), and about ninety horses were killed, while two Dakota deaths were recorded in the principal historical summary. Sibley, hearing the firing, sent reinforcements and then came up himself on September 3 with a larger force, and the Dakota withdrew. The Dakota lost fewer fighters than their opponents, but they had not achieved a decisive result, and the arrival of the American relief made clear that the military balance was shifting.

Birch Coulee also marked a widening disagreement among the Dakota. Little Crow, in a letter to Sibley dated September 7, explained something of the Dakota grievances and indicated an interest in discussion, although the letter and Sibley's reply must be read cautiously, since Sibley's reply demanded the release of captives before any negotiation. The Peace Party, meanwhile, was working to protect captives, to keep them alive and out of the fighting, and to send messages to Sibley assuring him that they were being held safely and asking for terms. Dakota leaders such as Wabasha, Taopi, and Paul Mazakutemani appear in the record as advocates for peace, and Gabriel Renville and others organized a camp in which opponents of the war gathered. Their position was dangerous. They were Dakota people trying to end a war fought by other Dakota people, against an army whose commander refused to promise terms. Sibley's insistence on unconditional surrender, which left them with few guarantees, would prove consequential.

By mid-September, therefore, the Dakota were divided between continued military resistance and growing efforts to negotiate or surrender. Little Crow and the fighters who remained with him moved up the valley, and the Peace Party's camp grew as families who wished to avoid the fighting joined it. The division would shape the final battle. It meant that when Sibley's army approached, there were two Dakota camps with different intentions, one preparing for battle and the other for surrender, and the Americans would have to decide how to treat the difference.

Casualty figures at Birch Coulee demonstrate a recurrent problem in nineteenth-century battlefield accounting. The Minnesota Historical Society's battlefield interpretation lists thirteen dead, more than fifty wounded, approximately ninety horses killed, and two recorded Dakota fatalities. An MNopedia article uses the formulation 'almost fifty' wounded. Neither description warrants pretending that every wounded person was identified with equal certainty. Their agreement on the broader scale is more important than forcing a single exact wounded total. The episode's military significance lay in the isolation of Brown's party, the effective Dakota investment of the camp, and the need for a substantial relief force.

Division IV Defeat, Military Justice, and the Mankato Executions
Section 73.13

The Battle of Wood Lake and Camp Release, September 1862

By the third week of September Sibley's army, reinforced and better supplied, was moving up the Minnesota River toward the Upper Agency and the Dakota camps. Little Crow and other leaders, who had not given up the war, planned an ambush for September 22 and 23 along the road near Wood Lake. The plan was to attack the American column from concealed positions in the grass and ravines. The Dakota fighters took their places before dawn on September 23.

The ambush went wrong. According to accounts, a group of soldiers left the column to forage for food, in some versions to dig potatoes in a field, and stumbled into the Dakota positions, which led to a premature engagement. The fighting that followed lasted a few hours. The American force, which outnumbered the Dakota and had artillery, drove them back. Several Dakota fighters were killed, among them the leader Mankato, who was struck by a cannon shot, and the Dakota withdrew. American casualties numbered several killed and a few dozen wounded. The battle became the decisive engagement of the war. It showed that the Dakota could not stop Sibley's army in open battle, it ended the military initiative of Little Crow's followers, and it freed Sibley to advance on the camp where the captives were held.

After Wood Lake, Little Crow and a number of his followers withdrew, some heading west to the Plains and others to the north. Several leaders and groups of families dispersed in different directions, intending to avoid the American army, and some would eventually reach Dakota Territory and Canada. Others, who had opposed the war or who now sought to surrender, remained in place. On September 26 Sibley's force reached the camp where the Peace Party had gathered, near the Upper Agency area, and the Dakota there released the captives to the army. About 270 people, white and mixed-ancestry, were handed over in what became known as Camp Release. The release was the result of the efforts of Dakota people who had protected the captives and arranged their return.

The subsequent events illustrate a fundamental problem. The Dakota Peace Party had sought to protect captives and end the war, and its members had released captives into American custody. Yet the army did not treat them as allies who had helped to resolve the crisis. Sibley demanded the surrender of the Dakota in the camp, and the men were soon being identified, questioned, and arrested. Those who had fought, those who had not, those who had protected captives, and those who happened to be present were all in the army's hands. The distinction between individual surrender and the activities of the Peace Party did not translate into reliable protection, and people who sought peace could nevertheless fall under military custody. The reasons are partly practical: Sibley had few means of distinguishing quickly among individuals. They are also political, since Minnesota's public demanded punishment and the army had no guidelines for separating the guilty from the innocent. The result was the trial process described in the next section.

Section 73.14

The Military Commissions: Trials, Evidence, and the Problem of Justice

On September 28, 1862, two days after Camp Release, Sibley appointed and convened a military commission of officers to try Dakota men accused of crimes in the war. The commission was an instrument of the army, created by its field commander and composed of officers under his command. A military commission differs from a civilian criminal court in its authority, its procedures, and its relationship to the chain of command. It is typically used in wartime to try offenses, including violations of the laws of war, in circumstances where ordinary courts are unavailable, and it has fewer of the procedural safeguards of a civilian trial. Sibley acted under authority derived from his command in the army's Department of the Northwest, whose commander, General John Pope, had signaled in his letters that he sought severe punishment of the Dakota. The Dakota prisoners were not United States citizens, and the army treated them as enemies in a war and also as criminals, a mixture of categories that the proceedings never resolved.

The trials began at once and moved quickly. The commission held proceedings in a tent and later in buildings at the Lower Agency, and it worked through dozens of cases a day. Many trials lasted only a few minutes. No defendant had a lawyer. Interpretation, essential because the prisoners spoke Dakota and the commissioners did not, was supplied by interpreters, including missionaries and mixed-ancestry intermediaries, and the quality of translation is difficult to evaluate. The evidence came from several sources: testimony of captives and of witnesses, including some Dakota who testified against others, statements by the defendants, and identifications made in the confusion of the camps. The commission was ready to convict men who admitted taking part in the major battles, even without evidence of killing a civilian, and many defendants answered questions about their own participation candidly, since in their understanding fighting in a war was a matter of honor and not of murder.

This distinction is at the center of historical and legal criticism of the proceedings. Participation in battle against soldiers, including at Fort Ridgely, New Ulm, and Redwood Ferry, is ordinarily treated, under the laws of war, as combat, and combatants are not typically executed merely for fighting. Alleged killings of noncombatants, which are crimes, require evidence about a particular act by a particular person. The commission's convictions often blurred the line. Prisoners were sentenced to death in large numbers on the basis of participation, and the evidence of individual responsibility for the killing of civilians was uneven. Scholars who have studied the trials, including legal historians, have judged the procedures deeply flawed: the absence of counsel, the speed, the reliance on translation, the vague charges, and the political context in which the officers sat all undermined the reliability of the verdicts.

The numbers must be used precisely. The commission tried 392 Dakota men. It sentenced 303 of them to death. Most of the rest were acquitted or given prison terms. Of the 303, 39 were later approved for execution by President Lincoln, and 38 were hanged after one was reprieved. These are separate stages of a process, not interchangeable figures. A student who says that "303 Dakota were executed" has made a serious error; the figure refers to the commission's sentences. The final number of executions, 38, reflects the review in Washington, which is the subject of the next section. Among those whose cases attracted attention was Joseph Godfrey, a man of African and French ancestry who had lived among the Dakota and who was sentenced to death and later had his sentence reduced to imprisonment.

The commission's work cannot be separated from the climate in Minnesota. Public anger was intense, officials called for retribution, and soldiers and civilians threatened to take matters into their own hands. At the same time, some of those who supervised the trials were conscientious in their own terms. The problem lay in the structure of the proceedings and the pressures on them, and not simply in the character of individual officers. The commission was authorized, in effect, to find the guilty among a population whose guilt was widely presumed, in a setting where the evidence was hard to test.

The army's terminology also requires examination. Participation in a recognized military engagement was not automatically equivalent to murdering a civilian. A commission investigating alleged crimes had to decide which acts were punishable and what evidence could identify an individual accused of them. Sibley's own subsequent correspondence indicates that the commission had been instructed to establish participation without undertaking an extended examination of degrees of guilt. That approach is central to the later legal critique. A record of the accused person's presence in a fight might support one finding; it could not, without more, establish every alleged attack on unarmed people. Historians should therefore resist treating the number of convictions as an independently verified count of civilian killers.

Historical Vocabulary
Military Commissions, Due Process, and Collective Responsibility

Definition: A military commission is a tribunal created by military authority to try offenses connected with war. Due process refers to the procedural protections that govern how a government may punish, including notice of charges, an opportunity to defend, evidence tested by cross-examination, and counsel. Collective responsibility is the attribution of guilt to a group, as distinct from the responsibility of individuals for their own acts.

Historical context: The 1862 commission operated with limited procedural safeguards and with the assumption that participation in the war could be treated as a capital crime. Later in the winter, the federal government extended the logic of collective responsibility from individuals to the entire Dakota nation through legislation.

Why it matters: The distinction between individual and collective responsibility is the key to evaluating both the trials and the expulsion. The commission at least sought to identify individual defendants, however imperfectly. The 1863 acts punished Dakota communities regardless of the acts of any individual member.

Potential misunderstanding: A military commission is not the same as a court-martial, which tries members of the armed forces, nor is it equivalent to a civilian trial. Nor does the existence of a formal process guarantee that the process was just.

Section 73.15

Abraham Lincoln, Presidential Review, and the Politics of Execution

The commission's capital sentences were referred to President Lincoln for review; the nature of that review and its legal foundation have remained subjects of historical and legal controversy. In early November 1862, after the commission completed its work, the sentences of 303 men were forwarded to Washington. On November 10 Lincoln asked General Pope to send the full record of the convictions, together with a list of those found guilty of rape or of killing civilians as distinct from taking part in battles. The request shows his first concern: Lincoln was not content to approve mass executions on the commission's verdicts, and he wished to know who had committed the offenses that the army and the public regarded as the gravest.

Pressure on Lincoln came from several directions. The governor of Minnesota, Alexander Ramsey, and Minnesota's congressional delegation pressed for rapid execution. In a message to the state legislature in September, Ramsey had said the Dakota should be "exterminated or driven forever beyond the borders of the State," and the governor and others warned that settlers might take vengeance if the federal government did not act. General Pope and Sibley supported the sentences, though they also feared mob violence. On the other side, missionaries such as Riggs and Williamson, Bishop Whipple, and other religious and humanitarian advocates urged clemency, and some questioned the fairness of the trials. Lincoln was also acutely aware of the political consequences, in Minnesota and in the nation, of his decision.

Lincoln's review was conducted by himself and his staff, who examined the trial records to identify cases of rape and of the murder of civilians. The review led to a drastic reduction. In an order issued in early December, Lincoln approved the execution of thirty-nine men, a small fraction of the 303 condemned, while leaving the others unexecuted and still imprisoned (many remained under unresolved sentences). In a message to the Senate, he explained his reasoning in terms that, in paraphrase, balanced the risk of leniency encouraging a new outbreak against the risk of severity amounting to cruelty. The review seems to have found few convictions for rape and a larger number involving the killing of civilians, and the approved list included both. One of the thirty-nine was reprieved before the date of execution, leaving thirty-eight. These distinctions matter: 303 sentences, 39 approved, 38 executed.

The controversy over Lincoln's decision has several dimensions. Some historians emphasize his unusual restraint under political pressure and his insistence on distinguishing between combatants and those who killed noncombatants. Others point out that, even in the narrowed list, the evidence on which men were condemned remained the product of flawed trials, and that Lincoln, working from written records of hurried proceedings, could not remedy the absence of counsel, the problems of translation, and the unreliability of identifications. A reduced list of executions did not turn an unjust process into a just one. At least one man was later said to have been hanged by mistake because of confusion over a name, an episode discussed in the literature with varying detail. The commutations spared the lives of most of the condemned, but they did not release them: those not executed remained in prison, and many would die in confinement.

The one last-minute reprieve also has a name. A Minnesota historical exhibition identifies the reprieved prisoner as Tatemina, rendered in variant spellings in nineteenth-century records. It is important to state this separately from the controversy over Chaska, or Caske, a man whose sentence was commuted but who nevertheless died on the scaffold. The distinction between an approved execution list and the identities of those actually executed exposes a limitation of simply repeating the figure thirty-eight. Lincoln's review reduced the number of authorized hangings, but the process by which names were matched to prisoners remained vulnerable to error or misconduct.

Historical Debate

Causes, Responsibility, the Legitimacy of the Commissions, and Collective Punishment

The question: How should historians assign responsibility for the war, evaluate the legitimacy of the military commissions, and characterize the federal response? Scholars disagree about emphasis, about terminology, and about the weight to give different causes and different groups' decisions.

Interpretation A — Structural dispossession as the primary cause: Some historians, including many who emphasize Dakota sources, stress that treaty fraud, the loss of land, and the failures of the annuity system created conditions in which war became likely, and that the settler and federal response was an extension of a long process of dispossession. They often prefer terms such as "U.S.–Dakota War," and some argue that the later punishments constitute ethnic cleansing or genocidal policy.
Interpretation B — Dakota decisions and civilian suffering as central: Other historians emphasize that the war involved the killing of hundreds of civilians, that these killings were carried out by particular individuals, and that Dakota leaders made choices for which they bore responsibility. They argue that grievances, however real, must not obscure the scale of civilian suffering, and some retain older terms for the conflict. Scholars in this tradition also note that Dakota people themselves were divided.
Interpretation C — Procedure and collective punishment as the key problem: A third approach, found particularly in legal scholarship, sets aside the question of causes to examine the trials and the federal legislation. It holds that, whatever the guilt of particular individuals, the commission's procedures were inadequate, and that the punishment of the entire Dakota nation through treaty abrogation and removal was an act of collective punishment that cannot be justified by the acts of a minority.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on treaty records, agency correspondence, trial transcripts, Lincoln's papers, newspapers, settler and captive narratives, and Dakota testimony. They differ in which sources they privilege and in how they treat the retrospective character of much Dakota testimony.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern terminology (the name of the war and whether terms such as genocide apply), causation (how much weight to give long-term grievance and how much to individual and collective decision-making), scale (estimates of civilian deaths), and interpretation (whether Lincoln's review was a model of restraint or an insufficient remedy).

Why it matters: How historians frame these questions affects public memory in Minnesota and in Dakota communities, and shapes how commemorations, museums, and school curricula describe the war. Interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and most careful accounts combine elements of all three.

Student task: Evaluate the three interpretations. Which evidence would best test each? How might a historian hold Interpretations A and B together, and does Interpretation C depend on either?

Section 73.16

December 26, 1862: The Execution of Thirty-Eight Dakota Men at Mankato

The condemned men had been held in a prison at Mankato, in the town that had sheltered refugees from New Ulm. In the days before December 26 the army prepared for the execution. Soldiers were stationed to guard the prisoners and the town, which was placed under military control, and the sale of liquor was restricted to prevent disorder. A large scaffold was built in a public square, designed to hang the men in a single drop. Crowds of civilians gathered from the surrounding area, and estimates of the size of the crowd run into the thousands, though precise numbers are unrecorded.

The days before the hangings were spent in preparation by the men themselves. Missionaries and priests, including Stephen Riggs and Father Augustin Ravoux, visited the prisoners, and many were baptized or took part in religious services. Some of the condemned wrote letters to their families, in Dakota, and some spoke of their wish that their relatives would be cared for. Families were allowed to see them on the eve of the execution. Accounts describe farewells, songs, and prayers. The men were bound and brought out on the morning of December 26, and many sang as they went to the platform, according to witnesses. The scene was observed by Minnesota residents and soldiers, and was recorded by newspaper accounts and by memoirs of those present.

The hangings took place at about ten o'clock. The thirty-eight men stood on the platform with nooses around their necks, and at a signal a rope was cut, dropping the platform. William Duley, a survivor of the attack at Lake Shetek who had lost family in the war, is recorded in accounts as the man who cut the rope. The bodies were left hanging for some time and then taken down and buried in a mass grave on the riverbank. That night, according to accounts, some physicians, including William W. Mayo, who later became famous, exhumed bodies for use as anatomical specimens, an act which caused lasting grief and became part of the history of the remains of the executed men. Disputes over the remains, and efforts to identify and return them, continued for generations.

The event is commonly described as the largest mass execution in United States history. Its historical significance lies not only in its scale but in what it represented: the culmination of a process in which federal and state authorities treated Dakota resistance as a crime to be punished in public spectacle. The legal responsibility for the executions lay with the federal government, since the President had confirmed the sentences and the army carried them out. The political demand came from Minnesota officials and the public. The distinction does not reduce federal responsibility, but it clarifies the relationship between federal authority and local pressure.

The executed individuals were historical persons, not merely a number. They were men with names, families, and communities, and their names are recorded in the commission's records and in Dakota memory. Some had killed civilians, according to the evidence the commission accepted, though the reliability of that evidence is contested. Others may have been convicted on weak testimony. The Dakota families who lost fathers, husbands, and sons did not have a chance to bury them. Their grief was compounded by what happened to those families in the weeks that followed, which are the subject of the next division.

The case of Chaska, also called Caske or Wicaŋḣpi Wastedaŋpi in different historical accounts, makes the issue concrete. Sarah F. Wakefield testified that he had protected her and her children while they were captives. His death sentence was commuted, yet he was among the men executed. Missionary Stephen Riggs later offered a mistaken-identity explanation involving the similar name Chaskadon. Wakefield, in a March 23, 1863 letter to Abraham Lincoln preserved by the Library of Congress, argued that the substitution could have been intentional. The surviving evidence does not conclusively establish whether the execution resulted from negligence or deliberate wrongdoing. The historian's responsibility is to describe the documented commutation, execution, and competing explanations rather than present one hypothesis as proven.

The handling of the bodies was another exercise of power after death. The Minnesota Historical Society confirms that physicians, among them William Worrall Mayo, removed bodies from their shallow graves for anatomical study; Mayo obtained the remains of Marpiya te Najin, also known as Stands on a Cloud or Cut Nose. The record warrants identifying his participation, but not suggesting that he acted alone or that the exact history of every individual's remains is known. Contemporary dissection practices must be examined alongside the denial of mourning and burial choices to Dakota families. These actions form part of the longer history of collecting and eventually repatriating Indigenous human remains.

Division V Imprisonment, Expulsion, Exile, and Dakota Survival
Section 73.17

The Forced March to Fort Snelling and the Winter of Imprisonment, 1862–1863

While the commission was still at work, the army decided to remove the Dakota noncombatants from the Lower Agency area. On November 7, 1862, a column of 1,658 Dakota people, mostly women, children, and elderly men, began a march of about a hundred and fifty miles under military guard. They traveled in wagons and on foot, with their possessions, along a route that took them through the settlements they had fled or attacked. The march lasted about a week, and the column reached Fort Snelling on November 14, according to the Minnesota Historical Society's reconstruction. Separately, the men who had been condemned or were awaiting trial were held at Mankato.

The experiences of those on the march were harrowing. Women, children, and elders who had taken no part in the war, or who had opposed it, walked past the farms of people who had lost relatives. At New Ulm, a crowd of townspeople, many of them women, attacked the column with stones, clubs, and other objects, and a number of Dakota people were injured. Similar hostility met the column in other places. The soldiers who guarded the march did not consistently protect their charges, and provisions were inadequate. Many of the Dakota had lost their husbands, sons, and fathers to capture, trial, and, soon, execution, and they were moving toward an uncertain future in a landscape where they were targets of anger.

The destination was a stockade built on the river bottom below Fort Snelling, on the low ground along the Minnesota River near its junction with the Mississippi. It is important to keep it distinct from the fort's Civil War training facilities, which were located elsewhere on the bluff. The stockade was an enclosure in which more than sixteen hundred people were held through the winter, in lodges and in crowded, poorly provisioned conditions, under military guard. The location was itself charged with meaning. The place was at Bdote, the site of Dakota origin and ceremony, so that the Dakota prisoners were confined on one of the most sacred places in their homeland.

Conditions in the camp were severe. Overcrowding, exposure, inadequate food, and lack of sanitation produced disease, and an epidemic, including measles, swept through the camp. The Minnesota Historical Society documents the removal of 1,658 Dakota people beginning November 7 and estimates that between 130 and 300 died during the winter. This uncertainty is real and must be stated. No complete mortality record survives. Burials were not systematically recorded, some deaths were noted by missionaries and by Dakota survivors, and the range of estimates reflects the difficulty of reconstructing the number from fragmentary evidence. Children and the elderly were especially vulnerable. Missionaries and others worked in the camp, providing aid, instruction, and baptism, and many Dakota people there embraced Christianity, a development whose motives historians interpret in different ways, since it combined spiritual commitment, the search for protection, and the pressures of confinement.

For the families in the stockade, the winter was also a period of mourning and uncertainty. Their men were in prison at Mankato, and in December news came of the executions. Women in the camp, many of whom had lost husbands, sons, and brothers, were responsible for the survival of children and elders under conditions in which they had little power. Their survival, and the continuity of Dakota families, depended on their efforts.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farming, Food, Family Separation, Imprisonment, Illness, and Survival

Before the war, daily life on the reservation combined gardens, hunting, fishing, gathering, mission schools, and visits to the agencies. Families worked fields of corn and potatoes, women tended gardens and processed food, men hunted where game remained, and children went to school or learned from relatives. The annuity season was a time of gathering and also of anxiety, when debts were settled and supplies replenished. In 1862 that cycle broke down, and hunger set in.

The war and its aftermath tore households apart. Fathers and sons were killed, imprisoned, or executed. Families were divided between Fort Snelling, Mankato, Crow Creek, Davenport, and the northern Plains. Women assumed responsibility for children and the elderly in camps where food, shelter, and medicine were scarce. Illness, particularly in the crowded stockade, was one of the major causes of death. Survival depended on kinship, on shared food, on religious and ceremonial practice kept alive in whatever form was possible, and on the determination of individuals to hold families together. Because much of the surviving written record was produced by officials, soldiers, and missionaries, historians reconstruct these experiences through Dakota testimony recorded later, through mission records, and through careful reading of official sources for indirect evidence.

Section 73.18

The Dakota Expulsion Laws of 1863 and the Removal of the Ho-Chunk

The legal consequences of the war were set by Congress in the winter of 1862–1863. These measures transformed wartime retaliation into collective legal punishment, and two acts must be kept distinct. On February 16, 1863, Congress passed an act abrogating the treaties made with the Sisseton, Wahpeton, Mdewakanton, and Wahpekute Dakota. The act declared prior treaties annulled insofar as they imposed future obligations on the United States, and it separately forfeited Dakota lands and rights of occupancy in Minnesota along with annuity and related claims. The funds that would have been paid to the Dakota were to be used to compensate settlers for losses in the war. On March 3, 1863, Congress passed a second act authorizing the removal of the Dakota from Minnesota and the assignment of a new reservation outside any state, in the West.

These acts punished the Dakota communities as a whole, regardless of the conduct of individual members. The Dakota who had fought, those who had remained neutral, those who had opposed the war, and those who had saved settlers and captives were treated alike in the abrogation of the treaties. A limited exception was made for some Dakota who could demonstrate that they had helped settlers, and a few families were allowed to remain, but the principle was collective. The Sisseton and Wahpeton, many of whom had played little or no part in the war, and some of whom served the army as scouts, were also affected, though they would later negotiate their own arrangements. In this way a legal instrument converted a war fought by some into the dispossession of all.

The Ho-Chunk, or Winnebago, were also expelled, though they took no part in the war. They had lived under their own treaty arrangements and, after earlier forced removals from Wisconsin and a period at Long Prairie, had been settled on a reservation near Mankato, where they farmed. On February 21, 1863, Congress enacted separate legislation providing for the removal of the Ho-Chunk and the sale of their Minnesota reservation. The justification was not their involvement in the war, which did not exist, but the demand of Minnesotans that Indian peoples be removed from the state and the desire for the land. The Ho-Chunk were shipped by steamboat in the spring of 1863 to Crow Creek in Dakota Territory, under conditions that caused many deaths. The Ho-Chunk must not be described as participants in the Dakota War. Their removal must be explained as a separate process, driven by different legislation, and its association with the Dakota conflict illustrates how the war was used to justify the expulsion of Native peoples more generally.

Students should keep four concepts apart. Legal dispossession was the abrogation of treaties and forfeiture of land and annuities. Military confinement was the imprisonment at Fort Snelling and Mankato. Forced exile was the removal from Minnesota to Crow Creek, Davenport, and other places. Attempted exclusion was the broader effort, expressed in the bounty laws and in public declarations, to prevent any Dakota presence in the state. These processes overlapped but were not identical, and each had its own legal basis, its own agents, and its own consequences.

The separate Ho-Chunk removal statute illustrates the breadth of the punitive response. A Minnesota Historical Society account dates the federal removal act to February 21, 1863, before the March 3 Dakota relocation measure. The Ho-Chunk community at Blue Earth had not joined the conflict; nevertheless, state pressure and demand for its reservation land secured the federal authorization for expulsion. In the spring more than nineteen hundred Ho-Chunk were forced toward Crow Creek. Because the Ho-Chunk and Dakota were distinct nations with distinct treaties, conflating their departures into one event would erase a crucial feature of Minnesota's policy: a war involving some members of one people became the rationale for removing another people that had not fought at all.

Historical Vocabulary
Expulsion, Exile, and Indigenous Sovereignty

Definition: Removal, in the language of nineteenth-century federal policy, meant the forced relocation of Indigenous peoples from their homelands. Exile is the condition of living away from one's homeland as a result of such compulsion. Sovereignty is a nation's inherent political authority, discussed in Chapter 1.

Historical context: Removal was an established federal instrument, examined in Chapters 65 and 66. In 1863 it was applied to the Dakota and the Ho-Chunk through legislation, and it was accompanied by confinement and the abrogation of treaties.

Why it matters: Removal differed from ordinary migration in that it was compelled, backed by force, and intended to sever a people from its land. Yet the forced departure of a nation from its homeland did not extinguish its political existence. Dakota communities maintained kinship, language, and governance in exile, and they continued to assert their relationship with Minnesota.

Potential misunderstanding: The word "removal" can sound administrative and neutral. It concealed coercion, loss of life, and the destruction of livelihoods. Equally, the word "exile" does not imply that a nation ceased to exist outside its homeland.

Fort Snelling Confinement Records, Dakota Family Testimony, and the 1863 Legislation

Creator: Congress and the army; missionaries; Dakota survivors · Date: 1862–1863 (records and statutes); later recollections · Type: Federal statutes, military and mission records, and Dakota testimony recorded afterward

Context: The February 16, 1863 act and the March 3, 1863 act are published in the federal statutes. Records of the confinement include army reports and missionary letters. Dakota family testimony, recorded years later, describes the march, the stockade, and the removal.

Paraphrase and brief quotation: The February 16 act declares the Dakota treaties "abrogated and annulled," and provides for the forfeiture of annuities and their use for settlers' claims. The March 3 act authorizes removal and the assignment of a new reservation. The confinement records describe numbers, provisions, and disease in general terms, and Dakota testimony describes loss and hardship in personal terms. Other content is paraphrased.

Analysis: The statutes show the federal government extending punishment from individuals to the whole community through law. The confinement records and testimony show the human consequences.

Limitations: The statutes say nothing of Dakota experience. Army and mission records are incomplete and do not provide reliable mortality counts. Dakota testimony was recorded later, in conditions that shaped what was said and how it was translated.

Perspective: The statutes and army records represent federal authority; mission records, a sympathetic but outside perspective; the testimony, Dakota survivors, though often through intermediaries.

Section 73.19

Crow Creek, Davenport, and the Dakota Diaspora after 1863

In the spring of 1863 the Dakota were transported out of Minnesota. Beginning April 22, approximately 270 Dakota men convicted or detained after the military trials were transported from Mankato down the Mississippi to the prison at Camp McClellan in Davenport, Iowa (the number varies slightly by source). They were held there for about three years. Disease and poor conditions killed many of them, and although exact figures are uncertain, scores died, the Minnesota Historical Society identifies at least 120 deaths. Many became Christians in prison and learned to read, and the camp also became a place in which mission and educational work was carried out. In 1866, after appeals and the intervention of missionaries, the survivors were released and joined their families in Nebraska.

The Dakota families held at Fort Snelling were sent, in May 1863, by steamboat down the Mississippi and up the Missouri River to Crow Creek, on the Missouri in Dakota Territory, together with the Ho-Chunk. About thirteen hundred Dakota people made the journey. Crow Creek was a poor location. The land was dry, the water poor, firewood and game scarce, and the crops that the Dakota planted were damaged by drought and insects. Provisions were inadequate, and the supply system, again, was subject to delay and corruption. The mortality in the first year and after was high, and hundreds died, though, as with other episodes, exact totals are difficult to establish. Families were separated, with husbands and fathers in Iowa or in flight, and women, children, and elders bore the hardship. The Ho-Chunk suffered similarly, and many left Crow Creek in 1864 for Nebraska.

Dakota communities were thus scattered. Some, after surviving Crow Creek, were moved in 1866 to a new reservation in northeastern Nebraska, the Santee Reservation near the Niobrara River, where they were joined by the released prisoners from Davenport. Other Dakota people, particularly those associated with Little Crow's group and others who had fled, moved west onto the northern Plains and north into Canada, where Dakota communities still live in Manitoba and Saskatchewan. Sisseton and Wahpeton Dakota, many of whom had served as scouts or kept away from the war, eventually secured reservations in what is now South Dakota and North Dakota in the late 1860s. Small numbers of Dakota remained in Minnesota or returned quietly, and the development of these communities is discussed in Section 73.20.

Little Crow died on July 3, 1863. He had returned to Minnesota with a small party and was shot near Hutchinson by a settler while picking berries with his son. The settler claimed a bounty. Minnesota had introduced bounties on Dakota scalps, at a rate that rose over the course of 1863, and Little Crow's body was desecrated and displayed. His remains were kept by the state historical society for years before being returned to his descendants in the twentieth century. The bounty system is an example of the policy of exclusion and violence directed against Dakota people as such. It should be remembered alongside the formal acts of Congress, because it extended the logic of collective punishment to individual Dakota who might be killed on sight.

The army also pursued Dakota people, and the military expeditions of 1863 under Sibley and General Alfred Sully moved into Dakota Territory, where engagements at places such as Big Mound, Dead Buffalo Lake, Stony Lake, and Whitestone Hill involved Dakota, Lakota, and other Plains people, including many who had nothing to do with Minnesota. This chapter does not narrate those campaigns, which belong to the broader treatment of the Plains conflicts in the chapters that follow. It is enough to note that the war in Minnesota became the occasion for a larger extension of military power onto the northern Plains.

The Davenport prison is particularly well documented through letters as well as government records. The Minnesota Historical Society's edition of The Dakota Prisoner of War Letters reproduces fifty letters translated by Dakota speakers Clifford Canku and Michael Simon. Many prisoners asked about absent relatives, attempted to secure news, and sought help from missionary correspondents. The edition describes approximately 270 men sent there and more than 120 deaths. Those letters reveal emotions and strategies that cannot be inferred from a prison admission ledger: how prisoners attempted to preserve family ties, sought information about their legal status, and used writing across distance. They also make clear that commutation of a death sentence did not mean liberty.

Section 73.20

Dakota Sovereignty after the War: Survival, Return, and Historical Memory

The consequences of 1862 and 1863 were immense. The Dakota lost the remainder of their land in Minnesota. The treaty relationship that had defined their dealings with the United States was abrogated, and their annuities were forfeited. Their communities were scattered across several jurisdictions: Nebraska, the Dakotas, Iowa for a time, and Canada. Hundreds had died in battle, in confinement, and in exile, and thirty-eight had been hanged. Families had been divided, and communities that had been continuous for generations were broken.

But the war ended in military defeat and devastating collective punishment, not in the end of the Dakota. Dakota people retained their identity, their language, their ceremonies, and their political organization. In exile, families kept alive kinship ties and relationships that spanned the new reservations. Dakota language continued to be spoken and was written in the orthography developed with the missionaries; Christian churches became important institutions among the exiled communities, alongside the persistence of older practices. Leaders emerged in the new communities and negotiated with federal officials, and the Sisseton-Wahpeton, Santee Sioux, Flandreau Santee, and Dakota communities in Canada each developed political institutions that continue today. Dakota women and men took on the work of rebuilding in conditions of poverty and dependency.

The Dakota also maintained, and in many cases reasserted, relationships with Minnesota. A few families who had been recognized as having aided settlers were permitted to stay, others returned quietly in the following years, and by the late nineteenth century small communities of Dakota were living again at places such as Prairie Island and near Redwood and Shakopee. Congress made appropriations for the Mdewakanton who remained in Minnesota, and in the twentieth century these communities developed into the present-day Dakota communities of Prairie Island, Lower Sioux, Shakopee Mdewakanton, and Upper Sioux, each with its own government. Their histories, together with those of Dakota communities elsewhere, are examined in later chapters on reorganization, self-determination, and tribal governance, including Chapters 97 and 114.

Historical memory of the war has been contested. For many years Minnesota's public memory emphasized the suffering of settlers and the narrative of an unprovoked uprising, and the term "Sioux Uprising" appeared in textbooks and monuments. Dakota people maintained their own memories and commemorations, and in recent decades scholars, communities, and institutions have revised the public record. The Minnesota Historical Society has presented the war in terms of treaties, hunger, and the consequences for Dakota people, and commemorations marking the 150th anniversary in 2012 included Dakota-led events. Each December Dakota people and others take part in memorial rides and gatherings to honor the thirty-eight men hanged at Mankato and two others, Shakopee and Medicine Bottle, who were captured in Canada and executed in 1865. Efforts to preserve evidence, to repatriate remains, and to restore Dakota place names are part of a broader movement discussed in Chapters 125 and 132.

The conclusion of this chapter is therefore twofold. The war and its aftermath were a catastrophe for the Dakota, produced by treaty dispossession, administrative failure, violence in which civilians died, and a federal and state response that punished a whole people. And Dakota sovereignty did not cease to exist in 1862 or 1863. It endured in the persistence of Dakota communities, in their governments, in their relationship to their ancestral homeland, and in their continuing work of remembrance and return. The history of the Dakota War is not only a story of defeat but of survival, and it opens Volume VII by showing how the reservation order of the later nineteenth century was built, and how Indigenous nations lived through it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Dakota Life before 1851, during 1862, and after Exile

What Changed

  • The territory open to Dakota use shrank from a wide homeland to a strip of the Minnesota River valley by 1858, and then to nothing in Minnesota by law in 1863.
  • The economy shifted from mixed hunting, gathering, gardening, and trade to dependence on annuities, trader credit, and agency goods, and then to confinement and ration economies in exile.
  • Dakota communities were dispersed across multiple jurisdictions, and leaders and families were killed, imprisoned, or displaced.
  • Many Dakota adopted Christianity and new forms of schooling, especially in confinement and on the new reservations.

What Stayed the Same

  • Dakota language, kinship ties, and ceremonial and community life persisted in exile.
  • The relationship to Bdote and Mni Sota Makoce remained central to Dakota identity.
  • Dakota nations continued to act as political communities, with leaders and councils, negotiating with federal authorities.
  • Dakota people continued to assert claims to land, to remembrance, and to recognition.

Pattern of change: Change was abrupt for the Dakota in 1862 and 1863, but it built on gradual transformations since 1851. Those who lost the most, including the families of the executed and imprisoned, experienced it most directly. Continuity was greatest in language, kinship, and the sense of belonging to a homeland, and it explains why the Dakota survived the catastrophe as peoples.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Dakota War of 1862 as a crisis produced by treaty dispossession, federal administrative failure, hunger, settler expansion, and political division, and as the prelude to a program of collective punishment far larger than the war itself. Its central question was how the destruction of Dakota land rights and the crisis of 1862 produced war in Minnesota, and how the United States transformed a six-week conflict into mass execution and forced exile.

The chapter began with the Dakota as sovereign peoples. The Mdewakanton, Wahpekute, Wahpeton, and Sisseton lived in Mni Sota Makoce, with Bdote at its center, and sustained themselves through a varied economy of hunting, fishing, gathering, gardening, and trade. Their politics rested on bands, councils, kinship, and soldiers' lodges rather than a single government, so that no one leader could commit the whole oyate, a fact that shaped both treaty-making and the decision for war.

The treaties of Traverse des Sioux and Mendota in 1851 ceded an enormous territory in return for trust-fund annuities, goods, and a reservation. The Senate removed the reservation provisions, and the Traders' Paper diverted substantial sums to creditors. The agreements of 1858 then took the northern half of what remained. These were land transactions and also a transformation of Dakota political economy, replacing a diverse subsistence base with dependence on payments and credit controlled by others.

By 1861 and 1862 that dependence had become dangerous. Crop failure, a hard winter, the loss of hunting land, corruption in the agency system, wartime delay of the annuity, and the refusal of traders to extend credit produced hunger. The famous remark attributed to the trader Andrew Myrick is poorly documented and must be treated cautiously, but the structural facts are clear: food was in the warehouses, payment was late, and families were hungry.

The killing of five settlers near Acton on August 17 by four young men triggered a night of debate among Dakota leaders and soldiers. Little Crow, who had doubts about war, agreed to lead. The chapter insisted on two distinctions: the four young men were not the Dakota population, and the Acton killings precipitated a decision whose underlying causes were years in the making.

The war that followed lasted about six weeks. Dakota fighters attacked the Lower Agency and ambushed soldiers at Redwood Ferry, then failed to take Fort Ridgely, fought two battles at New Ulm, and struck farms and settlements across a wide area, killing hundreds of civilians, with estimates varying widely, and taking captives. The Dakota were divided. Many fought, many did not, and a Peace Party of leaders and families protected captives and sought to end the war. Birch Coulee showed Dakota tactical strength, but Wood Lake on September 23 was decisive, and on September 26 about 270 captives were released at Camp Release.

The army then tried 392 Dakota men before a military commission, with brief proceedings, no counsel, and a tendency to treat participation in battle as grounds for execution. The commission sentenced 303 to death. Lincoln's review narrowed the list to 39, and after one reprieve, 38 men were hanged at Mankato on December 26, 1862. The figures are separate stages, and the review did not cure the flaws of the trials.

Dakota noncombatants were marched to Fort Snelling and held in a stockade on the river bottom below the fort, where an estimated 130 to 300 died during the winter. In February and March 1863 Congress abrogated the Dakota treaties and authorized removal, punishing all Dakota regardless of individual conduct. The Ho-Chunk, who had not fought, were expelled under separate legislation. Dakota families were sent to Crow Creek and prisoners to Davenport, while others fled to the Plains and Canada, and Minnesota paid bounties for Dakota scalps. Little Crow died in July 1863.

The chapter ended with survival. Dakota language, ceremony, kinship, and political organization persisted across the scattered communities, and Dakota people reasserted their relationship with Minnesota. Present-day Dakota communities in Minnesota, the Dakotas, Nebraska, and Canada descend from the survivors, and annual commemorations keep the memory of the executed and the exiled alive.

Taken together, the chapter shows that responsibility must be assigned precisely: to the treaty makers and administrators who created the crisis, to the individuals who killed civilians, to the officers who conducted the trials, and to the legislators and officials who imposed collective punishment. It also shows that Dakota history in Minnesota did not end in 1862 or 1863. This framing prepares for the chapters that follow on the wider wars of the Plains.

Chapter Reflection

The Dakota War of 1862 matters because it shows, in compressed form, how a treaty system could produce a crisis, how a crisis could produce war, and how a war could be used to justify the dispossession of an entire people. The chapter opens Volume VII because the patterns it reveals recur in the later history of the reservation order: promises that depend on distant administration, dependence engineered through land loss, and the readiness of governments to turn violence by some into punishment of all.

It also shows how carefully history must be written when violence and injustice coexist. Civilians died, and the people who killed them bore responsibility for their acts. Dakota communities had been pushed to the edge of starvation by choices made by others, and many Dakota people opposed the war and risked their lives to protect captives. A history that kept only one of these truths would be simpler and less accurate.

The consequences extended well beyond 1863. The scattering of the Dakota shaped the geography of present-day Dakota nations in Minnesota, the Dakotas, Nebraska, and Canada, and it affected later relations with the United States, including the broader Plains conflicts to come. The bounty system and the expulsion acts left a legacy of exclusion that Dakota people have confronted for generations, and the contested memory of the war continues to shape public life in Minnesota.

Above all, the chapter supports a principle that runs through this textbook: Indigenous nations are not remnants of an earlier North America but continuing political and historical communities. The Dakota survived the catastrophe of 1862 and 1863, preserved their language and ceremonies, rebuilt governments, and maintained their relationship with Mni Sota Makoce. Understanding that survival is essential to understanding the rest of the history of the continent.
