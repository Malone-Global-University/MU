The Residential School Reckoning in Canada

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On October 30, 1990, in a CBC interview, a Manitoba First Nations leader named Phil Fontaine described the abuse he had experienced as a child at a residential school. Survivors had spoken before that day, and many would speak after it. But the interview helped move a long-private history into national public view, and it raised a question that Canada and the churches that had operated the schools would spend the next three decades answering, imperfectly and under pressure: what was owed to the children, and to the families and nations from which they had been taken?

Earlier chapters of this textbook examined how residential schooling was created and how it operated, and how children, parents, and communities experienced and resisted it (Chapters 87 and 88). This chapter begins from a different question. It asks how a system that Canadian governments and churches had long described in the language of education and benevolence came to be publicly acknowledged as a system of removal, neglect, abuse, and cultural destruction, and who made that acknowledgment happen. It opens Part XXII of the textbook, which turns from the cultural and institutional resurgence traced in Part XXI to the struggle for accountability, memory, and justice.

The central argument of the chapter is that Canada’s residential-school reckoning was not simply a decision by governments to acknowledge past wrongdoing. It emerged from decades of Indigenous survivor testimony, organizing, legal action, and demands for accountability. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis survivors, their families, and Indigenous organizations told what had happened, formed networks, filed lawsuits, negotiated, sat as witnesses before a national commission, and, in the twenty-first century, led searches for children who never came home. The apologies, settlements, investigations, and reconciliation initiatives that resulted transformed public understanding of Canadian history. They also left significant questions of justice, responsibility, and Indigenous authority unresolved.

The chapter follows a sequence of overlapping stages. Survivor-led disclosure and organizing challenged institutional silence and public indifference. Litigation and negotiation produced the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement, which took effect in 2007, and the federal apology of June 11, 2008. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada then collected testimony, examined records, and issued its findings and 94 Calls to Action in 2015. Renewed investigation of missing children and unmarked burials, brought to national attention in May 2021 by Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc, exposed the gaps in the existing record and in institutional accountability. The final stage, still open, concerns the competing demands of survivors, Indigenous nations, governments, churches, and public institutions for justice, reparations, institutional change, and lasting accountability.

The chapter relies on several kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths and limits. Survivor testimony, gathered in court processes, in the Commission’s statement-gathering, and in community settings, is historical evidence in its own right, not an emotional supplement to official records. Government and church archives document policy, finances, enrolment, and deaths, but they were produced by the institutions that ran the system and are incomplete. Court records and settlement documents show what claims were legally recognized. Geophysical surveys, historical mapping, and archival research are used in the search for burials, and each of these methods answers only certain questions. A historian reading this chapter should ask, of every claim, what kind of evidence supports it, who produced that evidence, and whose account is missing.

The chapter also takes care with scope. “Survivors” and “communities” include First Nations, Inuit, and Métis people whose experiences, legal standing, and political circumstances differed. Some were covered by the national settlement and some were not; some schools were operated by Roman Catholic bodies, others by the Anglican, United, or Presbyterian churches, and the federal government held legal authority over the system as a whole. Where the evidence concerns one nation, region, or church, the chapter says so instead of generalizing across the whole country. Residential schools in the United States and the American investigations into them belong to Chapter 128, and the broader history of child removal to Chapter 131.

A word on the chapter’s relationship to the present is necessary. Many of the developments described here are recent, and some are unfinished as this chapter is written in late 2026. Investigations continue, records disputes continue, and the assessment of how far the Calls to Action have been implemented is itself contested. The chapter therefore avoids presenting reconciliation as complete, and it distinguishes carefully among the different things that governments, churches, and courts have done: acknowledging facts, apologizing, paying compensation, changing institutions, and recognizing Indigenous authority. These are not the same, and none of them automatically satisfies the others.

The distinction between acknowledging the historical truth and fulfilling the obligations that follow from it governs the whole chapter. Acknowledgment matters; it was won through the labor and courage of survivors and was never guaranteed. But a history that ended at the moment of apology would misread the evidence. Much of what survivors, Indigenous leaders, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission asked for concerned what Canada and the churches would do next: how records would be opened, how missing children would be searched for, how child welfare, education, justice, and health systems would change, and who would hold the authority to decide these questions.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Canada’s residential-school reckoning should be understood as the result of survivor-led activism, litigation, and public testimony rather than solely a government decision to acknowledge wrongdoing.
  2. Trace the sequence from survivor disclosure and organizing in the 1980s and 1990s through litigation, the 1998 Statement of Reconciliation, and the Aboriginal Healing Foundation.
  3. Describe the main components of the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement and distinguish a legal settlement from historical reconciliation.
  4. Analyze the federal apology of June 11, 2008, distinguishing official acknowledgment, political symbolism, and enforceable commitments.
  5. Assess the mandate, methods, and limits of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada as a truth commission rather than a criminal court.
  6. Evaluate survivor testimony as historical evidence, including the ethical protocols that governed how it was gathered, shared, and preserved.
  7. Interpret selected Calls to Action, identifying which institutions each addresses and which historical problem it seeks to remedy.
  8. Distinguish among documented deaths, children whose fate is unknown, cemetery records, geophysical indications of possible burials, and confirmed identifications.
  9. Explain the significance of community authority in burial-site investigations, using Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc and other First Nations as examples.
  10. Compare the responses of the federal government and of the Roman Catholic, Anglican, United, and Presbyterian churches, accounting for their different historical roles.
  11. Evaluate competing arguments about whether acknowledgment has produced meaningful justice, and identify the evidence that bears on each position.
  12. Connect the residential-school reckoning to related questions of language, education, child welfare, repatriation, memory, and Indigenous self-determination treated in neighboring chapters.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did survivors’ accounts remain marginal in official Canadian historical narratives for so long, and what changed in the 1990s?
  2. How did individual disclosure, collective organizing, and legal action reinforce one another in producing a national reckoning?
  3. What is the difference between a settlement agreement, an apology, a truth commission, and an act of reconciliation? What can each accomplish, and what can each not accomplish?
  4. Why do the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s legal limits as a non-judicial body matter for how its findings should be read?
  5. How should historians treat survivor testimony alongside archival records that were produced by the institutions that operated the schools?
  6. What can ground-penetrating radar establish about possible burials, and what can it not establish without further investigation?
  7. Why is the question of who decides how a search proceeds, and whether a site is excavated, central to the history of the missing-children investigations?
  8. How did the responsibilities of the Canadian state and of the churches differ, and why do these differences complicate assessments of accountability?
  9. Is reconciliation a process, an outcome, or a contested concept? Who has defined it, and who has challenged those definitions?

Historical Context

The reckoning examined in this chapter responds to a system whose origins and operation are traced in Chapters 87 and 88. For present purposes, three features of that system matter. First, residential schooling in Canada was a federally authorized and largely church-operated system that removed First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children from their families and communities, in many cases for much of their childhoods. According to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, roughly 150,000 children attended, in schools that operated across most of the country from the nineteenth century into the late twentieth; the last federally supported school closed in 1996. Second, the system was supervised by the federal government under the authority of the Indian Act (Chapter 85) and operated in partnership with Roman Catholic, Anglican, Presbyterian, and Methodist bodies; the Methodist church and much of the Presbyterian church became part of the United Church of Canada in 1925, while a continuing Presbyterian Church remained separate. Third, those who attended were often prevented from using their languages, were separated from siblings, were frequently underfed and overworked, and were exposed to physical and sexual abuse and to deadly disease, while their parents had little or no legal means to contest what was happening.

Survivors’ accounts were not, however, absent from Indigenous communities during these decades. Parents, grandparents, and children recorded their objections in letters and petitions, ran from the schools, and in some places refused to send children. After the Second World War, Indigenous political organizations began to press for changes in education. In 1972 the National Indian Brotherhood’s policy paper Indian Control of Indian Education called for First Nations authority over schooling, and in 1970 First Nations people in Alberta occupied the Blue Quills school, a protest that led to its transfer to Indigenous control. By the 1970s and 1980s the federal government had begun to close schools or hand some over to First Nations administration. But closure did not produce public accountability. The consequences of attendance, including trauma, family breakdown, and language loss, were widely experienced in communities, while public and political discussion of the schools remained limited.

Several conditions in the 1980s and early 1990s made a public reckoning possible. Indigenous political organizations had grown in strength and visibility, including the Assembly of First Nations, Inuit organizations, and Métis political bodies. The constitutional recognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights in 1982 (Chapter 117) strengthened the legal and political position of Indigenous peoples. The 1990 confrontation at Oka and Kanehsatà:ke drew national attention to the state of relations between Canada and Indigenous peoples and contributed to the creation of the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples. And the 1980s and 1990s saw wider public attention in Canada to institutional child abuse, which made it easier for survivors to describe sexual and physical abuse in ways that others could hear. Churches also began to respond: the United Church apologized to Indigenous peoples in 1986, the Oblates in 1991, the Anglican Church of Canada in 1993, and the Presbyterian Church in Canada in 1994, though these statements were uneven in scope and did not settle questions of legal liability.

The legal environment also constrained survivors. Claims arising from events decades earlier raised questions about limitation periods, evidence, and institutional responsibility. Whether the federal government, the churches, or both were legally liable for abuse committed by staff, and how liability should be shared, had to be established through court decisions. Before the settlement of 2006, survivors typically had to litigate individually or through class actions, with great emotional and financial cost. Understanding the settlement, the apology, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission requires seeing them as responses to this legal and political pressure, not as spontaneous initiatives.

Finally, the chapter’s subject spans two eras of historical writing. The older historiography of Canadian education treated residential schools primarily through government and church records and annual reports. The newer scholarship that emerged from the 1990s, and especially after the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, drew heavily on survivor testimony, Indigenous legal and political analysis, and a wider range of archival evidence. The chapter draws on both, and notes where they differ.

Division I · Sections 127.1–127.4

Survivors and the Emergence of a National Reckoning

How survivors challenged silence, organized collectively, and compelled Canadian institutions to acknowledge residential-school harms.

Section 127.1

The Residential School Legacy and the Beginning of the Reckoning

The reckoning began with an inheritance. By the time the last federally supported residential school closed in 1996, generations of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children had been separated from their families for months or years at a time, taught in institutions run by churches under federal authority, and in many cases prohibited from speaking their languages or practicing their ceremonies. The conditions that survivors later described were not uniform across schools or decades, and some former students recalled teachers who showed them kindness or gave them skills they valued. But the testimony that eventually reached courts, commissions, and the public described, with striking consistency, hunger, harsh discipline, forced labor, loneliness, and, for many, sexual and physical abuse. Chapters 87 and 88 trace how this system was built and how families responded; here it is enough to recognize that its consequences were still unfolding in the lives of survivors and descendants long after the schools themselves had closed.

Those consequences were carried largely within Indigenous families and communities. Survivors often returned home unable to speak the language of their parents, unfamiliar with the daily work and ceremonial life of their nations, and marked by experiences they had been taught to be ashamed of. Many struggled with the effects of trauma, and the harms could pass into the next generation through disrupted parenting, loss of language, and the social effects of poverty and displacement. Scholars and Indigenous communities refer to these as intergenerational effects, a term the chapter uses carefully: it describes patterns documented by survivors, communities, and researchers, not a uniform outcome for every family. Indigenous peoples did not experience these effects as past history. They were part of the present.

Why, then, did it take so long for the system to become a subject of national acknowledgment? Several conditions kept survivors’ accounts at the margins of official Canadian narratives. For much of the twentieth century, annual reports from the Department of Indian Affairs, church publications, and school newsletters described the schools as benevolent institutions that civilized and educated Indigenous children. Those were the records most accessible to historians, journalists, and the public, and they were written by the institutions responsible. Survivors had little access to publication, to legal counsel, or to a public audience, and Indian Act provisions in force from 1927 to 1951 restricted Status Indians from raising funds to pursue legal claims, limiting organized political and legal action. Many survivors also had reasons for silence: shame, fear of disbelief, the wish to protect their children, and the lack of anyone to tell.

Silence was never total, however. In Indigenous communities, accounts of the schools circulated in kitchens, ceremonies, and political meetings. Indigenous organizations had criticized the system for decades, and communities had taken control of schools or closed them. What changed in the late twentieth century was less the existence of survivor knowledge than the existence of institutions and audiences able to receive it. The Assembly of First Nations and regional organizations gave survivors a political voice. Courts began to hear their claims. Media organizations, especially public broadcasters, were more willing to carry their accounts. And a wider public conversation about abuse of children in institutions made it harder to dismiss what survivors said.

This section therefore treats the reckoning as an Indigenous-led process of making knowledge public, not as the moment when governments discovered what had happened. Officials in Indian Affairs and in the churches had long had information about disease, overcrowding, runaways, and abuse in their own files. What they had lacked was sustained public pressure to act. The rest of Division I traces how that pressure developed, from individual disclosures and survivor networks to lawsuits, a royal commission, and a first federal statement of reconciliation in 1998.

Section 127.2

Survivor Testimony, Public Disclosure, and Indigenous Organizing

Case Study 1 · Phil Fontaine and the Survivor-Led Reckoning, 1990

Survivors began to organize and speak publicly well before 1990. In the 1980s and early 1990s, survivors in several regions came forward to describe abuse by named staff, often at gatherings in their communities, and to ask police and prosecutors to act. Some cases reached court. In 1995 a dormitory supervisor from the Alberni Indian Residential School in British Columbia, Arthur Plint, was convicted of multiple counts of indecent assault against former students. The prosecution and the later civil litigation arising from it depended on survivors who were willing to testify years after the events, in public and under cross-examination. Survivors also began forming societies and support groups, among them the Indian Residential School Survivors Society in British Columbia, to offer counselling, help with legal processes, and a space in which people could speak with others who had gone through the same experience.

It was in this setting that Phil Fontaine gave his interview on October 30, 1990. Fontaine, a survivor of the Fort Alexander residential school and then a leader in Manitoba’s First Nations political organizations, described the sexual abuse he had experienced as a child. He later said that he spoke because he wanted others to know they were not alone and because he believed the truth had to be told publicly. His account was carried by a national broadcaster, and it was a prominent public disclosure by an Indigenous leader at a time when many survivors were still silent. Fontaine would go on to serve as National Chief of the Assembly of First Nations and to play a central role in the negotiation of the Settlement Agreement, so his disclosure also connected the personal and the political.

The significance of this episode is easy to overstate in one direction and to understate in another. It would be a mistake to treat October 30, 1990, as the moment when survivors began to speak; they had been speaking and organizing for years, and many other survivors, including those whose names are less widely known, told their stories before and after. Yet it would also be a mistake to dismiss the importance of the interview. National media attention changed what a Canadian audience could be expected to know, and the disclosure helped other survivors to come forward, with the knowledge that a respected leader had gone before them. Historians usually describe such moments as turning points not because they initiate a movement but because they mark a shift in who is listening.

The link between individual disclosure and collective organizing is the central lesson of this case. Disclosure was not simply personal catharsis; it was a political act that placed demands on institutions. Survivors’ accounts of abuse supplied the evidence for lawsuits. Survivor networks supplied the people and the organizational capacity to carry those lawsuits forward. Indigenous political organizations, in turn, took these accounts as the basis for demands directed at both the federal government and the churches. By the middle of the 1990s, residential schools were an established subject of Indigenous political advocacy, and the question facing governments and churches was no longer whether the accounts were true, but how they would respond.

Students should note the limits of what the evidence from this period allows. Early survivor testimony survives in court records, news reports, community publications, and later oral-history projects, but much of it was never recorded, and the voices that did reach the public were not necessarily representative of all survivors. Women, Inuit and Métis survivors, and those who had attended small or remote schools were often less visible. The Commission’s statement-gathering after 2010, discussed in Division III, would partly address these gaps.

Further reading and documentary access: the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation holds survivor statements and related records; consult its guidance on access and consent.

Section 127.3

Litigation, Public Inquiries, and the Demand for Accountability

Litigation turned survivors’ accounts into legal claims. During the 1990s, former students filed lawsuits alleging abuse and, in some cases, claiming damages for the loss of language and culture, naming both the federal government and the church bodies that had operated the schools. The legal issues were difficult. Courts had to decide how to treat events that had occurred decades earlier, whether limitation periods barred the claims, how to allocate responsibility between the federal government and church organizations, and how to assess damages. Individual lawsuits were expensive, slow, and emotionally demanding for survivors, who were often required to describe painful events in detail and to face cross-examination.

Several decisions shaped the legal landscape. In litigation arising from the Alberni school, the Supreme Court of Canada in 2005 (Blackwater v. Plint) upheld findings that both the federal government and the United Church bore legal responsibility for abuse suffered by students, and it apportioned liability between them. Class actions were certified in other cases, including a case concerning the Mohawk Institute in Brantford, Ontario. By the early 2000s, the federal government faced thousands of individual claims and was spending large sums on litigation, and the churches, which also faced liability, feared the financial consequences. In 2003 the government created an Alternative Dispute Resolution process to resolve abuse claims outside the courts. Survivors and their lawyers criticized that process as slow, limited in scope, and unfair, and it did not satisfy the demand for a comprehensive remedy.

Historical Vocabulary
Truth, Acknowledgment, Accountability, and Reconciliation

Definition. These four terms are often used together but describe different things. Truth refers to the establishment of facts, through testimony, records, and investigation. Acknowledgment is the public recognition, by an institution or society, that those facts are accurate and that wrong was done. Accountability means accepting responsibility and being answerable for consequences, whether through legal liability, financial obligation, institutional change, or the disclosure of records. Reconciliation describes the repair of relationships, a process that depends on the others but is not identical to any of them.

Historical context. In the residential-school reckoning, these elements arrived at different times and were sometimes in tension. Survivors’ testimony and lawsuits established much of the truth; the 1998 Statement of Reconciliation and the 2008 apology offered acknowledgment; the Settlement Agreement imposed certain forms of accountability; and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission named reconciliation as the goal that all of these should serve.

Why it matters. The distinctions help students avoid treating an apology as the end of a historical process. A government can acknowledge a truth without fulfilling the obligations that follow from it, and a commission can record the truth without having the power to enforce accountability.

Potential misunderstanding. “Reconciliation” in this context does not describe a return to a prior harmonious relationship, because that relationship never existed. Many Indigenous leaders and scholars emphasize that reconciliation requires change in the structures that produced harm, and some question whether the term is adequate at all, a debate examined in Section 127.20.

The Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples brought the schools into a broader national inquiry. Established in 1991 after the Oka crisis and chaired by René Dussault and Georges Erasmus, the Commission heard from Indigenous people across the country and issued its final report in 1996. It treated residential schooling as one component of a colonial relationship that included land dispossession, the Indian Act, and the suppression of Indigenous governments and languages, and it recommended a public inquiry into the schools’ effects. The government did not immediately establish that inquiry, and many recommendations of the Royal Commission went unimplemented. Even so, the report gave residential schooling a place in the national record and offered a framework that later efforts, including the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, would build upon.

The relationship between the courts and public inquiry was important. Litigation could establish liability and compensate individual survivors, but courts did not typically address the system as a whole or the collective harm to communities. A royal commission could examine the wider relationship but could not compel payment. This gap, between individual legal remedy and national historical accounting, shaped the demands that survivors and the Assembly of First Nations made in the early 2000s: a comprehensive resolution that would combine compensation, public acknowledgment, and a process for truth-telling. Criminal prosecution of individual perpetrators also remained limited; a small number of former staff were convicted, but many alleged offenders had died or were never charged, and survivors often found that the criminal process could not answer their larger questions about institutional responsibility.

Section 127.4

The 1998 Statement of Reconciliation and the Aboriginal Healing Foundation

On January 7, 1998, the federal government unveiled Gathering Strength: Canada’s Aboriginal Action Plan, including a Statement of Reconciliation delivered by Minister of Indian Affairs and Northern Development Jane Stewart. It was a major early federal acknowledgment of residential-school harms. The statement expressed profound regret for past federal actions that suppressed Indigenous cultures and weakened Indigenous institutions. It also specifically addressed physical and sexual abuse at the schools and told survivors, in an explicit expression of apology, “we are deeply sorry.” Alongside the statement, the government committed $350 million for community-based healing. Its language should be evaluated against the original text, rather than paraphrased as merely an expression of regret.

The statement’s reception was divided, and the reasons were substantive. Survivors and Indigenous leaders could recognize the significance of hearing a federal minister apologize while also questioning the statement’s framing. Its most direct expression of sorrow was addressed especially to survivors of physical and sexual abuse; the statement did not deliver the same comprehensive parliamentary repudiation of the assimilation policy itself that came in 2008. It was presented by a minister during the announcement of a policy program, not by the prime minister in the House of Commons. Those distinctions of scope, forum, and governmental responsibility mattered to survivors seeking recognition of the entire system of child removal—not only of abuses committed by particular staff members. It would nevertheless be inaccurate to say that the 1998 statement contained no apology.

The Aboriginal Healing Foundation (AHF) was established in 1998 to administer the healing fund. It was an Indigenous-led, non-governmental organization with a mandate to support community-based healing initiatives addressing the legacy of physical and sexual abuse in residential schools, including its intergenerational effects. Over its years of operation it funded many hundreds of projects in First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities. These included counselling and therapy, land-based and cultural programs, language initiatives, and training for community healing workers. The federal government added further funding in later years, but support was time-limited and the Foundation’s funding wound down by 2014.

Evaluations of the AHF’s work are generally positive about its community-directed approach and about its recognition that healing required cultural, spiritual, and community supports as well as clinical services. Indigenous communities controlled the design of many projects, an approach consistent with the broader movement toward Indigenous self-determination examined elsewhere in this volume. But the limits were also clear. The funding was finite and could not meet the scale of need. The Foundation was a response to harm, not an accounting for it. And because it supported healing without resolving questions of legal liability, compensation, or public history, it did not answer the demands that survivors and the Assembly of First Nations were pressing in the early 2000s.

By 2005, therefore, a gap remained between federal acknowledgment and what many survivors sought. The government had issued a ministerial statement that included apologetic language, and it had funded healing initiatives. Several churches had made their own apologies, and courts had recognized legal liability in particular cases. But there was still no national compensation mechanism for attendance within the residential-school system as a whole, no independent nationwide truth-telling process, and no comprehensive apology by the prime minister in Parliament. Continued litigation and sustained organizing by survivors and the Assembly of First Nations drove negotiations that culminated in the Settlement Agreement.

Primary document: Jane Stewart’s January 7, 1998 Statement of Reconciliation, including the full address announcing Gathering Strength.

Section 127.5

The Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement

Case Study 2 · The Settlement Agreement and the 2008 Apology (Part 1)

The Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement (IRSSA) emerged from the convergence of lawsuits, negotiation, and political pressure. By the mid-2000s, the Government of Canada faced a large and growing number of claims, the churches faced liability, and the Assembly of First Nations, led by National Chief Phil Fontaine, was pressing for a comprehensive solution that included compensation for all former students, not only those who could prove abuse. After negotiations involving representatives of the federal government, the Assembly of First Nations, Inuit representatives, lawyers for survivors, and the churches, an agreement was reached in 2006. Frank Iacobucci, a retired Supreme Court of Canada justice, acted as the federal government’s representative in these negotiations.

The agreement required approval by courts in several provinces and territories, which supervised the settlement as a class action. Courts granted approval in 2006 and 2007, and implementation began on September 19, 2007. Its principal elements included a Common Experience Payment (CEP) for all eligible former students who had resided at a recognized school; an Independent Assessment Process (IAP) for claims of sexual abuse, serious physical abuse, and other wrongful acts; a Truth and Reconciliation Commission; commemoration funding; and additional funding for healing programs. The parties were Canada, representatives of survivors, the Assembly of First Nations and Inuit representatives, and the church entities that had operated the schools. Those church entities agreed to contribute to the cost of the settlement and to provide certain records, under terms discussed in Section 127.18.

The agreement was the product of negotiation, and it reflected compromise. Survivors’ lawyers and the Assembly of First Nations obtained a collective remedy that would have been difficult to win through individual litigation. The federal government and the churches obtained a mechanism that would resolve the great bulk of outstanding claims and limit future litigation, and survivors who chose to remain in the class gave up the right to sue separately for matters covered by the agreement. The structure of the agreement also determined who was included and who was not. It covered schools listed in its schedules, and students at other institutions, including day schools and certain residential institutions that were not listed, had to seek recourse elsewhere. Métis people were included only if they had attended a listed institution, and the agreement did not separately recognize Métis as a collective party.

Understanding the agreement requires a distinction between legal settlement and historical reconciliation. As a legal instrument, it resolved claims, allocated money, and established processes. It could not repair family relationships, restore languages, or bring back children who had died. Its authors recognized this to some extent by including the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which was intended to address the historical and public dimensions that compensation could not reach. But the agreement was a negotiated instrument with limited parties, not a comprehensive statement of Canada’s obligations to Indigenous peoples, and the distance between its provisions and the wider demands for justice remains a theme of the rest of this chapter.

Section 127.6

Compensation, the Independent Assessment Process, and Its Limitations

The Common Experience Payment (CEP) acknowledged that attendance at residential school caused harm even when an individual did not prove a particular incident of abuse. The settlement provided $10,000 for the first eligible year of residence, or part thereof, and $3,000 for each subsequent eligible school year, or part thereof. In the federal government’s March 31, 2016 statistical update, 79,309 applications had resulted in payments totaling approximately $1.62 billion. These are dated implementation figures, not new payments in 2026. The federal government administered the process within specified application periods. Many survivors welcomed the payment as long-awaited acknowledgment; others faced incomplete records or exclusion because their institution was not covered, and the sums could never fully compensate for years of separation.

The Independent Assessment Process (IAP) separately assessed claims involving sexual abuse, serious physical abuse, and certain other wrongful acts causing serious psychological harm. Applicants generally appeared at private hearings before independent adjudicators, with access to support persons and cultural supports. A points-based schedule considered forms of abuse, their consequences, and other specified circumstances. The federal implementation statistics, as of March 31, 2019, recorded 38,257 applications, 38,178 resolved claims, and approximately $3.18 billion in approved IAP payments. These dated figures illustrate the scale of the program but do not describe the experience of every claimant or establish the final balance of every later administrative process.

The IAP’s achievements and limitations are both well documented. For many claimants, a hearing before an adjudicator was the first occasion on which they had told their story to someone with authority to believe it, and some described the process as validating. Others experienced the hearings as exhausting or retraumatizing, and many criticized the evidentiary requirements, the length of time claims took, and the way the points system translated deeply personal harm into categories. Because the process was confidential and its claim files were designated for destruction unless claimants consented to archiving, a question arose over the preservation of this historical record, a dispute that reached the Supreme Court of Canada in 2017 and is discussed in Section 127.12.

Historical Vocabulary
Compensation, Reparations, and Restorative Justice

Definition. Compensation is a payment intended to offset a specific, identifiable loss or injury to an individual. Reparations is a broader term for measures that seek to remedy a wrong: it can include financial payments but also restitution of land or property, access to records, institutional reform, memorialization, guarantees against repetition, and support for community-directed recovery. Restorative justice refers to approaches that focus on repairing harm and relationships, often involving those affected in deciding what repair requires.

Historical context. The Settlement Agreement relied primarily on compensation. Later demands, including those of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission and the Independent Special Interlocutor, call for a wider conception of repair that includes Indigenous-led searches for missing children, protection of burial sites, records access, and changes in the legal and political relationship between Indigenous nations and the Crown.

Why it matters. The distinction helps explain why survivors and Indigenous nations could regard a large financial settlement as important and also as insufficient.

Potential misunderstanding. Receiving compensation does not mean that survivors accepted the settlement as a final resolution of all claims, and it did not resolve claims excluded from the agreement. The language of “closure” sometimes used in public discussion can obscure how survivors themselves described their experience.

What the settlement could and could not accomplish is a persistent theme. Money could acknowledge particular injuries, and for many survivors it provided practical help and some measure of recognition. It could not by itself restore lost childhoods, rebuild damaged relationships, or recover languages and cultural knowledge. The Settlement Agreement also left out categories of harm and groups of people. Former students of institutions not named in the agreement, such as many day schools, had no claim under it. The federal government later addressed some of these exclusions through separate settlements, discussed in Section 127.17. The agreement also did not provide direct compensation for harm to families and communities, beyond funds for healing and commemoration, which meant that what Indigenous nations and descendants regarded as collective loss was addressed only indirectly.

Because the cost of the process, including legal fees and administration, was substantial, some critics also questioned how much of the funding reached survivors in the form of direct support. The figures are complicated and contested, and students should be careful to distinguish what the agreement provided in principle from what particular survivors experienced in practice. What is not contested is that the settlement transformed the legal landscape: it resolved the great bulk of the pending claims and created a public record of the system’s scale. That record would become part of the basis for the apology that followed.

Section 127.7

The Federal Apology of June 11, 2008

Case Study 2 · The Settlement Agreement and the 2008 Apology (Part 2)

On June 11, 2008, Prime Minister Stephen Harper delivered a formal apology to former students of residential schools in the House of Commons. Survivors, many of whom had travelled to Ottawa, and Indigenous leaders attended; leaders of national Indigenous organizations were invited onto the floor of the House to hear the apology and to respond. The apology was televised and was followed by statements from the leaders of the federal opposition parties and by speeches from Phil Fontaine of the Assembly of First Nations, Mary Simon of Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, and the leaders of the Congress of Aboriginal Peoples, the Native Women’s Association of Canada, and the Métis National Council. The event took place as part of the implementation of the Settlement Agreement and followed the beginning of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s mandate.

The text of the apology described the residential school policy as a sad chapter in Canadian history and acknowledged that the government had taken part in a system that forcibly removed children from their homes and communities, deprived them of the care of parents and grandparents, and in many cases exposed them to abuse and neglect. It stated that the policy had been intended to remove children from the influence of their cultures and assimilate them, and it recognized that the policy had been wrong. It acknowledged the harm to families and communities and the loss of language and culture, and it recognized the failure of the government to protect children from abuse. It extended an apology to those who had suffered and to their families and communities on behalf of the Government of Canada.

The apology was historically significant for several reasons. It was delivered by the prime minister in Parliament, with responses by representatives of national Indigenous organizations, and expressly addressed the assimilation policy that separated children from families and communities. The 1998 Statement of Reconciliation had already included a ministerial apology for abuse, so the significance of June 11, 2008, is not that it introduced apologetic language for the first time. Rather, the federal government offered a more authoritative, comprehensive public acknowledgment of the institutional policy itself. Even so, its statements about healing did not automatically resolve exclusions from compensation, archival disputes, or survivors’ demands for additional action.

The responses recorded in Parliament showed that diversity of opinion. Fontaine described the apology as a recognition that could assist healing, and he noted that survivors had been fighting for acknowledgment for many years. Simon spoke of the harm to Inuit children and communities, and Métis representatives raised the concern that Métis survivors had been left out of the settlement. Others pointed out that the apology did not address residential schools in Newfoundland and Labrador, which were not part of the agreement; the federal government would later apologize to former students of those institutions in 2017. Some critics also noted that the apology did not contain commitments to new legal obligations, and they stressed that the substance of reconciliation would depend on actions that followed.

The apology therefore needs to be analyzed as a primary source and as a political event. As a source, it records what the government chose to acknowledge, and the limits of that acknowledgment, in a specific language. As political symbolism, it marked a change in the official stance of the Canadian state. As an institutional act, it did not itself create enforceable commitments: those lay in the Settlement Agreement and in subsequent policy. Students should distinguish these three layers, and ask whether and how the later actions of the Government of Canada matched the words of June 11, 2008.

Section 127.8

The Creation and Mandate of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission

Case Study 3 · The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, 2008–2015 (Part 1)

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada was established in 2008 under the Settlement Agreement. Its purpose was to document the history and legacy of the schools, to hear from survivors and others, to promote public awareness, and to make recommendations. It was a creation of a legal settlement and not a statute-based commission, which affected its standing, funding, and authority. The settlement committed a five-year mandate and tens of millions of dollars; the Commission would later seek, and receive, an extension of time to complete its work.

The Commission’s early period was troubled. The original chair, Justice Harry LaForme, resigned in October 2008, citing disagreements with the other two commissioners over the Commission’s direction and role. The other commissioners resigned in early 2009. The resignations were a serious setback and raised doubt about whether the Commission could succeed. In June 2009, the Commission was reconstituted. Justice Murray Sinclair, an Anishinaabe judge from Manitoba who had been the first Indigenous judge appointed in the province, became Chair, and Marie Wilson, a journalist with long experience in northern Canada, and Chief Wilton Littlechild, a lawyer and Cree leader from Ermineskin Cree Nation in Alberta who was himself a residential school survivor, became Commissioners. Their appointment gave the Commission a leadership team with legal, journalistic, and community credibility.

The mandate of the Commission was broad but also limited. It was to reveal the complete story of the residential school system and to inform all Canadians about it; to guide and inspire a process of reconciliation; and to produce a report with recommendations. It was to work in a manner that was respectful of survivors and to follow Indigenous protocols. But it was also limited. It was not a court and did not have the power to compel testimony or to make legal findings of liability. Under the terms of its mandate, it was not to make findings of misconduct about named individuals unless already in the public record. Its independence from government was real but not complete, since it depended on government and the churches for records and on the federal government for funding.

Cause and Effect

Why a Truth Commission Was Created

Long-term causes

Generations of survivor experience and community knowledge, Indigenous political organizing, the churches’ gradual statements of regret, and the 1996 Royal Commission’s recognition of the schools’ role in the colonial relationship.

Short-term causes

The growth of litigation in the 1990s and early 2000s, the failure of the Alternative Dispute Resolution process to satisfy survivors, and negotiations led by the Assembly of First Nations that insisted on collective remedies beyond individual compensation.

Immediate trigger and context

The 2006 Settlement Agreement, whose schedule established the mandate of the Commission, and the federal apology in 2008 that began the implementation period.

Immediate effects

The creation of a national body to gather testimony and records, difficulty in its first year, and its reconstitution under Justice Sinclair in 2009.

Long-term consequences

A permanent historical record, a set of national recommendations, the creation of the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation, and a broad shift in public discussion of Canadian history.

Historical complexity

The Commission was not the product of a single decision. It emerged from litigation pressure on governments and churches, Indigenous insistence on public truth-telling, and negotiation among parties with different interests.

Why it matters: The Commission’s creation shows how survivors’ demands, expressed through litigation and political organizing, were incorporated into a legal settlement, and how even a negotiated commission remained subject to institutional limitations.

It is important for students to understand what it means to call the Commission a truth commission and not a court. A criminal court determines the guilt of individuals and may impose punishment; a civil court determines liability and awards damages. A truth commission, by contrast, collects accounts, reviews records, and publishes a narrative and recommendations. It can document patterns and name institutional failures, but it cannot enforce its recommendations. This distinction matters for assessing the Commission’s achievements: its principal contribution was historical and public, and the implementation of its recommendations depended on governments, churches, and the Canadian public.

Section 127.9

Survivor Testimony and the Ethics of Historical Witnessing

Case Study 3 · The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, 2008–2015 (Part 2)

The core of the Commission’s work was listening. Between 2009 and 2015 it gathered statements from survivors, family members, former school staff, and others, according to the Commission, from thousands of witnesses across the country. The statements were given in several settings: at the Commission’s national events, at regional and community hearings, and in private sessions with commissioners or statement gatherers. Some people spoke before large audiences; others chose small gatherings or one-to-one conversations, and some provided written or recorded statements. The Commission emphasized that participation was voluntary and that each person could decide how, where, and whether their statement would be shared.

These choices were more than administrative convenience. They reflected a view that how testimony is gathered affects what can be said and what can be known. Public sharing allowed survivors to speak before their communities and to have their experience witnessed, which many described as important. Private statements allowed those who could not speak publicly to contribute to the historical record without exposure. The Commission also had to consider confidentiality, the need for consent in the use and archiving of statements, and the possibility that testimony could name individuals who were living or deceased. Its mandate limited its ability to make findings against named individuals, and statement gatherers explained these limits to participants.

Support for those who testified was essential. The settlement provided health supports through a program administered by Health Canada, and the Commission’s events typically included counsellors, Elders, and cultural support workers, who offered help to survivors before, during, and after testifying. The events often began with ceremony and used Indigenous protocols that varied by region and nation, including opening and closing prayers, smudging, and the presence of Elders. These practices reflected the Commission’s commitment to ensuring that the process was respectful of survivors and consistent with Indigenous understandings of witnessing, in which listening and bearing witness carry responsibility.

For historians, the testimony poses distinctive questions. It is evidence of individual experience, memory, and meaning, and it also supplies information about institutional practices, such as discipline, diet, labor, and the handling of runaways and illness, that can be compared with archival records. Testimony may differ from archival records in emphasis, and when it does, the appropriate response is to ask what each kind of evidence can show and why it might differ, not to treat one as automatically authoritative. Records produced by the schools and the Department of Indian Affairs reflect the perspective and priorities of those institutions. Survivors’ accounts reflect the perspectives of children and young people who were subject to those institutions. Used together, they allow a fuller reconstruction than either could provide alone.

The testimony also reveals variation. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis participants described differences in the timing and character of schooling: many Inuit children, for instance, were sent to schools and hostels far from home from the 1950s and later, a pattern that shaped Inuit experience and politics in distinct ways, while Métis people’s experiences were sometimes obscured by the way schools and federal records classified them. Students should resist generalizing from any single account. The Commission’s record is large and varied, and part of its value lies in the way it preserves the range of experience.

Everyday Life and Social History

Giving and Receiving Testimony

Behind the national events and the final report were ordinary acts: a survivor deciding whether to attend, a family driving long distances to a hearing, a grandchild accompanying a grandparent, a support worker sitting with someone after they spoke. For many survivors, the decision to testify meant speaking about experiences they had not described even to their spouses or children. Some attended primarily to listen. Others brought photographs, school records, or objects, and some spoke in their own languages, which for survivors who had been punished for speaking them was itself a significant act.

Families and communities also took part in less visible ways. Elders and community members organized meals, ceremonies, and local arrangements for events, and cultural workers assisted survivors who needed support. In many communities the process opened conversations between generations that had not previously occurred, as children and grandchildren learned for the first time what their parents or grandparents had endured. The historical record of the Commission is therefore also a record of everyday relationships: of how families, Elders, and communities responded when long-held experiences were brought into public view.

These social dimensions also raise limits on what can be known. The Commission recorded those who spoke; it cannot record those who chose not to, those who had died, or those who could not attend. Historians should be cautious about assuming that the voices recorded represent every experience, and should recognize that silence in the record can reflect personal choice or lack of access as well as absence of experience.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might survivors choose private rather than public statements, and how does this choice affect the historical record?
  2. What kinds of support were needed for testimony to be given safely, and why?
  3. What does the use of Indigenous protocols suggest about the way the Commission understood the relationship between historical witnessing and community responsibility?
  4. Whose experiences might be less visible in the Commission’s record?
Section 127.10

National Events, Community Hearings, and Public Education

The Commission organized seven national events between 2010 and 2014 and a closing event in Ottawa in 2015. They were held in Winnipeg (2010), Inuvik (2011), Halifax (2011), Saskatoon (2012), Montréal (2013), Vancouver (2013), and Edmonton (2014). Each lasted several days and combined survivor testimony, ceremony, cultural performance, exhibitions, educational programs, and sessions in which commissioners and others discussed the legacy of the schools. The regional choices brought the events to places with different Indigenous histories, and they included communities of the Arctic and the Atlantic provinces as well as the Prairies and the Pacific coast.

Alongside the national events, the Commission held community and regional hearings, statement-gathering sessions, and meetings in many places, including small and remote communities. These local events allowed people who could not travel to participate and reflected the fact that the effects of the schools were experienced at the community level. The Commission also carried out archival research, collected documents from government and church sources, and commissioned research on the history of the schools and their consequences. Exhibitions at the national events displayed photographs, records, and objects, and gave visitors a visual and material introduction to the system.

The events were also designed to educate non-Indigenous Canadians. The Commission invited the public, schools, churches, and community groups to attend, and encouraged teachers to bring students. Some events included large public gatherings; in September 2013, a Walk for Reconciliation in Vancouver drew a very large crowd, which organizers and media described as tens of thousands of people. Churches were present at several events, and the Commission also featured expressions of reconciliation, in which individuals and organizations were invited to make public commitments. A bentwood box, created by the Coast Salish artist Luke Marston, was used at events to receive objects and expressions of reconciliation from survivors and others.

The Commission’s public work changed relationships among survivors, state institutions, churches, journalists, educators, and the Canadian public in ways that are difficult to measure but significant. Media coverage of the events increased public knowledge. Educators began to include the schools in curricula. Church leaders heard testimony directly. At the same time, the process had limits and critics. Not all Canadians engaged, and the Commission was operating in a political context in which the federal government’s commitment to implementing its eventual recommendations was uncertain. Some Indigenous participants questioned whether public education directed to non-Indigenous audiences placed an unfair burden on survivors to relive their experiences for the benefit of others.

Section 127.11

The 2015 Final Report and the 94 Calls to Action

The Commission released its findings in two stages. At the end of May and the beginning of June 2015, it held its closing event in Ottawa and published an Executive Summary of its findings, Honouring the Truth, Reconciling for the Future, together with its 94 Calls to Action. In December 2015, it released its full final report, in six volumes. Students should keep the two dates distinct: the Calls to Action became public in June 2015, while the complete multi-volume report appeared in December 2015. The final report included detailed history of the system, a volume on the experience of survivors, a volume on missing children and unmarked burials, a volume on the legacy of the schools, and a volume on reconciliation.

The Commission’s principal historical conclusion was that the residential school system was part of a broader policy aimed at eliminating Indigenous peoples as distinct political, cultural, and social entities. It described this as “cultural genocide,” meaning the destruction of the structures and practices that allow a group to continue as a group. This term was not casually chosen, and its use generated discussion. Chief Justice Beverley McLachlin had used the same expression earlier in 2015. The Commission distinguished cultural genocide from physical genocide, the killing of a group’s members, and biological genocide, the prevention of births, and it chose a term that it believed fit the evidence it examined. Later bodies, including the Independent Special Interlocutor, would use stronger language, and debates over terminology continue among historians, legal scholars, and Indigenous leaders.

The 94 Calls to Action were organized in two major groups: calls addressing the legacy of the schools, and calls on reconciliation. The first group included calls concerning child welfare (Calls 1 to 5), education (6 to 12), language and culture (13 to 17), health (18 to 24), and justice (25 to 42). The second group included calls on the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, on the relationship between Indigenous peoples and the Crown, on the churches (58 to 61), on education for reconciliation, on museums and archives, on missing children and burial information (71 to 76), on the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation, on commemoration, on media, sport, business, and newcomers to Canada. The calls named different institutions, including federal, provincial, territorial, and municipal governments, churches, universities and schools, health bodies, law societies, and the public, and so asked many parts of Canadian society to act.

The Commission’s ten principles of reconciliation framed the calls. They emphasized that the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples provides a framework, that Indigenous peoples hold treaty and Aboriginal rights, that reconciliation requires political will and public support, and that it requires an understanding of the ongoing legacy of colonialism. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau’s government, elected in 2015, committed to implementing all 94 calls, and the commitment became a standard against which later progress would be measured. The implementation record is examined in Section 127.20.

For historians, the Final Report is both a source and an interpretation. As a source, it contains testimony, documents, and research assembled by the Commission. As an interpretation, it reflects the choices of the commissioners about how to frame and emphasize the evidence. It was produced within a specific mandate and with limited access to some records, and its conclusions on matters such as the number of deaths rest on incomplete documentation. These caveats do not diminish its significance, but they remind students to read it critically.

Section 127.12

The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation and the Struggle over Records

The Settlement Agreement required a permanent home for the Commission’s records. The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation (NCTR) was established in 2015 at the University of Manitoba in Winnipeg, with an Indigenous governing circle and a mandate to preserve the statements, documents, and other materials gathered by the Commission and to make them available for research, education, and commemoration. Its holdings include survivor statements, millions of pages of records from government and church sources, photographs, and other materials. The NCTR also took responsibility for continuing work that the Commission had begun, including research on missing children.

The struggle over records began before the NCTR existed. The Commission’s ability to write a complete history depended on documents held by Library and Archives Canada, federal departments, church archives, and other institutions. The Commission reported that it faced delays and obstacles in obtaining records, and it went to court on a number of occasions to compel production. In 2013, for example, an Ontario court ordered the federal government to provide the Commission with documents relating to the St. Anne’s school in Fort Albany, Ontario. Churches also held records in archives that were often dispersed across dioceses, religious orders, and missions, which made systematic access difficult. The Commission’s final report called for governments and church bodies to provide records, and for archives to cooperate with the NCTR.

A further dispute concerned the records of the Independent Assessment Process, which contain detailed accounts of abuse given by survivors in confidential hearings. Under the settlement terms, these records were to be destroyed after a fifteen-year retention period unless claimants consented to their transfer to the NCTR. Survivors and the Commission argued that these accounts were a vital part of the historical record. The government and some claimants argued that confidentiality required destruction. After litigation, courts held that claimants should have the choice to have their records archived, and in 2017 the Supreme Court of Canada upheld the approach that records would be destroyed unless claimants chose to preserve them. The case illustrates a basic tension between privacy and consent on one hand and historical preservation on the other, and it shows why survivors’ control over their own testimony was central to the ethics of the process.

Records matter not only to historians but to families and communities. For relatives searching for information about children who died at school, the location of a burial, the circumstances of a death, or the name of a staff member can depend on documents that are scattered, unindexed, or restricted. The NCTR and other bodies have worked to digitize, index, and make records more accessible, but access remains uneven, and privacy law and survivor consent shape what can be released. The Commission’s Call to Action 77, which asks provincial, territorial, and church archives to provide records to the NCTR, and Call 78, on funding for the Centre, reflect the importance the commissioners placed on these issues.

The NCTR’s role has expanded since 2015. It has continued to build a memorial register of children who died in residential schools, as discussed in Section 127.13, to support communities conducting searches, and to provide education and public programming. Its existence also shows an important change: records that were once held by the institutions responsible for the schools are now, in part, under Indigenous governance. But the underlying archival problems, including incomplete government and church holdings and restricted access to some materials, remain central to the investigations discussed in Division IV.

Missing Children, Unmarked Burials, and Renewed Investigations

The evidence for deaths and missing children, the development of community-led searches, and the renewed national reckoning after 2021.

Section 127.13

Children Who Died and the Historical Record of Missing Students

Survivors and communities had long spoken of children who died at the schools and did not return. Many families were never told what had happened to a child, or were informed only after a death or burial had occurred. These accounts were part of the testimony that the Commission heard, and the Commission treated the question of what had happened to children who died as part of its mandate. Through its Missing Children Project, it examined government and church records to identify children who had died, the causes of death, and the locations of burials. The Commission reported on this work in its final report and in a volume devoted to missing children and unmarked burials.

The Commission’s research documented more than 3,200 deaths, based on the records it was able to find, and it stressed that this figure was incomplete and that the actual number was almost certainly higher. The records showed that many deaths resulted from tuberculosis and other diseases that spread in crowded, poorly ventilated, and inadequately funded schools, as well as from other causes, including fires, accidents, and exposure by children who ran away. The records were often fragmentary. In the Commission’s findings, roughly a third of recorded deaths lacked the name of the child, and nearly half lacked a cause of death. Federal and church policies on reporting and recording deaths were inconsistent, and parents were often not notified. Children were commonly buried in cemeteries near the schools, and the costs of returning a body home were often considered a barrier.

Students should learn to distinguish several categories of evidence, as the chapter’s historical safeguards require. A documented death is one for which a record, such as a register entry, a death notice, or a burial record, survives. A missing child, in the Commission’s usage, is a child who was recorded as having attended the school and whose fate is not documented in the available records. A cemetery record shows that a burial ground existed, though it may not identify all those buried there. A suspected burial location is a place identified through testimony, mapping, or geophysical survey as possibly containing graves. Each category supports different conclusions, and none should be treated as equivalent to another. Importantly, the absence of a record does not show the absence of a death, and the absence of excavation does not show the absence of evidence.

The Commission’s Calls to Action 71 to 76 asked governments and churches to produce death records, to establish a national residential school death register, to maintain an online registry of residential school cemeteries, and to work with communities to identify, document, and commemorate burial sites. These calls responded to the gaps the Commission had found and to the fact that it lacked the funding and time to complete the research. The NCTR took on the work, and it has since continued to identify children from archival evidence. In 2025 it reported that it had added 1,143 newly verified names to its memorial register. These are newly documented identities, not newly occurring deaths: they reflect progress in research and in the recovery of records, and the process of verifying names continues.

The work on missing children illustrates the way that historical research in this field depends on a collaboration of archives, communities, and families. Researchers identify names in records, and communities and families provide information about who was lost and how they should be remembered. The task is not only to count but to restore identity: to return names to children who had been recorded as numbers or not at all. For many families, these identifications carry deep significance, and the work is conducted in accordance with the wishes of communities and relatives.

Section 127.14

Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc and the 2021 Kamloops Investigation

Case Study 4 · Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc and the Kamloops Investigation, 2021–2026

The former Kamloops Indian Residential School, near Kamloops in British Columbia, operated from 1890 to 1969 under the direction of the Roman Catholic Church. In 1969 the federal government took over administration of the school and operated it as a day school until it closed in 1978. At its peak it was one of the largest schools in the system. Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc, the Secwépemc community on whose reserve the school’s former site is located, undertook a project called Le Estcwicwéy̓ (“the missing”) to investigate what had happened to children who had not returned home. The project was informed by survivors’ knowledge: for years survivors had spoken of children who died or disappeared and of the places where they believed those children were buried.

On May 27, 2021, the community announced that a survey using ground-penetrating radar had identified what it described as the likely remains of 215 children on the grounds. The announcement drew immediate national and international attention, and it was widely understood as the point at which the public discussion of missing children changed from a subject within the Commission’s record to a matter of urgent national debate. The announcement also drew expressions of grief from many survivors and families, who saw in it a confirmation of what they had long known, and it prompted a wave of memorials, flags at half-mast, and public ceremonies.

The evidence needed to be described with care, and the community and the specialist who conducted the survey, Sarah Beaulieu, an anthropologist trained in ground-penetrating radar, did so in later statements and reports. Ground-penetrating radar detects disturbances in the soil that may be consistent with graves, but it cannot identify human remains, determine whether a disturbance is a grave, or establish identity. The initial public language, which spoke of 215 children, was subsequently clarified: the community and others described the survey as having identified reflections consistent with possible burials, and later reporting referred to roughly 200 potential burials in the areas surveyed. These possible burials had not been excavated. Students should therefore distinguish a geophysical indication consistent with a burial from a confirmed burial, and a confirmed burial from the identification of an individual. This distinction does not diminish the significance of the community’s findings, and the community has emphasized that its work rests on survivors’ testimony, archival research, and multiple lines of evidence in addition to radar.

Historical Vocabulary
Unmarked Graves, Burial-Site Evidence, and Historical Identification

Definition. An unmarked grave is a burial without a surviving marker identifying the person buried. Burial-site evidence takes several forms: archival records (registers, maps, correspondence), testimony (survivors’ and community members’ knowledge of where children were buried), possible burial indicators (such as soil disturbances detected by ground-penetrating radar), confirmed burial locations (established by excavation or other direct evidence), and the identification of individuals (matching remains or a burial to a named person through records, community knowledge, or forensic methods).

Historical context. The investigations that followed 2021 relied on these different forms of evidence, which vary in strength and in what they can establish. Communities decided which methods to use and whether to excavate, and some chose not to disturb sites.

Why it matters. The distinctions allow students to follow public debate accurately. They also help to explain how a community can regard its evidence as serious and compelling while describing its findings cautiously.

Potential misunderstanding. The absence of excavation does not mean the absence of evidence, and radar indications are not the same as confirmed graves. Treating either claim as proof of the other, in either direction, misrepresents the investigation.

The investigation continued after 2021 and became multidisciplinary, combining geophysical surveys, archival research, survivor interviews, and other methods. In February 2026, Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc issued an update describing overlapping indicators from several lines of evidence that were consistent with possible burials, narrowing its focus to a more precise set of possible locations and ruling out some areas, while acknowledging that some areas remained unresolved. The community also stated that possible future outcomes for the site included excavation or the preservation of the orchard as a sacred place of memory and healing, and it indicated that full consensus on the way forward might never be achieved. This chapter follows the community’s own reported findings, and avoids numerical claims about confirmed burials that the community has not made.

The announcement also produced controversy, which students should understand without treating it as evidence about the underlying history. Some public commentators and groups promoted denial of the evidence for deaths and burials, a position that Indigenous organizations and many historians have condemned, and that has been accompanied by harassment of communities. There were also public debates over funding and transparency in the use of government money for searches. The community and Indigenous leaders emphasized that searches are conducted according to community decisions and cultural protocols. The Kamloops case therefore demonstrates both the capacity of community-led investigation to open a national reckoning and the pressures that communities face when doing so.

Section 127.15

Community-Led Searches, Ground Surveys, and Burial-Site Protection

Kamloops was not the first Indigenous-led search, nor was it the last. In 2018, Muskowekwan First Nation in Saskatchewan reported that radar surveys at the site of the former Lestock Indian Residential School had identified dozens of probable unmarked graves. After May 2021, numerous other First Nations announced findings or began investigations. In June 2021, Cowessess First Nation in Saskatchewan reported hundreds of unmarked graves at a cemetery near the former Marieval school; the community emphasized that the cemetery had been used by both the community and the church, and that the graves were not all those of children who had attended the school. Penelakut Tribe in British Columbia, Williams Lake First Nation, Sagkeeng First Nation in Manitoba, and many others also reported surveys or findings. The details of these cases, and the numbers announced, vary, and students should consult each community’s own reports.

The methods used in these investigations are similar but not identical. Ground-penetrating radar sends radio waves into the ground and records reflections from changes in soil composition. It can reveal disturbances consistent with graves, but its results are affected by soil type, moisture, tree roots, and the depth and age of burials, and it must be interpreted by trained specialists. Researchers may also use LiDAR and aerial imagery to detect changes in the landscape, historical air photographs and maps to locate former buildings and cemeteries, and archival records to identify who may have been buried. Survivors’ and Elders’ testimony often guides where to look, and may be the most important evidence for where children are believed to be buried. Forensic archaeology, including excavation and analysis of remains, provides direct evidence but is invasive, and many communities have approached it cautiously.

Geography Study

Schools, Homelands, and Search Sites

Using a map of Canada that shows First Nations, Inuit, and Métis homelands and treaty areas as well as modern provincial and territorial boundaries, students should consider the following:

  • Residential schools were concentrated in the Prairie provinces, British Columbia, Ontario, and the northern territories, where many were established under the numbered treaties and in areas of expanding settlement and mission activity; schools also operated in Quebec, the Maritimes, and, separately, in Newfoundland and Labrador
  • Many children were taken far from their home communities, so that the site of a school and the homeland of the children who attended it were often hundreds or thousands of kilometres apart, and a burial at a school site may be that of a child from a distant nation
  • Inuit children were often sent to schools and hostels in distant communities in the Arctic and to southern institutions, and Inuit homelands (Inuit Nunangat) span several modern jurisdictions
  • The search sites discussed in this section, including Kamloops, Lestock, Marieval, Penelakut Island, Williams Lake, and Sagkeeng, lie in Secwépemc, Cree and other nations’ homelands, Coast Salish, and Anishinaabe territories, and decisions about the sites rest with the nations connected to them

Geography Analysis Questions:

  1. Why might a burial site at a school be connected to several different Indigenous nations, and how does this complicate questions of authority over investigation?
  2. How do modern provincial boundaries both help and hinder an understanding of the system’s geography and of burial-site laws?
  3. What does the distance between children’s home communities and school sites suggest about the way the system separated children from families?
  4. Why might the placement of schools near treaty areas and settlement frontiers matter for understanding the system’s purposes?

Questions of authority were central to these investigations. The communities leading searches emphasized that decisions about methods, timing, disclosure, and excavation belong to affected Indigenous nations, families, and survivors, and must follow cultural protocols, including ceremony and care for the dead. This position reflects both Indigenous legal traditions and the history of the schools themselves, in which outside institutions made decisions about Indigenous children without consent. It also raises practical issues. Provincial laws on burials, coroners, and archaeological sites were not designed for this situation, and communities have sometimes had to negotiate with governments, landowners, and churches to gain access to sites and records. Many sites are on land that is no longer under the control of the communities concerned.

Governments responded with funding programs for searches, expanded after 2021, and with discussion of legal protections for burial sites. The effectiveness of these measures was debated. Communities reported that funding processes could be slow, administratively burdensome, and not matched to the time needed for work that might take years or decades. Indigenous leaders also stressed that protection of sites would require legislation and cooperation, and not only grants. These questions fed into the work of the Independent Special Interlocutor, discussed in the next section, whose mandate included recommending legal frameworks for protection.

On archaeological methods and interpretive caution, compare the Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc update; findings at Cowessess, Sagkeeng, and Penelakut should continue to be identified by community and evidence type rather than aggregated as one forensic result.

Section 127.16

The Independent Special Interlocutor and the Search for Justice

Case Study 5 · Kimberly Murray’s Independent Special Interlocutor Report, 2024

On June 8, 2022, Canada appointed Kimberly Murray, a Mohawk lawyer and former executive director of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, as Independent Special Interlocutor for Missing Children and Unmarked Graves and Burial Sites associated with Indian Residential Schools. The appointment followed survivors’ and Indigenous communities’ demands for an Indigenous-led process to recommend protection of burial sites, respectful investigation, disclosure of records, and institutional accountability. Murray’s office consulted survivors, families, Indigenous nations, governments, churches, and other institutions. After interim work in 2022–2023, it released its two-volume final report on October 29, 2024: Upholding Sacred Obligations: Reparations for Missing and Disappeared Indigenous Children and Unmarked Burials in Canada. The office identified 42 institutional obligations within its proposed Indigenous-led reparations framework.

The final report developed a framework grounded in Indigenous laws and the principle that Indigenous nations hold authority over the care of their ancestors and the sites where they lie. It argued that Canada had obligations arising from its role in the schools to support searches and to protect burial sites, and that governments and churches had been slow to provide records and resources. It made recommendations concerning the protection of burial sites in law, the access of communities to records and land, the funding of searches, the role of Indigenous legal traditions, reparations, and the responsibilities of governments and churches. The report also addressed the denial of the evidence for missing children and unmarked burials, which it described as a harm to survivors and communities. It employed the language of genocide to characterize the residential school system, a stronger term than the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s “cultural genocide,” and one whose use continues to be debated.

The report’s significance lies partly in its method. Murray’s office treated Indigenous law and community authority as sources of legal and ethical obligation, and it framed accountability in terms that went beyond apology and compensation. It asked how Canada would honor its responsibilities to the missing children, and not only whether it would acknowledge them. In doing so, it connected the investigation of burial sites to broader questions of Indigenous self-determination and the recognition of Indigenous legal orders, themes examined in Chapters 117 and 118.

Students should distinguish between recommendations and implemented protections. The Interlocutor’s report, like the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s calls, is a set of recommendations, and its authority depends on the actions of governments and other institutions. At the time of writing in late 2026, investigations at many sites continue, legislative and policy responses are at various stages, and disagreements persist over the pace and adequacy of government action and the release of records by governments and churches. The report is nonetheless a key source for understanding Indigenous-led definitions of accountability, and it indicates how the reckoning shifted, after 2021, from acknowledging what had happened to asking what obligations follow from it.

The report also illustrates a continuity across the chapter: the movement from survivor testimony to institutional action. The Interlocutor’s mandate was created because survivors and communities pressed for it, her consultation relied on their testimony, and her recommendations reflect their demands. The responses of the federal government, the provinces, and the churches will determine whether those recommendations become enforceable commitments. That question leads to the final division of the chapter.

Division V · Sections 127.17–127.20

Responsibility, Reconciliation, and Unfinished Justice

Institutional accountability, public memory, and the continuing debate over what reconciliation should require.

Section 127.17

The Canadian State, Legal Responsibility, and the Limits of Settlement

The federal government held ultimate legal and political responsibility for the residential school system. It authorized the system under the Indian Act, funded it, set its policies, inspected its operation, and exercised extensive legal authority over Status Indian children. Churches operated most schools under agreements with the government, but they did so under federal supervision and for federal purposes. This allocation of responsibility is reflected in court decisions that held both the federal government and church bodies liable, and in the Settlement Agreement, in which Canada was the principal paying party. The distinction between the legal liability recognized by courts and the wider political and moral responsibility discussed by Indigenous leaders, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and the public is an important one. Legal liability is defined by statutes, precedents, and the claims actually brought; political and moral responsibility concern what a society owes in light of what it has learned.

The Settlement Agreement resolved many claims but left others unaddressed. Former students of day schools, who attended school during the day and returned home, were not covered by the agreement, and many had experienced abuse and cultural loss. Their claims were pursued through separate class actions. A settlement in the McLean class action, approved in 2019, provided compensation to former day school students, and a separate settlement approved in 2023, in the Gottfriedson band class action, sought to address the collective loss of language and culture suffered by First Nations as a result of residential and day schools; it was valued at approximately $2.8 billion. The Gottfriedson settlement is notable because it recognized harms to communities and not only to individuals, a point that the 2006 agreement had addressed only through healing and commemoration funds. Students of Newfoundland and Labrador schools, which were not part of the national agreement, also pursued separate recourse, and in 2017 the Prime Minister apologized to former students of those institutions, including Inuit, Innu, and Southern Inuit survivors.

Métis survivors presented a particular difficulty. Many Métis children attended residential schools, but only those who had attended a school listed in the agreement could claim, and the federal government’s historical relationship with Métis people differed from its relationship with First Nations under the Indian Act. Métis organizations argued that the agreement did not acknowledge Métis as a distinct collective and that many Métis survivors were left without remedy. These disputes show that the question of who counts as a survivor, and which institutions count as residential schools, was determined in part by legal and administrative categories that did not match the lived experience of those affected.

Records disputes also continued. The federal government had committed in the settlement to provide relevant documents to the Commission, and the Commission reported difficulties in obtaining them. After 2021, communities and the Interlocutor reported continuing difficulties in obtaining records needed to locate burial sites and identify children, including records held by the federal government, provincial governments, churches, and other bodies. Governments responded with measures to improve access, but the sufficiency of these measures remained contested. The question is not only about the past: records are the means by which communities can find their missing children, and the government’s control of archives is a continuing expression of unequal authority.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Silence to Acknowledgment to Obligation

What Changed

  • Public acknowledgment: from the 1990s, residential schooling moved from marginal subject to recognized national history, through testimony, litigation, apology, and the Commission
  • Legal recognition: courts recognized liability and a national settlement provided compensation and a truth process
  • Control of the record: survivors’ testimony and Indigenous-governed archives such as the NCTR became central, alongside government and church records
  • The terms of debate: after 2021, discussion shifted from whether children had died to who has authority over searches, records, and burial sites

What Remained Continuous

  • Survivors and Indigenous communities remained the principal initiators of each stage of the reckoning
  • Governments and churches continued to control substantial records and resources, and disputes over access persisted
  • Disparities in child welfare, education, health, and justice, which the Commission linked to the schools’ legacy, continued to be matters of concern
  • The question of Indigenous authority, who decides on matters affecting Indigenous children, ancestors, and communities, remained at the center of disputes

Pattern of change. Change was gradual, uneven, and contested, with acceleration at moments such as 1990, 2007–2008, 2015, and 2021. Survivors and Indigenous organizations experienced the changes most directly, and also experienced the greatest continuity in the persistence of inequality. Why the pattern matters: It shows that acknowledgment can advance while obligations remain unmet, and that the two should be evaluated separately.

For the legal distinction between the individual Day Scholars agreement and the later collective Band Class remedy, see the Government of Canada’s January 2023 Gottfriedson settlement backgrounder.

Section 127.18

Church Responsibility, Apologies, Financial Commitments, and Archival Accountability

The churches’ roles differed, and any assessment of their responses must begin there. The Roman Catholic Church operated a large share of residential schools, according to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, through dioceses and religious orders, including the Oblates of Mary Immaculate and several orders of sisters, which were separate legal entities. Because the Catholic Church is not governed in Canada as a single legal body, the Settlement Agreement was signed by a number of Catholic entities, which negotiated separately and held records in different archives. The Anglican Church of Canada, the United Church of Canada, and the Presbyterian Church in Canada each operated schools, in the United Church’s case as heir to the earlier Methodist and much of the Presbyterian mission. These differences affected what each body could promise, what records it held, and how it responded.

Church apologies began before the national settlement. The United Church issued an apology to First Nations in 1986 and a specific apology concerning residential schools in 1998. The Oblates apologized in 1991, the Anglican Church of Canada in 1993, and the Presbyterian Church in Canada in 1994. These statements varied in scope and in the acknowledgment of institutional responsibility, and survivors’ responses were also varied: some found them meaningful, others considered them incomplete without financial and archival commitments. The Roman Catholic Church’s position was more complicated. Individual Catholic bodies, such as the Oblates, offered apologies, and in 2009 Pope Benedict XVI met a delegation led by Phil Fontaine and expressed sorrow, but no apology by the Pope was delivered in Canada. The Commission’s Call to Action 58 asked for one, and survivors and Indigenous leaders continued to press the matter.

Financial commitments under the Settlement Agreement differed across church entities and over time. In addition to cash and in-kind obligations, Catholic entities undertook a “best efforts” campaign with a $25 million fundraising goal; church-published accounts report that this campaign raised approximately $3.7 million. Those figures describe one particular settlement-related undertaking, not the total value of all Catholic financial contributions. Following renewed public scrutiny in 2021, the Canadian Conference of Catholic Bishops announced a separate $30 million commitment over five years for Indigenous healing and reconciliation, subsequently administered through the Indigenous Reconciliation Fund. The two campaigns should not be merged into a single pledge or presented as if the later initiative erased criticism of the earlier shortfall. Survivors and Indigenous leaders continued to contest whether the contributions, their administration, and access to church records amounted to adequate accountability.

Records were a second area of accountability. Churches held registers, correspondence, photographs, financial documents, and other materials that could help locate children and burial sites. After 2021, survivors and Indigenous leaders called for the full release of these records, and some church bodies agreed to provide access, with the Oblates and others making commitments to transfer materials to the NCTR. Indigenous leaders and the Interlocutor continued to raise concerns that access was incomplete and that records remained in archives in Canada and abroad, including in Rome. Students should treat these claims as part of an ongoing discussion, and should consult current reports, since the situation has been changing.

Pope Francis’s apology responded to years of pressure. In March and April 2022, delegations of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis survivors and leaders traveled to the Vatican, where the Pope expressed sorrow and asked forgiveness. On July 25, 2022, at Maskwacîs, Alberta, the site of a former residential school, he apologized in person, expressing deep regret for the harm done by many members of the church, including their participation in policies of cultural destruction, and then visited Edmonton, Quebec City, and Iqaluit. Many survivors welcomed the apology as a significant and moving acknowledgment. Others criticized its framing: some observed that it spoke of the actions of members of the church rather than of the institution, that it did not address sexual abuse explicitly, and that it did not by itself deliver the records, reparations, and justice that survivors sought. The Vatican also issued a statement in 2023 repudiating the Doctrine of Discovery, a response to Calls to Action 47 and 49 and to Indigenous demands. Whether the apology and these steps constituted adequate accountability continues to be debated among Indigenous leaders and Catholics.

Multiple Perspectives

Apologies and What Should Follow

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical syntheses of positions that survivors, Indigenous leaders, officials, and church members have publicly expressed. They are not quotations from individuals, and no group is unanimous.

Survivors who welcomed the apologies: Many survivors described the 2008 federal apology and the 2022 papal apology as moments of recognition they had waited decades for, which affirmed that their accounts had been believed and which helped some families to begin conversations.
Survivors and Indigenous leaders who stressed what was missing: Others argued that apologies without records, resources, and structural change were incomplete, noting that the 2008 apology did not cover all survivors and that the papal apology did not address specific harms, such as sexual abuse, in the terms they sought.
Governments and church officials: Officials presented apologies as the beginning of a longer process, and pointed to settlement payments, funding for searches, records agreements, and programs, while acknowledging that implementation had been slow, that jurisdiction was divided, and that more was required.
Métis, Inuit, and excluded survivors: Representatives of groups left out of the national agreement or the 2008 apology emphasized that acknowledgment had arrived unevenly, and pressed for recognition of the specific experiences of Métis children and of Inuit and Newfoundland and Labrador students.
Legal scholars and historians: Scholars have noted that apologies can be sincere and consequential yet remain symbolic if they do not change legal obligations, and have debated whether terms such as “cultural genocide” or “genocide” best describe the evidence, with different assessments of what each term implies.

Note on power: These groups did not hold equal power. Governments and churches controlled records, funding, and legal responsibility; survivors and Indigenous communities held the testimony and the moral and political claim. The purpose of this feature is to show complexity, not equivalence.

Section 127.19

Orange Shirt Day, Public Memory, and National Commemoration

Case Study 6 · Phyllis Webstad and Orange Shirt Day, 2013–Present

Orange Shirt Day began with one survivor’s account. Phyllis Webstad, a member of the Stswecem’c Xgat’tem First Nation (Northern Secwépemc), told how in 1973, at the age of six, she was sent to St. Joseph’s Mission residential school near Williams Lake, British Columbia, wearing a new orange shirt that her grandmother had bought for her. On arrival, the shirt was taken away and not returned. She has described the experience as symbolizing the loss of her identity and the sense that she did not matter. She shared her account at a commemoration event for the St. Joseph Mission school held in Williams Lake in the spring of 2013, and in September 2013 the event’s organizers held the first Orange Shirt Day, selecting September 30 because it was the time of year when children had been taken from their homes to the schools.

The initiative spread. Schools, communities, and organizations began to mark September 30 by wearing orange shirts, hosting events, and teaching about the schools, with the phrase “Every Child Matters” becoming widely associated with the day. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Call to Action 80 asked the federal government to establish a statutory holiday, in consultation with Indigenous peoples, to honor survivors, families, and communities and to ensure that public commemoration of the history and legacy of residential schools remains a vital component of reconciliation. After the Kamloops announcement, the federal government moved quickly: legislation creating a National Day for Truth and Reconciliation received Royal Assent on June 3, 2021, and the first federal observance took place on September 30, 2021.

The day has been significant in several ways. It created a regular occasion for public education and ceremony, and it gave schools and workplaces a means to engage with the subject. It also illustrated the relationship between individual testimony and national memory: a single survivor’s account became a symbol with national reach. Survivors, Elders, and Indigenous organizations took part in commemorations, and many communities held ceremonies, walks, and gatherings. The day has been observed unevenly across jurisdictions, since the federal statutory holiday applies to federal workplaces and provinces and territories have adopted different practices.

Commemoration has also raised questions. Some Indigenous people and organizations have cautioned that remembrance can become symbolic, a matter of wearing a shirt or attending an event, if it is not accompanied by action on the Calls to Action, on child welfare, education, and justice, or on the searches for missing children. Others have noted the commercialization of orange shirts and the risk that the day reduces a complicated history to a slogan. Phyllis Webstad and the Orange Shirt Society have encouraged that the day be a time for learning and for supporting survivors, and have connected it to Indigenous-led education. These debates echo the larger question of this chapter: whether public acknowledgment translates into institutional change.

Orange Shirt Day and the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation are discussed here as part of the residential-school reckoning. The broader history of monuments, museums, memorials, and public memory across the continent belongs to Chapter 132. What is important here is that the day grew out of survivor testimony and community commemoration, and was then adopted by the state, reversing the usual direction in which official memory shapes public understanding.

Documentary access: Orange Shirt Society’s history of Orange Shirt Day; federal information on the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation is available through the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Call 80 (NCTR).

Section 127.20

Reconciliation, Indigenous Justice, and the Unfinished Reckoning

How much of the Commission’s agenda has been carried out depends on who is counting and how. The federal government reports that more than 85 percent of the 76 Calls to Action that call for federal leadership, alone or with others, have been completed or are well underway. The Assembly of First Nations reported in September 2025 that about thirteen of the 94 calls had been fully completed in the ten years since 2015, and independent trackers have likewise found many calls not started or stalled. These figures are not necessarily contradictory: they use different definitions of completion, different baselines, and different judgments about whether a legal or procedural step meets the intent of a call. Some calls described as complete are criticized for falling short of what the Commission intended, and the federal government is responsible for only some of the calls, with provinces, territories, churches, institutions, and others responsible for the rest.

Some of the most significant developments concern structures of accountability. The Commission’s Calls 53 to 56 called for an independent National Council for Reconciliation to monitor and report on progress. After an interim and transitional process, the National Council for Reconciliation Act received Royal Assent on April 30, 2024, and came into force on July 2, 2024. The Council entered its institutional establishment phase in 2025 under an inaugural board; in September 2026 it announced its first permanent board of directors. Its 2026 inaugural report—listed by the Council under June 30, 2026, although the online report page carries a later posting date—recognized increased federal activity while warning that fundamental inequities and barriers remained. It called for sustained changes and better accountability rather than equating program activity with achieved reconciliation. Parliament also passed legislation in 2021 concerning implementation of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, a broader legal framework examined in Chapter 143. Whether these structures bring material changes remains a matter for continuing historical evaluation.

The reckoning has also extended across areas covered in neighboring chapters. The Commission’s educational calls influenced curriculum changes and the growth of Indigenous education initiatives (Chapter 124). Its language calls intersect with Indigenous language revitalization (Chapter 123). Child-welfare reform, a theme of the first Calls to Action, is examined in Chapter 131. Justice, policing, and the disproportionate involvement of Indigenous people in the criminal justice system are treated in Chapter 130, and the national inquiry into missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls in Chapter 129. Care for ancestors and burial sites relates to the repatriation themes of Chapter 125, though under Canadian frameworks. The residential-school reckoning is therefore one thread of a wider movement for justice and Indigenous authority, and its legacy is visible in each of these fields.

Developments through 2026 show that the reckoning remains open. Searches for missing children continue at many sites, including Kamloops, where the community’s February 2026 update described a narrowing but unfinished investigation. The NCTR continues to add names to its memorial register. Records disputes with governments and churches continue. Denial of the evidence persists in some public discourse, and Indigenous organizations have condemned it. Survivors continue to age and to die, a fact that gives urgency to the work of recording their testimony and ensuring that their experiences inform what follows. The reckoning is therefore not complete, and students should be wary of any account that presents it as finished.

Finally, the chapter’s organizing distinction can be stated more fully. Acknowledgment, the recognition of historical truth by governments, churches, and the public, has advanced significantly since 1990. Compensation, the payment of money for individual harm, has been provided through the Settlement Agreement and later settlements, though with exclusions. Institutional reform, the change of systems in child welfare, education, justice, health, and archives, has been partial and contested. Reparations, in the broader sense of repair that includes records, burial-site protection, land, and community-directed recovery, has been recommended more than implemented. And Indigenous self-determination, the authority of Indigenous nations to decide matters affecting their children, ancestors, and communities, is both an aim of the reckoning and the condition on which many Indigenous leaders believe its success depends. The history traced in this chapter is the history of how the first of these was won, and of the continuing struggle over the others.

Historical Debate

What Constitutes Meaningful Reconciliation When Acknowledgment Has Not Ended Inequality?

The question: Canada’s governments and several churches have acknowledged the history of residential schooling through apologies, settlements, and a national commission. Substantial inequalities and unresolved claims remain. What should count as meaningful reconciliation in these circumstances, and by what standard should progress be judged?

Interpretation A — Reconciliation as a process of institutional implementation: Some governments, officials, and commentators emphasize that reconciliation is a long-term process, and they point to measurable steps: settlements, funding for searches, legislation such as the National Council for Reconciliation Act, a federal day of commemoration, and progress on many Calls to Action. They argue that change takes time and that progress should be recognized while work continues.
Interpretation B — Reconciliation requires structural change and accountability: Many survivors, Indigenous leaders, and organizations stress that acknowledgment is not enough without implementation, and they point to the small number of calls reported complete by some trackers, unresolved disputes over records, continuing disparities, and the weakness of enforceable protections. They argue that reconciliation should be measured by changes in outcomes and in the distribution of authority and resources.
Interpretation C — Questioning the framework of reconciliation itself: Some Indigenous scholars, such as Glen Coulthard in Red Skin, White Masks, argue that a politics of recognition and reconciliation can reproduce colonial relations if it asks Indigenous peoples to accept state-defined forms of acknowledgment instead of changing the underlying relationship. Others emphasize Indigenous resurgence and the rebuilding of Indigenous legal orders and governance as the foundation of justice, with reconciliation secondary to or dependent on self-determination.

Evidence: Participants cite different evidence: the text and implementation of settlements and legislation; the federal government’s progress reporting; independent trackers and the Assembly of First Nations; the reports of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the Interlocutor, and the National Council for Reconciliation; and survivors’ and communities’ own assessments. Positions are not unanimous within any group, and individuals may hold elements of more than one view.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and standards of judgment more than basic facts. It involves how to define “completion” of a call, whether to emphasize process or outcomes, how to weigh symbolic acts against material change, and whether reconciliation is the right goal at all.

Why it matters: How historians and the public judge the reckoning affects what is demanded next. If acknowledgment is treated as the end of the process, pressure for implementation weakens; if reconciliation is treated as impossible without full structural transformation, incremental achievements may be overlooked.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Canada’s residential-school reckoning was not simply a government decision to acknowledge past wrongdoing. It was produced by decades of survivor testimony, Indigenous organizing, legal action, and demands for accountability. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis survivors, their families, and Indigenous organizations compelled Canada and the churches that had operated the schools to confront the system’s history and consequences. The result transformed public understanding of Canadian history while leaving significant questions of justice, responsibility, and Indigenous authority unresolved.

Division I traced the emergence of the reckoning. The schools’ legacy was carried within Indigenous families and communities, while official narratives, shaped by government and church records, long marginalized survivors’ accounts. Survivors began to speak and organize publicly in the 1980s and 1990s. Phil Fontaine’s CBC interview of October 30, 1990, was a prominent national disclosure that built on earlier testimony and on the work of survivor networks and Indigenous organizations. Litigation, including Blackwater v. Plint, and the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples brought institutional liability and the wider colonial relationship into view. The 1998 Statement of Reconciliation and the Aboriginal Healing Foundation were the first federal responses, welcomed by some and judged insufficient by others.

Division II examined the legal and political transformation that followed. The Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement, reached in 2006 and implemented from September 19, 2007, combined a Common Experience Payment, an Independent Assessment Process, commemoration and healing funding, and a truth commission. It resolved many claims but excluded some groups, including most day-school students and Métis survivors not attending listed institutions, and it could not address harms that money does not repair. Prime Minister Stephen Harper’s apology of June 11, 2008, was an acknowledgment that the assimilation policy was wrong, and it was received with a range of responses. It was a statement and not an enforceable commitment. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, troubled in its first year, was reconstituted in 2009 under Justice Murray Sinclair, Marie Wilson, and Chief Wilton Littlechild.

Division III followed the Commission’s work from 2008 to 2015. It gathered statements from thousands of witnesses through public and private processes, held seven national events and a closing event, and worked to educate the public. Its testimony-gathering followed Indigenous protocols and gave survivors choices about disclosure. In 2015 it released its 94 Calls to Action in June and its six-volume final report in December, characterizing the system as cultural genocide. The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation was established to preserve the record. Struggles over records, including disputes over documents and the fate of Independent Assessment Process files, showed that access to evidence was itself a matter of power and consent.

Division IV turned to missing children and unmarked burials. The Commission documented more than 3,200 deaths from incomplete records and stressed that the real number was higher; roughly a third of recorded deaths lacked a name. The NCTR’s continuing work, including 1,143 newly verified names added to its memorial register in 2025, documents identities that were previously unrecorded, not new deaths. Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc’s May 2021 announcement brought national attention, and later clarification made the evidence’s nature clear: radar indications consistent with possible burials, not excavated or identified remains. The community’s February 2026 update described overlapping indicators and a narrowing focus while acknowledging unresolved areas. Other First Nations conducted their own searches, and Kimberly Murray’s October 2024 final report, Upholding Sacred Obligations, framed accountability through Indigenous law, burial-site protection, records, and reparations.

The evidence base of this history is varied. Survivor testimony, collected in courts, the Commission’s process, and community settings, is historical evidence in its own right. Government and church records are essential but incomplete, shaped by the institutions that produced them. Court decisions and settlements show which claims were legally recognized. Geophysical surveys and archival research contribute to the search for burials but answer limited questions. Historians must therefore compare kinds of evidence, ask whose perspective each represents, and distinguish documented deaths, unresolved cases, possible burials, and identified individuals.

Division V examined responsibility and the continuing debate. The federal government held legal and political responsibility for the system; churches operated most schools and differed in their roles, apologies, financial commitments, and records. The Settlement Agreement’s exclusions led to separate settlements for day-school students and for collective losses of language and culture, and to the 2017 apology to Newfoundland and Labrador survivors. Pope Francis’s 2022 apology at Maskwacîs was welcomed by many survivors and criticized by others as incomplete. Orange Shirt Day, begun in 2013 by Phyllis Webstad’s account, became a federal National Day for Truth and Reconciliation in 2021, reversing the usual direction of official memory.

The chapter has shown continuity as well as change. Survivors and Indigenous organizations initiated each stage of the reckoning, governments and churches continued to hold records and resources, and disparities that the Commission linked to the schools’ legacy persisted. Change came in bursts in 1990, 2007–2008, 2015, and 2021, and it was uneven in its effects on different groups. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis survivors experienced it differently, with consequences for who was included and who was not.

By 2026, the National Council for Reconciliation had been created under legislation that received Royal Assent on April 30, 2024, and its first board named in 2025. The federal government reports that most federally led calls are complete or well underway, while the Assembly of First Nations and independent trackers report few complete, a disagreement rooted in differing definitions. The chapter’s governing distinction, between acknowledging the historical truth and fulfilling the obligations that follow from it, helps explain this. Acknowledgment has advanced substantially. Compensation has been provided with exclusions. Institutional reform, reparations, and Indigenous self-determination remain contested and, in important respects, unfinished.

Key Terms

Survivor
A person who attended a residential school, the term preferred by many former students and used throughout this chapter; also used in related contexts for those affected by the system.
Residential school
One of the church-operated, federally supported institutions that housed Indigenous children away from their families, treated in Chapters 87 and 88.
Intergenerational effects
Documented consequences of residential schooling for the children, grandchildren, and communities of survivors, including loss of language and disrupted family relationships; not a uniform outcome for every family.
Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples
A national inquiry (1991–1996) that examined the whole relationship between Indigenous peoples and Canada and gave residential schools extended attention.
Statement of Reconciliation
The January 1998 federal statement, part of Gathering Strength, that expressed regret for residential-school abuse but was not an apology in Parliament.
Aboriginal Healing Foundation
An Indigenous-led organization funded from 1998 to support community healing from the legacy of residential schools.
Class action
A lawsuit brought on behalf of a group with similar claims, used in several residential-school cases and as the court framework for the Settlement Agreement.
Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement
The 2006 agreement, implemented in 2007, among Canada, survivors’ representatives, the Assembly of First Nations, Inuit representatives, and churches, creating compensation processes and a truth commission.
Common Experience Payment
A payment to eligible former students based on years of attendance at a listed school, without proof of specific abuse.
Independent Assessment Process
A confidential claims process under the settlement for sexual abuse, serious physical abuse, and other wrongful acts, decided by adjudicators.
Apology
A public acknowledgment of wrong and expression of responsibility; distinct from a legal settlement or an enforceable commitment.
Truth commission
A body that collects testimony, examines records, and issues findings and recommendations, without the powers of a court.
Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada
The commission created under the Settlement Agreement, which operated from 2008 to 2015 and was led from 2009 by Murray Sinclair, Marie Wilson, and Wilton Littlechild.
Statement gathering
The Commission’s process of receiving testimony in public, semi-public, and private settings, with participants choosing how it would be used.
Calls to Action
The Commission’s 94 recommendations, released in June 2015, addressed to governments, churches, institutions, and the public.
Cultural genocide
The Commission’s term for the destruction of structures and practices that allow a group to continue as a group; distinguished by the Commission from physical and biological genocide and debated by scholars.
National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation
The archive and research centre at the University of Manitoba, established in 2015, that holds the Commission’s records and continues work on missing children.
Missing Children Project
The Commission’s research into children who died or disappeared while in the residential school system, whose work the NCTR continues.
Documented death
A death for which a record, such as a register entry or burial record, survives; distinct from a missing child whose fate is not recorded.
Ground-penetrating radar
A geophysical method that detects soil disturbances; it can indicate possible burials but cannot identify human remains or individuals.
Unmarked burial
A grave without a surviving marker; in this chapter, a category distinguished from a possible burial indicator and from an identified individual.
Le Estcwicwéy̓ (the Missing)
The name of Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc’s project investigating children who did not return from the Kamloops Indian Residential School.
Independent Special Interlocutor
The federally appointed Indigenous-led office (Kimberly Murray, from 2022) that examined unmarked burials and recommended legal protections, issuing a final report in October 2024.
Denialism
The rejection or minimization of the evidence for deaths and burials associated with residential schools, condemned by Indigenous organizations and many historians.
Doctrine of Discovery
A set of European legal and religious ideas used to justify claims over Indigenous lands, whose repudiation was sought by Calls to Action 47 to 49 and addressed by the Vatican in 2023.
Orange Shirt Day
A commemoration begun in 2013 on September 30, arising from Phyllis Webstad’s account; adopted federally as the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation in 2021.
Reparations
Measures of repair broader than compensation, which may include records access, institutional reform, memorialization, and community-directed recovery.
National Council for Reconciliation
An Indigenous-led body created under 2024 legislation to monitor and report on progress toward reconciliation.
Indigenous self-determination
The authority of Indigenous nations to make decisions about their own affairs, including children, ancestors, and territory; examined in Chapters 114, 117, and 118.

  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, present an evidence-based account of your chosen stage, explain how the evidence shapes the account, and use careful language, such as “the evidence indicates” or “this remains debated,” in place of overstatement.

Library resources to use: NCTR publications, Government of Canada records and reports, reports of the Independent Special Interlocutor and the National Council for Reconciliation, community publications from nations conducting searches, and the MGU History Library’s reference materials on Indigenous historiography and oral history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter opens Part XXII of the textbook by showing that the acknowledgment of a hard history can itself be a historical achievement, and one that was not given but won. Survivors told what had happened, sometimes at great personal cost, and through litigation, organizing, testimony, and community-led investigation they changed what governments, churches, and the Canadian public could claim not to know. This is an example of the agency that the textbook emphasizes: Indigenous peoples not only experienced the policies of settler states but, over generations, forced those states to answer for them.

The chapter also reveals how acknowledgment and obligation can diverge. An apology, a settlement, a commission’s report, and an independent investigation are different acts by different institutions, and none automatically satisfies the others. The continuing debates over records, burial-site protection, child welfare, education, and the pace of implementation show that the central question of the reckoning is how much follows from what has been acknowledged. Indigenous nations’ claims to authority over their children, ancestors, and communities connect the reckoning to the themes of sovereignty and self-determination that run through Volume X.

The reckoning shaped, and continues to shape, developments in neighboring fields. It informed language and education initiatives, the reform of child-welfare systems, debates about justice and policing, practices of commemoration, and the care of ancestors. It also connects to the investigations in the United States that are the subject of Chapter 128, where the histories of boarding schools differ in law and administration but share a pattern of survivors and communities pressing for truth. Comparison across the border, handled with care for the differences, can illuminate both.

Finally, the chapter leaves students with a task that history alone cannot complete. The evidence assembled here is substantial, but it is also unfinished: names are still being recovered, sites are still being investigated, and records are still being sought. Those engaged with this history, whether as students, teachers, or citizens, should read the evidence carefully, respect the authority of the survivors and communities whose testimony and decisions are at its heart, and understand that the story is not over. As Part XXII continues, later chapters on boarding-school investigations, missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls, child welfare, and historical memory will extend these questions of justice, memory, and reconciliation.
