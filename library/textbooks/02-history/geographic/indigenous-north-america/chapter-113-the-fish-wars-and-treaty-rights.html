Chapter Opening

In the 1850s, Indigenous nations of the Pacific Northwest signed treaties that surrendered millions of acres but kept one thing they refused to give up: the right to take fish. For more than a century afterward, the question was whether the United States would honor that promise.

Every autumn and, in some rivers, in other seasons, salmon return from the Pacific to the streams of their birth. For the peoples of Puget Sound, the Olympic Peninsula, the Washington coast, and the Columbia River basin, those returns were the foundation of food supply, trade, ceremony, and community life long before any treaty council was held. When Indigenous leaders negotiated with United States officials in the 1850s, the right to continue fishing was not a minor clause attached to a land sale. It was among the conditions that made the agreements acceptable at all.

This chapter follows what happened to that promise. The treaties remained legally binding, yet over the following century the practical ability of Indigenous people to fish was steadily narrowed by canneries, fish traps, dams, private landownership, and state regulation. Tribal fishermen who fished at places their families had used for generations were arrested, had their nets and boats seized, and were prosecuted in state courts. By the 1960s, in what newspapers and participants came to call the Fish Wars, the conflict had become one of the most visible struggles over treaty enforcement in the twentieth-century United States.

The Fish Wars were not simply protests over access to salmon. They were a struggle over three connected questions: whether treaties made with Indigenous nations were enforceable law, whether tribal governments had authority over their members' fishing and over the resource itself, and whether state governments could regulate treaty fishing out of existence in the name of conservation. Indigenous fishermen, women organizers, tribal councils, and intertribal coalitions answered those questions through direct action, in the form of the fish-ins, and through sustained litigation. Neither approach alone produced the outcome; the history shows them working together.

The central legal turning point came on February 12, 1974, when federal District Judge George H. Boldt issued his ruling in United States v. Washington, holding that the treaty tribes of western Washington were entitled to an equitable share of the harvestable fish. The Supreme Court largely confirmed that framework in 1979. These decisions are often summarized as granting tribes half the fish. As this chapter explains, that shorthand is misleading in several respects, and understanding the distinctions among recognition of a right, access to fishing sites, allocation of a harvest, permissible conservation regulation, and actual enforcement is a principal task of the chapter.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. It is not only a biography of Billy Frank Jr., the Nisqually fisherman whose repeated arrests became emblematic, nor only a study of Judge Boldt, whose decision reshaped the fishery. Both are indispensable, but the movement was sustained by families at river camps, tribal governments, lawyers and expert witnesses, women who organized and fished, and nations from Puget Sound to the Columbia Plateau. Salmon were at once food, income, ceremonial presence, and the subject of a dense regulatory system, and the legal conflict cannot be understood apart from those dimensions.

Historians draw on treaty texts and the records of the treaty councils, court opinions and trial records, state fisheries and game department records, newspapers, tribal government documents, oral history and memoirs, anthropological and historical expert reports prepared for the Boldt litigation, and fisheries science. Each carries limits that this chapter notes: treaty councils were conducted largely through Chinook Jargon and recorded by officials; court records preserve legal argument more fully than lived experience; and enforcement records reflect the view of the agencies that made arrests.

The chapter belongs to Volume X's account of the sovereignty era. It connects to the Red Power developments of Chapters 108 through 112, to the broader treaty-rights history of Chapter 106, and to the institutional consequences taken up in Chapter 114. It does not retell those chapters. Its responsibility is the specific campaign, in the Pacific Northwest between the 1950s and the early 1980s, through which treaty fishing rights were defended, adjudicated, and institutionalized.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the importance of salmon and other fisheries to the economies, ceremonies, and social organization of Pacific Northwest Indigenous nations.
  2. Describe the treaty negotiations of the 1850s, including the unequal conditions under which they were conducted and the fishing-rights provisions they contain.
  3. Distinguish among treaty recognition, access to fishing sites, allocation of a harvest, conservation regulation, and enforcement as separate legal questions.
  4. Explain the principle that treaty fishing rights were reserved by tribes rather than granted to them, as articulated in United States v. Winans.
  5. Analyze how commercial development, dams, and state regulation eroded the practical exercise of treaty rights despite their continued legal force.
  6. Trace the development of the fish-ins and explain how direct action and litigation reinforced one another.
  7. Assess the roles of Billy Frank Jr., Ramona Bennett, Hank Adams, Janet McCloud, and other organizers, and of families and tribal governments, in the movement.
  8. Interpret the Puyallup cases and Sohappy v. Smith and explain how they shaped the legal framework later applied in United States v. Washington.
  9. Evaluate the Boldt Decision and the 1979 Supreme Court ruling, including what the fifty-percent allocation does and does not mean.
  10. Explain why legal victory did not immediately end resistance, and how enforcement and co-management emerged.
  11. Assess the achievements and limits of the Fish Wars, including the continuing problem of declining salmon populations.

Historical Context

The Pacific Northwest's fisheries supported some of the densest non-agricultural Indigenous populations in North America, a subject treated in Chapters 10 and 33. Readers of this chapter need only keep in mind that salmon economies were old, productive, and managed through detailed systems of local knowledge, family rights to particular sites, seasonal movement, and ceremony. When the United States organized Washington Territory in 1853, these systems were intact and central to Indigenous life.

The treaties of the 1850s were part of a wider program of Indigenous land cession and reservation, discussed in Chapter 72 together with the wars that accompanied and followed them. The legal framework that governs this chapter is older still. In the early nineteenth century, United States courts developed the principle that Indigenous nations retained inherent sovereignty not explicitly surrendered, and that the federal government held special obligations toward them. Under the Constitution, treaties are part of the supreme law of the land. That legal position is the basis on which tribes later argued that state fishing laws could not override treaty provisions.

The mid-twentieth century added new pressures. Federal Indian policy in the 1950s shifted toward termination and relocation, examined in Chapters 104 and 105. In 1953 Congress enacted Public Law 280, and Washington later assumed partial state jurisdiction over Indian country within the state. Postwar growth in sport fishing, industrial development along rivers, hydroelectric construction, and urban expansion around Puget Sound intensified competition for a resource that was already under pressure. At the same time, the civil rights movement provided a model of direct action, and the Red Power developments described in Chapters 108 through 112 created a wider political setting in which treaty rights could be asserted publicly.

Finally, the state of Washington had developed its own regulatory structure, with separate agencies responsible for food fish and for game fish. This division mattered: salmon fell under one agency, steelhead (designated a game fish in the early twentieth century and prized by sport anglers) under the other. The administrative organization of the state's fisheries shaped who was arrested, for what, and in which legal conflicts the Fish Wars were fought.

Division I · Section 113.1

Salmon and the Indigenous Peoples of the Pacific Northwest

Five species of Pacific salmon (chinook, coho, chum, pink, and sockeye), together with steelhead, smelt, halibut, and shellfish, supported the economies of the peoples who became parties to the treaties examined in this chapter. In Puget Sound and the rivers draining the Olympic Peninsula and the Cascade foothills, these included the Nisqually, Puyallup, Squaxin Island, Skokomish, Muckleshoot, Suquamish, Tulalip, Lummi, Swinomish, Upper Skagit, Quinault, Hoh, Quileute, and Makah peoples. On the Columbia and its tributaries they included the Yakama, Umatilla, Cayuse, Walla Walla, Warm Springs, and Nez Perce. Each had its own language, political organization, and relationship to particular waters, and the shorthand "Northwest Indians" conceals that diversity, just as Chapter 1 cautioned against generic labels.

Fishing technologies were varied and place-specific. Weirs and traps were built across rivers and creeks. Dip nets and spears were used from platforms and canoes at rapids and falls, as at Celilo Falls on the Columbia. Straits Salish peoples used reef nets, a sophisticated technology that depended on family rights to particular locations in the salt water. Gillnets, first of native fiber and later of commercial twine, became common in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and are the form of fishing most often at issue in the conflicts below. Knowledge of runs, tides, and river conditions was transmitted within families and communities.

Salmon were also a basis of exchange and ceremony. Smoked and dried fish traveled along trade routes linking coast and interior, and sites such as Celilo Falls and The Dalles drew people from great distances for fishing and trade. Many communities observed first-salmon ceremonies, which marked the return of the fish with ritual treatment and shared meals; practices differed from nation to nation, and it would be inaccurate to describe a single "salmon religion." Rights to fish at particular places were generally held by families or villages and could be inherited, shared, or granted, and social standing was often tied to the management and redistribution of the catch. Archaeological evidence for fishing in the region is very old, but the precise antiquity and scale of particular fisheries remain subjects of research.

The point of this background is legal as well as cultural. When treaty negotiators later spoke of "usual and accustomed" fishing places, they were referring to an existing system of ownership, use, and obligation, not to an abstract privilege. Understanding fishing as a working economy, a body of knowledge, and a form of governance explains why tribes regarded its loss as a threat to their continuing existence as peoples, and why they resisted the idea that it could be reduced to a recreational "right to fish."

Division I · Section 113.2

The Treaties of the 1850s

Isaac Stevens arrived in Olympia in late 1853 as Washington Territory's first governor and as its superintendent of Indian affairs, charged with extinguishing Indigenous land claims to open the territory to settlement and with planning a northern railroad route. In December 1854 he convened the council that produced the Treaty of Medicine Creek with the Nisqually, Puyallup, Squaxin Island, and neighboring peoples. Treaties followed at Point Elliott and Point No Point in January 1855, at Neah Bay with the Makah, and with the Quinault and related peoples in 1855 and 1856. East of the Cascades, the Yakama, Nez Perce, and the Umatilla, Cayuse, and Walla Walla signed treaties at the Walla Walla council in June 1855, and the Tribes of Middle Oregon, including the Warm Springs, signed a treaty negotiated by Oregon's superintendent, Joel Palmer, that same month. Senate ratification followed, in some cases after delay.

The negotiations were conducted under conditions of marked inequality. Proceedings relied heavily on Chinook Jargon, a trade language of limited vocabulary, which could not readily convey the legal content of lengthy documents; Indigenous participants spoke many different languages; and officials prepared the treaty texts in advance. Stevens and his agents pressed for rapid agreement, and the reservations first proposed were small and, in some cases, poorly situated for fishing. At Medicine Creek, the Nisqually leader Leschi and others objected to reservation lands that lacked adequate access to water and resources. The disputes contributed to the outbreak of fighting in 1855–1856, often called the Puget Sound or Nisqually War. Leschi was later tried and hanged in 1858; a historical court of inquiry convened in 2004 concluded that he had been wrongly convicted. The violence is treated more fully in Chapter 72.

Historians must therefore read the treaties as documents produced by unequal negotiation, but also as documents that Indigenous leaders understood and used. Records of the councils, though made by officials, show that the tribes' representatives asked about their ability to continue fishing and gathering, and that the officials, who wanted the cessions, gave assurances on the point. Native understandings of the agreements were not identical to Stevens's, and the surviving record cannot fully reconstruct them. But it is clear that preserving access to fish was a condition tribal negotiators sought and that officials agreed to include. Later courts took precisely this evidentiary record into account, as Section 113.14 explains.

The treaties differ from one another in language and detail, and the reservations and fishing clauses of the Puget Sound treaties are not identical to those of the Yakama or Nez Perce. The Makah treaty, for example, uniquely mentions whaling and sealing. This variation is one reason the later litigation had to proceed tribe by tribe and river by river, even as courts drew common principles from shared language.

Division I · Section 113.3

Fishing at Usual and Accustomed Grounds

The central fishing provision of the Stevens treaties is short. In the western Washington treaties, it secures to the tribes the right of taking fish "at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations," "in common with all citizens of the Territory." The Columbia basin treaties use similar language, and the Yakama treaty additionally reserves an exclusive right to fish in streams running through or bordering the reservation. Each element of this wording would later be argued over in court.

First, the phrase "usual and accustomed grounds and stations" extended beyond the reservations. Tribes thus reserved the right to fish at places they had long used, whether or not those places lay inside the reserved lands, and later courts held that this included locations that had since passed into private or state ownership. Second, the phrase "in common with" meant that the tribes' right was shared with non-Indian citizens rather than exclusive off-reservation. What "in common with" required in practice, whether it meant merely equal access to the same waters or something closer to an equal share of the fish, would be the decisive interpretive question of the Boldt litigation.

Third, courts distinguished a treaty-reserved right from an ordinary privilege. A state fishing license is a permission that the state grants and may withdraw. A treaty right, by contrast, was reserved by the tribe in exchange for its cession of land, and the Supremacy Clause makes treaties superior to conflicting state law. For commercial, subsistence, and ceremonial fishing alike, the right was one that tribes held as a matter of law, subject only to the limits courts would later articulate. This distinction is easily blurred in public debate, where treaty fishing was often described as a "special privilege" or "special season." The legal history shows the opposite: the tribes had bargained for the right.

Historical Vocabulary
Usual and Accustomed Grounds and Stations

Definition: The phrase in the Stevens treaties describing the places where tribes retained the right to take fish. Courts later determined each tribe's usual and accustomed grounds as a matter of historical fact, using anthropological, historical, and tribal testimony.

Historical context: The language reflected existing Indigenous systems of fishing sites, many far from the reservations that were created. Boldt's 1974 decision defined the usual and accustomed areas of the participating tribes, including marine waters of Puget Sound and the Strait of Juan de Fuca in the case of some tribes.

Why it matters: It established the off-reservation scope of treaty fishing, which made conflicts with private owners, canneries, and state regulators inevitable.

Potential misunderstanding: The phrase does not mean that tribes could fish anywhere; each tribe's rights extend to its own documented grounds, which were determined case by case.

Primary Source Window

The Fishing Clause of the Treaty of Medicine Creek, 1854

Creator: Isaac Stevens and tribal representatives at Medicine Creek · Date: December 26, 1854 (ratified 1855) · Type: Treaty text, a public legal document

Context: The treaty, Article 3 of which addresses fishing, was drafted by United States officials and presented to leaders of the Nisqually, Puyallup, Squaxin Island, and related peoples in a council conducted largely in Chinook Jargon.

“The right of taking fish, at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations, is further secured to said Indians in common with all citizens of the Territory, and of erecting temporary houses for the purpose of curing, together with the privilege of hunting, gathering roots and berries, and pasturing their horses on open and unclaimed lands: …”

Editorial transcription note: Excerpt from Article 3. The ellipsis marks the omitted proviso concerning shellfish beds staked or cultivated by citizens and the control of horses. Read the complete article in the Washington Governor’s Office of Indian Affairs treaty transcription; the full provision, not just its fishing language, is the historical source.

Analysis: The clause is notable for what it treats as already existing: the right is "secured," not newly granted. It also covers curing, a reminder that fishing involved processing and not only capture. The word "privilege" attached to hunting and gathering, but not to fishing, was noticed by later interpreters.

Limitations: The text does not say what "in common with" means in practice, does not define "usual and accustomed," and does not address the possibility that non-Indian fishing might one day threaten the resource. It also shows only what the officials wrote, not how tribal participants understood the words as rendered in Chinook Jargon.

Perspective: A document drafted by the United States, to which Indigenous leaders agreed under pressure; Indigenous understandings survive mainly in later testimony, oral tradition, and the council records.

Division I · Section 113.4

Commercial Expansion and the Erosion of Treaty Access

Within a generation of the treaties, salmon had become an industrial commodity. Commercial canning began on the Columbia River in 1866 and spread to Puget Sound and the coast. Canneries relied on fish wheels, traps, and large nets, many of them operated by non-Indians, and the scale of the harvest rose rapidly. Fixed traps in particular intercepted large numbers of salmon before they reached the rivers, and Washington did not ban them until the 1930s. Even where Indigenous fishermen sold fish to canneries, the market was controlled by others, and the resource itself was increasingly depleted.

Land ownership changed access. Under allotment and settlement, shoreline and riverbank property passed into private hands, and owners sometimes barred tribal members from sites they had used for generations. Cities, ports, logging, and agriculture degraded spawning streams. Hydroelectric dams were more consequential still on the Columbia: Bonneville Dam (completed in 1938), Grand Coulee (1941–42), and later The Dalles Dam, which in 1957 flooded Celilo Falls and ended the dip-net fishery and trading center there. These changes harmed both Indigenous and non-Indigenous fishermen, but they fell especially heavily on communities whose economy and identity depended on specific places.

State regulation compounded the pressure. Washington and Oregon created fisheries and game agencies, adopted licensing and seasons, restricted gear, and closed rivers to net fishing. These laws were generally justified as conservation measures, and in part they were responses to real declines. But because they were designed around commercial and sport fisheries, they frequently ignored the treaties. The most consequential example was the classification of steelhead as a game fish reserved for hook and line, which effectively prohibited the net fishing on which many tribal families relied.

The result was an unusual legal condition. The treaties had never been repealed, and the Supreme Court had reaffirmed them, yet in practice they were enforced against the tribes more often than for them. The erosion of access was gradual and had many causes, a pattern that explains why the Fish Wars could not be resolved by a single ruling and why they involved habitat, regulation, and allocation all at once.

Division II · Section 113.5

Early Judicial Defenses of Treaty Fishing Rights

The Supreme Court addressed treaty fishing early. In United States v. Winans (1905), the Court considered a challenge brought on behalf of the Yakama Nation against non-Indian landowners who operated fish wheels on the Columbia River and claimed that their property rights excluded Yakama fishermen. The Court rejected that claim. In an often-quoted formulation, it held that the treaty was "not a grant of rights to the Indians, but a grant of right from them," a reservation of rights not granted away. The fishing right was therefore an existing right the tribe had kept, and it carried an easement of access across private land to reach fishing places.

The Winans principle, that treaties are read as reservations rather than grants, became the foundation of later treaty-rights law. The Court also read treaties in light of how the Indians would have understood them, a canon of interpretation that would prove decisive in the Boldt litigation. In Tulee v. Washington (1942), the Court addressed a Yakama man, Sampson Tulee, who had been prosecuted for fishing with a net without a state license. It held that Washington could not require a license fee of a treaty fisherman, since the fee was a charge on a right the tribe had reserved. The Court, however, added that the state could still impose regulations necessary for conservation.

These decisions were significant victories, but their effects were limited. They did not establish what share of the fish the treaties protected, and they left open the scope of state authority over conservation. State officials and courts read the conservation exception broadly, and the cases did not stop arrests. The gap between a favorable Supreme Court principle and its practical application is a recurring theme of this chapter: recognition of a right and enforcement of a right proved to be different things.

Division II · Section 113.6

State Regulation, Conservation, and the Criminalization of Indigenous Fishing

By the 1950s and 1960s, state game wardens and fisheries officers regularly arrested Indigenous fishermen for violating seasons, gear rules, and closures. Nets, boats, and fish were confiscated, and fines or jail terms could follow. For households that depended on the income from a run, a single seizure could be ruinous. State courts, applying state law, often convicted defendants who raised the treaty as a defense, treating treaty fishing as subject to the same regulations as everyone else's.

Competing interests shaped the conflict. Commercial fishermen and canneries wanted access to the same runs; sport anglers, a rapidly growing constituency, viewed steelhead and salmon as recreational resources; state agencies sought to manage runs they believed to be in decline; and tribal fishermen asserted a legal entitlement and an economic need. The state's conservation argument was serious, since runs were in fact threatened. But treaty tribes pointed out that the principal causes of decline, including habitat loss, dams, and heavy commercial and sport harvest, were not primarily the work of tribal fishermen, and that regulations frequently fell hardest on tribal gear while allowing larger non-Indian harvests.

The legal question was therefore not whether conservation mattered but how it could be pursued consistently with treaty obligations. Historians should distinguish regulations that were genuinely necessary to preserve a run from those that used conservation language to allocate fish away from tribal fishermen, a distinction that the courts later tried to formalize.

Historical Vocabulary
Conservation Necessity

Definition: The legal standard under which a state may regulate off-reservation treaty fishing only when the regulation is reasonable and necessary for the conservation of the resource and does not discriminate against Indian fishermen.

Historical context: The standard was set out by the Supreme Court in the Puyallup cases of the late 1960s and 1970s and was applied in the Boldt Decision, which also required the state to show that tribal self-regulation was insufficient.

Why it matters: It limited state power without removing it, and it forced courts to distinguish true conservation from allocation by regulation.

Potential misunderstanding: "Conservation" in this sense does not mean any state interest in managing fish; it is a restrictive standard that the state must justify.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fishing as Household Work

For many tribal families, fishing was not an occasional pursuit but a seasonal round that organized household labor. Men commonly handled boats and nets, while women and older relatives often processed the catch by cleaning, smoking, drying, or canning it, mended gear, and managed distribution. Fish were eaten at home, shared within extended families and at community and ceremonial gatherings, and sold to buyers for cash that paid for fuel, nets, and household needs. In many communities, the work was learned by children alongside their elders.

Enforcement thus reached into domestic life. A confiscated net might represent a season's earnings; an arrest removed a worker from the household economy; repeated prosecutions burdened families financially and emotionally. At the same time, the movement drew in whole families, including children who learned of the arrests and the courtroom, and women who organized, fished, and raised funds. Historians reconstruct these experiences from oral history, memoirs, newspaper reports, and court records, each of which preserves part of the picture.

Division II · Section 113.7

The Fish-Ins and the Strategy of Direct Action

By the early 1960s, Indigenous fishermen in western Washington had concluded that litigation alone would not secure their rights, because each prosecution had to be defended in state court and because conservation rules continued to expand. In 1964, activists began organizing coordinated fish-ins, a name that echoed the sit-ins of the civil rights movement. In a fish-in, tribal members fished openly at treaty-protected locations in defiance of state regulation, anticipating arrest and intending to challenge the regulation in court, while drawing public attention.

The strategy had several aims. It created test cases on the ground, since arrests would bring the treaty issue before judges. It generated publicity, and activists sought allies beyond the tribes. It mobilized intertribal support, since fishing communities from different nations could join a shared cause. And it asserted a political claim: that treaty rights existed independently of state permission. Participants included members of the Nisqually, Puyallup, Muckleshoot, and other nations, and supporters from outside Indian country took part as well. The actor Marlon Brando joined a 1964 fish-in on the Puyallup River and was arrested, and the comedian and activist Dick Gregory took part in the campaign in the mid-1960s. Such celebrity participation brought national attention, though Indigenous organizers insisted that the movement belonged to the tribes.

Organizations such as the Survival of American Indians Association, formed in the mid-1960s by activists associated with Frank's Landing and the Puyallup, provided structure. The fish-ins took place in a decade in which Indigenous activism was expanding elsewhere, and later chapters treat the links to Alcatraz (Chapter 109) and the American Indian Movement (Chapter 110). The Fish Wars are sometimes described as a precursor of Red Power, but they were also a distinct, treaty-centered movement with its own roots in family fishing and tribal law. Their tactical innovation was to make the ordinary act of fishing into an assertion of nationhood.

Division II · Section 113.8

Billy Frank Jr. and Frank's Landing

Billy Frank Jr. was born in 1931 and raised on the Nisqually River, where his father, Willie Frank Sr., and extended family fished. He was first arrested for fishing in 1945, when he was still a teenager, and was arrested many times thereafter; by his own and others' accounts the number exceeded fifty. These arrests were not isolated episodes. They exemplified the criminalization of treaty fishing described in Section 113.6, and Frank's persistence, over decades, made him a figure through whom the public came to understand the conflict.

Frank's Landing, on the Nisqually River near Olympia, was land held by the Frank family and became a base for fishing resistance. It served as a camp, meeting place, and staging area, and it drew other Indigenous fishermen, activists, lawyers, and supporters. State officers conducted raids and arrests in the 1960s, and tear gas and confiscations were part of the confrontations. Billy Frank's family, including his sister Maiselle Bridges and other relatives, participated in the work of the Landing, and it was in practice a household and a community, not simply a protest camp.

It would be a mistake to treat the movement as one man's story. Frank worked alongside Hank Adams, Janet McCloud, and many others, and the tribal governments, families, and lawyers who sustained the campaign were essential. He nonetheless became a symbol and, later, a principal tribal fisheries leader, serving for decades as chair of the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission, and was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015. His career illustrates the transition from protest to institution building traced in Section 113.19.

Division III · Section 113.9

The Puyallup River Confrontations

The Puyallup River flows through Tacoma into Commencement Bay, one of the most heavily industrialized waterfronts in Puget Sound. The Puyallup Tribe had treaty fishing rights under the Treaty of Medicine Creek, but the river ran through an increasingly urban landscape, and the state had restricted net fishing there, particularly for steelhead. Puyallup fishermen and their allies fished in defiance of those restrictions through the 1960s, and the river became one of the principal sites of the fish-ins.

The confrontations intensified at the end of the decade. In September 1970, a large force of state and local officers moved against an Indian fishing camp on the river, using tear gas and arresting dozens of people. Accounts of the raid, and of the use of force, circulated widely and provoked public controversy; supporters described it as an assault on treaty fishermen and officials defended it as law enforcement. Within the same month, the United States filed suit in federal court in United States v. Washington, the case discussed in Section 113.13. Historians generally see the raid as one of several events that made the conflict impossible for federal authorities to ignore, though the decision to sue had its own legal and political causes.

The Puyallup confrontations show the interplay of tactics. Direct action generated arrests and publicity, litigation converted the underlying question into a legal one, and each reinforced the other. They also show how local circumstances mattered: an urban river, intense industrial development, and a heavily regulated game-fish classification shaped the dispute and the case law that followed.

Division III · Section 113.10

The Organizers and the Intertribal Movement

Ramona Bennett, a Puyallup leader who served as tribal chair in the 1970s, was a central figure in the river confrontations and in strengthening tribal institutions. Hank Adams, an Assiniboine-Sioux strategist who had grown up partly in Washington State, worked with fishing activists on legal and political strategy and was wounded by gunfire in January 1971 while engaged in fishing-rights activity beside the Puyallup River near Tacoma—not at Frank's Landing. He recovered and later participated in national movements (see Chapters 111 and 112). Janet McCloud, a Tulalip activist, was a founder of the Survival of American Indians Association and a leading voice for treaty rights. Robert Satiacum, a Puyallup fisherman, took part in the fish-ins of the 1960s and 1970s. These individuals' names appear often in the sources, but the movement rested on many others whose names are less recorded.

Women's leadership deserves particular attention. Women organized fish camps, raised money, managed households under pressure, handled communications, and took part in confrontations and arrests. Tribal councils and tribal governments provided legal standing and resources; extended families offered the labor, boats, and locations on which fishing depended; and the intertribal character of the movement meant that Puyallup, Nisqually, Muckleshoot, Yakama, and other nations coordinated and supported one another across differences in size, resources, and treaty language.

Outside solidarity also mattered, including support from churches, civil rights activists, and public figures, but it operated at the invitation and under the direction of the tribes. The tension between a handful of well-known activists and a broad base of community participants is a methodological caution for students: sources, particularly newspapers, tend to foreground a few prominent individuals, while the organizational history was more collective.

Division III · Section 113.11

The Puyallup Treaty-Rights Cases

The Supreme Court heard three linked disputes commonly labeled Puyallup I (1968), Puyallup II (1973), and Puyallup III (1977). Each arose from concrete conflicts over steelhead fishing on the Puyallup River, but the questions differed. In Puyallup Tribe v. Department of Game, 391 U.S. 392 (1968), the Court recognized that the state could regulate treaty fishing at usual and accustomed off-reservation places when restrictions were reasonable, necessary for conservation, and nondiscriminatory. The Court did not declare all state regulations valid: it required examination of whether a particular rule actually satisfied that demanding conservation standard. The case thus established neither total tribal exemption from regulation nor general state control over the treaty right.

Puyallup II, Department of Game v. Puyallup Tribe, 414 U.S. 44 (1973), confronted a prohibition on Indian net fishing for steelhead while non-Indian anglers could continue fishing with hooks and lines. Formally identical rules did not yield equal results: there were many more sport anglers than treaty fishermen, and a complete net ban deprived the latter of an effective opportunity to harvest. The Court rejected that discriminatory outcome and required a fair apportionment between tribal net fishing and non-tribal sport fishing, leaving the amount to the state court on remand. The resulting allocation for this particular steelhead run was not a universal treaty formula for all Northwest fisheries.

Puyallup III, Puyallup Tribe, Inc. v. Department of Game, 433 U.S. 165 (1977), added a jurisdictional problem frequently lost in brief accounts. The Washington courts had entered orders against the Tribe itself and individual fishermen. The Supreme Court ruled that, without waiver or consent, the Tribe possessed sovereign immunity from suit in state court: orders requiring the tribal government to submit membership lists or report catches therefore could not stand against it. Yet immunity of the government did not automatically dispose of proceedings against individual tribal members properly before the state court. Tribal participation in a case did not by itself surrender immunity.

The same 1977 opinion also rejected an unlimited right to take steelhead passing through the reservation; the Court sustained a conservation-based limit on the catch in the particular record before it. This mattered because an exclusive right to use some reservation fishing locations did not mean an unlimited claim to all migrating fish. The opinion distinguished jurisdiction over the sovereign Tribe, the permissible conservation of a shared run, and the allocation of harvest. Students should read the 1977 opinion with those three questions in mind.

The trilogy therefore established connected but separate principles: treaty fishing is a real right to harvest, conservation restrictions must be supported rather than assumed, the resource must be fairly apportioned, and a tribal government's immunity from suit must be respected even where rules may be enforceable against individual fishers. In later litigation these principles informed the interpretation of the Stevens treaties, but the Puyallup rulings did not themselves require an across-the-board Boldt-style fifty-percent division for every run.

Division III · Section 113.12

The Columbia River Fishery and Sohappy v. Smith

On the Columbia River and its tributaries, the Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, and Nez Perce nations had treaty rights similar to those of the western Washington tribes, and the conflict there had its own history. Dams had transformed the river, and Celilo Falls, a fishing and trading center used for many generations, was inundated in 1957. Fishermen at the remaining sites, including members of the Yakama and Wanapum communities such as David Sohappy, continued to fish in the face of state regulation, and they were arrested and prosecuted. Oregon and Washington, which shared jurisdiction over much of the river, regulated heavily in the interest of the commercial and sport fisheries downstream.

In Sohappy v. Smith (1969), Judge Robert Belloni of the federal district court in Oregon held that the treaty tribes were entitled to a fair share of the fish and that state regulation could restrict treaty fishing only on the narrow conservation grounds the Supreme Court had recognized. The case became part of the long-running United States v. Oregon litigation, in which the court supervised the allocation of Columbia River runs among treaty and non-treaty fisheries, and which led eventually to detailed management agreements. The 1969 decision predated Boldt's ruling by five years and was a parallel development, not a derivative of it.

Judge Belloni's 1969 opinion is best understood as a directive about the practical opportunity to obtain fish, not a promise of the same number of fishing licenses or equal treatment under rules designed for non-treaty fisheries. It required Oregon's regulations to account affirmatively for the treaty tribes' right to a fair share. The judgment did not itself prescribe a universal fifty-percent percentage. That distinction helps explain why Columbia River allocation and management continued to require negotiations and judicial oversight even after the principle was recognized.

The Columbia and Puget Sound differed in ways that mattered. The Columbia was a heavily dammed, multi-state, and multi-tribe system, in which the allocation of fish had to account for hatchery production, upriver and downriver fisheries, and several overlapping jurisdictions; western Washington involved many rivers, marine waters, and a large number of tribes. Ecological conditions, enforcement patterns, and the structure of litigation were accordingly different. David Sohappy himself was later prosecuted in the 1980s in a federal undercover investigation of fish sales and served time in prison, an episode that shows how enforcement and treaty rights continued to collide after the major cases. That later history lies beyond this chapter's main endpoint.

Division IV · Section 113.13

United States v. Washington: The Federal Government Enters the Conflict

In September 1970, the United States, acting both in its own right and as trustee for seven western Washington treaty tribes, filed suit against the State of Washington in federal district court. The original group was joined by seven additional tribal plaintiffs or intervenors; the major 1974 allocation decision addressed fourteen tribes then before Judge George H. Boldt. The distinction matters: neither the set of 1970 plaintiffs nor the set of fourteen litigating in 1974 should be confused with the nineteen founding members of the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission later that year, or the still wider community of treaty nations. The lawsuit rested on the supremacy of federal treaties and the United States' obligations to protect rights reserved by its treaty partners.

The case asked the courts to decide, among other things, what the treaty phrase "in common with" meant; what share of the fish the tribes were entitled to; what powers the state retained to regulate treaty fishing; and which tribes and which waters were covered. It thus took the questions the Puyallup cases had left open and put them before a single court on a comprehensive record. Washington's Attorney General, Slade Gorton, defended the state's position, which held that the state could regulate treaty fishing in the interest of conservation and that the treaties guaranteed equal access to fishing, not a share of the fish.

This was not a prosecution of a single fisherman. The United States and tribal parties sought declarations and injunctions capable of changing the way the state regulated entire fisheries. That procedural shift allowed the federal court to compare multiple treaty texts, reconstruct the historical fishing areas of distinct tribes, assess evidence about harvestable runs, and determine management rules applicable beyond one arrest or one season. The breadth of the remedy was a crucial reason the case could alter state practice where earlier individual defenses had produced only scattered victories.

The federal government's decision to sue reflected decades of Indigenous litigation and protest as well as the policy climate of the early 1970s, when the administration of Richard Nixon was moving toward self-determination in Indian policy (see Chapter 114). The suit did not displace tribal action; it depended on it, and the tribes' own legal counsel and the intervening tribes actively shaped the case.

The federal government was itself in a complicated position. It was treaty partner and trustee, yet its agencies also operated dams, administered federal fisheries policies, and responded to political pressure from non-Indian constituents. The tribes did not merely wait for Washington, D.C., to act: their organizing, protests, legal preparation, and intervention shaped the controversy and the evidence brought to trial. The lawsuit is therefore a case study in both federal legal responsibility and Indigenous political initiative, not evidence that rights were benevolently granted anew in 1970.

Division IV · Section 113.14

Building the Case: Treaties, Testimony, and Historical Evidence

The trial was unusual in the extent to which it relied on historical and anthropological evidence. Lawyers for the tribes and the United States assembled treaty records, the journals and correspondence of territorial officials, and expert reports, including a detailed study by the anthropologist Barbara Lane on the economic and political context in which the treaties were negotiated. These materials were used to show how Indigenous negotiators and officials would have understood the fishing clauses in 1854 and 1855, a task made necessary by the Supreme Court's rule that treaties be interpreted as the Indians would have understood them.

Tribal members testified about traditional fishing locations and practices, and fisheries scientists addressed the condition of the runs and the effects of various harvest methods. The court heard evidence about Chinook Jargon and its limits as a medium of negotiation, about the importance of fishing to the tribes in 1855, and about the history of state regulation. The state and the non-Indian fishing interests presented their own evidence and argued for a different reading of "in common with."

An important task was the identification of each nation's “usual and accustomed” fishing grounds and stations. A generalized description of a regional salmon culture could not answer where particular peoples customarily fished at treaty time. Witnesses and specialists drew on oral histories, records of villages and seasonal camps, travel between waterways, trading networks, and descriptions by early outsiders. The court had to distinguish sustained customary use from an isolated visit and had to decide how the treaty's language applied to marine channels as well as rivers. Because one tribe's documented geography was not identical to another's, findings were necessarily specific rather than a blanket declaration that every plaintiff could fish everywhere.

The trial also brought legal and biological categories into conversation. Lawyers required a definition of the fish available for division, while biologists had to address separate salmon and steelhead runs, escapement for reproduction, and catches taken in different waters before fish reached a tribe's fishing grounds. A run could be intercepted successively by ocean fisheries, commercial gear, sport anglers, and treaty fishermen. Therefore a court order describing a fair share had to confront timing and movement: who had already harvested fish, where the remainder was available, and how to avoid treating the same fish as part of two independent stocks. Those practical questions later generated further subproceedings and disagreements.

The historical interpretation was no less consequential. Treaty texts recorded by territorial officials were evidence of a formal agreement; the courts also considered the circumstances of translation, the assurances made in council, and what Indigenous negotiators reasonably understood that they were preserving. Expert testimony could help explain those contexts, but it did not erase disagreements about the geographic limits of customary use or the weight to give different records. In reading the 1974 findings, students should distinguish what a particular witness reported, what an expert inferred, and what the judge ultimately found as fact.

For historians, the record is exceptional but not neutral. It reflects the adversarial structure of litigation, the selection of experts, and the questions the court posed. It nonetheless brought Indigenous testimony and historical research into the legal definition of a treaty, and it illustrates the role of expert knowledge in modern Indigenous rights cases, including the use of oral history and anthropology that Chapter 1 discussed as evidence.

Division IV · Section 113.15

The Boldt Decision of February 12, 1974

On February 12, 1974, after a trial in 1973, Judge Boldt issued his decision in United States v. Washington. He held that the treaties reserved to the participating tribes the right to take fish at their usual and accustomed places, and he identified those places for the tribes before him. He interpreted "in common with" to mean that the tribes and non-Indians were entitled to share the harvestable fish equally, and he concluded that treaty tribes could take up to fifty percent of the harvestable fish passing through their usual and accustomed areas, with adjustments for particular categories of fish. He further held that the state could regulate treaty fishing only to the extent necessary for conservation and only after showing that tribal self-regulation was insufficient, and he recognized the tribes' own authority to regulate their members' fishing.

Boldt, a federal judge appointed in the 1950s and not an obvious champion of Indigenous causes, grounded his ruling in the historical record and in settled principles, including the canon that treaties are to be read as the Indians understood them. The decision was a turning point because it translated decades of principle into an allocation and treated the tribes as governments with regulatory authority. It also meant that the state of Washington could no longer manage the fishery alone. In the longer history of Indigenous sovereignty, it became a landmark, cited across North America as an example of a court enforcing a treaty according to its terms.

The decision arose in a case involving fourteen participating treaty tribes at that point, but it did not make all tribal territories or fishing grounds interchangeable. Boldt's findings identified specific treaty parties and fishing locations, making the meaning of “usual and accustomed” a continuing question of geography, historical evidence, and tribal identity. That factual specificity gave the ruling practical value: a right described in principle could now be connected to particular rivers, marine areas, and management responsibilities. It also created a record to be interpreted or refined when later disputes concerned additional peoples or waters.

Division IV · Section 113.16

The Meaning of the Fifty-Percent Allocation

The allocation is often summarized as "half the fish," but its terms are more limited. It applies to harvestable fish, meaning the fish that remain available for harvest after enough have been allowed to escape to the spawning grounds to sustain the run. It is not half of all fish in the water, and the amount needed for escapement comes first. It also applies to fish passing through the tribes' usual and accustomed places, so that tribal and non-tribal fishermen share the same runs, with the division calculated run by run.

A simplified illustration reveals the accounting problem. If a particular run yields a legally determined harvestable portion after escapement, the calculation concerns that harvestable portion, not the total return including spawning fish. Tribal harvest within the relevant run is then counted across the legally applicable categories, including on-reservation and ceremonial or subsistence catches after the Supreme Court's correction. Actual orders require more than a single arithmetic operation: they address stock identification, where fish were taken, relevant tribal customary areas, and appropriate adjustments. The simplification “half the salmon in Washington belong to tribes” therefore obscures both the biology and the law.

The later Supreme Court interpretation made the percentage a ceiling, not an automatic minimum: an initial approximately equal division could be reduced where a lesser allocation would supply the tribes a moderate living. The phrase described a standard of livelihood, not a requirement that treaty fishermen prove poverty or surrender their rights whenever the fishery declined. The 1979 Court also corrected the accounting used in the earlier judgment. Fish caught on reservations, and fish taken for ceremonial and subsistence purposes, generally had to count against the tribes' share rather than being treated as extra fish outside the allocation. The Court expressly left open whether ceremonial and subsistence fishing might require priority when fish were insufficient to meet those needs. Students should not confuse the percentage cap, the accounting categories, and priority questions.

The rule mattered because the tribes' harvest had been very small, often estimated at only a few percent of the total, as state regulation and non-Indian fishing had come to dominate the resource. A sharing rule reversed that pattern, and it explains both the decision's importance to tribal communities and the intensity of the opposition it provoked.

Historical Vocabulary
"In Common With"

Definition: The treaty phrase describing the relationship between tribal fishing and the fishing of other citizens. In United States v. Washington, the courts read it to mean that treaty and non-treaty fishermen share the harvestable fish, not merely that both may fish in the same waters.

Historical context: The phrase appears in the Stevens treaties. The state long read it as equal access under common regulation; the tribes and, eventually, the courts read it in light of the Indians' understanding, which had been that they would continue to fish and not be shut out.

Why it matters: The interpretation determined whether tribes held a right to a share of the fish.

Potential misunderstanding: "In common with" does not mean exclusive tribal ownership off the reservation, nor does it mean that the tribes' rights are equal to a recreational license.

Historical Debate

Conservation and Treaty-Reserved Rights

The question: How can a shared, declining fishery be conserved while honoring treaty rights, and who should determine what conservation requires? The debate does not treat treaty rights as one preference among others. The rights are legally established; the dispute concerns the limits of state regulation and the allocation of a scarce resource.

Interpretation A — State-centered management: State agencies argued that biological management requires a single authority with uniform rules and that conservation justifies restrictions on all fishermen, including treaty fishermen.
Interpretation B — Treaty-centered management: Tribes and the federal government argued that treaties limit state power to what is necessary for conservation, that regulation must not discriminate, and that tribes have their own governmental authority and scientific capacity to regulate.

Evidence: Run-size data, harvest records, hatchery production, habitat loss, enforcement records, and the courts' interpretations of the treaties.

Points of disagreement: Interpretation of treaty language, the scale of the effect of tribal harvest versus habitat loss, and the authority to regulate. Courts largely resolved the legal question in favor of limited state authority and shared management; the biological and ecological questions remain in dispute.

Why it matters: It shaped co-management and continues to affect policy on salmon recovery.

Student task: Identify evidence that would show whether a given regulation was necessary for conservation or allocated fish away from tribal fishermen, and explain how courts might use it.

Division V · Section 113.17

Resistance to the Boldt Decision

The decision provoked fierce opposition. Non-Indian commercial and sport fishermen, whose catch was reduced, organized protests, and the judge himself was the target of public hostility and threats. Bumper stickers and public rhetoric framed the ruling as unfair, and some opponents resorted to harassment and confrontations on the water. State officials and courts resisted compliance, and state agencies were slow or unwilling to enforce the federal order. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the decision in 1975 and the Supreme Court declined to review it in 1976, but in practice the order was not carried out.

The difficulty was institutional. Enforcement of federal court orders depended on state officers who denied they could carry them out, and state courts at times held that state law prevented compliance. In 1977, the federal court took unusual steps to supervise the fishery directly, using federal officers to enforce its orders. The Supreme Court later described the resistance in strong terms, noting that it was among the most concerted official and private efforts to frustrate a federal court decree in the century, with the exception of some desegregation cases.

After the 1975 affirmance by the Ninth Circuit, the Supreme Court declined review in 1976. These steps left the district court's central interpretation standing, but they did not end the state's opposition or resolve the enforcement crisis. In 1977 Judge Boldt assumed direct supervisory powers over essential aspects of the fishery when state institutions failed to carry out federal requirements. The subsequent Supreme Court litigation thus concerned not only abstract treaty interpretation but the authority of federal judges to secure compliance when state agencies and courts resisted. This sequence is central to understanding why a legal ruling in 1974 did not translate into stable harvest arrangements overnight.

The episode shows that a landmark ruling does not settle a conflict by itself. Enforcement required sustained judicial supervision, tribal participation, and eventually changes in state practice, and a generation of non-Indian fishermen experienced the decision as a loss, a fact that historians must acknowledge even while distinguishing legal obligations from grievances.

Division V · Section 113.18

The Supreme Court and the Confirmation of Treaty Allocation

On July 2, 1979, the Supreme Court decided Washington v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Association, 443 U.S. 658, often shortened to Fishing Vessel. Justice John Paul Stevens wrote for the Court. Rejecting the state's “equal opportunity” theory, the majority reasoned that permission for each individual to attempt fishing on identical terms could leave treaty nations with virtually no fish when far more non-treaty users competed for the run. The treaties instead protected a meaningful share of the harvest. The Court approved an initial approximately equal division between treaty and non-treaty fisheries at usual and accustomed locations, with the division and subsequent calculations grounded in the specific fishery and record.

The Supreme Court made two important refinements. First, approximately fifty percent was a maximum, not an unconditional allotment: the tribes' allocation could be reduced if a smaller share would provide a moderate living. Second, catches on the reservation, and catches taken to meet ceremonial and subsistence needs, generally had to be included when calculating the tribal share. Earlier calculations had excluded such categories, potentially adding them on top of the allocation. The Court reserved the separate question whether ceremonial and subsistence catches might have priority if fish became so scarce that those needs could not otherwise be met. It also upheld the federal courts' authority to enforce the treaty decree in the face of persistent state resistance.

The 1979 opinion resolved the principal treaty-allocation controversy without settling every management problem. The distinction between Phase I, addressing entitlement and allocation, and Phase II, addressing hatchery fish and environmental claims, is important. In a 1980 Phase II decision, District Judge William Orrick concluded that hatchery-produced fish were included in treaty allocations and declared that treaty fishing also carried a corresponding habitat-protection obligation. A court of appeals decision in 1985 affirmed the inclusion of hatchery fish but vacated the generalized environmental declaration, finding that a sweeping declaration without concrete disputes and defined facts was an improper exercise of declaratory relief. That appellate disposition did not amount to a categorical ruling that treaty-protected fish habitat could never support a legal claim.

These distinctions illustrate both continuity and change in the courts' reasoning. The 1974 district ruling announced an enforceable share; the 1979 Supreme Court confirmed the principle but specified how allocation should be limited and counted; the 1980–1985 proceedings examined what qualified as fish and whether a general environmental obligation could be adjudicated in the abstract. Tribal advocates obtained recognition of harvest rights while having to pursue further legal and governmental strategies to protect the rivers that produced the harvest. This later history connects to Chapter 135, but the chapter's present task is to explain the judicial settlement that followed the Fish Wars.

Tribal Fisheries Management and the New Institutional Order

In 1974, nineteen federally recognized western Washington treaty tribes created the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission (NWIFC), an intertribal organization to support the fisheries responsibilities opened by the Boldt Decision. Its membership later reached twenty. The commission did not replace the sovereignty of its member nations: each tribe retained its own governmental authority and customary fishing areas, while the intertribal institution supplied shared biological expertise, technical planning, legal coordination, public information, and a platform for negotiations. Tribes also built their own fisheries departments, developed enforcement systems and rules for tribal members, hired scientists, and undertook hatchery and habitat work.

Management developed toward a negotiated state–tribal system, often called co-management. Governments increasingly had to coordinate seasons and harvest levels, account for fish returning to different basins, monitor harvest, and resolve disputes through scientific analysis and treaty law rather than treating tribal participation as discretionary. The federal court retained jurisdiction over enforcement and contested questions. Billy Frank Jr. first became chair of the NWIFC in May 1977 and served again from 1981 onward, becoming widely associated with the transition from fish-in organizer to fisheries leader. His trajectory should not be understood as a move away from treaty defense; helping to rebuild viable salmon runs became another way to sustain it.

The day-to-day work gave sovereignty an administrative form. Fishery managers estimated returning runs, considered how many adults needed to reach spawning grounds, designed openings and closures, assessed catches, and sought to prevent overharvest. Tribal enforcement officers and biologists had to work with legal decisions as well as local observations of water and fish. A management plan that existed only in a court order was insufficient: implementation required crews, staff, scientific equipment, communication among governments, and long-term funding. These tasks created professional institutions that could continue even after the peak years of public confrontation.

The commission also carried a public educational responsibility. Many non-Indigenous residents knew the fishery chiefly through headlines about arrests or the “fifty-percent” controversy, while tribal perspectives emphasized a long-standing legal obligation and the burden of maintaining the resource. Providing information about treaty geography, run conditions, and harvest rules was consequently part of institution building. The evolution from open confrontation toward regular meetings should not obscure continuing disputes, but it demonstrated that Indigenous governments were not simply parties demanding an allocation: they were active managers with expertise and obligations.

Co-management was neither an instant settlement nor a simple handover. Sharing authority required states that once arrested tribal fishermen to acknowledge tribal governments as indispensable partners, while treaty nations themselves had distinct historical fishing locations and sometimes different ecological priorities. Negotiations could be contentious and depended on budgets, data quality, and the willingness to recognize binding treaty commitments. Legal recognition did not itself reverse polluted streams, blocked migration routes, or depleted stocks. Later fisheries stewardship belongs to Chapter 135; the larger institutional movement toward tribal self-determination belongs to Chapter 114.

Division V · Section 113.20

The Historical Legacy of the Fish Wars

The Fish Wars established that treaty fishing rights were enforceable and that tribes were governments with regulatory authority, results with consequences well beyond the Northwest. The litigation provided a model for treaty enforcement elsewhere, and the movement contributed to the broader transition toward self-determination that Chapter 114 traces. It also demonstrated the interdependence of direct action and legal strategy, and it enlarged the role of Indigenous knowledge, women's leadership, and intertribal cooperation in public life.

The achievements had limits. Legal recognition did not restore salmon populations, which continued to decline because of habitat loss, dams, development, and climate change; by the 1990s several Pacific Northwest salmon populations, including Puget Sound chinook, were listed under the federal Endangered Species Act. Tribes gained a share of a shrinking resource, and the protection of habitat remained an unresolved front, with later litigation addressing tribes' treaty claims relating to the environment. Non-Indian fishing communities also bore costs, and relations between them and tribes remained uneasy for years.

The Fish Wars therefore belong to the larger story of Indigenous sovereignty in the twentieth century: a campaign that converted a century-old promise into enforceable law and new institutions, while leaving open questions about the condition of the rivers and the future of the fish.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Treaty Fishing, 1855 to 1979

What Changed

  • Fishing moved from a subsistence and trade economy to a regulated commercial and sport fishery in which tribal access was restricted
  • Tribal participation shifted from exclusion to co-management, through litigation and new tribal institutions
  • Courts moved from recognizing a right in principle to defining an allocation
  • The resource itself declined through habitat loss and heavy harvest

What Stayed the Same

  • The treaties remained legally binding throughout
  • Fishing remained central to tribal economies, culture, and identity
  • Families and communities continued to fish and to assert their rights
  • Disputes over conservation and allocation recurred

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven and contested, and those who experienced it most directly were tribal fishing families and non-Indian fishermen. The pattern shows that legal recognition and practical change were different processes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Fish Wars as a struggle over the enforceability of treaties, the authority of Indigenous governments, and the obligations of the United States, not simply as a dispute over access to salmon. Fishing was the foundation of Indigenous economies, ceremonies, and social organization across Puget Sound, the Olympic Peninsula, the coast, and the Columbia basin, and the treaties of the 1850s preserved the right to continue it.

The treaties were negotiated under unequal conditions, largely through Chinook Jargon, and the fishing clauses secured the right to take fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations in common with other citizens. Courts later treated the right as reserved by the tribes, not granted to them, as Winans held in 1905.

Over the following century, canneries, traps, dams, private landownership, and state regulation eroded the practical exercise of the right. Tulee in 1942 limited state licensing of treaty fishing, but favorable principles did not stop arrests, because recognition and enforcement were distinct matters.

From the 1960s, Indigenous fishermen responded with fish-ins that made fishing an act of political assertion. Billy Frank Jr. and Frank's Landing became emblematic, but the movement depended equally on women organizers, families, tribal governments, and intertribal cooperation, including Ramona Bennett, Hank Adams, Janet McCloud, and many others.

Litigation proceeded alongside direct action. The Puyallup decisions of 1968 and 1973 established conservation limits and equitable apportionment; the 1977 ruling also clarified tribal sovereign immunity and the state's ability to regulate the harvest of a shared run. Sohappy v. Smith applied a fair-share principle on the Columbia River. The 1970 Puyallup confrontation coincided with the federal lawsuit that became United States v. Washington.

That case produced a trial built on historical and anthropological evidence, and on February 12, 1974, Judge Boldt held that the treaty tribes were entitled to share the harvestable fish, up to half, with state regulation limited to what conservation required and tribal regulatory authority recognized. The allocation concerned harvestable fish after escapement and was not an unconditional right to half of every fishery.

Resistance from state officials and some non-Indian fishing interests delayed enforcement and required federal supervision. The Supreme Court confirmed the central allocation framework in Fishing Vessel (1979), treating fifty percent as a ceiling linked to a moderate living and counting on-reservation, ceremonial, and subsistence catches within the tribal share. Later Phase II rulings confirmed hatchery fish were covered but left general habitat claims for fact-specific litigation.

The consequence was a new institutional order: the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission, tribal fisheries departments, and state-tribal co-management under court supervision. Tribal governments exercised regulatory authority that had long been denied them.

The achievements were real but limited. Salmon populations continued to decline, several were later listed under the Endangered Species Act, and habitat protection remained contested. Non-Indian fishing communities experienced the decision as a loss, which historians must acknowledge while distinguishing legal obligation from grievance.

The Fish Wars demonstrate how Indigenous nations used law and direct action together to convert a nineteenth-century promise into enforceable law, and they connect to the transition toward self-determination and to the later history of fisheries stewardship examined in Chapters 114 and 135.

Chapter Reflection

The Fish Wars matter because they show how a promise made in the 1850s became enforceable in the 1970s only through sustained Indigenous effort. The treaties never lost their legal force, yet their meaning in practice depended on arrests endured, cases argued, evidence gathered, and institutions built. The chapter thus illustrates a general lesson of the sovereignty era: rights recognized on paper must be defended and operationalized.

The history also reveals how law and protest interact. Direct action created the cases and the public pressure, and litigation turned that pressure into rules. Neither would have sufficed alone, and the movement's strength lay in families, women organizers, tribal governments, and intertribal alliances as much as in individual leaders.

The chapter's outcomes shaped what followed. Tribal fisheries institutions became models of Indigenous governance, the legal principles influenced treaty interpretation elsewhere, and the experience of co-management informs the environmental stewardship discussed in Chapter 135. At the same time, the continuing decline of salmon cautions against equating legal victory with restored abundance.

In the larger textbook, the Fish Wars connect the treaty history of earlier volumes to the contemporary assertion of sovereignty. They show that Indigenous nations were not remnants but governments that insisted on treaty obligations, and that enforcement of those obligations remains an ongoing project.
