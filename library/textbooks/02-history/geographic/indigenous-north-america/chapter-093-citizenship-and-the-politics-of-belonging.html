Citizenship and the Politics of Belonging

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Indigenous peoples did not wait for the United States or Canada to confer political membership. They already belonged to nations with their own laws of belonging. The nineteenth- and early twentieth-century struggle was over whether those older relationships would be recognized, overridden, or translated into the categories of expanding settler states.

On a spring day in 1880, a man named John Elk went to a registration office in Omaha, Nebraska, intending to be entered on the voting rolls. He had been born into an Indian tribe, but by his own account he had left it, lived among non-Indians, and placed himself under the laws of Nebraska and the United States. The registrar, Charles Wilkins, refused to register him. Elk sued. In 1884 the Supreme Court decided against him in Elk v. Wilkins, holding that a person born into a tribe did not become a citizen merely by leaving it and living under American jurisdiction. The case left an unsettling contradiction. The United States claimed sweeping authority over Indigenous people, yet it also treated their political communities as distinct enough that membership in them stood in the way of citizenship.

Forty years later, in 1923, Cayuga chief Deskaheh of the Six Nations of the Grand River travelled to Geneva to ask the League of Nations to hear the case of the Haudenosaunee against Canadian interference with their government. A year after that, in the same calendar year that the United States declared all remaining non-citizen Indians born in its territory to be citizens, Canada imposed an elected council on the Six Nations community at Grand River. These episodes were not isolated. They show that one set of questions, who is a member, who is a citizen, who may vote, and who decides, was being answered in different and contradictory ways by Indigenous nations, by the United States, and by Canada.

This chapter separates four ideas that popular language often merges. Indigenous citizenship or nationhood is membership in an Indigenous political community according to that community's own laws, relationships, and institutions. State citizenship is legal membership in the United States or in the Canadian constitutional order, with the rights and obligations attached to it. Canadian Indian status is a statutory classification created and administered under Canadian law, and it is not the same thing as Indigenous identity or as membership in a First Nation. Voting rights are the legally recognized and practically exercisable ability to take part in elections, which did not follow automatically from citizenship. Keeping these four apart is the main analytical discipline of the chapter.

The chapter's chief contrast is between two legal systems that pursued overlapping goals by different means. In the United States, Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act on June 2, 1924, extending federal citizenship to Indigenous people born within the nation's territorial limits who were not already citizens, while stating that the grant would not impair tribal or other property rights. Many state governments nevertheless kept Native citizens from the polls for decades. In Canada, the Indian Act made "enfranchisement" a process by which a First Nations person could cease to be a legal Indian, lose band membership and the rights attached to it, and be absorbed into the general population. Unconditional federal voting rights for First Nations people who kept their status did not come until 1960. The two countries therefore did not move along one track at different speeds. They built different instruments for governing the same problem.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied, and it is unevenly distributed in ways that matter. Statutes, court opinions, legislative debates, annual reports of administrators, census rolls, and enrollment lists record how states defined people. Treaty texts record agreements between nations, though often in colonial transcription. Council records, petitions, newspapers, and the published statements of Indigenous governments record how Indigenous people understood the same events, and oral tradition preserves understandings of treaty and kinship that written texts rarely capture. Historians must ask of each source who produced it, for whom, and what it leaves unsaid. A statute can reveal what a legislature intended to accomplish. It cannot, by itself, reveal how a family lived under it.

The chapter keeps to a specific division of labor within the textbook. Land loss under allotment belongs to Chapter 86, the administrative architecture of the Indian Act to Chapter 85, and Indigenous economic life to Chapter 91. Journalism, scholarship, and political organization belong to Chapter 92, wartime service to Chapter 94, and organized reform to Chapter 95. Where those subjects bear on citizenship they appear here briefly and with cross-references. The primary focus is the period from roughly 1880 to 1930, with essential legal precedents reaching back to the Treaty of Canandaigua in 1794 and the Canadian legislation of 1857.

The argument unfolds across five divisions. Division I reconstructs Indigenous political membership and the colonial machinery of classification. Division II follows United States law from the Fourteenth Amendment through the Indian Citizenship Act and the barriers that followed it. Division III turns to Canadian status, enfranchisement, gender, and the federal franchise, and to the distinct positions of Métis and Inuit peoples. Division IV examines how these rules operated in Alaska, households, border communities, and the career of the Tlingit lawyer and legislator William Paul Sr. Division V considers Indigenous government, disagreement among Indigenous leaders, and the unsettled state of political membership around 1930. Throughout, the chapter argues that the struggle over citizenship was never simply a demand to enter American or Canadian political society. It was also a struggle over the continuing authority of Indigenous nations to define their own peoples.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish Indigenous citizenship, state citizenship, Canadian Indian status, and voting rights, and explain why they are not interchangeable.
  2. Explain how Indigenous nations determined membership through kinship, descent, adoption, residence, and community recognition, and why those rules changed over time.
  3. Contrast kinship-based membership with blood-quantum classifications and enrollment rolls developed through colonial administration.
  4. Analyze the treaty tradition as a relationship between political peoples, and distinguish treaty rights from citizenship rights.
  5. Reconstruct the reasoning of Elk v. Wilkins and explain how it connected tribal political relationships to the Fourteenth Amendment.
  6. Trace the routes to United States citizenship before 1924, including treaties, allotment, marriage, special statutes, and veterans' legislation.
  7. Assess the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 by distinguishing what the statute enacted from later claims about its effects.
  8. Explain why citizenship did not guarantee suffrage, using state restrictions, guardianship classifications, and administrative practice.
  9. Trace Canadian legal definitions from 1857 to 1876 and explain enfranchisement as a loss of status rather than an ordinary grant of equal citizenship.
  10. Analyze the gender discrimination in Canadian status law and its effects on women, children, and communities.
  11. Compare the experiences of Alaska Natives, Métis, Inuit, and border-crossing peoples with those of reservation and reserve communities.
  12. Evaluate competing Indigenous positions on citizenship and sovereignty, and judge whether state citizenship and Indigenous nationhood could coexist.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who held the authority to decide that a person belonged to an Indigenous nation, and how did that authority change under colonial pressure?
  2. Why did the United States treat tribal political relationships as a barrier to citizenship while asserting broad jurisdiction over Indigenous people?
  3. What was the difference between a treaty relationship between peoples and a legal relationship between a state and an individual?
  4. Why do rolls, censuses, and enrollment lists matter to the history of political membership, and what can they distort?
  5. If many Indigenous people were already citizens before 1924, what did the Indian Citizenship Act actually change?
  6. Why could a person be a citizen of the United States and still be excluded from voting?
  7. In what sense was Canadian enfranchisement a form of exclusion from Indigenous community rather than inclusion in a political order?
  8. How did the rules of status affect First Nations women, children, and families differently from men?
  9. How did international borders, Alaska's territorial status, and Métis and Inuit legal positions complicate any single story of citizenship?
  10. Could people defend Indigenous self-government while also seeking civil rights in a settler state, and how did they do so?

Historical Context

By 1880, the major wars of the nineteenth-century West were ending or had ended, the reservation and reserve systems described in Chapter 84 were largely in place, and the policy emphasis of both governments was shifting from conquest toward administration and assimilation. Chapter 85 explains how the Canadian state built its administrative control through the Indian Act, and Chapter 86 explains how the Dawes Act sought to break up communal landholding in the United States. Both policies depended on classifying people: deciding who was an Indian, who belonged to which band or tribe, who qualified for an allotment or an annuity, and who might be released from special legal status. Citizenship, in this setting, was not a neutral gift. It was a legal category that governments linked deliberately to land, education, and cultural transformation.

The legal ground for these policies had been laid earlier. In the United States, a line of Marshall Court decisions in the 1830s described tribes as "domestic dependent nations," neither fully foreign nor fully subject to state authority. The Constitution excluded "Indians not taxed" when counting population for representation, and the Fourteenth Amendment of 1868 granted citizenship to persons born in the United States and "subject to the jurisdiction thereof." Congress also claimed what later courts called plenary power over tribal affairs. In Canada, the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and the treaty practice of the following century had established Crown relationships with Indigenous nations, but colonial and later Dominion legislation increasingly defined who counted as an "Indian" for administrative purposes.

Indigenous political life had not been standing still. The Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, Lakota, Pueblo, Anishinaabe, Diné, and other nations maintained governing councils, kinship systems, and procedures for deciding membership that predated colonial law and continued, often under pressure, throughout this period. The Cherokee Nation had adopted a written constitution in the 1820s (Chapter 64). The Haudenosaunee Confederacy had a political tradition described in Chapter 26. Many nations had signed treaties that recognized them as political counterparts of the United States or the Crown. Their leaders did not generally regard those treaties as steps toward absorption.

Two further conditions shaped the period. First, in both countries, citizenship was tied to concepts of civilization, property, and independence that Indigenous people were often presumed to lack. Policy makers frequently assumed that individual landownership, English literacy, Christian affiliation, and abandonment of tribal ways were the markers of fitness for political membership. Second, constitutional structure divided authority. In the United States, states set most voting qualifications. In Canada, provinces controlled provincial franchises while Parliament set federal rules. The same Indigenous person could therefore be classified differently by a federal statute, a state or provincial election law, and an Indigenous council.

A final point of context concerns terminology. The word "citizen" translates imperfectly across these traditions. In Canada before the Canadian Citizenship Act took effect in 1947, people born in Canada were generally British subjects within a shared imperial nationality, and the modern term "Canadian citizen" should not be read back into earlier decades without care. In the United States, "citizen" had a constitutional meaning that was itself contested for Indigenous people until 1924. Indigenous languages and political thought supplied their own concepts of membership, obligation, and relationship. This chapter uses "citizen" for state membership, "citizen" or "member" for Indigenous national membership where a nation uses that term, and specifies the system whenever there is risk of confusion.

Indigenous Nationhood and the Meaning of Political Membership

Sovereignty, community belonging, treaties, and colonial classifications

Section 93.1

Citizenship Before Colonial Citizenship: Indigenous Nations and Political Belonging

Any account of Indigenous citizenship must begin before the citizenship laws of the United States and Canada, because the people those laws addressed already belonged to political communities. A Mohawk woman of the Turtle clan, a Cherokee man registered in his town, an Oglala Lakota child born into a tiyospaye, a Hopi or Zuni member of a pueblo, an Anishinaabe person who inherited a dodem, and a Diné person with a place in a web of clans all stood in relationships of rights and obligations that governed land use, ceremony, marriage, defense, dispute, and leadership. English words such as "citizen," "member," and "enrolled" translate these relationships only partially. They are nonetheless useful for historians if applied with care, because they point to a real feature of Indigenous politics: belonging carried duties and protections that were enforced by community institutions.

These nations were not interchangeable. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, whose origins in the Great Law of Peace are examined in Chapter 26, joined the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, and later the Tuscarora, in a confederal system in which clans cut across nations and clan mothers had recognized authority over the selection and removal of chiefs. Cherokee political life, treated in Chapters 28 and 64, was organized around towns and seven matrilineal clans before the Nation adopted written constitutional government in the early nineteenth century. Lakota society, discussed in Chapters 29 and 49, was organized into the Oceti Sakowin or Seven Council Fires, with bands and extended-family units called tiyospaye as the working political communities. Pueblo villages, discussed in Chapter 30, combined village governments with ceremonial societies and kin networks. Anishinaabe communities, treated in Chapter 27, were linked by patrilineal clan identities, shared ceremonial institutions, and alliances. The Diné, also discussed in Chapter 30, recognized descent through the mother's clan and a second relationship to the father's clan.

Political membership must be distinguished from several related things. It was not the same as ethnicity, a modern concept that can obscure the fact that nations incorporated outsiders and that individuals with similar ancestry might belong to different nations. It was not simply ancestry, since descent was one rule among several and could be overridden by adoption or by loss of community standing. It was not identical to residence, since a person might live far from a community and still belong, or live within it without full status. And it was not the same as cultural participation, though language, ceremony, and shared obligations often mattered to recognition. Nations combined these elements differently and revised their rules as circumstances changed.

The evidence for earlier membership practices has distinctive limits. Many descriptions were written by missionaries, traders, officials, and anthropologists who sometimes assumed that tribes were fixed units with uniform rules. Ethnographic accounts from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries describe societies that had already experienced colonial pressure, and historians must be cautious about projecting those descriptions into the distant past. Oral tradition, treaty records, council minutes, constitutions, and the testimony of Indigenous authorities provide corrective evidence, as Chapter 1 emphasized. The result is a picture with real variation and with real continuity: nations had membership rules, they applied them through institutions, and they changed them for reasons internal to their own political lives as well as in response to outside pressure.

This foundation permits the chapter's central distinction between two sources of political belonging. One is membership determined by an Indigenous nation through its own laws and relationships. The other is citizenship granted or recognized by an external state, which defines persons according to the state's own purposes. These two systems could overlap, as when a person was both a member of a nation and a citizen of the United States. They could also collide, when a state classified someone in ways that contradicted a nation's judgment, or when it declared that a person's tribal relationship barred him from state membership. Much of the history that follows turns on that collision.

Section 93.2

Kinship, Descent, Adoption, Community Recognition, and Indigenous Membership Laws

Indigenous nations determined belonging through several overlapping mechanisms, and their rules were neither uniform nor static. Descent was one principle. Among the Haudenosaunee, clan membership and nation membership passed through the mother. A child of a Mohawk mother belonged to her clan and nation whatever the nationality of the father. This rule remained a core feature of Haudenosaunee law long after United States and Canadian officials assumed that descent followed the father. Among the Anishinaabe, the dodem or clan identity was ordinarily inherited from the father, and the Diné traced the first clan from the mother. Matrilineal and patrilineal systems produced different arrangements for inheritance, marriage, and responsibility, and neither can be treated as the generic "Indian" rule.

Adoption was equally important. The Haudenosaunee incorporated captives, refugees, and allied peoples, and the admission of the Tuscarora as a sixth nation of the Confederacy in the early eighteenth century is the best-known instance of collective incorporation. Lakota people made relatives through the hunka ceremony, creating kinship obligations between people who were not related by birth. Diné clans include groups whose names point to the incorporation of outsiders over generations, a pattern documented in Diné accounts of clan origins. Cherokee councils recognized adopted citizens, and the Nation's constitutional laws after 1839 addressed questions of citizenship by descent, intermarriage, and adoption. These practices show that membership was a matter of social relationship as well as biological descent, and that nations could widen their communities deliberately.

Marriage, residence, and community recognition also mattered. A person who married into a community might acquire membership under some systems and not others. Residence could confirm membership, and long absence, or conduct regarded as a betrayal of community obligations, could jeopardize it. Participation in collective responsibilities, from ceremonial roles to defense and the sharing of resources, expressed and sustained belonging. Recognition by clan relatives, councils, and neighbors was in many places the ultimate test. Some nations maintained formal procedures, such as council decisions on adoption, while others relied more on informal acknowledgment embedded in kinship.

Membership rules could change, and nations changed them. Cherokee law after the Civil War had to address the status of Black people formerly held in slavery by Cherokee citizens, and the Treaty of 1866 between the United States and the Cherokee Nation provided for their citizenship, a provision whose implementation remained contested (see Chapter 64 and Chapter 144). Cherokee legislation and courts also repeatedly addressed the rights of non-Cherokee spouses and their descendants. Such episodes indicate that membership law was an active field of Indigenous governance in the nineteenth century, shaped by internal debate as well as by federal demands. The Haudenosaunee and other nations likewise maintained and defended their own rules in the face of outside interference.

It is important to distinguish these kinship systems from blood-quantum classifications. Fractional measures of "Indian blood" appeared in colonial and state laws as instruments for defining who counted as Indian for purposes of legal disabilities, land, or benefits. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, federal agents recorded degrees of "blood" on rolls such as those prepared for allotment, and the practice entered some tribal constitutions in the twentieth century through federally sponsored governing documents (Chapters 97 and 144). Historians disagree about how much of the modern enrollment landscape reflects imposed categories and how much reflects Indigenous decisions to adopt them. It would be inaccurate to describe every modern enrollment rule as either wholly traditional or wholly colonial. Some nations adopted descent or quantum standards for their own reasons, including protection of limited resources and political cohesion. Others retained or restored kinship-based approaches. What is clear is that Indigenous nations themselves, not merely federal agencies, have long histories of making and interpreting membership rules, and that colonial administration forced those rules into contact with categories designed for other purposes.

The collision became especially acute in Indian Territory. The Five Tribes maintained their own citizenship laws and, in some cases, their own bodies to hear claims of citizenship. After 1893, however, the Dawes Commission was given authority to determine who was entitled to enrollment, and its decisions could override or review those of the nations' own institutions. The Curtis Act of 1898 curtailed tribal courts and further reduced the capacity of the nations to determine their own membership for purposes of allotment. Disputes over adopted citizens, intermarried non-citizens, and freedmen therefore became disputes over whether the nations or the United States would have the final word. The episode shows how a federal enrollment process could displace an Indigenous nation's authority at the very moment when membership determined access to the nation's land.

Section 93.3

Treaties, Sovereignty, and the Relationship between Nations

Treaties were the principal instruments through which Indigenous nations and colonial governments defined their relationships, and the question of citizenship cannot be separated from what treaties meant. The Haudenosaunee tradition associated with the Two Row Wampum describes an arrangement between separate peoples traveling side by side in their own vessels without interfering in each other's course. Scholars debate how far back the tradition can be carried and how it should be read against surviving written records, but its significance is clear: it frames treaty relations as relationships among distinct political peoples, to be maintained through continuing renewal. The Covenant Chain, discussed in Chapter 56, expressed a related understanding of alliance as an obligation requiring regular attention.

The Treaty of Canandaigua, concluded on November 11, 1794 between the United States and the Six Nations, illustrates the point. Negotiated for the United States by Timothy Pickering during the presidency of George Washington, it acknowledged Haudenosaunee lands, pledged that the United States would not disturb them, and provided for annual goods to be delivered to the Confederacy. For the United States, the treaty became part of the body of federal law that courts would later construe. For the Haudenosaunee, it remained a living agreement between nations, commemorated through the annual delivery of treaty cloth and invoked in later assertions of separate nationhood. The same document could therefore support two readings: a federal statute-like instrument within American law, and a covenant between sovereign peoples that continued to bind both parties.

The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 shows the Plains version of this problem. Between the United States and the Lakota, along with Arapaho and others, it recognized the Great Sioux Reservation and reserved hunting rights beyond it. Its twelfth article provided that no cession of reservation land would be valid unless signed by at least three-fourths of the adult male Indians occupying or interested in it. That provision treated the consent of the community as the basis of collective authority over territory. It also showed how a treaty could contain provisions that moved toward assimilation, including encouragement of agriculture and education. Later events, including the seizure of the Black Hills discussed in Chapter 77, tested whether the United States would honor the consent requirement. The Supreme Court's 1903 decision in Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock, which affirmed congressional power to modify treaty arrangements with Indian nations, indicated how far federal doctrine had moved from the treaty-based conception.

In Canada, treaty making followed a different course but raised similar questions. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 had recognized the need for Crown dealings with Indigenous nations over land, and the numbered treaties of the 1870s, examined in Chapter 82, extended that practice across the prairies and beyond. Treaty 6, negotiated in 1876, and Treaty 7, negotiated in 1877, are typical in that they combined provisions for reserves, annuities, and assistance with Crown language of surrender. Indigenous participants, as Chapter 82 explains, often understood the agreements as creating relationships of kinship and mutual obligation with the Crown, in which the Crown's representatives became relatives. The written texts, produced in English, emphasized land cession. The divergence between spoken understandings and written terms became a central issue in later treaty interpretation.

Treaty rights and citizenship rights must be distinguished. Treaty rights belong to nations and to their members as members of those nations. They arise from agreements between peoples and are exercised collectively or through membership. Citizenship in the United States or Canada is an individual relationship to a state, defined by that state's laws. The two could overlap, as when an Indigenous person who was both a citizen and a treaty beneficiary enjoyed protection under each. They could also come into tension, if officials argued that citizenship ended special treaty relationships, or if Indigenous leaders feared that accepting citizenship would be read as consent to give up treaty rights. The 1924 statute addressed one version of this fear by providing that citizenship would not impair tribal or other property rights.

Section 93.4

Colonial Enumeration, Enrollment, and the State's Power to Classify

Colonial governments did not merely recognize Indigenous people. They counted, listed, and defined them, and the records they created became instruments of power. In the United States, agents prepared annuity rolls to distribute treaty payments, census rolls recording each reservation population, and, after the General Allotment Act of 1887, lists of eligible allottees. The federal decennial census enumerated "Indians not taxed" separately for many decades, reflecting the constitutional exclusion. Annual Indian census rolls compiled by agency superintendents between the late 1880s and the 1940s recorded names, ages, family relations, and sometimes degree of "blood." In Canada, treaty paylists and band lists identified recipients of annuities, and the Indian Act gave officials authority to decide who counted as a member of a band for purposes of land, funds, and residence.

The Dawes Commission is a revealing case. Congress created it in 1893 to negotiate with the Five Tribes in Indian Territory, and its enrollment work produced the Final Rolls, approved in 1907, that divided applicants into categories such as citizens by blood, freedmen, and intermarried citizens. These rolls determined who could receive allotments of the tribal domain. Some applicants were enrolled, some were refused, and families were sometimes divided among categories or between rolls. Stated degrees of blood were generally those reported by applicants or witnesses and recorded by clerks, not independent measurements. The rolls nonetheless became the legal basis for allotment and, later, for descendants' claims of eligibility for membership, showing how a record created for one purpose acquired lasting authority for others.

The consequences of classification were material. Inclusion on a roll could determine whether a family received land, annuity payments, access to schools or medical services, or recognition as a treaty beneficiary. Exclusion could mean loss of all of these. Classification also shaped political standing. A person classified as under federal supervision was treated differently by state and local officials from one classified as a citizen outside Indian country. Rolls could misspell or substitute names, record English names assigned by agents, omit people who were absent at enrollment, or mislabel kin relationships. They could include people who claimed membership opportunistically, and could exclude people whom communities regarded as members. Such records therefore do not simply reveal identity. They reveal how a state defined identity at a particular moment for particular administrative purposes.

In Canada, the same pattern appeared in different forms. The Department of Indian Affairs maintained band lists and annuity paylists, and officials adjudicated disputes over membership. The 1876 Act and its amendments, discussed in Chapter 85 and below, gave government officers power to determine entitlement. Enrolment could also be shaped by choices made at treaty time. Métis people who took scrip rather than treaty annuities, for example, were treated as outside the Indian Act's definition. The state thus used lists created for payment and land allocation to decide who would be treated as Indian under law, and then used that classification to determine who might be enfranchised, excluded from voting, or removed from band membership.

This machinery bridges the history of Indigenous nationhood and the competing U.S. and Canadian legal systems examined next. It meant that when governments debated citizenship, they were already working from classifications they had created and administered. It also meant that Indigenous nations negotiated with, adapted to, and sometimes resisted a bureaucratic definition of who they were. Later chapters on enrollment and identity (Chapters 97 and 144) show how nations adopted, modified, or replaced these categories.

Section 93.5

The Fourteenth Amendment, Indigenous Exclusion, and Elk v. Wilkins (1884)

The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, declared that all persons born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction were citizens of the United States and of the state where they lived. It was written to overturn the Supreme Court's 1857 decision in Dred Scott v. Sandford and to secure the citizenship of formerly enslaved people. The phrase "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" was added to qualify the birthright principle, and during the 1866 Senate debates on the preceding Civil Rights Act and the amendment, several senators stated that Indians who maintained tribal relations were not subject to United States jurisdiction in the intended sense. The Civil Rights Act of 1866 itself excluded "Indians not taxed," echoing language already present in the Constitution's provisions on representation. The drafting history is consistent with a view that tribal Indians were regarded as members of separate political communities, though historians continue to debate how settled that understanding was.

The question reached the Supreme Court through John Elk. Elk was born into an Indian tribe, but by the time of his lawsuit he had left it, lived in Omaha, Nebraska, and, according to his claim, had submitted himself fully to the laws of Nebraska and the United States. In April 1880 he attempted to register to vote and was refused by registrar Charles Wilkins. Elk sued for damages. The case was decided on November 3, 1884. Justice Horace Gray wrote for a majority of seven that Elk was not a citizen. The Court reasoned that Indians born as members of tribes were, although born within the geographic limits of the United States, not born "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States in the sense required by the Fourteenth Amendment, because they owed immediate allegiance to their tribes. Elk had not been naturalized by treaty or statute, and the Court held that an individual could not unilaterally alter his political status simply by leaving the tribe and placing himself under state and federal law. Justice John Marshall Harlan, joined by Justice William Woods, dissented, arguing that Elk had abandoned tribal relations, was subject to the laws, and should be treated as a citizen by birth.

The majority's reasoning relied on a view of tribes as distinct political communities with a status the Court compared, in some respects, to that of foreign nations. This treatment was not accompanied by any acknowledgment of tribal independence in practice. Congress had passed the Major Crimes Act in 1885, a year after Elk, extending federal criminal jurisdiction over certain offenses by Indians on reservations, and in United States v. Kagama (1886) the Court upheld that statute as a valid exercise of federal power over dependent Indian communities. The historical contradiction is therefore sharp. Indigenous people could be subjected to extensive federal authority, yet their legal recognition as members of separate political communities was the stated reason they were excluded from citizenship.

The consequences of Elk extended in two directions. It clarified that citizenship would require legislative or treaty action, and thus invited Congress to establish pathways, which it did in the Dawes Act of 1887. It also left unanswered what the status of tribal members actually was. They were not aliens in the ordinary sense, since they were born in the United States and the government claimed authority over them. They were not citizens. They were, in the language of the era, wards, subject to a distinctive guardianship. That ambiguity would shape decades of litigation and administration.

Section 93.6

Citizenship through Treaties, Allotment, Marriage, and Military Service

Citizenship for Indigenous people in the United States before 1924 came through several separate routes, none of them universal. One route ran through treaties. Some nineteenth-century treaties contained provisions allowing tribal members to become citizens under specified conditions. The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek of 1830, which provided for Choctaw removal, allowed Choctaw individuals who remained in Mississippi and registered to become citizens, and a number of treaties with Kansas and Midwestern peoples in the 1850s and 1860s contained comparable arrangements. Such provisions typically paired citizenship with land allotment and withdrawal from tribal relationships, reflecting the assumption that political membership in the United States was incompatible with continued tribal membership.

A second route ran through marriage. An act of 1888 provided that an Indian woman who married a United States citizen would herself become a citizen. The provision carried the era's assumption that a woman's political status followed her husband's, and it applied to Indian women in the same way it did to others. A third route ran through special legislation for particular peoples or regions. Congress in 1901 extended citizenship to Indians in Indian Territory, building on the allotment process there, and other statutes addressed individual groups.

The most consequential route ran through allotment. Section 6 of the General Allotment Act of 1887 declared that an Indian who received an allotment, and an Indian who voluntarily took up residence separate from any tribe and adopted "the habits of civilized life," would become a citizen of the United States, without impairing the right to tribal or other property. The provision connected three things that policy makers regarded as linked: individual landholding, separation from tribal relations, and citizenship. The Act's benefits were conditioned on alleged civilization, and administrators had wide discretion in deciding who qualified. In practice, early citizenship under allotment was complicated by the trust period during which allotted land was held by the United States and the allottee remained under supervision. In Matter of Heff (1905), the Supreme Court held that allottee citizenship ended federal power to regulate liquor sales to allottees. Congress responded in the Burke Act of 1906, which delayed citizenship until a fee patent was issued at the end of the trust period or earlier, and gave the Secretary of the Interior authority to issue patents to individuals judged competent. The Supreme Court in United States v. Nice (1916) overruled Heff and held that citizenship did not end federal authority to protect restricted land. The land-loss consequences of competency patents belong to Chapter 86. For this chapter, the key point is that citizenship could be tied to a judgment by federal administrators about an individual's fitness.

This created a distinction between citizenship as a legal policy and citizenship as a reward for assimilation. When treaties or statutes conferred citizenship on all members of a group, the status followed from law. When allotment or competency determinations conferred it, officials often assessed a person's way of life, which meant that citizenship could be withheld or granted according to administrators' ideas of civilization. The same system could also be used to strip protections. Individuals who received fee patents early frequently lost their land through sale, taxation, or fraud, and the citizenship that accompanied the patent did not shield them. Indigenous responses varied. Some individuals sought citizenship as a route to legal standing, protection against local officials, and eligibility to vote. Others rejected arrangements that required them to separate from their nations.

A fourth route emerged during the First World War. Congress in 1919 authorized honorably discharged Indian veterans of the war to apply for citizenship through a court procedure, with the right to tribal property preserved. The experience of Indigenous military service, its motivations, and its political consequences belong to Chapter 94. For present purposes, the 1919 act is significant because it created a group-specific, application-based route and thereby set a precedent for broader legislation five years later. Estimates of how many Indigenous people in the United States were already citizens by 1924 vary, but it is commonly stated that roughly two-thirds had acquired citizenship by one of these routes, while a substantial minority, often numbered in the low hundreds of thousands at most and commonly cited as about 125,000, had not. These figures should be treated as approximate.

Citizenship by patent also exposed individuals to local government in new ways. Once allotted land passed out of trust, it could be taxed by counties and sold for unpaid taxes, and citizen-owners were in principle subject to the same civil and criminal jurisdiction as other residents of the state. Some Indigenous people welcomed access to state courts. Others found that local officials and neighbors treated them as outsiders whatever their legal status. The subsequent loss of land, a subject of Chapter 86, meant that the citizenship acquired through allotment was frequently followed by dispossession, which is one reason that Indigenous opinion about the link between citizenship and land was divided.

Section 93.7

The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924: Federal Recognition and Its Limits

The Indian Citizenship Act of June 2, 1924 is one of the best-known statutes in the history of Indigenous North America, and one of the most misunderstood. It was short. It declared that all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States were citizens, and it contained a proviso that the grant of citizenship would not in any manner impair or otherwise affect the right of any Indian to tribal or other property. President Calvin Coolidge signed it. Representative Homer P. Snyder of New York, chair of the House Committee on Indian Affairs, was its principal sponsor.

The legislative context involved several converging pressures. The piecemeal system of citizenship pathways had left roughly a third of Indigenous people outside citizenship by the early 1920s, while allotment and the Burke Act had already made large numbers into citizens. Indigenous veterans of the First World War had drawn public attention, and the 1919 statute had shown the willingness of Congress to act. Reform organizations, including the Indian Rights Association and the Society of American Indians (Chapter 92), supported citizenship legislation, and in late 1923 a national advisory body called the Committee of One Hundred, convened at the invitation of the Secretary of the Interior, endorsed a reform program that included citizenship. Some legislators and officials favored citizenship as a step toward reducing federal responsibility. Historians disagree about the balance of motives, and the record shows that assimilationist hopes, reformist sympathy, recognition of military service, and political convenience all played parts.

Several features of the statute matter. First, it extended citizenship to those not already citizens. It did not make Indigenous people citizens for the first time across the board, because many already were. Second, it did not require an individual application, a competence finding, or a showing of assimilation, and it did not require abandonment of tribal membership. The statute created a collective grant by law. Third, its proviso preserved tribal and other property rights, a response to fears that citizenship would be used to dissolve tribal holdings. Fourth, it did not abolish federal trusteeship, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, or treaty obligations. Indigenous people who became citizens in 1924 remained subject to the special federal relationship that applied to Indians.

The statute's silence is also significant. It said nothing about voting, which was governed by state law. It did not address how federal citizenship related to membership in a tribal nation, though later doctrine would treat Indians as holding both. It did not address Indigenous people born outside the United States, including those born in Canada and living in the United States, whose status depended on immigration and nationality law. And it did not clarify whether citizenship altered the government-to-government relationship between tribes and the United States. Later courts held that citizenship and tribal membership could coexist, but that conclusion was reached through subsequent litigation and legislation, not through the text of the 1924 Act.

Claims about the Act's effects should be distinguished from what it enacted. It did not resolve Indigenous political inequality. It did not end state barriers to voting. It did not terminate tribal governments or treaty rights. Nor was it uniformly welcomed. The Onondaga Nation and other Haudenosaunee governments rejected the premise that the United States could impose citizenship on their citizens, a position examined in Section 93.18. Other Indigenous leaders and organizations welcomed the Act as recognition of legal standing, while criticizing the paternalism that continued. The statute is best read as a limited federal measure that resolved one uncertainty about status while leaving several larger questions open.

Section 93.8

Citizenship without the Vote: State Restrictions and Indigenous Disenfranchisement

Citizenship did not automatically bring the vote. The Constitution left the setting of voter qualifications largely to the states, subject to later amendments forbidding denial of the vote on account of race or sex. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) and the Nineteenth (1920) restricted certain forms of exclusion, but states retained wide discretion over residency, property, taxation, education, and other conditions. Federal citizenship in 1924 therefore left the question of whether a particular Native citizen could vote to state constitutions, statutes, and the practice of local election officials.

States used several mechanisms. Some state constitutions excluded "Indians not taxed," language that had been reconciled with citizenship only awkwardly, and some officials argued that residents of tax-exempt trust land fell within it. Some excluded persons under guardianship, and officials interpreted this to include Indians as wards of the federal government. In Porter v. Hall (1928), the Arizona Supreme Court held that Indians living on reservations were under guardianship and so could not vote. New Mexico's constitution contained "Indians not taxed" language that was used to exclude Pueblo and other Native voters until it was challenged after the Second World War. Other states imposed residency requirements that treated reservation residence as insufficient to establish residence in the county or state, and Maine and Utah were among the states that excluded reservation residents into the 1950s.

Literacy tests, poll taxes, and administrative practices created further obstacles. Even where no explicit exclusion existed, registration might require travel to a county seat, English-language proficiency, proof of residence, or payment of fees. Registrars had discretion that could be exercised against Native applicants. Language barriers affected people who spoke Indigenous languages, and long distances affected reservation residents. The difference between formal possession of a right and the administrative ability to exercise it was significant in practice.

The effects varied. In states with large numbers of Native citizens already established through allotment or special legislation, such as Oklahoma, Native people voted and held office. The Kaw politician Charles Curtis of Kansas, who served in the House and Senate and became Vice President of the United States in 1929, is a prominent example of Indigenous participation in federal politics, though his support for allotment policy was controversial among Indigenous people and his career should not be taken as representative. In other states, reservation residents were excluded for decades. Native citizens living off reservations in towns and cities might vote more readily than those on reservations, though they faced other forms of discrimination.

The postwar decades brought change. In 1948, a federal court in Trujillo v. Garley, brought by Miguel Trujillo of Isleta Pueblo, a Marine veteran, struck down New Mexico's exclusion, and the Arizona Supreme Court in Harrison v. Laveen the same year reversed its earlier position. Utah lifted its exclusion in 1957, and later federal action, including the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and its amendments, addressed literacy tests and language barriers. These developments belong to later chapters, including Chapters 104 and 114, and lie outside the period of this chapter. They matter here because they show that the gap between citizenship and the vote persisted for decades after 1924.

Section 93.9

The Gradual Civilization Act, the Indian Act, and Enfranchisement

The Canadian approach to Indigenous political membership grew from legislation that defined who counted as an "Indian" and offered a statutory way out of that classification. In 1857 the legislature of the Province of Canada passed the Gradual Civilization Act, whose title announced its aim of encouraging the gradual civilization of Indian tribes and amending the laws respecting Indians. It allowed an Indian man who was at least twenty-one, able to read and write in English or French, reasonably educated, free of debt, and of good moral character to apply to be examined by commissioners. After a probationary period, a successful applicant could receive a grant of land carved from the reserve and be declared enfranchised. Enfranchisement here did not mean obtaining the vote in the modern sense. It meant ceasing to be legally an Indian. Very few men applied, and Indigenous communities generally regarded the process as an attack on the collective land base and community membership.

After Confederation in 1867, the Dominion government extended and consolidated this approach. The Gradual Enfranchisement Act of 1869 expanded the powers of the Superintendent General, introduced a system of elected band councils that officials could use to replace hereditary or customary governments, and defined Indian status through the male line, as Section 93.10 examines. The Indian Act of 1876 consolidated existing legislation into a single statute. It defined who was an "Indian" for legal purposes, regulated reserve land through devices such as location tickets that recorded an individual's possession of a parcel with government approval, and set out the conditions under which an Indian could be enfranchised. Chapter 85 treats the administrative structure of the Act. This section concentrates on status and enfranchisement.

Enfranchisement operated through several mechanisms. A person could apply and, after meeting the statutory tests and a trial period, be enfranchised by order of the government. The 1876 Act also provided that Indians who obtained certain professional qualifications, including medical and legal degrees, or who entered clerical office, would be enfranchised by the operation of the Act. In many cases the enfranchisement of a man extended to his wife and minor children, meaning that other family members lost status even though they had not themselves applied. Enfranchised individuals ceased to be members of the band, lost the right to live on the reserve or share in its collective property, and might receive in exchange a payment representing a share of band funds or a parcel of land. They became legally ordinary subjects of the Crown, subject to the general law.

It is essential to distinguish voluntary enfranchisement from mechanisms that operated without individual consent. The inclusion of wives and children in a man's enfranchisement, the automatic enfranchisement of those with certain qualifications, and, from 1920, the authorization of compulsory enfranchisement all removed the choice of the people affected. In 1920, amendments to the Indian Act authorized the Superintendent General to order the enfranchisement of an Indian whom officials judged fit, without the person's application and without band consent. Duncan Campbell Scott, the Deputy Superintendent General, defended the policy in testimony before a parliamentary committee, expressing the aim that Indians be "absorbed into the body politic." Indigenous organizations and communities, including the Six Nations at Grand River, vigorously opposed the measure, and the compulsory provisions were repealed in 1922. A weaker form returned in the Indian Act amendments of 1933. These episodes show that the state treated enfranchisement as a tool of assimilation, not as the recognition of an equal right.

The term itself invites misreading. In ordinary modern usage, to enfranchise someone is to give the right to vote. In the Indian Act, it meant removal from the Act's definition of Indian. An enfranchised person might or might not be able to vote, depending on property qualifications and provincial rules, as Section 93.11 explains. For Indigenous communities, enfranchisement meant the loss of members, land, and political voice within the nation, and the Act gave them little control over who left or what was owed to the community. For the government, it was a step toward eliminating the legal category of Indian and ending the special relationship. It is therefore inaccurate to describe enfranchisement as an ordinary exercise of equal citizenship. It was a mechanism by which one lost a collective legal and community position in return for conditional absorption into the settler political order.

Uptake remained low across the period. Only a few hundred people were enfranchised by application in the decades after 1857, a figure that tells its own story about how Indigenous people judged the choice. Many who were enfranchised did so for individual reasons, including property, employment, education, or the desire to escape restrictions on movement, and some later sought reinstatement, which the law at times did not allow. Historians continue to examine individual cases as well as the aggregate statistics, since the statutory record offers little evidence of the reasoning of the people affected.

Enfranchisement also bore directly on the reserve land base. A man who was enfranchised might receive title to a parcel carved from the reserve or a payment in lieu of his share, and in either case the community lost a member and, often, part of its territory. Band councils and communities generally had little authority to prevent an enfranchisement approved by officials, and they had no power to reverse it. Officials sometimes treated each enfranchisement as progress toward reducing the number of Indians and the size of reserves. Indigenous communities understood the same transaction as a subtraction from the collective. The divergence in perspective helps to explain both the low rate of voluntary applications and the intensity of opposition to compulsory enfranchisement in 1920.

Historical text of the Indian Act, S.C. 1876, c. 18 (Indigenous Services Canada). Students should distinguish the statute’s definitions, marriage provisions and enfranchisement procedure.

Context: The statute consolidated earlier colonial and Dominion legislation and provided the legal framework for status, band membership, reserve land, and enfranchisement.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: The Act defines an "Indian" by reference to male persons of Indian blood reputed to belong to a band, their children, and women lawfully married to such men. It provides that an Indian woman who marries someone other than an Indian ceases to be an Indian under the Act, that enfranchisement removes a person from band membership, and that certain professional qualifications lead to enfranchisement.

Analysis: The statute shows definitions tied to descent through men, the legal significance of band membership, and the use of professional achievement and application as tests for removal from status. It displays the Dominion's integrated approach to land, membership, and assimilation.

Limitations: A statute cannot show how often provisions were applied, how officials exercised discretion, or how families experienced the rules. Administrative records, correspondence, and Indigenous testimony are needed. Students should read the Government of Canada's historical text directly and consult the specific sections cited by scholars.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the Dominion government. Missing are the views of First Nations governments, women affected by the status rules, and communities that opposed enfranchisement.

Section 93.10

Gender, Marriage, Descent, and the Legal Exclusion of First Nations Women

Gender was not a secondary feature of Canadian status law. It was one of its organizing principles. Earlier colonial statutes, including one of 1850 in Lower Canada, had defined Indians in ways that recognized non-Indian spouses who joined Indian communities. The Gradual Enfranchisement Act of 1869 and the Indian Act of 1876 shifted to a patrilineal and patriarchal definition. A woman who was a status Indian and who married a man without Indian status ceased to be an Indian under the statute, and the children of such a marriage were not counted as Indians. Conversely, a non-status woman who married a status man became legally an Indian, and their children had status. Canadian government historical documentation confirms the discriminatory rules and their unequal application.

The consequences were far-reaching. A woman who lost status lost her legal membership in her band, which meant losing the right to reside on the reserve, to share in band funds, to hold or inherit a share of reserve land, and could face exclusion or disputes over burial in a community cemetery, depending on local decisions and practice; such burial exclusion was not a uniform provision stated in the 1876 statute. Her children, who in a Haudenosaunee or other matrilineal community would have been full members by birth, were excluded. Women who retained status faced restrictions as well. Band council elections under the 1869 Act and later provisions limited voting to men, and women were not given the vote in band elections until the revision of the Indian Act in 1951. Nineteenth-century legislation also gave officials discretion over the rights of widows, including on grounds relating to alleged immorality.

These rules clashed with Indigenous practices in communities that traced descent through women. For Haudenosaunee people, where clan mothers held authority and membership followed the mother, the federal definition directly contradicted community law. A woman who married a non-Haudenosaunee man and lost federal status did not, in Haudenosaunee terms, cease to be a member of her clan and nation, and her children remained clan members. The Indian Act thus created a divergence between legal status and community belonging. The state classification could alter who lived on reserve land, who inherited property, and who could take part in community institutions, but it could not extinguish a person's kinship or her community's recognition of her, where the community chose to maintain it.

Documented experiences of women who lost status survive only in part. Official records show administrative decisions, and later testimony, litigation, and activism preserve personal accounts. Women who lost status often had to leave their communities, sometimes lost property, and in some cases returned only after decades. Their children grew up outside the legal category and often outside the community. The effects ran across generations: grandchildren might be unable to claim membership or land rights that their grandmothers had once held. The long campaign that ended with the 1985 amendments to the Indian Act, widely known as Bill C-31, and cases such as Attorney General of Canada v. Lavell (1973) and the complaint of Sandra Lovelace Nicholas to the United Nations Human Rights Committee (decided in 1981), belong to later chapters (Chapters 117 and 144). Their significance here is that the discriminatory rules were established in the nineteenth century and endured for well over a century.

The historical analysis should keep distinct what the state did and what communities did. The federal government's classification of a woman was a legal act with serious material consequences. It was not a statement of her identity, her ancestry, or her recognition by relatives. Many communities continued to regard such women as kin, and some actively resisted the application of the rules. At the same time, the legislation and the administrative structures built around it created pressures that affected community decisions, including disputes over who could remain on limited reserve land. Gendered status rules thus altered Indigenous communities from within as well as from outside, and Indigenous women's political efforts to reverse them became central to later Indigenous politics.

Cause and Effect

How Colonial Classification Reached Households and Community Membership

Long-term causes

European legal assumptions about descent through fathers and the legal subordination of married women, combined with policy aims of assimilation and the reduction of the reserve population.

Short-term causes

The consolidating legislation of 1869 and 1876, administrators' concern with controlling band membership and land, and concern over non-Indian men gaining access to reserve resources through marriage.

Immediate context

The Dominion's effort to create a single statutory definition of Indian and to administer reserves and annuities through rolls and band lists.

Immediate effects

Women who married non-status men lost status, their children were excluded, and men who married non-status women brought their wives into status.

Long-term consequences

Multigenerational loss of community connection, disputes over land and identity, and a long political movement for reform.

Historical complexity

The law was imposed by the state, but it interacted with Indigenous kinship systems, local pressures, and family decisions, so its effects varied by community.
Section 93.11

First Nations Voting Rights and the Canadian Federal Franchise

The history of First Nations voting in Canada was complicated and changed repeatedly, and it should not be reduced to a story of uniform exclusion. Before Confederation, colonial franchises were generally tied to property, and Indians held under the protection of the Crown held collective rather than individual property. After 1867, federal electoral qualifications were initially those of the provinces, so the question of who could vote in a federal election depended on provincial law. Because most provincial franchises were property-based and some excluded Indians outright, the federal vote reached few Indigenous men.

The Electoral Franchise Act of 1885, introduced by the government of Sir John A. Macdonald, created a uniform federal franchise and extended it to certain Indigenous men in eastern Canada who satisfied applicable property qualifications. The law excluded Indians in Manitoba, the North-West Territories, and British Columbia, and it excluded women. In the east, an Indian man who held real property valued above a stated threshold, individually possessed and improved, could qualify. Parliamentary debate over the provision was partisan, with opponents suggesting that the government hoped to benefit from Indigenous votes and supporters arguing that qualified Indians deserved the franchise. The number of Indigenous men who actually voted was small. Some Haudenosaunee and other communities objected that participation in Canadian elections would imply subjecthood and be inconsistent with their nationhood and treaty relations, a view taken by some leaders though not by all.

The federal Indian franchise was repealed in 1898, when new legislation returned federal voter lists to provincial lists. After that, most status Indians living on reserves were ineligible to vote in federal elections, because most provinces excluded them or applied qualifications they could not meet. Exceptions existed. An enfranchised person who no longer had Indian status and who met other qualifications might vote. During the First World War, the Military Voters Act of 1917 extended the federal military franchise to qualifying British subjects who were serving or had served in the armed forces, male or female, including First Nations people with Indian status and some personnel younger than twenty-one. The distinct Wartime Elections Act also expanded voting eligibility for specified relatives of service members while excluding other categories of electors. These wartime exceptions should not be mistaken for unrestricted federal suffrage for status First Nations people; Chapter 94 examines their military context. The Dominion Elections Act of 1920 maintained exclusion of status Indians living on reserves, with exceptions that included some veterans. Rules were amended in later years, and in 1950 a limited franchise was extended to Indians who waived their statutory tax exemption, a condition many refused. Unconditional federal voting rights for status First Nations people arrived in 1960, a development that lies beyond this chapter's main period.

Careful distinctions are necessary. Voting in federal elections, voting in provincial elections, and participation in the governance of a First Nation were different matters. Provinces set their own franchises, and the dates on which they permitted status Indians to vote varied widely, extending from the late 1940s to 1969 in the final province. Band council elections under the Indian Act were a separate institution, with their own rules, and many First Nations continued to select leaders through customary systems alongside or in opposition to imposed electoral rules. The legal position of a First Nations person therefore depended on period, province, status, property, military service, and other qualifications. It would be inaccurate to say that every First Nations person throughout Canada was excluded from every election at every date.

Indigenous views of the franchise diverged. Some leaders regarded voting rights as important for protecting interests and achieving political representation. Others feared that voting would be treated as acceptance of Canadian sovereignty or as a step toward the loss of treaty protections, including tax exemptions and the special legal relationship with the Crown. The 1950 waiver requirement intensified this concern by making the vote conditional on giving up a claimed treaty benefit. These debates show that First Nations people evaluated electoral participation in light of their own political commitments and treaty understandings, and that the Canadian state's terms for participation often required the surrender of collective rights.

Suffrage and Franchise

Definition. Suffrage and franchise both refer to the right to vote in public elections. The qualifications for exercising that right, such as age, sex, property, residence, race, literacy, or taxation, were set by law and varied by jurisdiction.

Historical context. In both the United States and Canada, voting qualifications were set at multiple levels of government, and an individual could be eligible in one election and ineligible in another.

Section 93.12

Métis, Inuit, and the Limits of Canadian Status Categories

Canadian legal categories did not map neatly onto Indigenous identities. Métis and Inuit peoples occupied positions distinct from those of status First Nations under the Indian Act, and treating them as variants of the same history would obscure the different legal and political experiences involved. Indigenous identity, treaty relationships, statutory Indian status, and electoral eligibility were four separate things, and a person could possess some without others.

The Métis, treated in Chapters 52 and 83, were not defined as Indians under the Indian Act. The Manitoba Act of 1870 set aside 1.4 million acres for the children of Métis heads of families in the new province, and the federal government later used scrip, certificates redeemable for land or money, to address Métis claims in the North-West. The scrip system was widely criticized, since many certificates were sold to speculators and the process required Métis people to accept a legal extinguishment of their claims in exchange for individual grants. The 1876 Indian Act excluded from the definition of Indian certain Métis who had taken part in the land distributions, and Métis who instead took treaty with First Nations were treated as Indians. Métis political membership rested on community and collective identity, and the relationship between citizenship and landholding was tied to the scrip system and to the political struggles of 1869 to 1870 and 1885 examined in Chapter 83. Because Métis were generally not subject to the Indian Act exclusions, their position on voting was shaped by property rules, provincial law, and local circumstances, though in practice dispossession and marginalization sharply limited their political power. Louis Riel's elections to Parliament in the 1870s, discussed in Chapter 83, show both the possibility of Métis electoral participation and the hostility it could encounter.

The legal position of the Inuit was different again. In 1939, in Re Eskimos, the Supreme Court of Canada held that Inuit were "Indians" for purposes of federal legislative authority under the British North America Act, which meant that Parliament could legislate for them. Yet Inuit were not generally subject to the Indian Act, and later legislation formally excluded them from it. They therefore lived in an unusual position: within federal jurisdiction but outside the main statutory status system. Their experience of voting and administration was shaped by remoteness, territorial administration, and the late arrival of sustained government presence, a history reserved for Chapter 100. The formal federal franchise for Inuit and its practical implementation in the Arctic did not coincide, and effective access came well after the period of this chapter.

A chronological clarification is necessary. Before the Canadian Citizenship Act took effect on January 1, 1947, Canadian nationality operated within the British-subject framework shared across the Empire, supplemented by domestic naturalization and immigration legislation. People born in Canada, including Indigenous people, were British subjects, and the use of "Canadian citizen" in earlier statutes was limited. It would be misleading to describe Indigenous people before 1947 as having been "denied Canadian citizenship" in the modern sense, or to assume that the modern term applied in earlier decades. The correct analysis distinguishes subject status, Indian status, treaty relationships, and the franchise.

These categories demonstrate a general point about Canadian law: the state constructed groups for administrative purposes, and those groups did not match Indigenous peoples' own self-understanding. A Métis person with relatives in a First Nation, an Inuit family under federal administration but outside the Act, and a status woman who had lost her status each illustrate the disjunction between legal classification and community belonging. The next division turns from legal frameworks to their operation in particular places and lives.

Section 93.13

Alaska Native Citizenship and the Politics of Territorial Voting

Alaska's place in the history of Indigenous citizenship was distinctive because it was a territory, not a state, and because the legal basis of Native status derived from the 1867 Treaty of Cession with Russia. The treaty's third article provided that inhabitants of the ceded territory, with the exception of "uncivilized native tribes," would be admitted to the rights of citizens of the United States. That exception, with its undefined and value-laden category, would be invoked for decades. Alaska Natives, including Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Iñupiat, Yupik, Unangax̂, Athabascan, and other peoples, were thus placed in a legal position that depended on whether officials judged them "civilized." The Organic Act of 1884 and the Second Organic Act of 1912, which created the Territory of Alaska with a territorial legislature, left many questions of Native political status unresolved. Chapter 99 treats Alaska Native communities and colonial administration more broadly.

In 1915, the Alaska Territorial Legislature enacted a measure establishing conditions under which Alaska Natives might be recognized as citizens. The law required applicants to show that they had severed tribal relationships and adopted the habits of what the statute called civilized life, supported by testimony from non-Native citizens. It was a rare case in which the citizenship of an entire Indigenous population was conditioned in law on demonstrating the abandonment of tribal ties. The measure placed the burden of proof on individuals, delegated judgment to local white citizens and officials, and linked political membership to cultural conformity. Native leaders, including those in the Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded in Sitka in 1912, challenged both the premise and the procedure, arguing that Natives had a claim to rights independent of such tests.

The 1924 federal Indian Citizenship Act applied in Alaska as elsewhere, extending citizenship to Alaska Natives who were not already citizens. Yet it did not settle the question of voting in the territory, where electoral qualifications were set under territorial and federal law. Territorial officials and some legislators sought to limit the practical effect of the 1924 Act. In 1925 the territorial legislature adopted a requirement that voters be able to read and write English. Two years later, in 1927, Congress enacted a federal provision establishing an English-language literacy qualification for voting in Alaska. These measures should be distinguished: the 1925 requirement was territorial legislation, while the 1927 provision was a federal act that placed the literacy qualification in federal law. Together they illustrate how citizenship legislation could be followed by measures designed to restrict its practical consequences, particularly for people whose first languages were Indigenous. The English literacy requirement remained a feature of Alaska election law for decades and was only displaced by federal action in 1970 and 1975, which suspended and then permanently banned literacy tests nationwide.

Alaska's documentary record offers valuable evidence. The state's official suffrage history records the 1915 citizenship law, the 1924 election of William Paul, and the literacy measures, while the Congressional Record preserves the 1926 debates in which proponents and opponents of the English literacy qualification stated their reasons. The Congressional Record records a concrete exchange on H.R. 9211 at page 7785: Representative Charles F. Curry defended an English-literacy rule, Representative Charles D. Carter questioned whether it would disenfranchise Indigenous voters, and Alaska delegate Dan Sutherland warned against removal of protection for people who had previously voted. In this sitting, consideration was deferred after a member objected, a procedural outcome that should not be mistaken for the final fate of the later literacy legislation. The debate is useful because it exposes the arguments used to justify limits on Native voting in the language of general civic qualification. Historians must compare these stated purposes with the likely effects, which fell heavily on Alaska Native voters whose communities had varied levels of English literacy, but not equally on all of them. Many Alaska Natives, including those educated in mission and government schools, were literate in English, and the Alaska Native Brotherhood actively encouraged registration and education.

Section 93.14

Citizenship and the Household: Marriage, Children, Property, and Inheritance

Legal classifications were not abstract. They entered households, shaping where families lived, who inherited, and whether children were counted as members. The Canadian and United States systems did this in different ways. In Canada, the central mechanism was status: a woman's marriage to a man without status could remove her and her children from the legal category of Indian, with the consequences for residence, property, and community participation described in Section 93.10. The same system could add a non-status woman to the band list on marrying a status man. Families could thus be divided by the statute, with sisters, brothers, and cousins holding different legal positions depending on whom they married.

In the United States, citizenship was tied to allotment, residence, and competence determinations. A family receiving allotments might see some members receive fee patents and become citizens earlier than others, while remaining members stayed under trust. Allotted land passed to heirs under rules that fractured parcels across generations, a story told in Chapter 86. Marriage also mattered. The 1888 law that made Indian women who married citizens into citizens linked political status to a husband's. Tribal membership rules continued to apply separately: a woman who married a non-Indian might remain a tribal member under her nation's law, even while federal rules treated her status differently, and some nations adopted disenrollment or exclusion policies for reasons of their own, which varied.

Children and inheritance revealed the stakes. Under Canadian rules, children of a status woman and a non-status father could not inherit reserve land or band benefits through her, and she might lose the ability to leave land to them. Under U.S. allotment rules, children of enrolled parents received allotments from the tribal domain, and children born after rolls closed might receive nothing, a result that arose from the arbitrary closure of enrollment lists such as the Dawes Rolls in 1907. Both cases show how legal timing, administrative lists, and family structure decided access to property.

It is important to distinguish legal eligibility for benefits and land from kinship and cultural belonging. A person excluded from a roll could still be recognized as a relative, a speaker of the language, and a participant in ceremonies, and communities developed informal ways to maintain ties. At the same time, legal exclusion could erode those ties over time, since exclusion from residence or property made participation difficult. Historians must therefore avoid two simplifications: that legal classification fully determined belonging, and that it had no effect on it.

Section 93.15

Political Belonging across Borders: Indigenous Nations and International Boundaries

Indigenous nations did not recognize the international boundaries that governments drew across their territories, and the boundaries complicated political membership in ways a single country's law cannot explain. The Canada–United States boundary crossed the homelands of the Haudenosaunee, Anishinaabe, Blackfoot, and others. The U.S.–Mexico boundary, set by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848 and adjusted by the Gadsden Purchase of 1853, divided the Tohono O'odham, Yaqui, Kumeyaay, and other peoples. Chapters 141 and 142 treat the borderlands in later periods. Here the focus is on how nationality and border control interacted with Indigenous belonging around 1880 to 1930.

The Haudenosaunee example is the clearest. Communities lived in what became New York, Ontario, and Quebec, and the boundary survey of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries bisected the territory of Akwesasne. A family might live on both sides, with relatives in the United States and Canada, and with citizenship or status determined by different governments. The Jay Treaty of 1794 between the United States and Great Britain stated that Indians dwelling on either side of the boundary could freely pass and repass. Haudenosaunee people regarded border crossing as an inherent right, not a treaty concession, and defended it. In 1926 Clinton Rickard, a Tuscarora leader, founded the Indian Defense League of America to uphold the right of Indigenous people to cross the border freely. Later decisions in both countries, including the Supreme Court of Canada's 1956 ruling in Francis v. The Queen, treated the Jay Treaty's protection as not enforceable in Canadian law without implementing legislation, and the United States later adopted statutory provisions on Indians born in Canada. These developments show that border rights depended on competing national legal frameworks, not simply on a single Indigenous or treaty claim.

The Lakota experience illustrates nationality and tribal belonging coming apart. After the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876, discussed in Chapter 77, Sitting Bull and a large following moved north into Canada in 1877. The Canadian government treated them as American Indians in temporary refuge, not as Canadian Indians entitled to reserves, and pressed them to return, which most did in the early 1880s. Their presence showed that national borders did not erase political and kinship networks, and that governments could treat Indigenous people differently depending on which side of the line they were deemed to belong to.

On the southern border, O'odham communities straddled Sonora and Arizona, and the Gadsden Purchase placed the boundary through their homeland. The Mexican state's policies toward Indigenous peoples, including the dispossession of communal lands in the nineteenth century and the repression of Yaqui resistance in Sonora, shaped movement across the line, and Yaqui refugees settled in southern Arizona, where their community, centered near Tucson, would not obtain federal recognition until 1978. In the mid-1920s, the creation of the U.S. Border Patrol and tightened immigration regulation added new layers of control, and Indigenous people crossing for family, ceremonial, or economic reasons encountered rules written for other populations. Not every community held the same rights to cross, and treaty provisions, customary practice, and administrative discretion varied widely.

The border cases demonstrate why continental Indigenous political history cannot be understood through a single state's laws. National citizenship, tribal membership, and the right to move across the border were three different things governed by different authorities. A person could be a citizen of the United States, a member of a nation straddling the border, and a person whom Canadian officials classified differently. The distinction between nationality and belonging to an Indigenous nation is therefore essential, as is the recognition that each community's situation had its own history.

Section 93.16

William Paul Sr., Alaska Native Political Leadership, and the Exercise of Citizenship

William Paul Sr. (1885–1977), a Tlingit leader raised in the Tongass region of southeastern Alaska, offers a documented case of how an Indigenous person pursued political participation in the years surrounding the Indian Citizenship Act. He received his early education in mission and government-run institutions, studied at Whitworth in Washington State, pursued legal training through LaSalle correspondence courses, and was admitted to the Alaska Bar in 1920. He became an attorney who represented Native clients, and he was an active leader in the Alaska Native Brotherhood, which promoted Native education, civic participation, and legal rights. His training as a lawyer gave him tools for challenging discrimination and for navigating the institutions that governed Alaska Native citizenship.

Paul's political career developed alongside the legal contests over Native status. In the early 1920s, the Brotherhood pressed for Native voting and challenged the exclusion of Native children from public schools in some Alaska communities, and Paul was involved in litigation and advocacy on such issues. In 1924, Paul was elected to the Alaska Territorial Legislature, an election that Alaska's official chronology identifies as a milestone in the history of Native political participation. His election occurred in the same year as the federal Indian Citizenship Act, though he and others had already been exercising citizenship rights under earlier routes, including the 1915 territorial law and the possibility of citizenship through allotment or other means. Paul later became deeply involved in efforts to secure a legal recognition of Tlingit and Haida land claims, an effort that culminated after the period of this chapter in the 1935 jurisdictional legislation that allowed those peoples to bring claims before the Court of Claims. That later history belongs to Chapter 99.

What Paul's career reveals is both possibility and limitation. A Native person with legal training and organizational support could win election to a territorial legislature, and Native voters in some districts could elect candidates who represented their interests. At the same time, the barriers described in Section 93.13 remained, including the English literacy requirements adopted soon afterward, and the broader discrimination Native people faced in schools, employment, and public accommodations. Paul's strategy combined electoral activity with litigation, organization, and education, treating the vote as one instrument among several. It also reflected a particular view of how to pursue Indigenous rights: through participation in the institutions of the territory while maintaining the Brotherhood as an independent Native organization.

The documentary record for Paul's life is largely institutional: election records, legislative proceedings, court filings, Brotherhood proceedings and publications, and later biographical and scholarly accounts. These sources record actions and circumstances but not his private reflections, which this chapter does not attempt to reconstruct. Historians therefore need to distinguish what the sources establish, such as his correspondence legal education, admission to the Alaska Bar in 1920, his 1924 election, and his affiliation with the Brotherhood, from interpretations of his motives and strategies, which depend on inference and on evidence from his public writings and activities.

Everyday Life and Human Experience

William Paul Sr. and the Exercise of Citizenship in Alaska

This documented case focuses on identifiable actions and institutional circumstances, not on private conversations or inner thoughts that the sources do not record.

What the sources establish. Paul, a Tlingit from southeastern Alaska, trained as a lawyer, practiced law, worked with the Alaska Native Brotherhood, and was elected to the Alaska Territorial Legislature in 1924, an election Alaska's official chronology marks as significant.

The setting. Native Alaskans in the 1920s lived under overlapping rules: the 1867 treaty's language on "uncivilized" tribes, the 1915 territorial citizenship law, the 1924 federal act, and territorial election laws. Native voters in Tlingit and Haida communities faced practical obstacles to registration, discrimination in schools and public places, and, soon after, English literacy requirements.

What the case shows. Citizenship and officeholding were possible, and Native voters and organizations could use them. The same record shows that the formal status of citizen did not remove discrimination or the legislative effort to limit Native voting. It also shows Native leaders combining several strategies at once.

What the case does not show. A single career cannot represent every Alaska Native person's experience, since Alaska's peoples, languages, and conditions varied widely, and Paul's legal education was unusual.

Competing Political Futures and the Meaning of Indigenous Citizenship

Section 93.17

Indigenous Government, Community Elections, and Political Participation

Indigenous governing institutions did not disappear when the United States and Canada extended their electoral systems, and the two kinds of political participation coexisted, uneasily and in different forms. Voting in a national or provincial election and taking part in the political life of an Indigenous nation were not the same activity. The first meant joining the electorate of a settler state. The second meant exercising rights and duties within a nation's own councils, clans, and ceremonies. A person could do one, both, or neither, and communities debated which combinations were consistent with their own legitimacy.

The Haudenosaunee case is the best documented. The Confederacy council at Grand River in Ontario continued to operate through hereditary chiefs selected by clan mothers, in accordance with the Great Law. The Canadian government, relying on the elected band council system authorized by the Indian Act, regarded the traditional council as an obstacle. In 1923 Deskaheh, a Cayuga chief and speaker for the Confederacy council, traveled to Geneva to present the Haudenosaunee case to the League of Nations, traveling on a Haudenosaunee passport. The League declined to take up the matter. On September 17, 1924, the Canadian government adopted an order in council replacing the traditional governing system at Six Nations of the Grand River with an elected-band-council framework under the Indian Act. Mounted Police and Indian Affairs officials subsequently excluded the hereditary council from its council house, and an election was held on October 21, 1924. The police intervention and the election were related but distinct events, and Indigenous people at Grand River disagreed over the legitimacy of the imposed change. Deskaheh, who had been unable to return to Canada, died in 1925 in the United States. Haudenosaunee supporters of the hereditary system continued to assert its legitimacy, and the dispute over which body speaks for the community persists in modified forms. The episode shows a state using the language of elected government to displace an Indigenous government, while the targeted nation claimed standing in international affairs.

Other nations show different arrangements. The Navajo Tribal Council was established in 1923 under federal auspices, largely to provide a body that could sign oil and gas leases, and it developed into a representative institution whose relationship to Diné political traditions was debated. Pueblo communities maintained village governments, including officers drawn from Spanish-era offices alongside religious leadership, and in 1922 they organized the All Pueblo Council to oppose legislation threatening their land, an episode discussed in Chapters 92 and 95. The Cherokee Nation's government, along with those of the other Five Tribes, was curtailed by the Curtis Act of 1898 and the transition to Oklahoma statehood in 1907, with chiefs appointed by the President for limited purposes, yet the nations and their institutions persisted and later reorganized (Chapter 97). On Plains reservations, councils and societies continued to function alongside agency-directed courts and councils, and the Lakota tiyospaye remained a working political unit despite agency efforts to replace it.

Federal and provincial officials influenced political institutions in many ways. Agents recognized or refused to recognize particular leaders, approved or vetoed council decisions, controlled funds, and shaped who could take part in elections. Elected councils under the Indian Act, and similar federally sponsored bodies in the United States, were sometimes accepted by communities as useful and sometimes resisted as illegitimate. Communities debated the relationship between customary leadership and imposed structures, and in many places both existed in parallel. Women's roles in selecting and advising leaders in matrilineal communities were affected by rules that limited voting in imposed councils to men.

The primary question for historians is whether and how multiple forms of political membership could coexist. The evidence suggests that they often did, in practice, though not without tension and not on equal terms. Individuals could be tribal members, subjects or citizens of a settler state, and participants in local government at once. The tension arose where states claimed that one of these statuses displaced the others, or where one set of institutions was used to override another. Indigenous communities responded by asserting the priority of their own institutions, by participating selectively in outside politics, or by using both to pursue their aims.

Methods of choosing leaders also differed, which complicated any equation of political participation with voting. Haudenosaunee councils decided by consensus among their chiefs, with clan mothers holding recognized authority over the titles. Cherokee citizens had elected their principal chief and national council under their written constitutions since the nineteenth century, so the idea of balloting was not foreign to them. Pueblo communities generally selected officers through processes involving religious and village leadership. Where the Indian Act or an agency imposed an election, it substituted a different mechanism for these existing ones, and the question for the community was not simply whether to vote but whose authority the vote would confirm. That is why participation in imposed elections could be read by some as cooperation and by others as an act of surrender.

Section 93.18

Citizenship, Sovereignty, and Indigenous Political Disagreement

Indigenous intellectuals, organizations, and governments did not agree about citizenship. Some saw it as a protection and a means of political representation. Others saw it as a threat to treaty relationships and nationhood. Still others held that the two could be combined. The disagreement was a genuine political debate within Indigenous communities and not simply a division between accommodation and resistance.

Carlos Montezuma, the Yavapai physician who practiced in Chicago, was an outspoken critic of the Bureau of Indian Affairs and of the reservation system. He argued that federal paternalism kept Native people dependent and that the Bureau should be abolished, and he expressed these views in speeches and in his newsletter Wassaja, published between 1916 and 1922. Montezuma favored full citizenship and the removal of special federal supervision, believing that equal legal standing would free Indigenous individuals from bureaucratic control. He died in January 1923, before the 1924 Act, and his arguments are best understood as part of a pre-1924 debate about the relationship between citizenship and federal guardianship. Other members of the Society of American Indians, discussed in Chapter 92, shared support for citizenship while disagreeing about the Bureau and about reservations, and some, such as Gertrude Bonnin (Zitkala-Ša), later worked for citizenship and for broader reform.

Haudenosaunee governments articulated a different position grounded in treaty nationhood. The Onondaga Nation's published historical account states that the Haudenosaunee do not accept the United States' claim to have imposed citizenship on their citizens through the 1924 Act. This is the identifiable position of a nation and its government. It rests on treaty relationships such as Canandaigua and on the principle that the Haudenosaunee are citizens of their own nations, with their own laws and territory. It should not be read as evidence that every Indigenous person, or even every Haudenosaunee person, held the same view. Haudenosaunee individuals served in the U.S. and Canadian military, voted in some settings, and held various views, and the positions of the Confederacy council, of individual nations, and of individual citizens could differ.

Other communities took different positions. Some Native leaders welcomed citizenship because it addressed a status they considered demeaning and gave access to courts and elections. Pueblo leaders, whose communities had a distinct legal history rooted in Spanish and Mexican law, focused on land protection. Many people held layered views, supporting citizenship while insisting that it not diminish treaty rights, a position reflected in the property proviso of the 1924 Act. The disagreement cannot be reduced to a simple division between traditionalists and progressives, since each camp included people of varied backgrounds.

Treaty-based arguments and rights-based arguments were also not mutually exclusive. A leader might argue that citizenship should be freely chosen, that treaty rights should be honored, and that Native people should have access to the vote. The tension was between collective political rights, which belong to nations, and individual civil rights, which belong to persons. The next section examines how historians have framed that tension.

The Society of American Indians itself illustrates the range. Its members agreed on little beyond a general commitment to Native advancement, and they disagreed about the Bureau of Indian Affairs, about the pace of assimilation, and about the role of Indigenous traditions in modern life. Indigenous veterans who returned from the First World War held varied views as well, some regarding citizenship as the overdue recognition of service and others noting that their service had been given in defense of nations whose rights were still unsettled. Such evidence cautions against assigning a single "Native opinion" to the question and supports the chapter's emphasis on a plural Indigenous politics.

Section 93.19

Historical Debate: Could State Citizenship and Indigenous Sovereignty Coexist?

The central interpretive question of this chapter is whether state citizenship and Indigenous sovereignty could coexist. In law, the answer eventually became yes: courts and Congress came to treat Native people in the United States as citizens of the United States, of their states, and of their tribal nations. In 1924, however, the question was open, and the statute's text did not answer it. The debate below sets out major interpretations and the evidence for each, and asks students to evaluate them.

Historical Debate

Could State Citizenship and Indigenous Sovereignty Coexist?

The question: Did the extension of federal citizenship primarily expand individual rights, or did its imposition threaten the recognition of Indigenous political communities? And could people reasonably support both access to civil rights and the defense of self-government?

Interpretation A — Citizenship as an expansion of rights: Citizenship removed an anomaly, gave Native people standing in courts and the claim to equal protection, and created a basis for later voting rights litigation. The property proviso protected tribal holdings, and many Native people had already been citizens without losing their nations.
Interpretation B — Citizenship as a threat to sovereignty: A unilateral grant of citizenship by Congress, without consultation of Indigenous nations, asserted the United States' authority over Native people as individuals and could be used to argue that tribal political status had ended. Historical practice of tying citizenship to allotment and assimilation supported this concern.
Interpretation C — Coexistence and negotiated meaning: Citizenship and nationhood were not mutually exclusive in practice, and their relationship was worked out through later litigation, legislation, and Indigenous political action. Indigenous communities assigned different meanings to citizenship, from protection to imposition.

Evidence: The text of the 1924 statute, which protects tribal and other property rights but is silent on tribal governance; treaty arguments from the Haudenosaunee and others; Indigenous-authored criticism and support; and evidence of continued voting restrictions that show citizenship alone did not produce equal political standing.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than basic facts. Scholars differ on the weight to give federal intent as opposed to actual operation, and on how to weigh Indigenous testimony from different communities. Terminology also matters, since "citizen" has different meanings for the state and for Indigenous nations.

Why it matters: If citizenship is seen mainly as inclusion, the continuing barriers appear as failures of implementation. If it is seen as an imposition, the barriers appear alongside a deeper issue of consent and sovereignty. Reading the history as coexistence emphasizes Indigenous agency in shaping the meaning of both statuses.

Student task: Using the 1924 statute, the Haudenosaunee position, and the evidence of state voting restrictions, evaluate each interpretation. Then identify what additional evidence would help decide among them, such as community records or testimony from different nations.

These interpretations are not simple opposites. The false choice between assimilation and political isolation does not describe what many Indigenous people sought. Some pursued access to civil rights, courts, and elections while defending self-government, treaty rights, and their languages and ceremonies. The law's intended effects, its actual operation, and the meanings Indigenous people gave to citizenship were three different things, and historians gain the most by examining all three.

Section 93.20

Indigenous Citizenship and Political Belonging by 1930

By about 1930, the shape of the problem was visible across North America. In the United States, most Indigenous people held federal citizenship, either from earlier routes or from the 1924 Act, but many faced unequal access to the ballot, ongoing federal guardianship, and unresolved disputes over tribal jurisdiction. The Meriam Report of 1928, discussed in Chapter 96, documented poverty and administrative failure, and the policy changes of the 1930s would alter the relationship between tribes and the federal government (Chapter 97). In Canada, status policy remained a powerful instrument of exclusion and assimilation. The Indian Act's definitions and enfranchisement provisions continued to remove people from legal status, status women and their children bore the cost of descent rules, and First Nations men on reserves were largely excluded from federal elections. Métis and Inuit peoples lived under different legal arrangements and different forms of marginalization.

Indigenous nations meanwhile continued to maintain political institutions, interpret their treaty obligations, make membership decisions, and challenge outside classifications. The Haudenosaunee asserted their sovereignty in Geneva and at home. The Alaska Native Brotherhood organized, litigated, and ran candidates. Pueblo councils defended their lands. Border-crossing peoples defended their right to move. These efforts show that political membership was not simply handed down by states. It was contested and negotiated, with Indigenous nations acting as political communities in their own right.

The later milestones that bear on the chapter's legacy include the Canadian Citizenship Act of 1947, the 1948 federal and state court decisions opening voting in Arizona and New Mexico, the 1951 revision of the Indian Act that gave women the vote in band elections while retaining other discriminatory rules, and the removal of Canadian federal voting restrictions on status First Nations people in 1960. They also include the federal Voting Rights Act of 1965 and its amendments, the 1985 Canadian amendments addressing the loss of status through marriage, and the continuing legal development of tribal membership authority. Their detailed histories belong to subsequent chapters, including Chapters 104, 114, 117, 118, and 144.

The chapter's end point should not be read as a resolution. Political membership was not a settled question in 1930. It remained a central issue in the continuing history of Indigenous sovereignty and rights, as questions of citizenship, enrollment, identity, and jurisdiction continue to be contested in the twenty-first century. What the period from 1880 to 1930 establishes is that the struggle over citizenship was never merely a demand to enter American or Canadian political society. It was also a struggle over whether Indigenous nations would retain the authority to define their own peoples, exercise self-government, and maintain political relationships distinct from the states that claimed jurisdiction over them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Political Belonging: Before the Citizenship Laws, 1884–1924, and by 1930

What changed?
  • Settler states increasingly defined, enumerated, and classified Indigenous people through rolls, statutes, and court decisions.
  • In the United States, most Native people became citizens, and in Canada, status policy added a gendered system of inclusion and exclusion.
  • Indigenous governments adapted, sometimes by accommodating imposed structures and sometimes by resisting them.
What remained continuous?
  • Indigenous nations continued to assert their own membership laws and treaty relationships.
  • Kinship, language, and ceremony continued to shape belonging despite exclusions from legal lists.
  • Indigenous leaders continued to challenge classifications through diplomacy, litigation, and organization.

When and how: Change was uneven and contested, with sharp legislative moments (1869, 1884, 1920, 1924) set within slower administrative transformation. Who experienced change most directly: women under Canadian status rules, allottees under the Burke Act, and communities whose governments were displaced. Who experienced greater continuity: communities that retained customary institutions and kinship systems and continued to apply them. Why it matters: The pattern shows that Indigenous political belonging was never extinguished, and that the question of who decides remained open.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Indigenous peoples did not move from having no citizenship to receiving it from colonial governments. They already belonged to political nations, and the conflicts of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries concerned how those existing relationships would interact with the expanding claims of the United States and Canada. The central analytical task has been to keep four things apart: Indigenous citizenship, state citizenship, Canadian Indian status, and voting rights.

Division I established that Indigenous nations determined membership through kinship, descent, adoption, residence, and community recognition, and that they revised those rules over time. Haudenosaunee matrilineal clans, Anishinaabe patrilineal dodemag, Diné clan relationships, Lakota kin-making, Pueblo village membership, and Cherokee citizenship law show variety rather than a single model. The chapter distinguished these kinship systems from blood-quantum classifications and rolls imposed or adopted through colonial administration, and cautioned against calling every modern enrollment rule wholly traditional or wholly colonial.

Treaties were presented as relationships between political peoples. The Two Row Wampum tradition, the Treaty of Canandaigua, the Treaty of Fort Laramie, and the numbered treaties illustrate how Indigenous leaders understood agreements as continuing relationships, while federal authorities increasingly asserted jurisdiction over individuals and, through doctrines such as plenary power, over the treaties themselves. Treaty rights and citizenship rights were distinguished as collective and individual, though they could overlap.

Colonial enumeration was shown to be an instrument of power. Annuity rolls, census lists, the Dawes Rolls, and Canadian band lists determined access to land, payments, and recognition, and records created for one purpose acquired lasting legal authority for others. They could exclude, misidentify, or divide families, which is why classification belongs at the center of any history of political membership.

In the United States, the Fourteenth Amendment's phrase "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" was read in Elk v. Wilkins (1884) to exclude Indians who owed allegiance to tribes. The result was a contradiction: people subject to extensive federal authority were denied citizenship because of their tribal political relationships. Congress then created pathways through treaties, marriage, special legislation, allotment under the Dawes and Burke Acts, and, in 1919, veterans' legislation, linking citizenship in many cases to administrators' judgments of fitness.

The Indian Citizenship Act of June 2, 1924 extended citizenship to non-citizen Indians born in United States territory, required no application or abandonment of tribal membership, and protected tribal and other property rights. It did not create citizenship for all Indigenous people at once, did not end federal guardianship, and did not address voting. State restrictions including "Indians not taxed" clauses, guardianship rulings such as Porter v. Hall, residency rules, literacy requirements, and administrative obstacles kept many Native citizens from the polls for decades.

In Canada, legislation from 1857 to 1876 defined who was an Indian and made enfranchisement the way out of that status. Enfranchisement meant loss of legal status and band membership and was frequently extended to family members without consent, and in 1920 it was briefly made compulsory. It was not an ordinary exercise of equal citizenship. Gendered descent rules deprived women who married non-status men, and often their children, of legal status. The federal franchise was granted to some Indigenous men in the east in 1885, withdrawn in 1898, subject to wartime and later exceptions, and not made unconditional for status First Nations people until 1960. Métis and Inuit peoples occupied distinct legal positions, and Canadian nationality before 1947 operated within the British-subject framework.

Division IV examined how these rules worked in practice. Alaska's territorial status, the 1867 treaty's "uncivilized" exception, the 1915 territorial citizenship law, and the 1925 and 1927 literacy measures show citizenship followed by restrictions. Households experienced classification through marriage, children, and inheritance. Border-crossing peoples, among them the Haudenosaunee, Lakota, O'odham, and Yaqui, faced overlapping nationality rules that no single state's law can explain. The career of William Paul Sr. documented both what political participation made possible and what barriers remained.

Division V considered Indigenous governments and disagreement. Deskaheh's appeal to Geneva and the 1924 displacement of the Grand River council, the Navajo Tribal Council, the Pueblo councils, and the changed institutions of the Five Tribes show that national voting and participation in an Indigenous nation were different activities. Carlos Montezuma's critique of paternalism and the Onondaga Nation's rejection of imposed citizenship represent real, different positions, and the historical debate examined whether state citizenship and sovereignty could coexist, reaching the conclusion that many people sought both civil rights and self-government.

By 1930, many Indigenous people in the United States were citizens with unequal access to the vote, Canadian status policy remained an instrument of exclusion and assimilation, and Indigenous nations continued to govern themselves, interpret treaties, set membership rules, and challenge outside classifications. Later milestones, including the Canadian Citizenship Act, postwar voting decisions, the 1960 Canadian reform, the Voting Rights Act, and the 1985 status amendments, show that political membership was not a settled question in 1930 and remains a central issue in the history of Indigenous sovereignty.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that citizenship, so often described as a gift, was a contested terrain on which states and Indigenous nations pursued different aims. The legal categories examined here were instruments of policy as well as of recognition, and understanding them clarifies why debates over membership, enrollment, and identity remain so charged.

The chapter also reveals a larger process, the translation of Indigenous political relationships into the vocabulary of settler states. That translation was never complete or uncontested. Indigenous nations continued to apply their own laws of belonging, to defend their treaties, and to participate selectively in outside politics, and their persistence is a form of continuity that outlasted the specific statutes studied here.

The developments traced here shaped what followed. The patterns of citizenship without equal voting, status rules that divided families, and tension between collective and individual rights reappear in the reorganization of tribal governments (Chapter 97), the termination era (Chapter 104), the constitutional politics of Canada (Chapter 117), and modern debates over tribal citizenship and identity (Chapter 144).

Finally, the chapter links regions that are often studied separately. The Haudenosaunee, Tlingit, Lakota, O'odham, Métis, and Inuit each encountered different instruments, but the underlying question was shared: who decides who belongs? In 1930 that question was open, and it remains a central issue in the continuing history of Indigenous sovereignty and rights.
