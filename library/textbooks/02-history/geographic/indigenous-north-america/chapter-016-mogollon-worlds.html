Mogollon Worlds

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the rugged uplands of what is now southwestern New Mexico and eastern Arizona, and across the lower valleys, plateaus, and desert margins that surround them, farming communities built lives that archaeologists today group together under a single working label: Mogollon. That label describes neither a nation nor a single people who called themselves by that name. It describes a broad and varied set of recurring patterns in architecture, farming, pottery, and community life, patterns that themselves changed considerably across space and time.

Drive today along the mountain roads that wind through the Gila and Mimbres watersheds of southwestern New Mexico, or walk the low ridges above the San Francisco and Blue Rivers in eastern Arizona, and it is easy to imagine a single, continuous ancient world stretching across the region. The archaeological record tells a more complicated and, in many ways, more interesting story. Communities living across this broad and ecologically varied territory shared certain general tendencies: they built partially subterranean pithouses before gradually shifting, in some places and periods, toward above-ground masonry rooms; they cultivated maize, beans, and squash while continuing to rely heavily on hunting and the gathering of wild plants; and, above all, they produced brownware and later painted pottery traditions that archaeologists use as one of the principal tools for identifying and dating Mogollon sites. But within this broad pattern, communities in the cool, well-watered mountain valleys of the Mogollon Highlands lived rather differently from communities on the drier plateaus to the north, in the low desert basins to the south, or along the international borderlands that stretch today into northern Chihuahua and Sonora.

This chapter picks up directly from Chapter 15's account of how mobile foraging populations across the Southwest gradually incorporated maize, then beans and squash, into economies that continued to rely on wild resources, and how this slow process eventually produced village life, storage technology, and increasing residential permanence across a wide and environmentally diverse region. Chapter 15 deliberately avoided treating any single trajectory as representative of the whole Southwest. This chapter narrows the focus to examine, in detail, one of the major regional trajectories that emerged from those shared foundations: the constellation of communities, sites, and traditions that archaeologists have, since the early twentieth century, grouped together as "Mogollon."

The word itself is instructive before any history can even begin. "Mogollon" was not a name any ancient community used for itself, so far as the archaeological and linguistic evidence allows historians to say. It derives from Juan Ignacio Flores Mogollón, a colonial Spanish governor of New Mexico in the early eighteenth century, whose name was later attached to a mountain range, and from there to an archaeological tradition defined by twentieth-century researchers studying sites within and near those mountains. Understanding Mogollon history, then, requires understanding not only what archaeologists have found in the ground, but also how the analytical category itself came into being, what it can responsibly claim to describe, and what it cannot.

This is not merely an academic technicality. It shapes how the entire chapter must be read. A student who assumes that "Mogollon" identifies a single ancient people, in the way that a national or tribal name might identify a political community, will misunderstand nearly everything that follows. A student who instead treats "Mogollon" as a working label for a recognizable, geographically extensive, but genuinely diverse set of overlapping cultural and technological patterns will be equipped to follow this chapter's central argument: that farming, architecture, pottery, ceremony, and exchange developed along broadly similar but locally distinctive paths across a vast and ecologically varied stretch of the greater Southwest and northern Mexican borderlands.

Nowhere is this diversity more vivid than in the Mimbres Valley of southwestern New Mexico, where, roughly a thousand years ago, potters produced painted vessels of extraordinary technical skill and imaginative range, many of which were deliberately placed with the dead, a hole broken through their centers before burial. These vessels have become, for many people encountering Mogollon archaeology for the first time, the most recognizable material legacy of the entire tradition. This chapter gives the Mimbres world sustained, careful attention, but it insists throughout on a distinction that is easy to blur and important to hold onto: Mimbres represents one particularly well-studied and visually striking regional and temporal expression within the broader Mogollon tradition. It is not a synonym for that tradition as a whole, and centuries of Mogollon history, in dozens of communities across a wide territory, unfolded with little or no connection to Mimbres painted pottery at all.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about how archaeologists know what they know. Unlike the documented political councils and treaty diplomacy examined in later volumes of this textbook, Mogollon communities left no written records, and archaeological classification alone cannot establish an uncontested, one-to-one correspondence between a specific ancient site or archaeological category and a specific present-day community's own oral traditions and historical memory. This is a limitation of archaeological method, not a claim that Indigenous oral history has itself been lost. What survives instead, for the archaeologist's purposes, is material evidence: house foundations, storage pits, hearths, agricultural features, broken and whole pottery vessels, burials, and the debris of daily work. Learning to read that evidence responsibly, without either dismissing its value or overstating what it can prove, is one of the chapter's central instructional tasks.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Mogollon not as a finished, uniform culture that simply appeared, persisted, and vanished, but as a set of historically dynamic communities that adapted farming and building practices to varied mountain, valley, and desert-margin environments; that developed distinctive and changing pottery traditions; that organized community and ceremonial life in ways archaeologists continue to study and debate; and that remained in ongoing contact with neighboring Hohokam, Ancestral Pueblo, and northern Mexican communities throughout the centuries this chapter examines. The chapter closes by tracing how, in the centuries after roughly 1300 to 1450 CE, many communities within the broader Mogollon region reorganized, relocated, and became part of the historical trajectories examined in Chapters 17 and 18, rather than mysteriously disappearing from the historical record.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what archaeologists mean by "Mogollon," including the term's origin, its use as an analytical category, and its limitations as a description of ancient identity.
  2. Describe the geographic extent and ecological diversity of the Mogollon archaeological world, including its mountain, valley, plateau, desert-margin, and borderland subregions.
  3. Trace the transition from early mixed farming and foraging communities, examined in Chapter 15, to the more residentially stable pithouse-based communities associated with early Mogollon sites.
  4. Analyze pithouse architecture and household organization, and explain what this evidence can and cannot reveal about family structure, community life, and social organization.
  5. Describe Mogollon agricultural practices, including their reliance on rainfall and runoff farming, their continued dependence on hunting and gathering, and their variation across different environments.
  6. Explain the development of Mogollon pottery traditions, including their technological, functional, and stylistic dimensions, and evaluate the risks of equating pottery style directly with ethnic or political identity.
  7. Trace changing settlement patterns across the Mogollon world without assuming a single, universal, or strictly linear progression from pithouses to above-ground pueblos.
  8. Evaluate archaeological evidence for communal and ceremonial architecture and practice, using careful, evidence-based terminology rather than uncritical labels.
  9. Distinguish the Mimbres regional tradition from the broader Mogollon archaeological world, and explain Mimbres painted pottery's significance and interpretive limitations.
  10. Assess evidence for exchange and interaction linking Mogollon communities with Hohokam, Ancestral Pueblo, and northern Mexican societies.
  11. Explain the reorganization and migration of Mogollon-region communities in the centuries after approximately 1300 CE, and why "disappearance" is a historically inaccurate description of this process.
  12. Apply the concept of an archaeological tradition as an analytical tool, distinguishing it from claims about language, ethnicity, or political identity.

Historical Context

Chapter 15 traced the slow, uneven adoption of agriculture across the greater Southwest, beginning with the arrival of maize from Mesoamerica many centuries before continuous village life became common, and continuing through a long period in which farming supplemented rather than replaced hunting and gathering. That chapter emphasized that no single environment or economic model describes the entire Southwest, and that increasing residential stability, storage technology, and eventually formal village architecture developed at different paces in different places, shaped by local rainfall, soil, elevation, and the availability of wild resources as much as by the mere presence of domesticated crops.

By the centuries following roughly 200 CE, several distinct regional trajectories within this broader Southwestern pattern had become sufficiently recognizable, in their architecture, pottery, and settlement organization, that archaeologists working in the early and middle twentieth century began assigning them separate names. One of these trajectories, concentrated across the mountainous uplands of southwestern New Mexico and eastern Arizona and extending into the surrounding valleys, plateaus, and desert margins, together with connected communities across the modern international border in northern Chihuahua and Sonora, became known as Mogollon. Two other major trajectories, examined in Chapters 17 and 18, developed at roughly the same time in the irrigated Sonoran Desert basins to the west and southwest, and across the Colorado Plateau and its surrounding canyon and mesa country to the north.

These three broad archaeological traditions, Mogollon, Hohokam, and Ancestral Pueblo, are best understood not as three separate, isolated civilizations but as three overlapping and interacting regional expressions of processes that had begun much earlier and more broadly across the Southwest as a whole. Communities near the boundaries of one tradition frequently shared significant features with communities identified with a neighboring tradition, and archaeologists continue to debate exactly where, and how firmly, the boundaries between these traditions should be drawn. This chapter treats those boundaries as useful but imperfect analytical tools rather than as sharp lines separating fundamentally different peoples.

It is also essential to state plainly, before the narrative begins, that the archaeological label "Mogollon" says nothing certain about the language, ethnicity, or specific political organization of the communities it describes. Several Pueblo nations, including Zuni, Acoma, and Hopi, maintain ancestral relationships to communities and landscapes included within what archaeologists call the Mogollon region, though the precise pathways connecting specific archaeological sites to specific present-day nations remain the subject of ongoing research and community understanding rather than settled archaeological fact. Apache peoples and other Indigenous nations also have longstanding histories tied to these same landscapes, histories that are not equivalent to a claim of direct Mogollon archaeological descent. Archaeological classification cannot, by itself, resolve the precise ancestral relationships claimed or remembered by present-day communities, and this textbook does not attempt to adjudicate or reduce those relationships to a single archaeological narrative. This chapter presents the archaeological evidence and its standard scholarly interpretation while recognizing that archaeological classification and Indigenous self-understanding are not always, and need not be, the same thing.

Section 16.1

What Does "Mogollon" Mean?

Before examining the communities this chapter studies, students must understand precisely what kind of claim the word "Mogollon" makes, and what kind of claim it does not make. "Mogollon" is an archaeological label, coined by researchers in the early and middle twentieth century to describe a recurring set of material patterns, pithouse and later masonry architecture, particular pottery technologies and styles, and particular farming and settlement patterns, observed at sites across a broad region of the greater Southwest. It is not a name that any ancient community is known to have used for itself, and it does not, by itself, indicate a shared language, a shared political organization, or a single shared ethnic identity across the entire area where these patterns appear.

This distinction matters because popular accounts of Southwestern archaeology sometimes present "Mogollon," alongside "Hohokam" and "Ancestral Pueblo," as though each label named a single ancient nation or civilization comparable to a modern state. The archaeological reality is more complicated and, properly understood, more historically interesting. Within the area archaeologists identify as Mogollon, evidence suggests considerable variation over time and across subregions in architecture, pottery, settlement size, and probably social organization. Communities in the well-watered mountain valleys of the Mogollon Highlands developed somewhat differently from communities on the drier plateaus to the north or in the desert basins to the south, even though all of them are conventionally grouped under the same archaeological label because they share enough recurring features to make the grouping analytically useful.

The label's origin illustrates this constructed quality directly. The Mogollon Mountains, a rugged range in what is now southwestern New Mexico, take their name from Juan Ignacio Flores Mogollón, who served as a colonial Spanish governor of New Mexico in the early eighteenth century. Twentieth-century archaeologists, working chiefly at sites within and near these mountains, applied the range's name to the archaeological pattern they were documenting. The connection between the term's origin and the ancient communities it now describes is therefore entirely a matter of modern scholarly convention, not an inheritance from the ancient communities themselves.

None of this means the category is arbitrary or useless. Archaeologists have real, repeatedly documented reasons for grouping these sites together: broadly similar house forms appearing in roughly the same centuries, broadly similar farming strategies adapted to broadly similar (though not identical) environments, and pottery technologies and, later, painted styles that show clear continuities across a wide but bounded geographic area. Section 16.6 of this chapter, together with this chapter's Historical Method feature, examines in detail how archaeologists build and test such categories using multiple independent lines of evidence. For now, the essential point is this: "Mogollon" describes a useful, evidence-based, but ultimately interpretive category constructed by modern researchers, applied to a genuinely diverse set of ancient communities who almost certainly did not think of themselves as a single people.

Section 16.2

Landscapes of the Mogollon World

The area archaeologists identify as Mogollon spans a remarkable range of environments, and this ecological diversity is essential to understanding why the tradition itself proved so internally varied. At its core lie the mountain uplands of the Mogollon Highlands in southwestern New Mexico and adjacent eastern Arizona, a landscape of forested ridges, narrow river valleys, and relatively reliable summer rainfall compared to the surrounding lowlands. The Gila, San Francisco, Mimbres, and Blue River valleys, among others, cut through this highland country, providing water, fertile floodplain soils, and access to a wide range of plant and animal resources.

To the south and southwest, the highlands give way to lower, drier basin-and-range country, where communities faced greater aridity and relied more heavily on runoff farming, the practice of directing seasonal flash-flood water toward fields, rather than the more consistent rainfall available at higher elevations. Further south still, Mogollon-related archaeological patterns extend across the modern international border into present-day northern Chihuahua and Sonora, where they intersect with later developments such as Paquimé (Casas Grandes) in Chihuahua, whose relationships with Mogollon-region communities, other northern Mexican societies, and wider Mesoamerican networks remain subjects of archaeological study, examined more fully in connection with Mesoamerican and northern Mexican history in other chapters of this textbook. To the north and northeast, the Mogollon world's boundary with Ancestral Pueblo communities on the Colorado Plateau was never sharp; communities near this zone show a blending of features associated with both traditions, and archaeologists continue to debate exactly how to classify some of these borderland sites.

Section 16.3

From Early Farming Communities to Mogollon Traditions

Chapter 15 traced how maize agriculture spread gradually across the Southwest over many centuries, arriving well before it became a dominant part of any community's subsistence strategy, and how communities across the region combined farming with continued hunting and gathering for a very long period before residential permanence, storage technology, and formal village architecture became widespread. The communities that archaeologists eventually classify as early Mogollon emerged directly from this longer, broader process rather than representing a separate or unrelated development.

By the centuries around 200 BCE to 200 CE, communities across the uplands and valleys of the future Mogollon region show increasing evidence of residential stability: deeper and more substantial pit structures, larger and more numerous storage features, and settlement locations chosen with greater attention to reliable water and farmland access. This did not mean communities abandoned mobility or wild food resources; hunting, particularly of deer and smaller game, and the gathering of wild plants such as agave, various seeds, and pinyon nuts, remained central to subsistence throughout the entire span of Mogollon history examined in this chapter. What changed was the balance and the degree of investment communities were willing to make in fixed locations, an investment that makes sense only once farming, storage, and local knowledge of a particular landscape had together become reliable enough to reward staying put for longer periods.

Archaeologists mark the change conventionally by identifying an "Early Pithouse period," beginning by around 200 CE and continuing for several centuries, during which small villages of pithouses become archaeologically visible across much of the region. This periodization, like all periodization discussed in this textbook, is an analytical convenience rather than a sharp historical boundary; the shift from earlier, more mobile farming communities to Early Pithouse period villages was gradual, uneven across subregions, and almost certainly experienced differently by different communities depending on local conditions.

Section 16.4

Pithouses, Households, and Early Villages

The pithouse is the architectural feature archaeologists most closely associate with early and, in many subregions, later Mogollon communities. Builders excavated a pit, typically ranging from shallow basins to depressions well over a meter deep, and then constructed a roof and upper wall structure over the excavation using wooden posts, brush, and earth, often with a single entryway, sometimes an entry ramp or shaft, and typically a central hearth. Pithouses offered practical advantages in many Southwestern environments: the earth surrounding a partially subterranean structure helped moderate interior temperature across hot summers and cold mountain winters, and construction required less above-ground material than fully constructed surface rooms.

Archaeologists can reconstruct a good deal about household life from excavated pithouses: the presence and placement of hearths, storage pits, and work areas; evidence of specific activities such as grinding, tool production, or food processing, based on tools and residues left in place; and, from the arrangement of multiple pithouses at a single site, some sense of how households related spatially to one another. What pithouse architecture cannot reliably reveal, without much more extensive corroborating evidence, is the precise composition of the household that occupied a given structure, whether kinship, marriage, or some other principle organized membership in a household, or how authority and decision-making operated within or among households at a given village. This textbook is careful throughout to distinguish what pithouse evidence directly supports from more speculative reconstructions sometimes offered in older or more popular accounts.

Early Mogollon villages typically consisted of a modest number of pithouses, often loosely clustered rather than tightly planned, together with associated storage pits and outdoor work areas. Village size and permanence varied considerably by subregion and period; some sites show evidence of occupation across multiple generations, with pithouses rebuilt or replaced in roughly the same locations over time, while others suggest shorter-term or more seasonal use. As with the timing of the shift from earlier mobile economies, students should resist imagining a single, uniform "typical" Mogollon village; the evidence instead points to considerable local variation shaped by environment, available resources, and, quite plausibly, community choice.

Section 16.5

Agriculture in Varied Environments

Mogollon communities cultivated maize as their primary domesticated staple, supplemented over time by beans and squash, following the general pattern of Southwestern agriculture introduced in Chapter 15. What distinguished agricultural practice across the Mogollon world was less the crops themselves than the range of strategies communities used to grow them successfully across such varied terrain. In the higher-elevation valleys of the Mogollon Highlands, communities could generally rely on a combination of summer monsoon rainfall and floodplain soils along perennial or seasonally reliable streams, allowing forms of what archaeologists term dry farming or rainfall farming, in which fields depend primarily on direct precipitation rather than constructed irrigation.

Further south and at lower elevations, where rainfall was less abundant and less predictable, communities more often practiced runoff agriculture, directing water from seasonal flash floods and hillside drainage toward fields using simple check dams, terraces, and diversion features. This approach differed fundamentally from the large-scale canal irrigation systems that Chapter 17 examines among Hohokam communities of the Sonoran Desert; Mogollon runoff farming generally operated at a smaller, more localized scale, adapted to the shorter, steeper drainages typical of upland and basin-margin terrain rather than the major perennial rivers that made extensive canal systems possible in the Hohokam heartland. Students should therefore avoid describing Mogollon agriculture as simply a smaller version of Hohokam irrigation; the two represent different technological and environmental adaptations rather than points on a single scale of agricultural sophistication.

Throughout the periods this chapter examines, hunting and wild plant gathering remained economically important alongside farming. Deer, rabbits, and other game supplemented cultivated foods, and wild plants, including agave, various wild seeds, acorns, and pinyon nuts in higher elevations, contributed meaningfully to diet, particularly in years when farming yields were reduced by drought or frost. This mixed economy, rather than full agricultural dependence, characterized Mogollon subsistence across nearly the entire span of the tradition's history, even as farming's relative importance likely increased over time in many communities.

Section 16.6

Pottery and Technological Change

Pottery occupies a central place in this chapter for a specific reason: it is one of the most important classes of evidence archaeologists use to identify, date, and subdivide Mogollon sites, and understanding how and why this is so is essential to understanding archaeological method more broadly. Before pottery, Southwestern communities stored, cooked, and transported goods using baskets, hide containers, and other perishable materials that survive only rarely in the archaeological record, chiefly in unusually dry cave and rockshelter contexts. The adoption and subsequent development of ceramic technology across the Mogollon region therefore represents both a genuine technological transformation in daily life and an archaeological gift: pottery survives well, breaks in datable and traceable ways, and changes in style and technique relatively quickly and consistently enough to serve as a reliable chronological marker.

The earliest widespread Mogollon pottery tradition consisted of plain brownware, undecorated or minimally decorated vessels made from locally available clays and used primarily for cooking and storage. Over the centuries that followed, painted traditions developed, beginning with relatively simple designs in red on brown or similar combinations, and eventually producing, in the Mimbres Valley and neighboring areas, the elaborate black-on-white painted tradition examined in detail in Sections 16.9 and 16.10. Pottery technology also changed and diversified over time in ways archaeologists can trace through careful study of paste composition, firing technique, and vessel form, changes that likely reflect generations of accumulated potters' knowledge passed down and refined within particular communities, rather than a simple progression from cruder to superior wares; plain brownware continued to serve important cooking and storage functions even after painted traditions developed.

Historical Method

How Do Archaeologists Define an Archaeological Tradition?

Defining a category such as "Mogollon" requires archaeologists to combine several independent lines of evidence rather than relying on any single trait. Consider the following types of evidence and what each can, and cannot, establish on its own.

Ceramic style and technology: Recurring pottery forms, paste recipes, and painted designs, tracked through careful classification and seriation, the ordering of styles by their relative frequency and stratigraphic position, allow archaeologists to identify communities of practice and to build reliable relative chronologies. On its own, however, shared pottery style shows only that potters shared certain techniques and aesthetic conventions, whether through common training, trade, intermarriage, or simple imitation, not that the people who used the pottery shared a single ethnic or political identity.

Architecture: Recurring house forms, such as particular pithouse shapes or later masonry roomblock layouts, suggest shared building knowledge and, plausibly, shared approaches to household and community organization. Architecture can be influenced by environment and available materials as much as by cultural preference, so similar structures in different subregions do not automatically indicate close cultural connection.

Settlement pattern and chronology: The size, spacing, and dating of sites across a region reveal population distribution and change over time, but cannot by themselves establish language, ethnicity, or formal political boundaries.

Subsistence and environmental adaptation: Similar farming strategies suited to similar environments may reflect independent, parallel adaptation to comparable conditions rather than direct cultural connection between the communities practicing them.

Geographic distribution: Archaeologists look for the boundary at which one recurring combination of traits gives way, gradually or more abruptly, to a different recurring combination, recognizing that such boundaries are rarely sharp and that borderland sites often combine features associated with more than one tradition.

The central lesson: an archaeological tradition such as Mogollon is defined by the convergence of multiple independent lines of evidence across a bounded space and time, and it functions as a research tool that helps archaeologists organize and compare evidence. It is not, by itself, evidence of a specific ancient ethnic label, language, or unified political community, and responsible historical writing must keep this distinction clear.

Because pottery is so central to Mogollon archaeology, it is also central to the risks of overinterpretation this textbook has emphasized since Chapter 1. A shared pottery style across a wide area might indicate direct cultural continuity, but it might equally indicate trade, intermarriage between neighboring communities, the movement of individual potters, or simple imitation of a popular and widely admired style. Archaeologists therefore combine ceramic evidence with architectural, settlement, and subsistence evidence before drawing conclusions about cultural relationships, precisely the kind of multi-source reasoning this chapter's Historical Method feature describes.

Section 16.7

Villages and Changing Settlement Patterns

Across the centuries archaeologists group into the Late Pithouse period, conventionally dated to roughly 550 to 1000 CE as an approximate and regionally variable boundary, many Mogollon communities show evidence of larger village populations, more substantial and sometimes more elaborately constructed pithouses, and, at some sites, the appearance of unusually large structures interpreted as serving community-wide rather than purely household functions, discussed further in Section 16.8. In some subregions, communities began experimenting with above-ground masonry construction well before it became widespread, while in others, pithouse architecture persisted for centuries longer.

By the period conventionally identified with Classic Mimbres developments, roughly 1000 to 1130 CE, communities in the Mimbres Valley and some neighboring drainages had shifted substantially toward above-ground masonry roomblocks, contiguous suites of rectangular rooms built of stone masonry, arranged around plazas or in linear and clustered arrangements. This shift toward surface pueblo architecture in the Mimbres area occurred earlier and more completely than in many other parts of the broader Mogollon region, where pithouses, or a mixture of pithouse and surface construction, continued well past 1000 CE. This unevenness is historically important and is examined more closely in this chapter's Continuity and Change feature: architectural transformation across the Mogollon world was not a single, simultaneous event, but a set of locally paced processes that unfolded differently from one community and subregion to another.

After roughly 1130 CE, in the period sometimes called Postclassic or Reorganization in different parts of the region, settlement patterns continued to change. Some areas saw population decline or dispersal, others saw continued or renewed construction of masonry pueblos, and, as Section 16.12 explains, the final centuries of the sequence saw significant population movement and reorganization across much of the broader Mogollon world.

Section 16.8

Ceremony, Community Space, and Social Life

At a number of Mogollon sites, archaeologists have identified structures notably larger than typical residential pithouses or rooms, structures that show floor plans, features, or artifact assemblages suggesting use by more than a single household, possibly for community gatherings, ritual activity, or specialized communal functions. Archaeologists conventionally call some of the especially large examples of these structures "great kivas," based on recurring architectural features, such as size, floor features, and formal entryways, and on associated evidence suggesting use by more than a single household. This term is well established in the Mogollon and Mimbres archaeological literature and is used throughout this textbook as standard archaeological shorthand. At the same time, students should not treat the term as proof that every ancient Mogollon great kiva served precisely the same religious functions, or carried precisely the same meanings, as ceremonial kivas documented in later and living Pueblo communities. The label identifies a recurring architectural and functional pattern in the excavated record; it does not by itself establish a specific, unbroken line of religious continuity between an eighth-century Mogollon great kiva and a modern Pueblo ceremonial structure, a distinction this textbook maintains consistently when using culturally significant terminology across different periods and communities.

What can be responsibly said is that the appearance and increasing scale of great kivas during the Late Pithouse period demonstrates growing investment in community-level ritual and social integration, whether that activity centered on decision-making, exchange, feasting, or some combination of these. In the Mimbres Valley specifically, many of the largest great kivas were deliberately retired during the tenth century, before the Classic Mimbres period began, suggesting that communal and ritual organization changed substantially as communities shifted toward the above-ground roomblock villages examined in Sections 16.7 and 16.9, rather than simply continuing to expand great kiva construction without interruption. Burial practices at Mogollon sites, examined further in connection with Mimbres pottery in Section 16.10, similarly suggest structured beliefs about death and its proper treatment, though the specific content of those beliefs, beyond what burial position, associated objects, and site context can reveal, generally remains beyond what archaeological evidence alone can establish with confidence.

Section 16.9

The Mimbres World

Among the many regional expressions of the broader Mogollon tradition, the communities of the Mimbres Valley and adjoining drainages in southwestern New Mexico have received more sustained archaeological attention than perhaps any other, largely because of the striking painted pottery examined in the following section. Before turning to that pottery, however, this section establishes the Mimbres world on its own terms, as a particular regional and temporal community within Mogollon history rather than as a stand-in for the tradition as a whole.

Communities along the Mimbres River built substantial pithouse villages during the Late Pithouse period, and, during the Classic Mimbres period beginning around 1000 CE, shifted to above-ground masonry roomblocks arranged in compact village clusters, some containing dozens of contiguous rooms. These villages supported farming along the Mimbres River's floodplain, supplemented by the same combination of hunting and wild plant gathering documented throughout the broader Mogollon region. Population during the Classic period appears to have been relatively dense for the area, and settlement was more tightly clustered along the river corridor than in many surrounding, more dispersed Mogollon subregions.

It is important to state clearly that the label "Mimbres" refers to this specific regional and largely Classic-period tradition, not to the Mogollon world as a whole. Vast stretches of Mogollon history, across centuries and across most of the tradition's geographic extent, involved communities that never produced Mimbres-style painted pottery and that organized their villages differently from the tightly clustered Mimbres Valley roomblocks. Treating "Mimbres" and "Mogollon" as interchangeable, an error this chapter's introductory material has already flagged, would badly distort both the chronology and the geography of the tradition this chapter as a whole is meant to convey.

Section 16.10

Mimbres Pottery and Archaeological Interpretation

Classic Mimbres black-on-white pottery, produced chiefly between roughly 1000 and 1130 CE, is widely regarded as among the most technically accomplished and visually striking pottery traditions of ancient North America. Potters, working with locally available clays and applying iron-based mineral paint over a white or cream slip, produced bowls painted with an extraordinary range of designs: precise geometric patterns, and, in a substantial number of surviving vessels, figural imagery depicting animals, including fish, birds, insects, rabbits, and other creatures, along with human figures engaged in activities that have been interpreted, with varying degrees of confidence, as related to hunting, ceremony, or narrative storytelling.

Material Evidence Window

A Burial Bowl from Elk Ridge Pueblo

Object type: Style III Mimbres Black-on-white bowl · Approximate date: Classic Mimbres period, c. 1000–1130 CE · Context: Excavated from Room 101 at Elk Ridge Pueblo, a Classic Mimbres pueblo in the Mimbres Valley of southwestern New Mexico, associated with professional archaeological fieldwork and subsequent published analysis

Description: The bowl was recovered in association with the burial of an adult female beneath the floor of Room 101, placed over her skull, with a hole deliberately punched through its center before interment, a practice archaeologists commonly refer to as "killing" the vessel. The bowl's interior is decorated with the Style III black-on-white painting characteristic of the Classic Mimbres period, in which designs are rendered using an iron-based mineral paint applied over a white or cream slip.

What the evidence can establish: This excavated, published context, together with comparable documented burials at other Mimbres Valley sites, confirms that placing a painted bowl over the head of the deceased and deliberately perforating it was a recurring and therefore very likely intentional practice, part of a widely shared mortuary convention in Classic Mimbres communities. Careful excavation and recording at sites such as Elk Ridge Pueblo also allow archaeologists to establish the bowl's precise findspot, stratigraphic position, and association with a specific individual, information that is lost whenever a vessel is removed from the ground without professional documentation.

What the evidence cannot establish with confidence: Documented mortuary practice at Elk Ridge Pueblo and comparable sites also shows considerable variation, some burials included painted bowls and some did not, some vessels were perforated and others were not, and burial position and associated goods differed from one interment to the next. This variation means the recurring pattern supports a widely shared mortuary convention involving vessels and deliberate perforation, but it does not by itself reveal the specific meaning of the painted imagery, nor does it establish a single, uniform explanation, whether to "release" some quality associated with the vessel, to prevent reuse, or to mark the vessel as belonging to the deceased, for why perforation was practiced. Multiple explanations have been proposed, and this textbook presents them as reasoned interpretation rather than settled fact.

Collecting history and its consequences: Because Mimbres painted pottery has long been prized by collectors, many vessels were removed from archaeological context by unregulated digging during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, before modern legal protections for archaeological sites were established. Vessels removed in this way lose most of their scientific value, since the specific burial context, associated objects, and stratigraphic position, information essential to responsible interpretation, cannot be reconstructed once a vessel has been separated from its find-site. This history is itself part of the story of how Mimbres pottery has been studied, valued, and sometimes misused, and connects to the ethical questions concerning archaeological collection and interpretive authority introduced in Chapter 1.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the recurring pattern of perforated bowls in burial contexts allow archaeologists to conclude with reasonable confidence?
  2. Why can the specific meaning of a painted image not be determined from the image alone, without additional context?
  3. How does the history of unregulated collecting affect what can be learned from Mimbres vessels that survive without documented excavation context?
  4. Why is it important to distinguish between what a piece of material evidence directly demonstrates and what remains a plausible but unproven interpretation?

Archaeologists studying Mimbres imagery generally caution against reading painted designs as literal illustrations of myth or straightforward historical record, in the same way this textbook's Chapter 1 cautioned against uncritical use of any single kind of evidence. Figural scenes may represent narrative, ritual, humor, close observation of the natural world, or some combination of these purposes that modern viewers cannot fully recover. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that Mimbres potters possessed sophisticated artistic training and that particular motifs recur across many vessels in ways suggesting shared conventions and perhaps shared narrative or symbolic traditions. The repeated association of painted bowls with burials, and the recurring practice of perforation, together support an intentional and widely shared mortuary convention involving vessels and deliberate perforation. This pattern does not, by itself, reveal the meaning of the painted imagery, nor does it establish a single, uniform explanation for the practice across every Mimbres community and burial, since documented mortuary practice also shows considerable variation from one burial to the next.

Section 16.11

Exchange and Regional Interaction

Mogollon communities, across every period this chapter examines, participated in exchange networks connecting them with neighboring regions. Marine shell, originating from the Gulf of California, reached Mogollon sites through exchange relationships that very likely passed through, or connected with, Hohokam communities of the Sonoran Desert, examined in Chapter 17, who maintained more direct access to coastal shell sources and who are well documented as prolific shell workers and traders. Turquoise, mined at several locations across the greater Southwest, moved along similar or related exchange paths, appearing as beads, pendants, and inlay at Mogollon sites.

Pottery itself provides evidence of exchange and interaction: vessels made in one subregion's distinctive style are sometimes found at sites well outside that subregion, suggesting trade, gift exchange, or the movement of individuals, quite possibly including potters themselves, through marriage or other social ties. Interaction with Ancestral Pueblo communities to the north intensified in some border areas over time, particularly in the centuries examined in the following section, when population movement brought Mogollon-region communities into closer and more sustained contact with communities to the north.

To the south, connections with communities in northern Chihuahua and Sonora were substantial and, in some respects, deepened over time. By the centuries after Classic Mimbres developments, the large settlement of Paquimé, or Casas Grandes, in northern Chihuahua emerged as a significant regional center whose material culture shows connections to both Mogollon-tradition communities to the north and to Mesoamerican trade networks farther south, including the presence of macaws, copper items, and other materials associated with long-distance Mesoamerican exchange, a subject this textbook examines more fully where Mexican and Mesoamerican connections to the wider Southwest are discussed elsewhere in this volume. This chapter's point is more limited but important: the Mogollon world was never sealed off from wider regional and even Mesoamerican-linked exchange systems, and understanding its history requires looking outward across what later became an international border, not only within the present-day United States.

Section 16.12

Transformation, Migration, and Continuing Histories

In the centuries after roughly 1130 CE, and increasingly between about 1300 and 1450 CE, communities across much of the broader Mogollon region underwent significant reorganization. In some subregions, population appears to have declined or dispersed; in others, communities aggregated into larger settlements, in patterns that echo, and in some cases directly connect with, similar aggregation processes occurring at the same time among Ancestral Pueblo communities to the north, examined in Chapter 18. Archaeologists interpret some architectural and ceramic evidence as consistent with movement of Mogollon-region communities northward over the course of these centuries, plausibly contributing to the ancestry of later Pueblo communities such as Zuni and Acoma, though the exact routes and mechanisms of such movement are difficult to reconstruct once distinctive Mimbres material styles disappear from the archaeological record, and this remains an interpretation rather than a directly documented migration route. Other evidence has similarly been interpreted as consistent with communities moving or reorienting southward, toward developments connected with the Casas Grandes (Paquimé) region and the northern Mexican borderlands discussed in the previous section, again as an archaeological interpretation rather than an established itinerary.

This textbook deliberately avoids describing this process as a "disappearance," a term this chapter's introductory material and Chapter 1 both identify as historically misleading whenever it is applied to Indigenous communities. The archaeological evidence does not support the idea that Mogollon-region populations vanished; rather, it supports a picture of substantial population movement, community reorganization, and, in many cases, integration into the ancestral histories of communities that continued, and continue, to live in the broader region, including many present-day Pueblo nations and other Indigenous communities of the Southwest and northern Mexico. The precise details of these movements, which specific communities moved where, and how directly particular archaeological sites connect to particular later or present-day communities, remain subjects of ongoing archaeological research and, importantly, of Indigenous communities' own historical and oral traditions, which this textbook does not presume to override or fully reconcile with the archaeological record.

Understanding this final phase of Mogollon history correctly matters for the chapters that follow. Chapter 17 examines the Hohokam world of the irrigated Sonoran Desert, whose own history of transformation in this same broad era involved comparable questions about reorganization rather than disappearance. Chapter 18 examines Ancestral Pueblo communities of the Colorado Plateau; as this section has indicated, archaeological evidence has been interpreted as consistent with population movement and cultural interaction connecting parts of the Mogollon world with some of those later communities during these centuries of movement and change. Reading Mogollon history as a story of adaptation, reorganization, and continuing connection, rather than an ending, positions students to understand these next two chapters as continuations of an interconnected regional history rather than as unrelated new subjects.

Historical Vocabulary
Archaeological Tradition and Culture Area

Archaeological tradition refers to a recurring, historically connected pattern of technology, architecture, and material culture identified by archaeologists across a bounded area and period of time, based on converging evidence rather than any single trait. "Mogollon," "Hohokam," and "Ancestral Pueblo" are all examples of archaeological traditions as this textbook uses the term.

Culture area, a related but distinct concept, refers to a broader geographic region within which multiple related or interacting archaeological traditions and, in later periods, historically documented peoples shared certain general environmental adaptations and cultural patterns, without implying a single shared identity across the entire area. The Southwest as a whole functions as a culture area within which Mogollon, Hohokam, and Ancestral Pueblo traditions each developed distinct but related and interacting trajectories.

Potential misunderstanding: Neither term identifies a specific ancient ethnic group, language, or political community in the way a national or tribal name would. Both are modern scholarly tools for organizing archaeological evidence, and both should be used with the same caution this textbook has applied to similar categories since Chapter 1.

Historical Vocabulary
Pithouse and Pueblo

Pithouse, as used in this chapter, describes a partially subterranean dwelling built by excavating a pit and constructing a wood, brush, and earth superstructure over it, characteristic of early and, in many subregions, later Mogollon communities, as well as many other Southwestern communities of the same general periods.

Pueblo, used here in its architectural sense rather than as a proper name for a specific modern nation or community, describes above-ground construction using masonry or adobe, typically organized into contiguous rooms or roomblocks, of the kind that became increasingly common in parts of the Mogollon world, especially the Mimbres Valley, from around 1000 CE onward. Students should distinguish this general architectural usage from "Pueblo" as the name of specific present-day Indigenous nations and communities of the Southwest, examined in Chapter 18 and Chapter 30.

Historical Debate

How Should Archaeologists Draw the Boundaries of "Mogollon"?

The question: Should "Mogollon" be treated as a single, coherent archaeological tradition spanning the entire area from the mountain highlands to the northern Mexican borderlands, or should archaeologists instead recognize several more narrowly defined regional traditions, of which the Mimbres tradition is only the best known, loosely grouped together for convenience rather than treated as one unified whole?

Interpretation A — Mogollon as a single broad tradition: Some archaeologists argue that the recurring combination of pithouse and, later, masonry architecture, broadly similar farming adaptations, and related pottery technologies across the whole region justifies treating Mogollon as a single, if internally diverse, archaeological tradition, comparable in scope to Hohokam or Ancestral Pueblo.
Interpretation B — Mogollon as a loose grouping of distinct regional traditions: Other archaeologists emphasize that the differences among highland, desert-margin, and borderland communities, in settlement pattern, architectural timing, and material culture, are substantial enough that treating them as a single tradition risks obscuring more than it reveals, and that subregional labels better reflect the actual diversity of the archaeological record.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of excavated sites, pottery sequences, and architectural data; they differ in how much shared pattern is required before archaeologists should treat a set of communities as one tradition rather than several related ones.

Why it matters: This textbook uses "Mogollon" as a single overarching label, consistent with common scholarly practice, while emphasizing throughout, as this chapter has done, the substantial internal diversity the label contains. Students should understand that the label's convenience does not resolve the underlying scholarly question of how finely archaeological traditions ought to be subdivided.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 16.2, 16.7, and 16.9 of this chapter, construct an argument for each interpretation, and explain which you find more persuasive, or whether you think both approaches serve different, equally valid purposes.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Within a Changing Mogollon Community

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical viewpoints grounded in archaeological evidence about plausible roles and choices, not quotations or claims about specific named individuals.

A household head deciding whether to invest in a masonry roomblock: Building above-ground masonry rooms required more labor and material investment than maintaining pithouses, a choice that made most sense where a household expected to remain in one place for an extended period and where sufficient stone and labor were available.
A potter working within an established community tradition: Producing painted pottery required years of accumulated skill in clay selection, pigment preparation, and firing technique, skill very likely passed down and refined within particular families or communities of practice, and painted designs likely carried meanings understood within the community that are only partly recoverable today.
A farmer adapting to a drier, lower-elevation environment: Communities in the desert-margin basins south of the Mogollon Highlands needed different strategies than highland communities, relying on runoff farming techniques suited to flash-flood water rather than the more consistent rainfall available at higher elevations.
A participant in exchange with neighboring Hohokam or northern Mexican communities: Access to marine shell, turquoise, and other valued materials depended on maintaining relationships with communities beyond one's own village, relationships that required trust, reciprocity, and sustained contact across considerable distances.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Pithouses to Pueblos—But Not Everywhere at Once

What Changed

  • Architecture shifted, in some subregions and periods, from partially subterranean pithouses toward above-ground masonry roomblocks
  • Pottery technology and style developed from plain brownware toward increasingly elaborate painted traditions, most notably Mimbres black-on-white ware
  • Village size and, in some areas, population density increased across the centuries leading into the Classic Mimbres period
  • Settlement patterns reorganized substantially in the centuries after roughly 1130 CE, including population movement toward Ancestral Pueblo and northern Mexican regions

What Stayed the Same

  • A mixed subsistence economy combining farming with hunting and wild plant gathering persisted throughout nearly the entire span of Mogollon history
  • Exchange relationships with neighboring Hohokam, Ancestral Pueblo, and northern Mexican communities continued across every period this chapter examines
  • Local adaptation to varied mountain, valley, and desert-margin environments remained a defining feature of the tradition from its earliest to its latest phases

Why the pattern matters: The shift from pithouses to pueblos did not occur simultaneously or uniformly across the Mogollon world. The Mimbres Valley shifted earlier and more completely than many surrounding subregions, where pithouse construction, or a mixture of both architectural forms, persisted for generations longer. Presenting this transition as a single, universal event would misrepresent the pace and unevenness the archaeological record actually shows.

Cause and Effect

Why Mogollon Settlement Patterns Changed After 1130 CE

Long-term causes

Centuries of population growth, increasing investment in fixed agricultural land, and shifting land-use pressures and social organization within valleys and basins that had supported farming communities over many generations. Older explanations emphasizing straightforward environmental degradation from long-term farming have been substantially revised by more recent scholarship, which treats population, land use, social organization, and climate as interacting variables rather than a single cause.

Short-term causes

Periods of drought and climatic variability documented through environmental evidence such as tree-ring records, which affected farming reliability in specific subregions during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

Effect

Communities in various parts of the Mogollon world reorganized settlement, in some cases aggregating into larger villages and in others dispersing or relocating toward neighboring regions to the north and south.

Historical significance

These movements connected Mogollon-region populations to the later histories of Ancestral Pueblo communities on the Colorado Plateau and to developments in the northern Mexican borderlands, rather than ending Mogollon history in isolation.

Historical complexity: Archaeologists caution against attributing this reorganization to any single cause, such as drought alone. Social factors, including conflict, shifting exchange relationships, and changing community preferences about settlement scale and organization, likely worked together with environmental pressures in ways that varied from one subregion to another.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Work and Domestic Life in Mogollon Communities

Behind the architectural and ceramic sequences this chapter has emphasized lay the ordinary daily work of Mogollon households: planting and tending maize, beans, and squash; grinding maize using manos and metates (handstones and grinding slabs) found in abundance at excavated sites; hunting deer, rabbits, and other game; gathering wild seeds, nuts, and other plant foods according to their seasonal availability; producing and maintaining pottery, textiles, and stone tools; and building and periodically rebuilding pithouses or, in later periods and places, masonry rooms.

Archaeological evidence, including grinding tools, textile fragments, and the spatial organization of household work areas, allows historians to reconstruct general patterns of domestic labor, though it rarely permits confident conclusions about how specific tasks were divided among household members by age or gender, a caution consistent with this textbook's broader treatment of household archaeology across earlier chapters. Burial evidence, including grave goods and, in the Mimbres case, painted pottery placed with the dead, provides some of the richest surviving evidence for how Mogollon communities understood death, individual identity, and the relationship between the living and the deceased, though, as Section 16.10 emphasized, the specific content of those understandings often remains only partly recoverable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined "Mogollon" as an archaeological tradition, a modern scholarly category built from converging lines of evidence, architecture, pottery, settlement pattern, and subsistence, rather than a single ancient people's own name for itself. Understanding this distinction is essential to reading the rest of the chapter's narrative accurately, since the Mogollon world, properly understood, encompassed considerable internal diversity across its mountain, valley, plateau, desert-margin, and borderland subregions.

The chapter traced a direct line of development from the mixed farming and foraging economies examined in Chapter 15 to the increasingly residentially stable pithouse communities that archaeologists identify as early Mogollon, beginning by around 200 CE. It examined pithouse architecture in detail, explaining both what this evidence reveals about household organization and daily life and what it cannot responsibly establish about kinship, authority, or belief without additional corroborating evidence.

Agriculture across the Mogollon world varied by environment, from rainfall farming in the well-watered mountain highlands to runoff farming in drier basin-margin country, a variation this chapter distinguished carefully from the larger-scale canal irrigation examined among Hohokam communities in the following chapter. Hunting and wild plant gathering remained economically significant throughout the tradition's history, reinforcing this textbook's consistent emphasis on mixed rather than purely agricultural economies across the ancient Southwest.

Pottery received sustained attention because of its central importance to how archaeologists actually define and date the Mogollon tradition. The chapter's Historical Method feature explained how ceramic style, architecture, settlement pattern, subsistence evidence, and geographic distribution must be combined, rather than relied upon individually, before archaeologists responsibly identify an archaeological tradition, and why shared pottery style alone cannot establish shared ethnic or political identity.

Settlement patterns changed considerably across the centuries this chapter examined, moving in some subregions and periods from pithouses toward above-ground masonry construction, but this transition occurred unevenly rather than simultaneously across the Mogollon world, as the chapter's Continuity and Change feature demonstrated using the Mimbres Valley's comparatively early and complete architectural shift as a specific case.

The chapter gave the Mimbres world sustained, separate attention, distinguishing it clearly from the Mogollon tradition as a whole while examining its distinctive above-ground roomblock villages and its renowned black-on-white painted pottery. The Material Evidence Window on a Mimbres burial bowl demonstrated both what this celebrated pottery tradition can responsibly tell historians, about burial practice, artistic skill, and structured belief, and what remains genuinely uncertain, including the specific meaning of painted imagery and the exact purpose of the deliberate perforation of burial vessels.

Exchange and regional interaction connected Mogollon communities throughout their history with Hohokam communities to the west, Ancestral Pueblo communities to the north, and communities across the modern international border in northern Chihuahua and Sonora, including eventual connections to the significant later center of Paquimé. These connections demonstrate that the Mogollon world was never isolated, and that its history must be understood as part of a wider, interconnected regional and even Mesoamerican-linked system of exchange.

Finally, the chapter addressed the significant reorganization, aggregation, and migration that affected much of the Mogollon world in the centuries after roughly 1130 CE, and especially between 1300 and 1450 CE, explaining why "disappearance" is a historically inaccurate description of this process. Communities relocated and reorganized, and archaeological evidence has been interpreted as consistent with population movement and cultural interaction connecting parts of the Mogollon region with later Pueblo communities examined in Chapter 18 and with northern Mexican communities connected to Paquimé, connections that present-day Indigenous communities also understand, in part, through their own oral traditions and historical memory alongside the archaeological evidence.

These themes, the careful use of archaeological categories, the recognition of regional and temporal diversity within a single named tradition, the responsible interpretation of pottery and other material evidence, and the framing of later transformation as reorganization rather than disappearance, prepare students directly for Chapter 17's examination of Hohokam irrigation communities and Chapter 18's examination of Ancestral Pueblo worlds, both of which developed alongside, interacted with, and in some respects show evidence of population movement and cultural interaction connecting them to the Mogollon world this chapter has examined.

The study of layered archaeological deposits, used to establish the relative sequence in which material was deposited over time.
Exchange network
A system of trade and reciprocal relationships through which materials such as shell, turquoise, and pottery moved among neighboring and distant communities.
Turquoise
A blue-green mineral mined at several Southwestern locations and widely exchanged and worked into ornaments across Mogollon and neighboring traditions.
Marine shell
Shell originating from the Gulf of California, exchanged widely across the Southwest, often through Hohokam intermediary networks, and used in ornament production.
Paquimé (Casas Grandes)
A significant later settlement in northern Chihuahua whose material culture shows connections to both Mogollon-tradition communities and Mesoamerican exchange networks.
Aggregation
The process by which dispersed communities or populations consolidated into larger, more concentrated settlements, documented across the Mogollon world especially after roughly 1130 CE.
Reorganization
The historically preferred term, used throughout this chapter, for the settlement, social, and population changes affecting Mogollon-region communities after roughly 1130 to 1300 CE, in place of the misleading term "disappearance."
Borderlands archaeology
The study of archaeological patterns that extend across the modern United States–Mexico border, essential to understanding the full geographic extent of the Mogollon tradition.
Great kiva
Standard archaeological term for an especially large communal pithouse or structure identified at some Mogollon and Mimbres sites, used as shorthand for a recurring architectural and functional pattern rather than as proof of identical religious meaning across different periods and communities.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold two ideas together at once: that "Mogollon" identifies a genuinely useful and evidence-based archaeological pattern, and that this pattern encompasses far more internal diversity, across mountain, valley, plateau, desert-margin, and borderland environments, than any single site or period can represent. Learning to hold both ideas at once, rather than collapsing the category into either meaningless vagueness or false uniformity, is itself one of the central historical skills this textbook has been building since Chapter 1.

The chapter's sustained attention to Mimbres painted pottery illustrates why this care matters. A single, visually striking body of evidence can easily come to stand, in popular understanding, for an entire, much larger and more varied historical world. This chapter has tried to give Mimbres pottery its full due, as a genuinely remarkable technical and artistic achievement and as valuable evidence for burial practice and belief, while insisting that centuries of Mogollon history, across most of the tradition's geographic extent, unfolded without producing anything resembling a Mimbres painted bowl.

This chapter also reinforces a broader lesson about how archaeological categories work, a lesson that will recur directly in Chapter 17's treatment of Hohokam irrigation communities and Chapter 18's treatment of Ancestral Pueblo worlds. Each of these traditions represents a genuine, evidence-based regional pattern, and each also contains internal diversity, blurred boundaries with its neighbors, and a history of ongoing exchange and interaction that refuses to fit neatly inside a single label. Understanding Mogollon as this chapter has presented it, a broad, adaptive, internally varied, and thoroughly interconnected regional world rather than a single ancient nation, prepares students to read the following two chapters with the same careful attention.

Finally, this chapter's treatment of the reorganization and migration that reshaped much of the Mogollon world after roughly 1130 to 1300 CE connects directly to this textbook's larger and recurring argument, introduced in Chapter 1 and repeated throughout this volume: that Indigenous history does not end at moments of apparent transformation, but continues through adaptation, movement, and the ongoing life of descendant communities. The story of the Mogollon world does not conclude in this chapter; it continues into the Hohokam and Ancestral Pueblo worlds examined next, and into the histories of present-day Indigenous nations of the Southwest and northern Mexico whose own understanding of this deep past remains an essential part of the larger history this textbook is committed to telling.
