Indigenous Economies after Conquest

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Between the 1880s and the 1920s, Indigenous families across North America sold wool, wheat, fish, baskets, and labor; ran cattle and sheep; harvested crops for settler farmers; worked on railroad grades and in canneries; and moved between homes, workplaces, and towns, often within a single year. They did so under laws that limited where they could live, what they could sell, and how they could hold land. Conquest changed the terms of Indigenous economic life. It did not end it.

Consider the range of working lives that coexisted in the decades after the major military conflicts and treaty settlements examined in Volumes VI and VII. In one part of the Southwest, a Diné household might depend on a flock of sheep and goats whose wool was carded, spun, and woven by women into textiles that were sold through a trading post and, eventually, to buyers who never visited the Navajo Nation. On the Pacific coast of present-day British Columbia, families from many nations traveled each summer to cannery settlements at the mouths of salmon rivers, where men fished and women and children worked in the processing sheds. On the northern Plains, a Lakota family might keep a small herd of cattle and a few horses while also leasing allotted land to a non-Indigenous rancher, hauling freight, or working for wages at an agency. In the Sonoran Desert borderlands, Yaqui workers laid track and worked mines whose products moved on railroads that did not exist a generation earlier. These were not variations on a single story of decline. They were distinct economic responses to distinct environments, laws, markets, and opportunities.

Chapter 91 is the first chapter of Part XVI, "Indigenous Peoples in the Modern World," and it marks a change in the angle of vision. Chapters 85 through 90 examined institutions and events that sought to control, transform, or destroy Indigenous communities: the Indian Act, allotment, boarding and residential schools, the suppression of ceremonial life, and the violence at Wounded Knee. Those chapters necessarily emphasized state power. This chapter asks a complementary question: how did Indigenous peoples make a living, support their kin, and build economic institutions while living under those constraints? The answer requires attention to production, exchange, labor, credit, and mobility, and to the households, extended families, and communities within which economic decisions were actually made.

The central argument is that conquest transformed Indigenous economic opportunities and institutions but did not eliminate Indigenous economic agency. Indigenous peoples combined older systems of production and exchange with new activities: wage employment, livestock ownership, commercial farming, industrial labor, craft sales, commercial fishing, and migration to follow work. Few families abandoned one way of living for another. More commonly they assembled several livelihoods at once, shifting the mixture as markets, weather, regulation, and family circumstances changed. The consequences varied significantly by nation, region, gender, and access to land, water, and resources. A people with secure access to a productive river, a functioning irrigation system, or a strong market for its goods could prosper in ways that a people dispossessed of land or cut off from fisheries could not.

The chapter also insists on a distinction that popular memory often blurs. Economic integration is not economic equality, and commercial adaptation is not cultural assimilation. Indigenous workers were often paid less than non-Indigenous workers for similar tasks, confined to particular occupations, and denied access to capital, credit, and legal protections that settler producers took for granted. Indigenous producers sold goods into markets whose prices and rules they did not control. At the same time, they used wages, livestock, and sales income to sustain households, support ceremonies, meet kinship obligations, and keep communities on or near their homelands. Wage labor and a continuing relationship to land and kin were not opposites; for many families they were parts of one strategy.

Historians reconstruct these economies from sources that are uneven and sometimes in tension with each other. Annual reports of the United States Office of Indian Affairs and the Canadian Department of Indian Affairs offer statistics on acres cultivated, livestock owned, and income earned, but they were written by officials with reasons to emphasize "progress" or to justify policy. Trading-post ledgers, cannery records, railroad payrolls, and company correspondence record transactions but rarely explain the decisions behind them. Photographs capture workers at their tasks while saying little about wages or consent. Oral histories, family accounts, and Indigenous-authored memoirs provide perspectives that official records omit, though they too are shaped by memory, audience, and the circumstances of their recording. Throughout the chapter these sources are weighed against one another, and the limits of each are stated openly.

The geographic frame follows this textbook's continental commitment. The chapter moves among the northern Plains, the Southwest, the Pacific coast from Alaska to Puget Sound, the Great Lakes, the northern forests of Canada, and the borderlands of northern Mexico and the southwestern United States, treating the international boundaries of the period as conditions that Indigenous workers and traders sometimes crossed and sometimes had to negotiate rather than as natural limits of the story. The primary chronological focus is roughly 1880 to 1930, though the chapter looks backward to earlier economic traditions and forward to developments examined in later chapters.

Within the larger textbook, Chapter 91 assumes the land policy and educational history of earlier chapters and concentrates on their economic effects. It prepares the way for Chapter 92 on Indigenous writers and political leaders, Chapter 93 on citizenship, Chapter 94 on the First World War, Chapter 95 on reform movements, and Chapter 96 on the condition of Indigenous North America on the eve of the New Deal. Many of the intellectuals, organizers, and reformers who appear in those chapters were themselves farmers, ranchers, wage earners, and the children of such families. Their political ideas cannot be understood apart from the economic worlds in which they lived.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Indigenous economic life after conquest is best understood as adaptation and continued agency rather than as a simple replacement of "traditional" economies by "modern" ones.
  2. Distinguish among subsistence production, commercial exchange, wage employment, and community-centered economic obligations, and explain how they overlapped in Indigenous households.
  3. Analyze how reservations, reserves, allotment, and administrative controls restricted access to land, water, timber, grazing, and markets, using economic mechanisms rather than legal description alone.
  4. Assess the effects of railroads and commercial markets on Indigenous economic geography, including both new opportunities and increased competition.
  5. Compare Indigenous agriculture and ranching in the United States and Canada, including the institutional obstacles created by the Canadian peasant-farming policy of 1889–1897.
  6. Describe Diné pastoral production and the commercial textile trade, distinguishing Indigenous artistic and economic decisions from trader influence.
  7. Explain how Indigenous fishing systems contributed to commercial fisheries on the Pacific coast and the Great Lakes, and how regulation and capital reshaped access.
  8. Evaluate Indigenous participation in salmon canneries, railroad construction, mining, seasonal agriculture, forestry, and domestic work, including wage inequality and workplace hierarchy.
  9. Interpret Indigenous craft production as technical knowledge, artistic innovation, and household enterprise rather than only as souvenir production.
  10. Trace patterns of seasonal, circular, and permanent migration, including Indigenous urban presence before the post-1945 relocation programs and labor across the United States–Mexico borderlands.
  11. Analyze the labor of women and children within family economies, distinguishing family work from exploitative or coerced employment.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether market participation increased Indigenous economic independence or deepened colonial dependency, using documentary evidence while recognizing differences among nations and regions.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did Indigenous nations and households bring to the late nineteenth-century economy, and why is it inaccurate to describe their economic life as beginning with European or American markets?
  2. How did reservations, reserves, and allotment change the mechanisms through which Indigenous families gained income, and who benefited from those changes?
  3. Why did so many Indigenous families combine subsistence activities with wage work and commercial sales rather than moving fully into one or the other?
  4. When government agricultural programs claimed to promote self-sufficiency, in what ways did they actually support, limit, or control Indigenous farmers and ranchers?
  5. How do historians separate the decisions made by Indigenous artisans and producers from the preferences and pressures of traders, buyers, and officials?
  6. Why were Indigenous fishers, cannery workers, and railroad laborers essential to regional industries, and why did their participation not produce equality?
  7. What does mobility reveal about Indigenous economic strategy, and how can historians distinguish voluntary migration from coercion and displacement?
  8. How did gender, age, and family organization shape the distribution of economic labor in Indigenous households?
  9. What kinds of evidence are strongest, and what kinds weakest, for reconstructing the economic lives of Indigenous families who left few written records?
  10. Why should economic integration not be confused with cultural assimilation, and what evidence supports the distinction?

Historical Context

By the 1880s, the military phase of continental conquest had largely ended in the United States and was ending in Canada, even though conflict continued in particular places, as Chapters 79 and 80 explain for the Apache Wars. Treaty negotiations and agreements, examined for Canada in Chapter 82 and for the United States across Volumes VI and VII, had set boundaries for reservations and reserves. The destruction of the buffalo economy, treated in Chapter 81, had removed the economic foundation of many Plains nations. The Red River and North-West Resistances, examined in Chapter 83, had ended with the Canadian state asserting firmer control over the Prairies. Chapter 84 compared the reservation and reserve systems that resulted. These developments meant that the economic setting of Indigenous life in 1880 differed sharply from that of 1830 or 1850.

The economic institutions of Indigenous nations did not begin in this period, however. Long before the conquest era, Indigenous peoples had practiced irrigation and floodwater agriculture, maize and bean cultivation, intensive salmon and whitefish fishing, bison hunting, trapping, specialized craft production, and long-distance exchange. Volumes I through V traced these systems and their transformation through contact and the fur trade. Indigenous economies had also incorporated European goods and animals for centuries: horses and cattle, metal tools, firearms, wool, and cloth entered Indigenous economic systems through exchange and were adapted to Indigenous purposes. The people who entered the 1880s were therefore experienced traders, producers, and managers of resources, not newcomers to economic life.

Between 1880 and 1930 several larger transformations altered the setting in which they worked. Railroads reached most regions, connecting formerly remote areas to national and international markets. Commercial agriculture expanded across the Plains, the Prairies, the Pacific Slope, and the irrigated Southwest. Mining, logging, and fishing became industrial enterprises, employing large labor forces and demanding large capital investments. Towns and cities grew, creating markets for goods and sources of employment. Settlers occupied land and water that Indigenous peoples had used, and governments regulated hunting, fishing, timber, and trade in ways that often disadvantaged Indigenous producers. Indigenous peoples entered this new economic geography with fewer resources than settler competitors and under legal systems that gave government agents substantial authority over their property and movement.

The legal and administrative frameworks differed by country and changed over time. In the United States, the allotment policy introduced by the Dawes Act of 1887 divided many reservations into individual parcels, as Chapter 86 explains, and later legislation made it easier for allotted land to be leased or sold. Federal agents supervised annuity distribution, rations, leasing, and the management of tribal resources, and Indian Office policy repeatedly emphasized farming as the proper basis of Indigenous livelihood. In Canada, the Indian Act, examined in Chapter 85, gave the Department of Indian Affairs extensive authority over reserves, and regulations governed whether and how Status Indians could sell produce, hold property, or travel. In Mexico, Indigenous communities faced a different set of pressures from a national government that prioritized the commercial development of land, mines, and railroads and that frequently regarded communal Indigenous landholding as an obstacle. Chapter 141 returns to Indigenous Mexico and the northern borderlands in the contemporary period.

Historians approaching these economies must resist two opposite errors. The first treats Indigenous economic activity after conquest as merely survival at the margins, as though Indigenous peoples had been excluded from the regional economies that developed around them. The evidence contradicts this: Indigenous labor was integral to salmon canneries, railroad construction, seasonal harvests, and ranching, and Indigenous producers supplied wool, fish, crops, livestock, and crafts to national markets. The second error treats market participation as proof of prosperity or assimilation. Participation frequently occurred on unequal terms, through dependency on traders, employers, and agents, and within systems that extracted more from Indigenous workers and resources than they returned. The chapter holds both truths together.

Indigenous Economic Life in a Changing Continental Order

Economic foundations, colonial constraints, and community adaptation

Section 91.1

Indigenous Economies after Conquest: Continuity, Adaptation, and Transformation

A persistent misconception holds that Indigenous economic history begins when European markets arrive, or that it ends when military defeat removes the older foundations of Indigenous production. Both ideas misread the evidence. Indigenous peoples entered the late nineteenth century with economic traditions that were old, diverse, and continually adjusted. The peoples of the Southwest irrigated and farmed in desert and upland environments, as Volume II described. The peoples of the Northwest Coast organized seasonal harvests of salmon, eulachon, shellfish, and sea mammals and preserved them for storage and exchange. Great Lakes communities fished whitefish and lake trout, harvested wild rice and maple sugar, hunted, and trapped. Plains peoples combined bison hunting with trade and, in many cases, with village agriculture. Northern forest and Arctic peoples hunted, fished, trapped, and traded across large territories. None of these systems was static; each had been repeatedly reorganized through the horse, the fur trade, European metal goods, and epidemic disease.

Nor were these economies purely local or subsistence-oriented. Exchange networks moved goods across long distances before and after European contact, a pattern traced in Chapters 20 and 36. Indigenous traders and producers had supplied furs, provisions, horses, and other goods to colonial and national markets for generations, as Chapters 45 and 46 showed for the fur trade. The late nineteenth-century economy therefore continued practices of commercial exchange that Indigenous peoples understood and had often shaped. What changed was the scale, speed, and legal setting of the market, and the degree to which governments and settler competitors controlled access to land, water, and resources.

Indigenous communities entered the 1880s from different foundations, and these differences are essential to explaining later outcomes. Some, such as the Pueblo nations, the Akimel O'odham, and many Haudenosaunee and Great Lakes communities, possessed established farming systems and had maintained them through the conquest era. Others, including many Pacific coast peoples, relied on fisheries and marine resources that remained abundant and valuable. The Diné rebuilt their livelihood around sheep, goats, and horses after the return from Bosque Redondo. Many Plains nations had depended on bison and now had to build new economic bases from livestock, rations, wages, and leases. Northern Subarctic peoples continued to rely on hunting, trapping, and fishing, increasingly integrated with the fur trade and wage employment. These foundations determined what each community could do with new opportunities and how vulnerable it was to new pressures.

The analysis in this chapter uses four overlapping categories. Subsistence production refers to the harvesting, growing, and making of goods for direct household or community use, including food, shelter, clothing, and tools. Commercial exchange refers to the sale or barter of goods to outside buyers or traders for cash, credit, or other goods. Wage employment refers to work for pay in an enterprise owned by someone else. Community-centered economic obligations refers to the sharing, gift-giving, reciprocal labor, ceremonial exchange, and kin support through which many Indigenous societies distributed resources and maintained social relationships. Historians use these categories as analytical tools, not as sharp divisions. A single sheep might be eaten at a family gathering, its wool sold at a trading post, and its offspring lent to a relative, so that a single animal could serve all four purposes in a single year. A cannery wage might finance both the purchase of a boat and the hosting of a feast.

This overlap is why historians have increasingly rejected the older model of Indigenous economic history as a linear shift from "traditional" to "modern" life. In that older model, wages and market sales appeared as signs that Indigenous people had abandoned their own economies and cultures, and continued subsistence activity appeared as a sign of "backwardness." The documentary record shows something quite different: Indigenous families regularly adopted new economic opportunities when these were available and useful, while maintaining the subsistence, kinship, and ceremonial practices that gave those opportunities meaning. This chapter treats that pattern as a strategy of adaptation rather than a transitional stage.

Section 91.2

Reservation Lands, Reserve Administration, and Economic Restrictions

Reservations in the United States and reserves in Canada reorganized Indigenous access to the resources on which economic life depended. Chapter 84 compared the two systems as instruments of confinement and administration; here the emphasis falls on how they worked economically. A reservation or reserve defined a territory within which a community had recognized rights, but it also narrowed the area from which Indigenous people could draw resources. Hunting and gathering territories shrank, seasonal movement across wide areas was restricted or prohibited, and access to timber, fisheries, grazing land, and water depended on rights that governments could define, qualify, or override. Many nations retained some off-reserve rights by treaty, as later chapters on treaty rights explain, but practical access to those resources was frequently limited by regulation, settler occupation, or enforcement.

Administrative control compounded these limits. In the United States, agents supervised the distribution of rations and annuities, approved or disapproved leases, managed tribal funds, and often controlled the sale of livestock and crops. Funds derived from land sales, timber, or leases were frequently held in trust accounts in Washington rather than in community hands, so that spending required approval. In Canada, Department of Indian Affairs agents held broad authority over reserve resources. Indian Act provisions supported permits for specified off-reserve transactions in produce and other reserve goods; separately, officials enforced an administrative pass system on the Prairies to control departures from reserves, although that pass system had no basis in Canadian legislation. Both constraints could impede trade, travel, and employment, but their legal foundations must not be conflated. Both systems treated Indigenous economic decisions as matters of government oversight rather than ordinary private or community choice, a condition that settler farmers and ranchers did not face.

In the United States, allotment changed the economic structure of many reservations. Chapter 86 describes the Dawes Act and its political purposes; its economic effects are the subject here. Allotment divided land held in common into parcels assigned to individuals, with "surplus" land opened to non-Indigenous settlement. For households, an allotment could be farmed, grazed, leased, or sold, but each of these options depended on resources the allottee might not have: tools, seed, draft animals, water, fencing, cash for early years, and knowledge of local markets. Many allotments were in areas ill suited to the small-plot farming the policy assumed, particularly on the semi-arid Plains where viable farms required larger holdings than most allotments provided. Heirship divided parcels further among descendants, creating fractional ownership that made efficient use difficult.

Leasing became the dominant economic use of many allotments. Legislation from the late 1880s onward allowed allottees to lease land, particularly when they were judged unable to farm it themselves, and non-Indigenous farmers and ranchers frequently held leases on large areas of reservation land. Lease income could give households a modest cash supplement and sometimes a significant one, but it also moved the productive use of land into non-Indigenous hands and often left the lessor dependent on payments rather than on production. Lease rates were often low, administered by agents, and affected by competition among lessees and by the difficulties allottees had in negotiating on equal terms. The Burke Act of 1906 and the more aggressive issuing of fee patents after 1917 made it easier for allottees to obtain unrestricted title, which also made it easier to lose land to sale or tax foreclosure. These developments redistributed income and land in ways that generally favored non-Indigenous purchasers and lessees, though outcomes varied among communities and individuals, and some allottees used sales or leases to finance other ventures.

Restrictions on investment were another mechanism. Because trust land generally could not be mortgaged, Indigenous farmers and ranchers had difficulty obtaining the credit that settler producers used to buy equipment, seed, and livestock. Where agents or reservation rules limited who could own machinery or sell products, Indigenous producers faced additional barriers. The aggregate effect was that Indigenous households often had land but lacked the capital and legal flexibility to use it competitively. In Canada, similar constraints applied to Status Indians on reserves, whose land could not be seized for debt but also could not easily serve as security for loans. Communal landholding and trust status protected land from sale in some circumstances, a point many Indigenous leaders valued, while also limiting its use as capital. Whether those protections were worth the economic limits was a question Indigenous communities debated, and it remained contested into the era of the Indian Reorganization Act, examined in Chapter 97.

Section 91.3

Railroads, Commercial Markets, and the Reorganization of Regional Economies

Railroads transformed the economic geography of North America between the 1860s and the early twentieth century. They shortened the time and lowered the cost of moving goods and people, tied remote regions to national and international markets, and brought settlers, tourists, and industrial investors to places that had been difficult to reach. For Indigenous peoples these changes were double-edged. A railroad could make a distant wool, fish, or craft market accessible and could create jobs in construction, maintenance, freight handling, and associated industries. The same railroad could bring competing producers, speed the extraction of timber, minerals, and grazing resources, and increase pressure on land and water.

The Diné experience illustrates both effects. In the early 1880s, the Atlantic and Pacific Railroad was built across northern New Mexico and Arizona along the southern edge of Diné country, and its stations became shipping points for wool, livestock, and, eventually, textiles. Trading posts on and near the reservation had existed since the 1860s and 1870s, but rail transport lowered the cost of moving wool to eastern buyers and brought manufactured goods, flour, coffee, cloth, and tools to the posts more cheaply. Diné households that had rebuilt their flocks after the return from Bosque Redondo found an expanding market for the wool they produced, and traders found it profitable to buy more of it. Rail construction and maintenance also provided wage work for some Diné men, an opportunity pursued alongside herding and farming rather than as a replacement for them. As §91.7 shows, the railway also brought tourists and collectors who became buyers of Diné textiles.

Similar patterns appeared elsewhere, with different outcomes. On the Canadian Prairies, the Canadian Pacific Railway, completed across the continent in 1885, connected reserve farmers to eastern grain markets in theory, but administrative restrictions often prevented them from taking advantage of it, as §91.5 explains. On the Pacific coast, railways connected canneries to inland and eastern markets and made canned salmon a global commodity, increasing the demand for Indigenous fishing and processing labor, as §91.10 shows. In the Great Lakes region, railroads and steamships expanded markets for fish and timber and altered the competitive position of small Indigenous operators against larger commercial fleets. In northern Mexico, railroads built during the Porfirian era served mining and agricultural export interests and hired large Indigenous labor forces, a pattern examined in §91.13 and §91.18.

Commercial agriculture, mining, and industrial processing reorganized regional economies in ways that affected Indigenous communities even when they were not direct participants. Expanding wheat farms on the Plains and Prairies, sheep and cattle operations across the West, copper and silver mines in the Southwest and northern Mexico, and sawmills in the Great Lakes and Pacific forests all increased demand for land and labor. They also increased competition for water, grazing land, fish, and timber. The reorganization generated income for some Indigenous people, particularly those who could sell labor or products to the new enterprises, and it undermined others whose resources were taken or degraded. Economic geography therefore shifted: places previously peripheral to national markets, such as Diné country, became suppliers within them; places that had been central to Indigenous trade, such as old river and portage routes, lost importance as railroads replaced them.

Historians should avoid treating the railroad as an automatic cause of any particular outcome. Rail transport lowered costs, but who benefited depended on who owned the processing facilities, who set the buying prices, who controlled the trading posts, and what rules governed Indigenous sales and movement. A railroad that made wool shipping cheaper for a trader might not raise the price a Diné household received if the trader had little competition. A railroad that opened a Prairie reserve to eastern markets meant little if reserve farmers were prohibited from selling produce freely. The significance of the railroad lay less in the technology than in the institutions, laws, and relationships through which it connected people to markets.

Section 91.4

Mixed Economies: Households, Kinship, Subsistence, and Cash Income

The organizing concept of this chapter is economic diversification as a family and community strategy. Indigenous households in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries rarely depended on a single source of livelihood. A Diné family might own sheep, goats, and horses, farm a small field, gather piñon nuts and other plants, produce textiles, buy goods at a trading post, and send a son to work on a railroad crew during part of the year. A Coast Salish family might fish for household needs, sell fish to a cannery, pick hops for wages in the late summer, and gather shellfish and berries in their seasons. An Anishinaabe household in the Great Lakes might fish, hunt, tap maple, harvest wild rice, work in a logging camp or sawmill in winter, and sell baskets or beadwork to tourists in summer. The pattern appears across regions, with the specific combination varying by local environment and opportunity.

Economic diversification served several purposes. It spread risk, since a failure in one activity, such as a drought-damaged crop, a collapsed fish run, or a wool price drop, could be offset by another. It matched labor to seasonal cycles: the demands of herding, planting, harvest, and fishing varied through the year, leaving periods in which household members could take outside work without abandoning home production. It allowed households to obtain goods that could be bought only with cash, such as flour, cloth, tools, fuel, and, increasingly, items associated with school attendance and church membership, while maintaining access to foods and materials produced locally. And it supported the maintenance of kin networks, since income earned in different places could be pooled and redistributed.

Kinship obligations structured the use of income. In many Indigenous societies, resources moved among relatives through expectations of sharing, hospitality, and mutual aid that outsiders often misread as improvidence. A worker who received wages might support parents and siblings who remained at home, contribute to a relative's ceremony, or lend money, livestock, or tools to extended family. Livestock holdings, in particular, could serve as both capital and social obligation, as the Diné case in §91.7 explains. Agents and missionaries frequently criticized these practices as obstacles to individual saving and accumulation, a criticism that reflected their own assumptions about proper economic behavior and not an objective judgment about Indigenous economic rationality. Indigenous households were calculating the long-term security that kin networks provided in environments where individual accumulation could be fragile.

Money moved through these communities in complex ways. Wages earned in distant canneries, railroad camps, or harvest fields came back to home communities in the form of purchases, gifts, and the contributions that returning workers made to family farms or herds. Remittances were not always formal, and they are difficult to trace in documentary records, but the effects can be seen in the survival of rural communities that could not have been supported by on-reserve production alone. In this sense, wage work outside the community sometimes supported, rather than replaced, local production: it financed livestock, fencing, seed, and tools, paid taxes and fees where they applied, and sustained the households of elders and young children.

Historical Vocabulary
Mixed Economy

Definition. A household or community livelihood that combines several kinds of economic activity, such as subsistence production, commercial sales, wage employment, and reciprocal exchange, rather than depending on one.

Historical context. Indigenous families between roughly 1880 and 1930 typically drew on several sources of support, adjusting the combination through the year and across generations according to opportunity, season, and need.

Why it matters. The concept explains why Indigenous workers appear in wage records, farm censuses, trading-post ledgers, and ethnographic accounts of subsistence all at once, and why these sources do not contradict each other.

Potential misunderstanding. The term does not describe a halfway stage between a "traditional" economy and a "modern" one. A mixed economy was often a deliberate, stable strategy, and settler farm families also combined activities such as farming, wage work, and local trade. The distinguishing feature of the Indigenous case was the extent of legal restriction and discrimination within which households assembled their mixtures.

Cause and Effect

Why Indigenous Households Diversified Their Livelihoods

Long-term causes

Indigenous economies had long combined hunting, gathering, farming, fishing, and exchange, making multiple livelihoods a familiar practice. Seasonal and ecological variation encouraged flexible use of resources.

Short-term causes

The loss of land and mobility, the collapse of bison and other resources in some regions, and the limits on any single source of income encouraged households to add new activities. Need for cash grew as goods and taxes or fees required payment.

Immediate context

Railroads, canneries, farms, mines, and towns created seasonal wage demand and new markets, while administrative restrictions limited the scale of independent commercial production.

Effects

Households spread risk and continued subsistence practices while participating in regional markets, supporting continuity of community life and kin networks while exposing workers to wage inequality and price cycles.

Historical complexity: Diversification was both opportunity and constraint. Families adopted multiple livelihoods because they could not depend fully on any one; the same pattern reflected resourcefulness and the narrowing of choices. The balance differed by nation, region, and household.

Section 91.5

Indigenous Agriculture on Reservations and Canadian Reserves

Governments in both countries made farming the centerpiece of their economic programs for Indigenous communities. The assumption behind these programs was that Indigenous peoples should become small, self-sufficient farmers on individual plots, an expectation that drew on settler agrarian ideals and on the belief that agriculture would lead to "civilization." That assumption was historically inaccurate. Many Indigenous nations had practiced agriculture for centuries or millennia, as Chapter 13 showed, and others had substantial experience with livestock. The question after 1880 was not whether Indigenous people could farm but under what conditions, with what equipment, on what land, and for which markets.

On the Canadian Prairies, the numbered treaties examined in Chapter 82 included provisions for agricultural assistance, such as tools, seed, and livestock, and many First Nations took up farming in the years after the treaties. Historians who have studied the reserves of the 1880s have documented Cree, Saulteaux, Assiniboine, Blackfoot, Dakota, and other farmers who planted crops, kept livestock, and in some cases sold grain and produce to nearby settlements, forts, and government purchasers. These efforts began under difficult conditions: reserve soil and location were sometimes poor, seed and implements arrived late or in inadequate quantity, and the collapse of the buffalo economy meant that many communities faced hunger while their first crops were being established. Early farming nevertheless showed considerable initiative, and in some districts reserve farmers produced surpluses and sold them in local markets.

Administrative policy then constrained that development. Indian Act provisions and amendments supported a permit system regulating certain off-reserve sales or transfers of agricultural produce and other goods; agents could thereby influence when farmers reached buyers and at what terms. Separately, officials imposed a pass system on parts of the Prairie reserves, especially after 1885, demanding written permission to leave and thus obstructing travel to markets, workplaces, and relatives. Unlike the produce-permit rules, this pass system had no statutory basis; its use varied by place and official and was resisted by First Nations people. Authorities invoked protection and order, but both forms of control could restrict commercial independence. The legal distinction matters: a measure might impede livelihoods either through enacted law or through coercive administrative practice. The most notable example of policy obstructing the activity it was meant to promote was the "peasant farming" policy introduced by Indian Commissioner Hayter Reed in 1889. Under this approach, reserve farmers were directed to cultivate small plots using hand tools or simple implements, with machinery such as reapers and binders discouraged or withheld, on the reasoning that Indigenous farmers should first learn to farm modestly before expanding. Officials also argued that machinery could lead to dependence or to the rapid dispossession of Indigenous farmers by creditors. The effect was that Indigenous farmers were prevented from using the labor-saving equipment on which settler farmers' commercial success increasingly depended, and individuals who farmed larger acreages sometimes met official disapproval. Contemporary and later critics, including some Indian agents and Indigenous farmers, pointed out that the policy made it difficult for reserve farmers to compete in grain markets. The policy was formally abandoned in 1897, though its effects on productivity and on the relationship between reserve farmers and the Department lasted longer. Historians, notably Sarah Carter in her study of Prairie reserve farming, have used this episode to argue that the decline of reserve agriculture owed more to government policy than to Indigenous unwillingness or inability to farm.

In the United States, agricultural programs followed a different path but encountered comparable difficulties. Agency farmers, boarding-school training in agriculture, and allotment all aimed at promoting individual farming. Indigenous farmers on many reservations raised crops and livestock, and in places where land, water, and markets were favorable, such as parts of the Pacific Northwest, the upper Midwest, and some irrigated Southwestern areas, farming provided real income. In many others, particularly on the semi-arid Plains, allotments were too small or dry to support commercially viable farms, farmers lacked capital and machinery, and distance from markets raised costs. Reports from the agencies frequently noted acres cultivated and bushels produced, but they also revealed the shortcomings of the programs they described. Over time, as §91.2 explained, many allottees leased their land to non-Indigenous farmers, which moved farming activity off Indigenous hands rather than building an Indigenous farming economy.

The key historical question is whether colonial agricultural programs supported Indigenous prosperity or attempted to control the forms of economic independence Indigenous farmers could achieve. The evidence suggests that both elements were present, in varying proportions. Officials sincerely hoped to promote farming and sometimes provided useful assistance. But restrictions on sales, machinery, and movement, together with limited credit and unfavorable land, reflected assumptions that Indigenous farmers should remain supervised and modest in scale, and these assumptions undercut the programs' stated goals. Indigenous farmers responded in different ways, from working within the rules to petitioning, evading restrictions, shifting toward ranching or wage work, or building their own markets through kinship and community networks.

Section 91.6

Cattle Ranching, Horse Breeding, and the Plains Livestock Economy

The destruction of the buffalo economy, examined in Chapter 81, left Plains nations with a shortage of the food, hides, and trade goods on which they had relied. Rations issued by the government under treaties and agreements provided some food, sometimes inadequately, and agency beef distribution acquainted many families with cattle. Livestock raising offered a way to rebuild an economy using skills and values that had developed during the equestrian era: knowledge of horses, grazing land, and animals, and a social emphasis on herds as wealth. Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Blackfeet, Comanche, Kiowa, and other nations developed livestock enterprises of different sizes and types.

The initial steps were often modest. Families received breeding cattle from government issues or purchased animals, and kept them on grazing lands within the reservation. In some places, Indigenous cattle owners expanded their herds substantially and sold animals to agency and military contractors, to cattle buyers, or to meatpackers who shipped them by rail. Horses, long central to Plains economies, remained valuable for work, transport, and exchange, and Indigenous horse breeders continued to supply local and regional markets, even as mechanization later reduced demand. Commercial ranching required access to grass and water, usually communal on reservations at first and increasingly subdivided as allotment advanced. It also needed winter feed, fencing, and some capital, and it was vulnerable to drought, harsh winters, livestock disease, and price swings.

Three forms of Plains livestock economy can be distinguished. Household herds were small holdings of cattle and horses owned by individual families and used for food, trade, and prestige. Community or tribal livestock initiatives involved herds managed collectively or with tribal funds, sometimes through agency supervision. Employment on outside-owned ranches provided wages for Indigenous cowhands, who brought skill in handling horses and cattle and who worked on operations owned by non-Indigenous ranchers. The three were not mutually exclusive. A man might work as a ranch hand for part of the year and tend his family's herd for the rest, and a community might both operate its own herd and lease grazing rights to outside ranchers.

Leasing was the point at which Indigenous ranching and the larger cattle economy met most directly, and also where conflict was greatest. Large cattle companies sought access to reservation grassland, and agents, tribal councils, and individuals negotiated leases, sometimes with real benefit to the community and sometimes under pressure. Leasing brought revenue but displaced Indigenous herds from pasture, particularly where leases covered large areas. In some reservations, scholars studying Rosebud and other Lakota reservations have documented how leasing and agency policy favored non-Indigenous operators and how Indigenous cattle owners were crowded out of range they might otherwise have used. Allotment and fencing increased these pressures by subdividing grazing land and requiring individual owners to manage small parcels in a livestock system that favored large, contiguous ranges.

Environment and disease added further risk. Severe winters, especially in the late 1880s, killed large numbers of cattle across the northern Plains, ruining many non-Indigenous and Indigenous owners alike. Drought in particular years reduced grass and forced herd reductions. Contagious livestock diseases and quarantine rules could restrict sales. Indigenous cattle owners, with limited capital reserves and lacking the credit that settler ranchers sometimes drew on, had less ability to absorb these shocks. Still, the evidence for Indigenous ranching in this period is substantial. In places where families kept access to grass and water and where agents did not impede sales, Indigenous livestock raising supported households and communities and became a foundation of reservation economies that lasted into the twentieth century. It was a case of continuity as well as adaptation: horses, herd management, and mobility between pastures drew on older Plains practices.

Section 91.7

Diné Sheep, Wool, Weaving, and Pastoral Production

The Diné economy offers a substantial case study of how an Indigenous nation built a distinctive commercial and subsistence economy after conquest. The experience of forced removal and confinement at Bosque Redondo, examined in Chapter 71, ended with the treaty of 1868 and the return of the Diné to a portion of their homeland. Livestock were central to recovery. The government distributed sheep and goats to returning families, and Diné herders, drawing on long experience with Spanish-introduced Churro sheep and with goats and horses, rebuilt flocks over the following decades. By the late nineteenth century, sheep and goats supported many households and formed the basis of a pastoral system tied to seasonal movement between pastures, often between summer and winter locations, and organized around extended family groups.

Livestock represented several kinds of value at once. They were a source of food, especially mutton, and of wool and hides. They were wealth that could be bought, sold, inherited, and lent. They were security against hardship, since animals could be sold or traded in difficult years. They were bound up with kin relations, as animals were often held by a woman and passed through female lines, and herds were managed by families and shared in ways that sustained matrilocal households. And they carried cultural meaning, connecting the Diné to ceremony, to place, and to the practices of earlier generations. Historians, including Colleen O'Neill in her study of Navajo labor and Marsha Weisiger in her study of livestock, have emphasized this combination of economic and cultural significance, which helps explain why Diné families resisted pressures to reduce or abandon their herds in later decades, a story that belongs primarily to later chapters and is only foreshadowed here.

Women's labor was at the center of the system. Women commonly owned and managed sheep, sheared them, processed wool by washing, carding, and spinning, dyed yarn using plant and later commercial dyes, and wove the finished textiles. Weaving was both a household craft and an increasingly commercial enterprise, and it was done in addition to other work, including herding, farming, childcare, and household management. Weavers drew on a technical knowledge of fibers, dyes, loom construction, and design that was transmitted within families and that evolved over time.

Trading posts were the point of contact between this household economy and the wider market. From the 1870s onward traders established posts on and near the reservation, buying wool, hides, pelts, and later textiles, and selling flour, coffee, sugar, cloth, tools, hardware, and canned goods. Transactions mixed cash, store credit, and barter, a system examined in §91.16. The coming of the railroad in the early 1880s, discussed in §91.3, made it easier to ship wool and bring in goods, and posts multiplied. Lorenzo Hubbell's post at Ganado, which survives as a historic site with substantial records, became one of the best-known. Traders also operated as middlemen who graded wool, set prices, and extended credit, and who often occupied a position of considerable economic power in their districts.

From about 1890 to 1915, the market for Navajo textiles changed. Earlier weaving had produced blankets and clothing for Diné use and for trade with other Indigenous nations, and Diné blankets had also been sold to outsiders. In the later period, collectors, tourists, and retailers, including those connected to railway and hotel systems of the Southwest, created demand for rugs suited to Euro-American homes. Traders encouraged changes in size, weight, and design, supplied commercial yarns and dyes, and in some cases promoted regional styles and named patterns. Historians who have examined this commerce emphasize that weavers were not passive: they decided what to weave, how to price their work in negotiation with traders, and whether to adopt or ignore suggestions. Scholars of the Southwest craft market, including Erika Marie Bsumek, have shown how traders and retailers shaped the idea of the "authentic" Navajo rug and how that idea affected prices and consumer expectations. The evidence suggests a collaboration marked by inequality: weavers exercised artistic and technical judgment, while traders and retailers controlled much of the market and captured much of the profit.

The result was a household economy in which wool, mutton, and weaving each contributed to income and security. Textile income was often a significant supplement and in some households a primary support. It also meant that economic decisions, such as how many sheep to keep, whether to sell wool or weave it, and when to sell, were made by families balancing market prices, household needs, and ecological conditions. The system was resilient but exposed: it depended on stable markets, access to grazing, and relations with traders, and it would face severe disruption from the livestock-reduction programs of the 1930s, discussed in later chapters.

Section 91.8

Irrigation, Dryland Farming, and Agricultural Resilience in the Southwest

Southwestern Indigenous farmers operated some of the continent's most sophisticated agricultural systems, long before colonial agricultural instruction. Volume II traced the origins of Pueblo, Hohokam, and Mogollon agriculture, and Chapters 17 and 18 described irrigation engineering and Ancestral Pueblo farming. Their descendants continued to cultivate maize, beans, squash, cotton, wheat introduced during the Spanish period, orchards, and other crops, using methods adapted to arid conditions. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries these systems met new pressures: settlers claiming land and water, federal irrigation projects, changing markets, and administrative policies.

Southwestern agriculture was not uniform. Irrigation agriculture along rivers such as the Rio Grande, the Gila, and the Salt used ditches and canals to bring water to fields; Pueblo communities along the Rio Grande maintained irrigation systems with community organization of labor and maintenance, and Akimel O'odham farmers on the Gila built canals and farmed extensive fields. Floodwater farming used the seasonal flow of washes and arroyos, directing runoff onto fields at the mouths of drainages. Dryland farming, practiced prominently by the Hopi, depended on precipitation and on the careful selection of field sites and crop varieties, with knowledge of soils, moisture, and local microclimates handed down over generations. These methods demonstrate environmental specialization and accumulated knowledge that colonial instruction did not originate.

Markets for Southwestern Indigenous farm products existed before the period and expanded in some cases. The Akimel O'odham of the Gila River, for example, produced substantial wheat surpluses in the 1860s and 1870s that were sold to travelers, military posts, and local markets. Pueblo and Hopi farmers sold or traded produce, and local food markets within and between communities continued. Wheat, corn, beans, melons, fruit, and other crops supplied households and, where transport allowed, local buyers.

Water was the central conflict. As non-Indigenous settlers occupied land upstream of Indigenous farms in the late nineteenth century and diverted river water for their own irrigation, flow to Indigenous fields declined. The Akimel O'odham experience is among the best documented: upstream diversions on the Gila in the 1870s and following decades sharply reduced the water available to Pima farms, leading to crop failures and hardship, and ending a period of agricultural prosperity. The loss was a consequence of settler water use and the lack of legal protection for Indigenous water claims in practice, not of any failure of Indigenous farming skill. The policy questions raised by these conflicts, including federal irrigation projects, treaty-based water rights, and the legal doctrine that later came to be associated with Indigenous water claims, are examined in Chapter 120, and the Reclamation Act of 1902 and subsequent federal projects shaped water allocation across the region in ways that often favored non-Indigenous users, though some projects also served Indigenous lands.

Land title added another source of uncertainty for Pueblo farmers. Over the nineteenth century non-Indigenous claimants had occupied parts of lands that Pueblo communities regarded as their own, creating disputes over title and water. The Pueblo Lands Act of 1924 sought to resolve these claims through a board that examined competing titles, with mixed results for the Pueblos, as later chapters discuss. The point for present purposes is that Pueblo agriculture, though technologically sophisticated, depended on secure land and water, and its security was repeatedly contested.

Agricultural resilience in the Southwest rested on several features: environmental knowledge accumulated across generations, diversification of crops and plots to spread risk, community organization of irrigation and labor, and the integration of farming with livestock, wage work, and craft production. Many Pueblo, Hopi, and O'odham households combined farming with sheep or cattle herding, pottery or basket sales, and seasonal wage work, as §91.4 described. The farming economy therefore survived and adapted despite pressure. Its persistence, like those of other Indigenous economies, depended less on colonial programs than on Indigenous knowledge and community institutions.

Section 91.9

Pacific Coast Indigenous Fisheries and Commercial Expansion

The Pacific coast of North America, from southeastern Alaska through British Columbia to Puget Sound and the Columbia River, supported fisheries that Indigenous peoples had managed for millennia. Chapter 10 traced the ancient Northwest Coast economies and Chapter 33 described the societies of the coast on the eve of colonization. Coast Salish, Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Heiltsuk, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth, Nisga'a, Gitxsan, Stó:lō, and other peoples harvested salmon, halibut, herring, eulachon, shellfish, and other species using weirs, traps, nets, spears, hooks, and reef nets, built canoes and, later, boats suited to different waters, and preserved fish by smoking, drying, and, in some places, trading it in prepared form. Fishing sites were owned or managed by families, houses, or nations, and access was governed by inherited rights, protocol, and community law. These systems supported dense populations, ceremonial life, and extensive trade.

The arrival of commercial fishing in the later nineteenth century did not replace these systems but built on them, and in important ways depended on them. Canning companies located their plants at river mouths and inlets where salmon runs were rich, and they recruited the people who already fished those waters. Indigenous fishers brought knowledge of runs, tides, and fishing places, and techniques and equipment adapted to local waters. In many districts, Indigenous fishers were the main suppliers of fish to the early canneries, working with canoes and gill nets before companies introduced larger boats and different gear. Their participation helped create the commercial fisheries of British Columbia and the Pacific Northwest.

It is useful to distinguish between customary fisheries and commercial participation. Customary fisheries supplied food and ceremonial needs, and they were organized by Indigenous law and household obligations. Commercial participation meant selling fish to canneries and other buyers, usually at prices set by the buyer, in a market linked to global demand for canned salmon. Many families did both, taking fish for household use during the same seasons in which they sold fish for cash. The two were interconnected: income from sales financed boats and nets used for subsistence fishing, and subsistence harvests allowed families to take wage-paying work without risking their food supply.

Regulation increasingly shaped access. Canadian fisheries law, developed in the late nineteenth century and administered by the federal government, set licensing rules, gear restrictions, closed seasons, and limits on commercial participation. Indigenous people were generally permitted to fish for food, but the rules governing commercial licenses, and later limits on the number of licenses and the type of gear, altered Indigenous opportunities. Historians of the coastal fisheries, such as Dianne Newell, have documented how licensing and enforcement increasingly favored large operators and, as the Japanese Canadian fishing community grew, became entangled with racial competition. In the United States, treaty fishing rights in Puget Sound and the Columbia Basin were recognized in agreements of the 1850s, as Chapter 72 discussed, yet state regulation, private fish traps, and non-Indigenous commercial fisheries made enforcement of those rights a source of conflict that culminated in the controversies treated in Chapters 106 and 113. In Alaska, the commercial salmon industry grew rapidly from the late 1870s, and Alaska Native fishers, processors, and communities participated in and were affected by it. Regulatory and market pressures thus worked to narrow the fishing opportunities of Indigenous nations whose economic and cultural lives were built around the water.

Section 91.10

Salmon Canneries and Indigenous Industrial Labor

The Pacific salmon-canning industry grew quickly after the first commercial canneries opened on the Columbia River in the 1860s. Canning began on the Fraser River in the 1870s and spread north to the Skeena, Nass, and Rivers Inlet and other coastal sites during the following decade, while canneries opened in southeastern Alaska from 1878 and spread across the territory. Canned salmon was shipped to markets in Britain, eastern North America, and elsewhere, and in good years the industry was among the principal commercial enterprises of the Pacific coast. It required large seasonal labor forces concentrated in the weeks when salmon runs peaked.

Indigenous people were integral to that labor supply. Historians of British Columbia labor, including Rolf Knight in his survey of Indigenous work in the province, have documented that First Nations families formed a central part of the canneries' workforce, both in the fishing fleet and in the processing plants. Men fished from canoes and later company or personally owned boats, delivering fish to the cannery. Women, and often children and elders, worked inside, cleaning, cutting, and filling cans, tasks that were repetitive, fast-paced, and often paid by piece. Families traveled each summer from villages along the coast, sometimes over long distances by canoe and later by steamer, and lived in housing provided or tolerated by the company near the cannery, often segregated by ethnic group. Cannery settlements could become temporary meeting places for people from many nations, and they sustained social, family, and ceremonial connections as well as work.

The employment relationship took specific forms. Companies provided boats and nets to some fishers, bought fish at prices they set, and deducted charges for equipment or supplies from payments. Company stores sold goods to workers, sometimes on credit, and workers' pay could be reduced or delayed by accounts settled at the end of the season. Housing was typically basic and controlled by the employer. Work hours were long during runs and uncertain between them, and pay depended on the size of the catch and on piece rates. Despite these conditions, many Indigenous families chose cannery work because it provided cash at a time of year when it could be combined with subsistence harvesting and village life, and because refusal meant losing a major source of income.

The workforce was not Indigenous alone. Chinese workers formed an important part of the processing labor in many British Columbia canneries, often hired through contractors, and were typically excluded from fishing licenses and from many other opportunities. Japanese immigrant fishers became numerous on the fishing side from the 1890s, and European settler fishers and cannery managers occupied the most senior positions. These groups worked alongside one another under a hierarchy that assigned roles and wages differently, and their experiences should not be treated as identical. Indigenous workers possessed a prior connection to the fisheries and often a stronger bargaining position in some districts because of their local knowledge, while immigrant workers faced legal exclusions and, in the case of Chinese workers, particular forms of discrimination. Relations among these groups included cooperation, competition, and conflict, shaped by employers' management practices and by racial attitudes of the era. Mechanization and changes in the structure of the industry in the early twentieth century altered the composition of the workforce, reducing some Indigenous roles in processing while opening others.

The cannery case is one of the chapter's strongest documented examples of Indigenous industrial labor because it combines extensive archival records, photographic evidence, regional histories, and community memory. It also demonstrates the limits of equating wage participation with prosperity. Indigenous families earned money and sustained their communities by working in the industry, and their labor produced substantial profits for owners. Yet they were paid under terms set by employers, lacked access to capital to purchase their own plants, and saw regulation increasingly constrain their independent fishing. The canneries were both a source of livelihood and a setting of dependence.

The Indigenous Cannery Labor System

Long-term causes

Indigenous knowledge and management of salmon runs, seasonal fishing economies, and established trade created a skilled labor base located where the fish were.

Short-term causes

The expansion of global demand for canned salmon, new canning technology, and investment in coastal plants created large seasonal labor demands in the late nineteenth century.

Immediate context

Companies recruited locally available workers, supplied housing and equipment, and set pay by piece or price, while regulations shaped who could fish commercially.

Effects

Indigenous families gained cash and maintained seasonal ties to fishing places, while companies obtained labor and fish on terms they largely controlled. Over time regulation and mechanization reduced Indigenous opportunities in some sectors.

Section 91.11

Great Lakes Fisheries, Inland Waters, and Treaty-Based Economies

The Great Lakes supported some of North America's oldest and richest inland fisheries. Anishinaabe peoples, including Ojibwe, Odawa, and Potawatomi communities, fished whitefish, lake trout, sturgeon, and other species using spears, dip nets, gill nets, and weirs, and fishing was central to settlement patterns, ceremony, and seasonal movement, as Chapter 27 described. In the nineteenth century these communities sold fish to the fur-trading companies and later to commercial dealers, adapting their fishing to market demand while continuing to supply their own food needs. Commercial sale was therefore not new in the late nineteenth century, but its scale and the conditions under which it occurred were changing.

Treaties established an important foundation for Indigenous fishing. Agreements in the 1830s and 1850s, including treaties negotiated with Anishinaabe nations in the upper Great Lakes, recognized continuing rights to hunt and fish in ceded territories, and Indigenous negotiators regarded access to the fisheries as vital. How these rights were interpreted and enforced varied, and the detailed litigation and modern recognition of treaty fishing rights belong to Chapters 106 and 113. For this chapter's period the significance is that Indigenous communities understood the fisheries as a legal and economic inheritance, even as state regulations, conservation rules, and competition increasingly limited the practical exercise of those rights.

Technological and structural changes in the late nineteenth century shifted advantage toward larger operations. Commercial fishing on the Great Lakes expanded rapidly, driven by growing urban markets, railroads, and refrigeration and shipping technologies. Pound nets, large gill-net operations, steam tugs, and company-owned fleets allowed fishing enterprises to harvest far greater quantities than small boats using traditional or small-scale gear. Fishers with capital could buy these boats and nets, hire crews, and ship fish to distant markets, while Indigenous fishers who relied on smaller boats, limited cash, and local sales found it harder to compete. Historical collections in Michigan, including the Clarke Historical Library at Central Michigan University, document both Indigenous commercial fishing and the growing disadvantages small-scale fishers faced by the late nineteenth century.

Access to capital was an especially important constraint. Boats, nets, and equipment were expensive, and reservation trust status and limited credit made it difficult for Indigenous fishers to borrow. Some obtained gear through fish dealers, which could create debt obligations tying them to particular buyers. State licensing and gear regulations, enacted in part as conservation measures in response to overfishing, sometimes applied to Indigenous fishers in ways that curtailed their traditional methods and sales. Overfishing and ecological change in the lakes also reduced catches, a consequence of industrial-scale harvest, not of Indigenous practice.

Even so, many Great Lakes Indigenous families continued to fish. They combined fishing with other livelihoods, including logging, farming, work in towns and on boats, sugar-making, wild rice harvests, and the sale of crafts, a clear instance of the mixed economy discussed in §91.4. Fishing remained an important food source and a basis for community identity and obligation, and it continued to supply local markets. The treaty-based claim to the fisheries, kept alive through memory and community knowledge, would become a foundation for the legal and political campaigns of later decades.

Section 91.12

Forest Industries, Trapping, Hunting, and Northern Resource Economies

Forests and northern lands supported a different set of economies. In the Great Lakes region and the Pacific forests, logging and sawmilling expanded rapidly after the Civil War era and into the early twentieth century, creating demand for labor in camps and mills. Indigenous men worked as loggers, river drivers, mill hands, and laborers, often seasonally, and some Indigenous communities owned or managed their own timber resources. The Menominee Nation of Wisconsin, with a large reservation forest, developed a tribal logging and milling enterprise in the early twentieth century and became known for managing its timber over the long term. Others sold timber or stumpage under agency supervision, and the handling of timber revenue and reservation forests was a continuing source of dispute between communities and the government.

Trapping and the fur trade remained central across the northern interior of Canada. As Chapters 46 and 52 showed, the fur trade had long organized much of the northern economy, and Cree, Dene, Innu, Anishinaabe, Métis, and other peoples continued to trap furs, hunt, and fish while exchanging furs for goods with the Hudson's Bay Company and independent traders. The treaties negotiated for northern Canada in this period, including Treaty 8 in 1899 and Treaty 11 in 1921, were closely tied to resource development and the protection of hunting and trapping economies. Trappers combined fur harvesting with fishing, hunting for food, seasonal wage work, and, increasingly, guiding and packing for sport hunters, prospectors, and surveyors. Their detailed knowledge of land and animals was a valuable commercial resource.

This work generated income but also exposed communities to market risk and to new regulation. Fur prices fluctuated with fashion and global markets, and trappers depended on traders' credit and on prices they did not set. Government game laws and licensing regimes, intended to conserve animals and regulate access, sometimes restricted Indigenous hunting and trapping, and competition from non-Indigenous trappers and the arrival of settlers and resource companies increased pressure on lands and animals. Resource depletion, whether from overtrapping, habitat change, or industrial activity, undermined some northern economies. New transportation routes, including railways, steamers, and eventually roads and aircraft, opened the north to prospectors, loggers, and traders and changed the terms of Indigenous employment and trade.

In Alaska, Alaska Native communities participated in commercial fishing, cannery work, trapping, mining labor, and, in some regions, the sale of furs, ivory, and crafts, while subsistence hunting and fishing continued. The fur-seal harvest on the Pribilof Islands was carried out largely by Unangan labor under the control of commercial lessees and government agents, an arrangement that exposed workers to tight control and limited compensation. In the Arctic, Iñupiat communities participated in commercial whaling as crew, guides, and suppliers at shore stations, and Inuit and Inuvialuit trapping, particularly of white fox in the early twentieth century, tied northern households to fur markets. Detailed twentieth-century histories of Alaska Native and Inuit administration belong to Chapters 99 and 100; the aim here is to show that northern resource economies combined commercial work with established environmental practice, and that this combination was an Indigenous strategy as well as an imposition.

Across these regions, the common theme was that Indigenous people combined commercial work with established environmental knowledge. Guiding, trapping, and logging all drew on skills of travel, observation, and resource management developed over generations. At the same time, the commercial economy that valued these skills also placed them under outside control, through licensing, credit, and the policies of companies and governments. The pattern anticipates the contemporary struggles over fisheries stewardship and resource sovereignty examined in Chapters 135 and 136.

Section 91.13

Railroad Construction, Mining, and Industrial Employment

Industrial expansion between the 1870s and the 1920s generated large demands for manual labor, and Indigenous workers filled a significant share of them. Railroad construction and maintenance, mining, smelting, logging, and building trades employed Indigenous men, in many cases on a seasonal or temporary basis and often alongside Chinese, Mexican, European immigrant, and African American workers. The evidence for this work is uneven, since payrolls, company records, and government reports seldom identified workers by Indigenous nation and often grouped them with others, but enough documentation survives to show that Indigenous labor was widespread and important.

Recruitment took varied forms. Some workers sought employment on their own, traveling to railroad camps, mines, or towns. Others were recruited through labor contractors, agents, or kin networks, and communities sometimes negotiated arrangements with companies. In the United States Southwest, railroads employed Indigenous workers from several nations, and arrangements could be formalized: the Laguna Pueblo, for instance, developed a long relationship with the Atlantic and Pacific and later Santa Fe Railway, and Laguna men worked on track crews and in shops, with some families later living in railway work colonies. Diné and Apache men also worked on track gangs and in construction. In Canada, Indigenous people worked as guides, packers, boatmen, and laborers on railway surveys and construction, though in the western mountain sections much of the heavy construction labor was performed by Chinese workers. The Kahnawà:ke Mohawk, from a community near Montreal, entered bridge building in the 1880s when a railway bridge was constructed near their community, and Mohawk men became widely recognized for work on high steel. The Québec Bridge collapse of 29 August 1907 killed 76 workers, including 33 Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawà:ke, according to a Government of Canada history. This documented loss demonstrates both the specialized skills Indigenous workers brought to major industrial projects and the risks they faced in workplaces shaped by others' engineering decisions.

Wages and working conditions reflected the racial hierarchies of the period. In many workplaces, employers assigned jobs and pay by perceived race or ethnicity, so that Indigenous workers were frequently paid less than white workers for comparable tasks or confined to lower-status positions, a pattern historians call occupational segregation. Work was hazardous: railroad construction, mining, and high-steel work caused frequent injuries and deaths, and compensation for injury was limited or absent. Seasonal employment brought insecurity, since jobs ended with the construction season or with a downturn in demand. Company housing, camp stores, and deductions for food and supplies could reduce take-home pay.

A documented case from northern Mexico illustrates both the scale of Indigenous railroad employment and its inequality. Historian Miguel Tinker Salas reports that in 1883 the Sonoran Railroad listed 1,949 employees: 810 Yaquis, 664 Mexicans, and 475 Americans. His account identifies a three-tier wage hierarchy and notes that the listed Yaqui jobs were track repair and construction labor, rather than the privileged professional and supervisory occupations. The Yaqui had a longer history of wage work on haciendas, in mines, and on public works while maintaining communities in the Yaqui River valley. These numbers are taken from Tinker Salas, In the Shadow of the Eagles (1997), in the section “Construction of the Sonoran Railroad” and “Racial Stratification”; the original company's employment record was not separately examined for this chapter. The case demonstrates reliance on Indigenous workers and discriminatory wage and occupational structures without pretending that an employer tally reveals those workers' motives or household incomes.

Historians distinguish voluntary labor from coerced recruitment. Many Indigenous workers chose wage work, often to obtain cash, and returned home when they chose. Others were pressured by debt, by loss of land or resources, or by government or employer coercion. The distinction is not always clear in the record, since economic pressure can shape apparently voluntary decisions. In Mexico, the contrast is particularly stark: while many Yaqui and Mayo workers sought wages voluntarily, the Mexican state's campaigns against Yaqui resistance in the early twentieth century included the deportation of Yaqui people to labor in other regions of Mexico, an episode of forced removal and coerced labor, not voluntary migration. §91.18 returns to this contrast.

Definition. Work performed for pay, in money or sometimes in goods or credit, for an employer who owns the enterprise or the means of production.

Section 91.14

Seasonal Agricultural Labor, Forestry Work, and Domestic Employment

Seasonal agricultural and forestry work was among the most common forms of Indigenous wage labor. Large commercial farms in the Pacific Northwest, California, the Great Lakes, and the Plains hired temporary workers for harvests, and many Indigenous families traveled to take them. In the Puget Sound and Yakima valleys, hop harvests drew thousands of Indigenous workers, including families from Washington's reservations and from British Columbia, who traveled by canoe, steamer, wagon, and train to the hop yards. Berry, fruit, and vegetable harvests, potato digging, sugar-beet work, and cranberry harvests in the upper Midwest offered similar opportunities. Ranch work, including haying, roundup, and general labor, employed Indigenous men on the Plains, in the Southwest, and in the intermountain West, and Indigenous workers also joined logging crews, river drives, and tree-planting and clearing work.

These jobs were organized through several kinds of recruitment. Farmers and contractors sent word to reservations and reserves, family and community networks passed information about work, and agents or missionaries sometimes arranged employment. Workers often traveled in family groups, setting up temporary camps near the fields. Wages were typically paid by piece, such as by the pound or bushel, and living arrangements were basic and often supplied by employers. Insecurity was substantial: crops could fail, harvests could end early, and employers could delay or reduce payment. Travel costs and the loss of time at home also affected the net benefit of the work.

Seasonal migration could support home economies. Families used the cash earned during the late summer and fall to buy winter supplies, repay credit, purchase equipment for farms and herds, and support extended family. Time in the harvest camps also created social occasions: families from different communities met, exchanged news, arranged marriages, and held social and ceremonial events. At the same time, employment away from home meant separation from fields, flocks, and school, and could cost households the work of family members at home at critical times. Children traveled with their parents and often worked in the harvest, which created tensions with government and missionary expectations about schooling, discussed in §91.19.

Domestic employment and service work formed another significant sector, especially for women and girls. Indigenous women worked as laundresses, cooks, housekeepers, and domestic helpers in nearby towns, on ranches, and in the homes of settler families. Government and mission programs placed Indigenous schoolgirls in domestic service, for example through the "outing" arrangements associated with boarding schools examined in Chapter 87, which combined training with unpaid or low-paid labor and whose coercive features are considered in that chapter and in Chapter 88. Voluntary employment as a laundress or domestic servant, by contrast, could supply cash for a household when other opportunities were scarce. Distinguishing among these situations requires attention to who arranged the placement, who received the pay, and whether the worker or her family had a real choice.

Women's labor extended beyond paid employment. The same women who earned wages in canneries or fields also produced food, clothing, textiles, and crafts, cared for children and elders, and managed household resources. This unpaid household production was economically significant and is often missing from payroll records and official statistics, which tend to count only paid work performed outside the home. A historian who relies solely on wage records will thus underestimate women's economic contribution. The Everyday Life feature below provides a documented example.

In 1958 she narrated her life story to anthropologist Nancy Oestreich Lurie; it was edited and published as Mountain Wolf Woman, Sister of Crashing Thunder: The Autobiography of a Winnebago Indian (1961). It is one of the relatively few extended first-person accounts by an Indigenous woman of this period and has been widely used by historians and anthropologists. This feature relies on an edited, retrospective first-person autobiography; consult the named chapter “Livelihood” (pp. 8–17) and Lurie's editorial commentary (pp. 92–108) before attributing particular words or events to the narrator.

What the account shows. Her narrative describes a life in which seasonal movement, household work, and subsistence were interwoven. As described in the account and by scholars who have used it, families moved through the year to gather and harvest, including berry-picking and the cranberry harvest in Wisconsin, and to do other seasonal work, while maintaining their ceremonial and kin ties and returning to home communities. Women's work, including cooking, preparing food, child care, and household management, is central to the narrative, and the economic and social worlds of work and ceremony are presented as one life.

Why it matters. The account illustrates the mixed economy described in §91.4 at the scale of a single family. It shows that wage and harvest work was fitted into a broader round of activities, and that economic decisions were made within kin networks. It also shows what official records omit: the experience of individuals and the importance of unpaid and informal work.

Cautions. A single life cannot stand for all Ho-Chunk or all Indigenous women. The account was recorded decades after the events it describes, in a particular setting and with an editor, and it reflects memory and the purposes of the narrator and recorder. It should be read alongside archival and community sources.

Section 91.15

Basketry, Pottery, Beadwork, Textiles, and Indigenous Craft Markets

The commercial production and sale of Indigenous material culture grew substantially between the 1880s and the 1920s. Tourists traveling by rail, collectors for private and museum collections, trading posts, curio dealers, world's fairs and expositions, and mail-order and retail businesses created a national market for baskets, pottery, textiles, beadwork, carvings, and other goods. Indigenous artisans responded as skilled producers who understood the demand and adjusted their work, not as passive suppliers.

The regional examples are varied. In the Southwest, Pueblo potters sold pottery to tourists and collectors, and individual artists became known. Nampeyo, a Hopi-Tewa potter from the village of Hano on First Mesa, drew inspiration from ancient Sikyátki pottery and produced work sold to traders and visitors from the 1890s. Maria Martinez and Julian Martinez of San Ildefonso Pueblo developed black-on-black pottery in the 1910s and 1920s that became widely sought. Diné weavers sold rugs and textiles, as §91.7 showed. In California and the Great Basin, women of many nations wove baskets for sale. Pomo basketry, known for fine coiling and feather decoration, was collected widely. The Washoe weaver Dat So La Lee, working with a Nevada merchant who marketed her baskets, became one of the best-known basket makers of her time. In the Plateau and Plains, beadwork on clothing, bags, and other items was made for community use and for sale. Northwest Coast carvers produced argillite carvings, totem-pole models, boxes, and other goods for visitors and collectors, and Haida carvers in particular worked in argillite for outside buyers. Alaska Native communities sold carved ivory, baskets, and other goods. Great Lakes and northeastern communities sold birchbark and splint baskets, sweetgrass work, and beadwork to tourists and resort visitors.

Commercial pressures influenced production. Buyers wanted objects they perceived as "authentic," which often meant objects that conformed to their expectations of Indigenous tradition, and dealers sometimes promoted this idea. Artisans adjusted size, form, and decoration to fit markets: smaller and more portable pieces for tourists, larger decorative pieces for collectors, new shapes such as trays and vases, and designs that were popular with buyers. They sometimes adopted new materials, such as commercial yarns, dyes, and glass beads, and they also drew on revived or preserved older styles. Prices varied widely, and intermediaries often captured a large share of the final selling price. Competition from factory-made imitations and the effects of exposition and curio-trade fashion added instability. Some artisans, such as Nampeyo and Maria Martinez, gained recognition and relatively favorable returns, while many others remained anonymous and poorly paid.

Interpretation requires care. It would be inaccurate to portray these goods merely as tourist souvenirs. They were expressions of technical knowledge, including the selection and preparation of materials, mastery of forming and firing, weaving structures, and design systems, and they were sites of artistic innovation. They also served community functions: many of the same artisans made objects for ceremonial, household, and gift use. Craft production was household enterprise, often organized by women and passed between generations, and income from it supported families, sometimes more reliably than other sources. At the same time, the market's demands could narrow what artisans felt able to make profitably and reinforced stereotypes of Indigenous people as makers of "primitive" art. Historians continue to debate how much the craft market constrained or sustained Indigenous artistic traditions. The evidence supports both: it provided livelihood and encouraged innovation and continuity of skills, and it also tied artists to external taste and power.

Organized efforts to shape the market began to emerge in the 1920s, including exhibitions and fairs in the Southwest that promoted quality and, in some cases, recognition of individual artists. Federal intervention in the craft economy belongs to a later period, though the beginnings of institutional interest in marketing Indigenous arts are visible by the end of this chapter's time frame.

Section 91.16

Trading Posts, Indigenous Merchants, Credit, and Local Entrepreneurship

Trading posts, general stores, and local merchants were the interface between Indigenous households and the wider market. A trading post could be the place where a family sold wool, hides, furs, baskets, or livestock, bought flour, cloth, tools, and groceries, cashed wages or government checks, obtained credit, and received mail and news. For many communities, especially in the Southwest, the northern interior, and the Plains, the trader was the most visible link to the commercial economy and often held considerable power.

Exchange took several forms. Barter, in which goods were exchanged for goods, remained common, especially in remote areas. Cash payments became increasingly common as wages and lease income circulated. Store credit allowed families to obtain goods in advance of income from wool clips, livestock sales, or harvests, and it created continuing relationships between traders and households. In the Diné country, pawn was an important institution: jewelry and other valuables were deposited with the trader as security for credit and could be redeemed later, a system that gave households access to credit while keeping valuables within reach. Traders usually marked up goods, charged for transport, and kept the ledgers, and the transport costs of reaching remote posts affected prices. Indebtedness could tie households to particular traders and limit their ability to shop elsewhere.

Bargaining power was unequal, though not uniformly so. Where a trader had no competition, he could set buying and selling prices with little constraint. Where several posts operated within reach, competition could improve the terms available to Indigenous customers, and households could choose among traders. Government regulation of trading, including licensing of traders on reservations, attempted to limit abuses but was inconsistently enforced. Households also exercised informed judgment, comparing prices, timing sales, and using relationships. Some traders were deeply embedded in the communities they served, married into them, and spoke Indigenous languages, and their interests could align with those of their customers in some respects. Their commercial role nonetheless gave them leverage that customers lacked.

It would be a mistake to assume that every merchant and employer was non-Indigenous. Documented Indigenous commercial activity existed across the continent, though it is sometimes less visible in the records. In the Indian Territory, Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Muscogee citizens operated stores, farms, and ranches within their nations' commercial economies. On the Pacific coast, Indigenous fishers owned boats and gear, and Indigenous families took part in local trade. At Metlakatla, in Alaska, the Tsimshian community that had relocated from British Columbia in the 1880s developed its own sawmill and cannery, an example of community-owned enterprise. Métis freighters, traders, and farmers in the Canadian northwest operated within and alongside the fur-trade economy. In many communities, Indigenous individuals ran small shops, hauled freight, operated ferries, guided, and raised livestock for sale. Because these enterprises were often small and informal, the documentary record is thin, and historians have to look for them in local records, newspapers, and oral accounts. Their existence challenges the assumption that Indigenous economic life was only that of customers and employees.

A carefully documented ledger or commercial account can illuminate how these relationships worked at the household level, as Primary Source Window 2 explained. Such a record shows what a family produced, when, and for how much, what it bought, and how debts accumulated and were paid. The historian's task is to read the ledger together with other sources, including oral accounts and government reports, to understand how the numbers fit within household and community strategies. Trading posts are therefore best understood as commercial institutions in which Indigenous participants exercised economic judgment under constraints, rather than only as sites of exploitation or only as sites of mutual benefit.

Section 91.17

Indigenous Migration, Seasonal Mobility, and Emerging Urban Communities

Mobility was integral to Indigenous economic life, both before and after conquest. Seasonal rounds of hunting, fishing, and gathering had long moved families across their territories, and trade had linked distant communities. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, mobility took new forms as workers traveled to canneries, hop yards, railroad camps, mines, ranches, towns, and cities. Movement could represent economic opportunity, survival strategy, cultural continuity, or forced displacement, and the same journey could combine several of these meanings.

Historians distinguish among kinds of movement. Seasonal migration followed predictable cycles, such as annual travel to a cannery or harvest, with return to a home community. Circular migration involved repeated trips between home and work sites over years, often by the same people and families. Permanent relocation meant settling in a new place, whether a town, an industrial settlement, or another community. Forced displacement resulted from government removal, deportation, or coercion. Much Indigenous mobility in this period was seasonal or circular, which preserved ties to homelands and kin while using wage opportunities elsewhere. Home communities, in turn, remained the center of social and often legal identity, particularly where reservation or reserve membership governed access to land and services.

Government policy complicated movement. In Canada, the pass system and related regulations restricted travel from Prairie reserves for a period beginning in the 1880s, and agents could limit employment away from home. In the United States, agents also sought to regulate movement, and policies attempting to keep Indigenous people on reservations conflicted with the economic need to travel. Boarding schools sent children far from home, and some graduates remained in towns or cities where they found work. Yet policy was uneven and often unenforced, and Indigenous people moved widely despite it, sometimes by negotiating permission and sometimes by simply leaving.

An Indigenous urban presence existed well before the large federal relocation programs of the mid-twentieth century, which Chapter 105 examines. Indigenous people lived and worked in towns and cities across the continent in the decades around 1900. In Vancouver, Squamish and other Coast Salish people lived on reserves located within the growing city's boundaries and worked in the lumber mills, on the docks, and in other local industries, and Squamish longshoremen became an established part of the waterfront workforce. In Puget Sound communities such as Seattle and Tacoma, Indigenous people worked in industry, trade, and domestic service and maintained ties with nearby reservations. Pueblo railway families settled in work colonies in California, and Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawà:ke later formed a community in Brooklyn. Smaller towns near reservations and reserves, from the Plains to the Great Lakes, had Indigenous residents who worked in local businesses, households, and farms. In some cases cities grew around or onto Indigenous lands and communities, so that Indigenous people in the city were not migrants at all but residents displaced by urban expansion.

These facts challenge the misconception that Indigenous urban life originated only in the mid-twentieth century. Urban and town residence was part of Indigenous economic strategy long before the relocation era, sometimes as a stage in circular migration and sometimes as permanent settlement. The scale was smaller than after 1945, and the surviving records are sparse, but the pattern of Indigenous participation in urban labor markets was established. This earlier history helps explain the networks and institutions that later migrants used and the long Indigenous relationship to cities that Chapter 140 explores.

Section 91.18

Indigenous Labor across the U.S.–Mexico Borderlands

The borderlands between northern Mexico and the southwestern United States illustrate the continental scope of Indigenous economic life. The international boundary drawn after the U.S.–Mexico War and adjusted by the Gadsden Purchase of 1853, examined in Chapter 69, divided homelands and kinship networks of the Yaqui, Mayo, Tohono O'odham, Akimel O'odham, Apache, Kickapoo, and other peoples. Economic opportunity, family ties, and conflict led Indigenous people to cross the line repeatedly, and the borderland labor markets of mines, railroads, ranches, and farms drew workers from both sides.

The Yaqui and Mayo of Sonora offer the clearest documented cases. As §91.13 noted, Yaqui workers were employed on railroads, in mines, and on haciendas and farms in Sonora, and the Yaqui had a long tradition of leaving their river-valley communities for wage work and returning to them. Their situation changed with the Mexican government's pressure on Yaqui lands and autonomy under the Porfirian regime, which promoted commercial development and the sale of Yaqui valley land and which responded to Yaqui resistance with military campaigns. During the early twentieth century, many Yaqui were deported from Sonora to labor on plantations in other regions of Mexico, such as the henequen estates of Yucatán, under conditions of coercion. Estimates of the number deported vary and are uncertain, and historians differ on figures, but the policy is well documented as a form of forced removal and coerced labor. Others fled north across the border, and Yaqui refugees and migrants settled in southern Arizona communities, where they worked in mines, railroad construction, agriculture, and local trades, creating communities, such as those near Tucson, that endure today. Wage migration, refugee flight, and deportation overlapped in time and place, and the distinctions among them matter: some Yaqui traveled for work of their own choosing, some left to escape violence, and some were removed by force.

The Tohono O'odham and Akimel O'odham, whose homelands span the present boundary, worked in cotton and other agricultural harvests, on ranches, in the copper mines of southern Arizona, and on railroad and construction projects, while also pursuing their own farming and livestock raising and traveling to ceremonies and relatives across the line. Mayo and other Indigenous workers from Sonora and Sinaloa also traveled to Arizona and California for wages. Apache and other peoples whose territories had been cut by the boundary faced additional constraints after the Apache Wars, as Chapters 79 and 80 described. The movement of people across the boundary was not strongly restricted in the period, since border controls were minimal before the 1920s, and Indigenous workers often moved freely along kinship and work networks that took no account of the line.

Northern Sonora is particularly useful for examining the relationship between Indigenous livelihoods, mining expansion, and wage differentials. Foreign-capitalized copper and silver mines, including the large operations at Cananea, drew workers from across the region and from the United States and created an unequal labor structure in which American workers typically held better-paid positions than Mexican and Indigenous workers. Wage differences between mines on either side of the border encouraged workers to cross for higher pay. Indigenous workers, along with mestizo Mexican workers, were part of this movement, and employment in mines, railroads, and ranches provided cash for families whose farmlands were under pressure.

This section keeps the continental scope of the textbook without becoming a separate history of the Mexican Revolution. The upheavals of the 1910s, which displaced many people and increased movement north, are treated here only as part of the context of migration. The key analytical distinction is between wage migration, in which workers moved in search of better income, and forced removal, deportation, and coerced labor, in which they were compelled by state or employer power. Indigenous borderlands history includes both, and treating them as a single phenomenon would erase the violence that shaped some movements. Chapter 141 returns to Indigenous peoples of the northern borderlands in the contemporary period.

Section 91.19

Women, Children, Families, and the Distribution of Economic Labor

Across the regions examined in this chapter, the family was the basic unit of production and economic survival. Decisions about who would herd, farm, fish, weave, take wage work, or attend school were made within households and extended families, and the distribution of labor varied by gender, age, and circumstance. Understanding the family economy requires attention to roles that formal records often leave out.

Women's labor was central in nearly every system discussed. Diné women owned and managed livestock, sheared and processed wool, and wove textiles. Pueblo and other Southwestern women made pottery, prepared food, and often held rights in houses and fields. Pacific coast women processed fish in both household and cannery settings, and Great Lakes women harvested wild rice, made maple sugar, dried and prepared fish, and produced baskets and beadwork for sale. Women worked in hop yards, harvest camps, laundries, and domestic service, and they raised children and cared for elders while doing so. In many Indigenous societies, women also exercised substantial authority over household resources and in community decision-making, though colonial administrators often dealt primarily with men and favored male household headship in programs such as allotment, which affected women's property and authority in some communities. The economic roles of women should therefore be understood as both extensive and shaped by colonial policy.

Children contributed to family economies in ways appropriate to their age and community: herding sheep, carrying water, gathering plants, picking berries and hops, working in canneries, caring for younger siblings, and helping with household tasks. Historians distinguish family work, which was typically part of learning and belonging within a household and community, from exploitative employment, in which children were used as cheap labor under control of outsiders. The distinction is not always sharp. Cannery and harvest work often combined family participation with piece-rate or employer-controlled conditions, and children's labor in these settings could contribute substantially to income while also interfering with schooling. Institutional coercion further complicated the picture: boarding and residential schools, examined in Chapters 87 and 88, required students to perform labor in kitchens, laundries, farms, and workshops as part of their training, and the "outing" system placed students in unpaid or low-paid work for outside employers. These arrangements were frequently economically exploitative, and they removed children from the family economies in which they would otherwise have worked and learned.

Families made decisions about schooling in light of these economic circumstances. Some saw schools as sources of useful skills, others resisted removal of children's labor and cultural instruction, and many weighed the need for children's work, the risk of illness or abuse in distant schools, and the value of literacy. Seasonal migration and harvest work could conflict with school calendars, and government efforts to enforce attendance sometimes clashed with family subsistence needs. Chapter 88 discusses family separation and survival in detail; the point here is that schooling decisions were also economic decisions, made within constraints.

Community institutions supported these family economies. Mutual aid, sharing of food and equipment, cooperative labor for fishing, harvesting, and building, ceremonial exchange, and the redistribution of resources through gift-giving and feasting helped households through hard times and maintained social ties. Intergenerational knowledge, from elders' knowledge of environments and techniques to older women's textile and craft expertise, was an economic resource as much as a cultural one. In this sense, the family and community were not obstacles to economic participation but the structures that made participation possible and bearable.

Section 91.20

Indigenous Economic Life by 1930: Adaptation, Inequality, and Continuity

By about 1930, Indigenous economic life across North America combined wage employment, subsistence production, livestock raising, farming, fisheries, craft markets, resource industries, and Indigenous enterprise in a variety of mixtures. No single pattern characterized the continent. A Diné household might rely on sheep, weaving, and wage work; a Lakota family on small herds, lease income, rations or agency employment, and ranch labor; a Coast Salish family on fishing, cannery and hop work, and gardening; a Pueblo household on irrigated fields, pottery sales, and railroad wages; a Yaqui family in Arizona on mine, railroad, and farm work and on community ties across the border. The persistence of each combination reflected community knowledge, access to resources, and the strategies of households and leaders.

Differences among communities were substantial. Access to land, water, natural resources, infrastructure, capital, and commercial markets shaped outcomes. Communities with productive land and water, such as some farming and fishing nations with intact resource bases, could sustain relatively stronger economies than those whose land had been lost through allotment and sale or whose water had been diverted. Those near railroads, towns, and industries found wage work more easily than isolated ones, though proximity also meant more competition for resources. Nations with valuable resources under their land, such as timber or, in a few cases, oil and minerals, had sources of income that others lacked, though management by the government and outside lessees often limited the benefit to the community and individuals. Within communities, inequalities of gender, age, and household resources also mattered.

Economic participation did not necessarily produce equality or security. The Meriam Report of 1928, a survey of conditions in the United States, documented widespread poverty, poor health, inadequate diets, low incomes, and administrative failures across many reservations, and it criticized the allotment system and the management of Indigenous property. Though its authors shared some assumptions of their era, its findings confirmed that the wage and market participation described in this chapter coexisted with deep deprivation. In Canada, Indigenous communities faced comparable conditions under administrative controls that limited their commercial development. Indigenous workers were typically paid less, held less secure jobs, and had little legal recourse. Indigenous producers sold into markets that gave them little pricing power and faced competition from better-capitalized settlers. Indigenous economic persistence is better understood as adaptation under unequal conditions than as success or failure.

Indigenous economic persistence also cannot be reduced to a narrative of inevitable assimilation. The households and communities examined here adopted new activities without abandoning their own values and institutions. Livestock, wage income, and craft sales financed ceremonies and supported kin; the seasonal return to home communities maintained languages and social ties; and Indigenous knowledge of land, water, and animals was an economic asset in commercial fisheries, ranching, farming, guiding, and trapping. Economic integration thus occurred alongside the continuity of Indigenous identities, kinship systems, and political communities. Assimilation policy sought to link wage work and individual property to cultural change, but Indigenous families used economic participation to sustain rather than abandon their communities.

The economic experiences of this period shaped the political and intellectual initiatives examined in the following chapters. Indigenous workers, farmers, fishers, and craft producers developed grievances over land, water, fishing rights, wages, and administrative control that became the substance of political organization. The economic networks and community institutions that sustained families also supported organizing, as people met in canneries, harvest camps, and towns and carried information and ideas across regions. Chapter 92 examines the writers, scholars, and political leaders who emerged in this setting; Chapter 93 considers citizenship and belonging; Chapter 94 examines the First World War; Chapter 95 follows the reform movements that criticized the existing system; and Chapter 96 assesses Indigenous North America on the eve of the New Deal. The economic life described here was the ground on which these developments rested.

Historical Vocabulary
Economic Sovereignty

Definition. The capacity of an Indigenous nation or community to make its own decisions about land, resources, production, labor, trade, and the distribution of income.

Historical context. The term is a modern analytical concept, and communities of 1880–1930 did not necessarily use it. It is useful for describing what colonial administration restricted, such as control over resource sales, leasing, and trade, and what Indigenous communities sought to maintain through community-controlled production, kin-based exchange, and later political action.

Why it matters. The concept helps distinguish between participation in markets and control over the terms of participation. A community may earn income without possessing economic sovereignty, and a community with limited income may retain substantial control over its resources.

Potential misunderstanding. Economic sovereignty does not mean isolation from markets. Indigenous nations traded widely before colonization and continued to do so. The question is who sets the terms.

Historical Debate

Did Entry into Expanding Commercial Markets Create Greater Indigenous Economic Independence, or Deepen Colonial Dependency?

The question: Did Indigenous participation in wool, fish, crafts, wage labor, and other markets between about 1880 and 1930 expand Indigenous economic autonomy and options, or did it tie communities to outside employers, traders, and prices in ways that increased dependency? Historians have approached the question in different ways, and the answer is not necessarily the same for every nation or region.

Interpretation A — Markets created opportunity and agency: Some historians emphasize that Indigenous producers and workers actively entered markets, used wages and sales to support households and communities, innovated in crafts and ranching, and maintained decision-making within kin networks. From this viewpoint, market participation was a form of adaptation and resourcefulness that allowed communities to remain on and near their lands.
Interpretation B — Markets deepened dependency: Other historians emphasize that Indigenous people entered markets on terms set by traders, employers, and governments, with limited capital, restricted rights, and unequal pay, and that market incorporation made communities vulnerable to price swings and to the loss of land and resources. The classic argument for dependency in this era drew on studies of how trade and administrative control narrowed Indigenous choices.
Interpretation C — Outcomes depended on conditions: Many historians avoid a single answer and instead identify conditions that shaped outcomes: control of productive resources, competition among buyers, legal rights, capital access, and the strength of community institutions. Where these favored Indigenous producers, market participation could support greater independence; where they did not, dependency could deepen.

Evidence: The debate draws on trading-post and cannery records, government reports, wage evidence, and community accounts. Evidence of Indigenous initiative includes Diné weavers' design decisions, Plains cattle herds, and Pacific coast fishing families' strategies. Evidence of constraint includes the Canadian peasant-farming policy, restrictions on produce sales, upstream water diversion, regulation of fisheries, and wage inequality.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis rather than whether either set of facts occurred. Historians differ about how to weigh Indigenous choices against structural constraints, whether dependency is the right concept for communities that retained substantial subsistence production, and what counts as independence.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects whether Indigenous economic history is told as a story of agency, of subordination, or of agency exercised within subordination, and it shapes how later policy and activism are understood.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Economic Life, c. 1850 to c. 1930

What Changed

  • Access to land, water, and resources narrowed through reservations, reserves, allotment, settlement, and regulation.
  • Buffalo and other large-game economies gave way, in the Plains and elsewhere, to ranching, farming, rations, leasing, and wage work.
  • Cash and wages became necessary parts of household income in most regions.
  • Railroads, canneries, mines, and towns created new employment and markets and new forms of dependence.

What Stayed the Same

  • Households continued to combine several livelihoods and to use kin networks for resource distribution.
  • Indigenous environmental knowledge continued to underlie farming, herding, fishing, and trapping.
  • Women's labor remained central to production, even where records overlook it.
  • Seasonal movement and links to home communities persisted.

When and how: Change was uneven and, for most communities, occurred as a series of pressures between the 1860s and the 1920s rather than a single break. Those who lost land or water experienced rapid change; others, such as some fishing and pastoral communities, experienced more continuity until later regulation. Men in wage labor experienced different changes from women in household and craft production. Why the pattern matters: It shows that Indigenous economic history is neither a story of unchanged tradition nor one of wholesale replacement, but of continuing adaptation under pressure.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that conquest transformed Indigenous economic opportunities and institutions but did not eliminate Indigenous economic agency. Between about 1880 and 1930, Indigenous nations and families sustained, adapted, and rebuilt their economic lives amid land dispossession, colonial administration, industrialization, commercial expansion, and changing labor markets. They did so by combining older systems of production and exchange with livestock raising, farming, wage employment, commercial fishing, craft sales, resource work, and migration, and they brought to each of these activities knowledge, skills, and institutions that predated the colonial economy.

Reservations, reserves, and allotment reorganized access to land, water, timber, grazing, and markets. In the United States, allotment and leasing moved much productive land into non-Indigenous hands and replaced production with lease income for many households, while trust status and heirship limited the use of land as capital. In Canada, administrative regulation of produce sales, movement, and equipment, culminating in the peasant-farming policy of 1889–1897, restricted the commercial development of reserve agriculture. Both systems subjected Indigenous economic decisions to supervision that settler producers did not face. The documented effects, which varied by region and over time, were reduced investment, limited credit, and narrower opportunities.

Railroads and commercial markets reorganized regional economies. They made wool, fish, crops, crafts, and labor marketable at a distance and created jobs, but also brought competition, resource extraction, and settlement. The Diné case showed how a nation with a secured homeland rebuilt flocks, developed a wool and textile economy tied to trading posts, and sustained household production through women's labor, while traders and retailers controlled much of the market. The same pattern of opportunity and constraint appeared across regions, and the significance of the railroad depended on the institutions and laws through which it linked communities to markets.

Households responded to these conditions through diversification. Mixed economies combined subsistence production, commercial sales, wage work, and kin-based obligations, spreading risk, matching labor to seasons, and sustaining kin networks. This strategy, rather than a simple transition from traditional to modern life, is the organizing concept of the chapter. It explains why Indigenous families appear simultaneously in wage records, trading-post ledgers, and accounts of subsistence, and why wage work often supported rather than replaced home production.

In agriculture and ranching, Indigenous peoples drew on both older practices and new opportunities. Prairie reserve farmers began successfully and were then constrained by regulation. Plains nations rebuilt livestock economies after the buffalo economy collapsed, through household herds, community initiatives, and ranch labor, while leasing, fencing, drought, and disease created pressure. Southwestern irrigation, floodwater, and dryland farming demonstrated environmental specialization developed over centuries, and the loss of water to settler diversions, as among the Akimel O'odham, showed how outside actions rather than Indigenous failure undermined agricultural success.

Fisheries and forests were major sources of both livelihood and conflict. Pacific coast peoples contributed their knowledge and labor to the canneries and commercial fisheries, working as fishers and processors in family groups, in a system that provided cash and sustained seasonal ties but that placed workers under employer control and was increasingly shaped by regulation that narrowed Indigenous commercial access. Great Lakes Anishinaabe communities continued to fish under treaty claims while facing mechanized competition and limited capital. Forest, trapping, guiding, and northern resource economies combined commercial work with established environmental knowledge and exposed communities to price cycles, licensing, and resource depletion.

Wage labor and craft production extended Indigenous participation in regional industries. Indigenous workers built railroads, worked in mines and on high steel, harvested crops, labored in forests, and served in households, usually within racial hierarchies that gave them lower pay and less secure positions, as the Sonora Yaqui case illustrated. Craft producers, including potters, weavers, basket makers, beadworkers, and carvers, supplied national markets as skilled artisans and household entrepreneurs, adapting to buyers' demands while maintaining technical knowledge and artistic traditions. Trading posts and merchants structured exchange through barter, credit, and pawn, and documented Indigenous enterprises show that Indigenous people were also merchants, boat owners, and employers.

Migration, family organization, and borderlands labor completed the picture. Seasonal and circular mobility connected home communities to canneries, harvests, mines, and towns, and an Indigenous urban presence existed well before the relocation programs of the mid-twentieth century. In the borderlands, Yaqui, O'odham, and other peoples crossed the international boundary for work and kinship, and the contrast between wage migration and forced deportation showed why distinctions among kinds of movement matter. Women's labor, including both paid work and unpaid household production, and children's contributions were central to family economies, and the line between family work and exploitation was shaped by employers and by institutions such as boarding schools.

By 1930, Indigenous economic life was characterized by adaptation, inequality, and continuity. Communities differed greatly in access to land, water, resources, capital, and markets, and economic participation did not produce equality or security, as the Meriam Report documented. The historiographical debate over whether markets created independence or dependency does not admit one answer across all nations, and the evidence supports both Indigenous initiative and structural constraint. Economic integration must not be confused with economic equality, or commercial adaptation with cultural assimilation: Indigenous families used wages, livestock, and sales to sustain their communities, kin networks, and ceremonial and political life.

These patterns prepare the ground for the chapters that follow. The writers, scholars, and political leaders of Chapter 92, the debates over citizenship in Chapter 93, the wartime mobilization of Chapter 94, the reform movements of Chapter 95, and the assessment of conditions on the eve of the New Deal in Chapter 96 all emerged from communities with the economic experiences described here. The economic history of Indigenous North America in this period is the history of people who were participants in, and builders of, the continent's regional economies, even as they were subjected to systems that limited their rewards.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Mixed economy
A household or community livelihood combining subsistence production, commercial sales, wage work, and reciprocal exchange, often as a deliberate strategy to spread risk.
Subsistence production
The growing, gathering, hunting, fishing, and making of goods for direct use by a household or community.
Commercial exchange
The sale or barter of goods to outside buyers or traders for cash, credit, or other goods.
Wage labor
Work for pay in an enterprise owned by someone else, as in canneries, railroads, mines, and harvests.
Kin-based economy
A system in which obligations among relatives govern the sharing, lending, and redistribution of resources, labor, and income.
Economic sovereignty
The capacity of an Indigenous nation or community to make its own decisions about land, resources, labor, and trade; a modern analytical term for what colonial administration restricted.
Allotment
The division of communally held reservation land into individual parcels, authorized in the United States by the Dawes Act of 1887.
Lease income
Payments received by landowners, including allottees, from non-owners who farm, graze, or otherwise use their land.
Fee patent
A deed granting unrestricted title to an allotment, allowing it to be sold, mortgaged, or taxed.
Heirship land
Allotted land divided among multiple heirs, producing fractional ownership that complicated use and management.
Peasant farming
A Canadian policy of 1889–1897 that directed reserve farmers toward small plots and simple implements, limiting commercial scale; abandoned in 1897.
Pass system
An administrative practice imposed on some Canadian Prairie reserves after 1885 to control departure through written passes; unlike the produce-permit system, it had no statutory basis.
Trading post
A commercial establishment, common in the Southwest and the north, where producers sold goods and bought supplies, often on credit.
Store credit
Goods or money advanced against future income, such as a wool clip or wages, creating continuing obligations between customer and merchant.
Pawn
The practice, notably in Diné country, of leaving valuables such as jewelry with a trader as security for credit, subject to later redemption.
Customary fishery
Fishing governed by Indigenous law and household or community rights and supplying food, ceremonial, and trade needs.
Cannery
A plant that processes and cans fish, notably salmon, employing large seasonal labor forces from the late nineteenth century.
Occupational segregation
The assignment of workers to particular jobs and pay levels on the basis of race or ethnicity.
Seasonal migration
Predictable annual movement to work or harvest sites, with return to a home community.
Circular migration
Repeated movement between a home community and work sites over years, often by the same individuals and families.
Forced displacement
Movement compelled by state or employer power, such as removal or deportation, as distinct from voluntary wage migration.
Irrigation agriculture
Farming that uses canals or ditches to bring water to fields, practiced by Pueblo, Akimel O'odham, and other Southwestern peoples.
Floodwater farming
Cultivation that directs seasonal runoff from washes and arroyos onto fields.
Dryland farming
Cultivation without irrigation, depending on precipitation and careful site and crop selection, as among the Hopi.
Churro sheep
A hardy breed introduced by the Spanish and central to Diné pastoralism.
Craft market
Commercial exchange in baskets, pottery, textiles, beadwork, carvings, and similar goods sold to tourists, collectors, and dealers.
Outing system
A boarding-school arrangement placing students in outside households or businesses, often as unpaid or low-paid labor.
Dependency
A relationship in which a community's economic options are shaped by outside employers, merchants, or governments controlling prices, credit, or access to resources.
Meriam Report
A 1928 survey of conditions in United States Indian administration, documenting poverty and criticizing allotment.
Hop harvest
A late-summer seasonal harvest, notably in Puget Sound and the Yakima Valley, that drew large numbers of Indigenous family workers.
Trust land
Land held by the government for the benefit of a tribe or individual, generally restricted from sale and difficult to mortgage.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects two common pictures of Indigenous history after conquest. One imagines Indigenous peoples as having been pushed outside the modern economy, surviving on rations and the margins. The other imagines market participation as proof of assimilation or prosperity. The evidence assembled here supports neither. Indigenous peoples worked in, supplied, and helped build the regional industries of the continent, while remaining members of nations and communities whose obligations and values shaped how they worked and what they did with what they earned.

The chapter also reveals a larger process: the way that law, administration, and markets together determined who captured the value of Indigenous labor and resources. Allotment, reserve regulation, licensing, water law, and trade structure were not abstractions. They determined whether a weaver's work reached buyers at a fair price, whether a farmer could use a binder, whether a fishing family could keep its place on a river, and whether a worker could expect equal pay. The economic history of the period is therefore inseparable from its political history, and the grievances that arose from economic experience helped shape the political movements of the following decades.

Continuities extended well beyond 1930. The strategy of mixed livelihoods, the importance of women's labor and kin networks, the use of seasonal and circular migration, and the attachment of economic life to land and water all persisted, as later chapters on economic development, treaty rights, and urban communities show. So did the structural problems of limited capital, restricted control over resources, and unequal markets, which came to the fore in the crisis of the 1930s examined in Chapters 96 and 97 and in the modern efforts at economic development treated in Chapter 121.

Within the larger textbook, this chapter opens the examination of Indigenous modernity in Part XVI. It shows that Indigenous modernity did not begin when Indigenous people entered the modern economy, since they had long been economic actors, but when they sought to define on their own terms the conditions under which they would participate in it. That effort, carried on through work, enterprise, and eventually political organization, is the thread that connects this chapter to those that follow.
