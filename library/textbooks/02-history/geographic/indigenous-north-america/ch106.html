Chapter Opening

A treaty is a promise only if the government that made it can be held to it. In the decades after the Second World War, Indigenous nations on both sides of the Canada–United States border discovered how difficult that could be when the same government that had signed the treaty also licensed the fishery, built the dam, and sent out the enforcement officer.

Frank’s Landing lies along the Nisqually River in western Washington, on land held by the family of Billy Frank Jr., a Nisqually man who would become one of the best-known defenders of Indigenous fishing rights in the twentieth century. The Nisqually River flows from Mount Rainier to southern Puget Sound, and for as long as the Nisqually people have told their history, salmon and steelhead have returned to it. By Frank’s own later account, he was first arrested for fishing as a teenager in the 1940s. In the 1960s, family members and supporters set nets from the landing and fished in the face of state regulations that classified their methods as illegal, and state fisheries and game officers watched, warned, arrested, and seized equipment. The confrontation was not an accident of local temperament. It was the visible edge of a long dispute over what a nineteenth-century treaty had actually guaranteed.

The people fishing at Frank’s Landing did not describe themselves as asking for a new privilege. They understood themselves to be exercising a right that their ancestors had reserved when they signed the Treaty of Medicine Creek in December 1854. That treaty, negotiated between Governor Isaac Stevens of Washington Territory and delegations of Nisqually, Puyallup, Squaxin Island, and neighboring peoples, secured the right of taking fish at “usual and accustomed” grounds and stations. The state of Washington, in contrast, treated fishing as a regulated activity subject to its own licensing, seasons, and gear rules, and argued that conservation required it to control the harvest of salmon and steelhead in the interest of all its citizens. The two positions could not easily be reconciled, because each rested on a different account of who held authority over the river.

Frank’s Landing was one place among many in which this problem appeared. In upstate New York and northwestern Pennsylvania, the Seneca Nation of Indians fought the construction of the Kinzua Dam, which flooded a large part of the Allegany Reservation despite a 1794 treaty in which the United States had promised not to disturb the Seneca in the free use and enjoyment of their lands. On the Missouri River in North Dakota, the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara peoples of the Fort Berthold Reservation lost their most productive bottomlands to the reservoir behind the Garrison Dam. Along the lower Colorado River, the Chemehuevi, Cocopah, Colorado River, Fort Mojave, and Quechan reservations were drawn into a long contest over water in which the central question was whether a reservation carried with it a right to the water that made farming possible. In western Canada, Cree, Dene, and other First Nations whose treaties referred to hunting, trapping, and fishing found themselves facing provincial game wardens, while on Vancouver Island two Nanaimo men argued that an 1854 agreement protected their right to hunt deer.

These cases involved different peoples, different legal systems, and different resources, but they were linked by a question that this chapter treats as its central historical problem: what did a treaty guarantee mean when the government responsible for honoring it also regulated the resource, authorized the development project, or enforced the restriction that interfered with its exercise? The question is not only legal. It concerns the structure of colonial and settler states, which claimed to be bound by treaties while also asserting a broad power to manage land, water, wildlife, and economic development in what they defined as the public interest.

Historians reconstruct these struggles from a varied body of evidence: treaty texts and the journals of negotiators; Indian Office and Bureau of Indian Affairs correspondence; court records and judicial opinions; congressional hearings; tribal council records and petitions; engineering and agency reports; newspaper coverage; and oral histories and memoirs, including the recollections of participants such as Billy Frank Jr. Each type of source carries limits. Treaty texts were written in English by government negotiators and translated, if at all, through intermediaries and trade languages such as Chinook Jargon; judicial opinions explain what courts decided, not necessarily what Indigenous negotiators understood in the 1850s; and agency reports often reflect the priorities of the agency that produced them. The chapter therefore tries to distinguish what the documents establish from the interpretations later placed upon them.

This chapter occupies a specific place within the textbook’s division of historical labor. It follows Chapter 104, which examined termination, and Chapter 105, which followed Indigenous people into cities through the relocation program, and it shows that the defense of treaty rights proceeded alongside, and partly independently of, those federal policies. It looks forward to Chapter 107, on national Indigenous organizations, and Chapter 108, on the beginnings of Red Power. It does not narrate the full Pacific Northwest Fish Wars or the 1974 Boldt decision, which belong to Chapter 113, and it leaves the later evolution of modern water-rights claims to Chapter 120. Its chronological center lies between the 1940s and the late 1960s, though it reaches back to treaties of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries on which every later argument depended.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why treaty rights are understood as continuing legal and political obligations rather than as privileges granted by governments, and why a right could survive a cession of land.
  2. Distinguish among treaty rights, reserved rights, statutory benefits, and administrative permissions.
  3. Trace the legal foundations of treaty protection through the Stevens treaties, United States v. Winans (1905), Winters v. United States (1908), and Tulee v. Washington (1942).
  4. Analyze how postwar dam-building, fisheries management, highway construction, and economic development placed new pressure on treaty-protected lands and resources.
  5. Compare the authority of federal, state, provincial, and tribal governments and explain how jurisdictional overlap shaped the enforcement of treaty rights in the United States and Canada.
  6. Assess state conservation arguments and Indigenous treaty arguments in disputes over fishing, hunting, and gathering, using the historical evidence available for each.
  7. Describe the Canadian treaty context, including the numbered treaties, the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930, and the case of R. v. White and Bob.
  8. Evaluate the Seneca, Fort Berthold, and Colorado River cases as examples of conflicts over land and water that did not depend on fisheries.
  9. Explain how everyday practices, tribal governments, petitions, litigation, and direct action together sustained the defense of treaty rights.
  10. Interpret primary sources, including treaty language and judicial opinions, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitations.
  11. Distinguish between a favorable legal principle and effective enforcement, using Puyallup Tribe v. Department of Game (1968) as an example.
  12. Connect treaty-rights activism to the wider Indigenous political mobilization of the late 1960s without treating it as merely a prelude to that mobilization.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did it mean for a right to be “reserved” in a treaty, and how did courts come to interpret treaties differently from ordinary contracts?
  2. Why could a treaty right remain legally valid while becoming difficult or impossible to exercise in practice?
  3. How did the divided authority of federal, state, provincial, and tribal governments shape what treaty rights meant day to day?
  4. In what ways did postwar economic development revive and intensify older disputes over Indigenous land and resources?
  5. Why did fishing, hunting, flooded land, and water allocation produce different kinds of conflict, and what do they share?
  6. How did Indigenous communities combine everyday practice, formal government action, litigation, and direct action in defending treaty rights?
  7. Why were conservation arguments both legitimate in some respects and, in practice, often unequal in their treatment of Indigenous and non-Indigenous harvesters?
  8. How do Canadian and United States legal frameworks for treaty harvesting differ, and what explains those differences?
  9. What does the history of treaty-rights activism suggest about the origins of the political mobilizations of the late 1960s and 1970s?

Historical Context

The treaty relationships at the center of this chapter were created across more than a century and a half. In the United States, the federal government negotiated treaties with Indigenous nations from the late eighteenth century until Congress, in a rider to an appropriations act in 1871, ended the practice of recognizing additional tribes as parties to treaties, while preserving the obligations of existing ones. Formal agreements continued to be made by other means for several decades afterward. Each treaty was a distinct negotiation shaped by its own circumstances, and this chapter avoids treating “the treaties” as a single document. The Treaty of Canandaigua (1794) with the Haudenosaunee, the Stevens treaties of 1854–1856 in the Pacific Northwest, and the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) addressed different peoples, territories, and concerns.

The Stevens treaties are the source of many of the fishing disputes treated below. Isaac Stevens, the first governor of Washington Territory and its superintendent of Indian affairs, negotiated a series of treaties in 1854 and 1855, including Medicine Creek, Point Elliott, Point No Point, Neah Bay, and the treaties with the Yakama, the Nez Perce, and the peoples of the Walla Walla and Umatilla region. In these agreements Indigenous nations ceded large areas of land to the United States and retained reservations and specified rights, including the right of taking fish at their usual and accustomed places. The treaty negotiations were conducted under pressure and with limited translation, and the Treaty War of 1855–1856 followed in Puget Sound. Leschi, a Nisqually leader who had questioned the Medicine Creek boundaries, was convicted of murder and hanged in 1858; a historical court in Washington State later concluded in 2004 that he had been wrongly convicted. The episode illustrates why Indigenous communities remembered these treaties as unequal agreements whose guarantees they had every reason to protect.

In Canada, the Crown negotiated the eleven numbered treaties between 1871 and 1921, covering large parts of the prairies, northern Ontario, northeastern British Columbia, and the Mackenzie Valley, along with earlier agreements such as the Robinson treaties of 1850 and the Douglas treaties on Vancouver Island in the early 1850s. Many of these agreements referred to hunting, trapping, and fishing rights, usually subject to government regulation and to the taking up of land for settlement and development. First Nations treaty accounts, preserved in the knowledge of Elders and in community histories, often emphasize that the treaties established sacred relationships and mutual obligations, an understanding that differed from the narrower land-cession reading found in many government documents. The legal frameworks that followed the treaties differed in important ways from those of the United States.

By the Second World War, a body of legal principles protecting treaty rights existed in the United States, but it was thin, unevenly enforced, and largely untested against large-scale economic change. Postwar prosperity changed the setting. The federal government built dams on rivers across the continent for flood control, hydroelectric power, navigation, and irrigation, while state agencies expanded their management of fisheries and game for a growing population of sport and commercial harvesters. At the same time, federal Indian policy, examined in Chapters 104 and 105, shifted toward termination and relocation, which encouraged the view that treaty relationships were ending. The struggle over treaty rights unfolded in this contradictory setting: governments were reducing or reinterpreting their obligations at the very moment when Indigenous nations were organizing to enforce them. Throughout the chapter, readers should keep in mind two analytical distinctions: the difference between a legal right and the practical ability to exercise it, and the difference between a sovereign right and a benefit that an agency may grant or withdraw.

Division I Treaty Rights, Sovereignty, and the Postwar Crisis Sections 106.1–106.4 · Historical and legal foundations
Section 106.1

Treaty Rights as Continuing Obligations

Treaties between Indigenous nations and European or settler governments were not invented by the governments that later enforced or ignored them. Indigenous nations had long made agreements with one another about territory, trade, hunting grounds, and peace, and many of them brought those diplomatic practices into negotiations with colonial powers. The Haudenosaunee, for example, understood their relations with the Dutch, the English, and later the United States through the language of the Covenant Chain and the Two Row Wampum, systems of diplomacy that treated the parties as separate political communities bound by mutual obligations. When the United States and, earlier, Britain and France made treaties with Indigenous nations, they were participating in a form of political relationship that presupposed that the other party possessed sovereignty of its own. This is why the United States Constitution lists treaties among the supreme law of the land, and why the Supreme Court, in the 1830s, described Indigenous nations as distinct political communities with territorial rights that state law could not simply override.

That foundational premise has a consequence that is easy to overlook. If an Indigenous nation entered a treaty as a sovereign people, then what it ceded in the treaty was something it chose to give up, and what it did not cede remained with it. American courts eventually described this as the reserved-rights doctrine: a treaty is read as a grant of rights from the Indigenous nation to the United States, not the other way around, so that any rights not expressly surrendered continue to exist. This is the reason a treaty right to fish or hunt could survive a large land cession. An Indigenous nation might give up most of its territory and retain a reservation, and yet also retain the right to fish at places it had always used and to hunt on lands it no longer owned. The right to fish at a river site hundreds of miles from the reservation was not a gift of the government. It was a portion of an older relationship with the river that the nation had decided, in the treaty, not to surrender.

Courts also developed rules for interpreting treaties that differed from the rules governing ordinary commercial contracts. Because treaties were negotiated in English, often through interpreters, with Indigenous parties who could not read the written text, American courts came to hold that ambiguities should be resolved in the way the Indigenous signatories would naturally have understood them, and that treaties should be construed liberally in their favor. These canons of interpretation did not guarantee favorable results, and judges applied them inconsistently. In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), the Supreme Court also asserted that Congress possessed broad power to modify or override treaty provisions unilaterally. This produced a lasting contradiction in American law: treaties were the supreme law of the land, yet Congress claimed the authority to abrogate them. The distinction between a treaty right that remained legally in force and an executive or legislative decision that burdened it would be central to nearly every dispute in this chapter.

Students should distinguish between particular treaty rights and general claims of Indigenous rights. A right to take fish “at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations” is a specific right created by a specific treaty with a specific nation, with its own wording and legal history. A claim that Indigenous peoples hold an inherent right to govern themselves, to use their ancestral territories, or to be consulted about development is a broader claim that may rest on treaties but also on sovereignty, aboriginal title, and international and moral arguments. This chapter is largely concerned with the first category, but the two are connected: Indigenous communities often described their treaties as confirmations of rights that existed before the treaties, and they resisted the idea that governments had the power to define the scope of those rights unilaterally.

The same distinction appears, with different legal vocabulary, in Canada. There the Crown made treaties in the same period and the courts later treated them as solemn agreements, but the legal environment differed. Canadian law subordinated treaty harvesting provisions in several ways that Section 106.8 explains, and the constitutional recognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights lay decades in the future. Throughout this chapter, the same historical question applies on both sides of the border: whether a treaty was treated as a continuing obligation of the government or as a historical document of diminishing practical importance.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Right, Reserved Right, and Privilege

Definition. A treaty right is a legal entitlement that arises from or is confirmed by a treaty. A reserved right is a right the Indigenous nation kept for itself when it ceded other land or rights. A privilege is a permission that a government grants and may withdraw at its discretion.

Historical context. In United States v. Winans (1905), the Supreme Court explained treaty fishing rights as rights reserved by Indigenous nations rather than rights granted to them. Government agencies, by contrast, often administered hunting and fishing access as regulated permissions, a framework that implied that officials could define and limit them.

Why it matters. The difference between a right and a privilege determines who has the burden of justification. If fishing is a privilege, the individual must obey the state’s rules. If it is a reserved treaty right, the state must justify any restriction.

Potential misunderstanding. Reserved rights are not unlimited. Even in the strongest cases, courts recognized some regulatory authority over how rights were exercised, and Congress claimed power to modify or abrogate treaty provisions. The dispute was over the extent of these limits and who would define them.

Section 106.2

The Historical Foundations of Treaty Protection

The legal basis for the Pacific Northwest fishing disputes was laid in the 1850s. Between late 1854 and 1856, Governor Isaac Stevens and his colleagues negotiated treaties that opened much of what is now western and eastern Washington, northern Oregon, and parts of Idaho to American settlement. The treaties followed a recognizable pattern. Indigenous delegations ceded vast areas of land, accepted reservations that were often small and sometimes poorly suited to their economies, and received promises of payments, services, and protection. In exchange for what they gave up, the treaties also included provisions securing continued access to fisheries. The Medicine Creek treaty, the first of the series, secured the right of taking fish at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations, in common with the citizens of the territory, together with the privilege of hunting, gathering roots and berries, and pasturing horses on open and unclaimed land. Similar language appears in the other Stevens treaties.

Historians approach the Stevens treaties with caution about what they reveal. Stevens wanted rapid land cession to support settlement and the planned northern transcontinental railroad survey, and the negotiations, often conducted in Chinook Jargon with limited vocabulary, took place with remarkable speed. The records show that Indigenous negotiators repeatedly asked about their fisheries and said that they needed access to them. At the Walla Walla council in 1855, the evidence suggests that Indigenous leaders understood the treaty as confirming the fisheries and traditional resource areas that sustained their communities. The surviving documents are largely colonial records, so Indigenous understandings are reconstructed from reported statements, later testimony, and community tradition. These sources give strong evidence that fishing was central to the bargain, though they cannot fully reproduce the negotiators’ understanding of every phrase.

The first major test of these provisions in the Supreme Court was United States v. Winans (1905). The Winans brothers owned land along the Columbia River where they operated fishwheels that effectively cut off Yakama access to a traditional fishing site. The Yakama had been guaranteed the right to fish at their usual and accustomed places by the Treaty of 1855. The Court, in an opinion by Justice Joseph McKenna, held that the treaty right survived the transfer of the land to private owners. The Court’s reasoning, outlined in Section 106.1, was that the treaty was not a grant of rights to the Yakama but a grant of right from them, with the reservation of rights not surrendered. The Winans decision therefore established two principles on which later litigation relied: that treaty fishing rights could be exercised at off-reservation sites, and that they imposed a kind of servitude on land subsequently acquired by others, including the right to cross private property to reach fishing places.

A second foundation arrived three years later with Winters v. United States (1908), in which the Court held that the agreement establishing the Fort Belknap Reservation in Montana implicitly reserved enough water from the Milk River to fulfill the purposes of the reservation. Section 106.11 develops this case. For present purposes, Winters shows that the reserved-rights reasoning in Winans was not limited to fish. It could be extended to other resources essential to the reservation’s purposes.

The third pillar was Tulee v. Washington (1942), decided as the nation was occupied with the war. Sampson Tulee, a Yakama man, was arrested for fishing with a dip net without a state fishing license. The state argued that the treaty right was subject to state regulation, including licensing and payment of a fee. The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Hugo Black, held that the state could not require a license fee as a condition of treaty fishing. The fee served the state’s revenue and administration purposes and was not necessary for conservation. At the same time, the Court recognized that the state could impose regulations that were necessary for the conservation of the fish, provided they were consistent with the treaty. This qualified recognition of state power both protected the fishers and left open the question that the following decades would contest: where the boundary between legitimate conservation regulation and unlawful interference with treaty rights lay.

What these decisions established was therefore real but incomplete. They confirmed that treaty fishing rights existed, could be exercised off reservation, and could not be taxed by the state through licensing. They did not settle what “in common with” meant, whether it entitled Indigenous fishers to a specific share of the harvest, how much conservation regulation the treaties tolerated, or who could decide when a regulation was truly necessary. Nor did they address the practical question of enforcement: a favorable ruling in a particular case did not bind state officers in other situations, and the burden of litigation fell on individuals and tribes with limited resources. These unresolved problems made the decades after 1942 a period of contestation rather than settlement.

Section 106.3

Postwar Development and New Pressures on Treaty Lands

The legal principles described in the previous section were largely untested when the Second World War ended, and the postwar economic expansion altered the conditions in which they would be applied. Wartime industry had already reshaped some Indigenous homelands. Bonneville Dam, completed on the Columbia River in 1938, and Grand Coulee Dam, completed in 1942, were products of New Deal and wartime planning, and Grand Coulee, built without fish passage, blocked salmon from the upper Columbia, affecting Indigenous communities including the Colville and Spokane. After 1945, a much larger program of river engineering followed, supported by federal agencies seeking flood control, hydroelectric power, irrigation, and navigation for expanding cities and agricultural regions.

Four kinds of postwar development produced the most important pressures on treaty lands and resources. First was dam construction, which flooded land, blocked migratory fish, and changed river conditions. Second was the growth of regulated commercial and sport fisheries, as more fishers, better boats, and new nets increased the pressure on salmon and steelhead in Puget Sound and along the Pacific coast. Third was urban and industrial expansion, including highways, ports, and housing developments, which altered riverbanks and intertidal areas and sometimes required lands within or adjacent to reservations. Fourth was the strengthening of state wildlife and fisheries agencies, which developed more elaborate systems of seasons, licenses, bag limits, and gear restrictions applicable to the whole population. In Washington, for example, state law had long treated steelhead as a game fish reserved for hook-and-line sport fishing, which meant that Indigenous net fishing for steelhead was prohibited on state terms even where treaties protected fishing at usual and accustomed places.

Postwar federal Indian policy compounded these pressures in subtle ways. The Indian Claims Commission, established in 1946, allowed tribes to file claims against the United States for lost land and unfulfilled obligations, but its authority was largely monetary: it could award compensation, not restore territory or fishing places. Its creation also reflected a policy outlook in which historic grievances could be settled and closed, an outlook closely connected to the termination policy examined in Chapter 104. House Concurrent Resolution 108 (1953) declared the intention to end federal supervision of certain tribes, and while it did not abolish treaties, it signaled to state governments and development interests that the federal government’s trust relationship was diminishing. Indigenous leaders and their allies read this shift with concern, and many of them linked the defense of treaty rights directly to resistance to termination.

The effect on Indigenous economies was uneven but severe in the places most affected. Fishing had long been important to cash incomes as well as subsistence for many Northwest families, who sold fish in commercial markets and also sustained their households through seasonal harvests. When a dam inundated a fishery or a state ban closed a traditional gear, the loss extended beyond the fish. It affected food supplies, seasonal rhythms of labor, ceremonial life, and the ability to remain in a community rather than seek wage work in a distant city, a choice that relocation programs described in Chapter 105 encouraged. For Indigenous families in these regions, defending treaty rights could therefore be a way of making it possible to stay.

The chapter will show that these pressures did not appear all at once or take the same form everywhere. A dam threatened the Seneca by flooding land that a treaty had guaranteed. State game laws threatened Northwest fishers by criminalizing their methods. Provincial wildlife regulations threatened Cree and Dene hunters in northern Canada by restricting access to the animals on which they depended. What connected them was the fact that each government justified its action as serving a public interest that the treaties did not clearly address, and each action forced the Indigenous nations affected to decide whether to comply, negotiate, litigate, or resist.

Section 106.4

Federal, State, Provincial, and Tribal Authority

Understanding the struggle over treaty rights requires understanding the overlapping jurisdictions involved. In the United States, the Constitution gives the federal government the power to make treaties and regulate commerce with Indian tribes, and the Supreme Court held in the 1830s that state law could not extend into Indian country without federal authorization. But the federal government is itself divided. The Department of the Interior, through the Bureau of Indian Affairs, bore the trust responsibility to protect tribal interests, while the Army Corps of Engineers and the Bureau of Reclamation, also federal agencies, designed dams that sometimes harmed those same interests. The Department of Justice, which represented the United States in litigation, had to decide whether to support tribal treaty claims against other federal agencies or states. These internal conflicts meant that Indigenous nations often confronted not a single government but several federal actors with competing objectives.

State authority was equally layered. States did not have jurisdiction over a tribe’s internal affairs on a reservation absent federal authorization, but they did regulate fishing and hunting by non-Indians and, they argued, by Indians who left the reservation. In 1953, Congress enacted Public Law 280, which transferred some criminal and civil jurisdiction over Indian country to certain states and allowed others, including Washington, to assume it later by their own legislation. The law included a provision stating that it did not deprive Indians of any right, privilege, or immunity protected by federal treaty or statute with respect to hunting, trapping, or fishing. That savings clause shows that Congress, even while expanding state power, recognized that treaty harvesting rights should remain intact. It did not say how conflicts between state regulation and those rights should be resolved, so the clause was often invoked in court rather than applied automatically in the field.

Tribal governments held authority too, though it varied widely. Many tribes regulated the fishing and hunting of their own members on the reservation, and some had tribal courts and conservation codes. But tribal governments in the 1950s and early 1960s often lacked funding, trained staff, and recognized authority over off-reservation fishing. In western Washington, many treaty fishers operated as individuals or families, not under comprehensive tribal management, which weakened their ability to answer the state’s argument that Indigenous fishing was unregulated. Several tribes later developed fisheries departments and biological programs, a development tied to the 1970s and treated in Chapter 113. The relatively limited tribal capacity in the earlier period was therefore not a sign that tribal authority did not exist. It reflected the long underfunding of tribal governments and the absence of federal recognition of their regulatory role.

In Canada, the distribution of power was different. The Constitution Act, 1867 assigned to the federal Parliament authority over “Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians,” while provinces controlled property and civil rights and, after 1930 in the prairie provinces, natural resources. The numbered treaties were made with the Crown in right of Canada, but the provinces, which owned the land and wildlife, regulated hunting and fishing through provincial legislation. The Indian Act of 1951 provided that provincial laws of general application applied to Indians, subject to the terms of any treaty, a provision later renumbered in the Act. That structure meant that the extent of treaty protection depended on how courts interpreted both the treaty and the relationship between federal and provincial law. First Nations governments, operating largely through band councils under the Indian Act (see Chapter 85), had limited recognized authority to regulate harvesting.

The practical consequences of this jurisdictional complexity were substantial. A fisher stopped by a state officer could not easily determine which authority controlled the encounter. A tribe petitioning for protection could find that the Interior Department agreed in principle but would not oppose another federal agency’s project. A First Nation hunter charged under a provincial statute had to bring a treaty defense in a criminal court, with the risk of conviction and the expense of appeal. These circumstances meant that treaty rights were typically defended not through administrative processes but through individual prosecutions, which placed Indigenous people in the position of defendants asserting rights rather than rights-holders being consulted.

Section 106.5

Treaty Fishing in the Pacific Northwest

Salmon are central to the history of the Pacific Northwest, and their life cycle helps explain why treaty fishing rights were so valuable. Pacific salmon hatch in freshwater, migrate to the ocean, and return as adults to spawn in the rivers of their birth. Five species return to the region’s rivers at different times of year, and steelhead, a sea-run form of rainbow trout, returns in other seasons. These runs gave Indigenous communities a succession of harvest opportunities across many months, and over generations the peoples of Puget Sound, the coast, and the Columbia Basin developed technologies suited to particular rivers and tides: weirs and traps in streams, dip nets at rapids and falls, reef nets used by Coast Salish communities in saltwater, and later gillnets and other gear. Fish were eaten fresh, smoked or dried for winter, exchanged with neighbors, and sold. Many communities honored the first salmon of the season in ceremonies that expressed obligations of respect and reciprocity toward the fish.

For this reason, fishing was not simply an economic activity in the narrow sense. It organized the movement of families through the seasons, defined ownership and access arrangements among kin groups and villages, and provided a framework for law, ceremony, and teaching. When treaty negotiators in 1854 and 1855 heard Indigenous delegations insist on protecting fishing places, they were hearing a request to preserve the basis of community life. The treaty language that resulted protected fishing at “usual and accustomed” grounds and stations. Those words were important in two ways. They extended the right beyond reservation boundaries to places the nation had traditionally used, and they implicitly tied the right to history, because the places in question were identified by long use rather than by survey lines.

Over the decades after the treaties, Indigenous fishing was gradually squeezed by other users. Canneries and commercial fleets developed in the late nineteenth century, and fish traps, large fixed devices operated by canneries, harvested enormous quantities of salmon before Washington voters banned them by initiative in 1934. Logging, farming, mills, and urban settlement damaged spawning streams and altered river channels. Hatcheries, introduced to compensate for habitat loss, changed the character of runs. State law increasingly restricted gear and seasons for conservation and allocation purposes, and by the middle of the twentieth century the state’s regulatory system treated the salmon and steelhead fishery as one that served commercial, sport, and Indigenous fishers under state rules.

Indigenous fishers nevertheless continued to fish at traditional sites, and for many families this fishing remained essential to household food supplies and income. The Puyallup, Nisqually, Muckleshoot, Quinault, Makah, Lummi, and many other Washington nations, as well as the Yakama, Warm Springs, Umatilla, and Nez Perce on the Columbia, understood their treaties to protect this activity. Their descendants emphasized that the protection extended to places that were not reservation land. On the Columbia, fishing at sites such as Celilo Falls had been a major economic and social institution for generations, drawing fishers and traders from across the region. On Puget Sound, families fished at river mouths and tidal sites near the places where they lived. The legal question was whether the state could prohibit methods such as net fishing in those places on conservation grounds, and who would decide whether the prohibition was necessary.

Section 106.6

State Fishing Regulations and Treaty Conflict

State fishing regulation in the mid-twentieth century operated through a combination of licenses, closed seasons, restrictions on gear, designated game fish, and enforcement by wardens with authority to arrest and seize equipment. Washington’s fisheries and game departments argued that these tools were necessary to protect fish runs that were under strain from overfishing, dams, logging, and pollution. Their rules applied across the state, and in the state’s view, an exemption for treaty fishers would make regulation unworkable. In practice, however, the rules were designed around commercial and sport fisheries and often fell especially hard on Indigenous fishing methods. A ban on net fishing for steelhead, for example, fell on a method central to Indigenous fishing while preserving the hook-and-line fishery favored by sport anglers.

For Indigenous fishers, enforcement could be severe. State officers arrested fishers, confiscated nets and boats, and brought criminal charges. Because boats and nets were often a family’s means of earning an income, a seizure could be costly even if a charge was later dropped. Fishers who were prosecuted could raise a treaty defense, but doing so required legal counsel and persistence, and state trial courts and the Washington Supreme Court generally upheld state regulation. In the Puyallup litigation that eventually reached the United States Supreme Court, the Washington Supreme Court sustained the state’s authority to prohibit net fishing for steelhead on the Puyallup River, setting the stage for the 1968 decision examined in Section 106.16.

The state’s legal position drew on the qualified recognition of regulatory power in Tulee. If the state could regulate for conservation, it argued, then it could determine what conservation required, and courts should defer to the expertise of biologists and wardens. Indigenous nations and their advocates replied that conservation was not a self-defining term. Regulations that restricted Indigenous gear while allowing non-Indigenous commercial and sport harvest raised the question of whether conservation, or allocation among competing user groups, was the real purpose. They also argued that the Washington agencies had no authority to define the scope of a federal treaty. The unresolved question was not whether conservation mattered, since Indigenous communities depended on the continued return of the fish, but who had the power to decide how it should be pursued and who should bear the burden of restrictions.

Evidence from the period supports parts of both arguments. Fish runs in many Washington rivers were indeed under pressure, and the state’s concern about declining stocks was not invented. At the same time, by many accounts the Indigenous harvest in this period represented a small fraction of the total catch compared with the commercial and sport fisheries, and the state’s regulatory framework did not distinguish between the effects of different user groups on the resource. Dams, logging, and development, which the state and federal governments themselves authorized, often caused greater damage than Indigenous fishing. This asymmetry became a central theme in Indigenous arguments: that treaty fishers were being asked to bear the costs of resource decline that others had largely caused.

Historical Debate

Conservation or Control? How Far Could States Regulate Treaty Fishing?

The question: When the state of Washington restricted Indigenous net fishing in the name of conservation, was it exercising a legitimate and limited regulatory power consistent with the treaties, or was it using conservation language to limit a federally protected right in favor of other user groups?

Interpretation A — Conservation as a legitimate, equally applied necessity: State officials and many contemporary commentators argued that a single set of rules was needed to protect shared fish populations, and that fish did not distinguish between kinds of fishers. Supporters of this view stressed that Tulee itself recognized state conservation power.
Interpretation B — Conservation as a pretext for unequal allocation: Indigenous advocates and treaty scholars argued that many regulations targeted Indigenous gear while leaving other harvests and habitat damage unaddressed, and that the state lacked the authority to define the scope of the treaties. In this view, conservation measures had to meet a strict necessity standard and could not discriminate.
Interpretation C — Shared management as an alternative: A third approach, which developed more fully later, held that tribal governments should participate in managing the fisheries rather than being treated solely as regulated parties.

Evidence: Historians examine state enforcement records, arrest and seizure reports, fish-run data, treaty texts, and judicial opinions. The evidence does not support a claim that state agencies acted with a single motive, since many officials sincerely believed in conservation. It does show that the regulatory system was shaped by the interests of commercial and sport harvesters and that tribal governments were largely excluded from decision-making.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and scale: how much regulatory authority Tulee allowed, how much Indigenous fishing affected the runs, and whether the state’s regulations were applied evenhandedly.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians assess the legitimacy of enforcement actions in the 1950s and 1960s and how they interpret later developments, including the court decisions examined in Chapter 113.

Section 106.7

Hunting, Trapping, Gathering, and Resource Access

Although fishing dominates popular memory of treaty-rights struggles, many treaties protected other kinds of harvesting, and Indigenous nations defended these rights in different ways. The Stevens treaties, for example, included hunting and gathering provisions alongside fishing: Article 3 of the Medicine Creek treaty mentions hunting, gathering roots and berries, and pasturing horses on open and unclaimed land, and the 1855 treaties with the Yakama and Nez Perce include similar language. These provisions protected the seasonal movement of families to upland meadows for berries and roots and to hunting areas for deer and elk. Yet they were rarely tested in court as often as fishing rights, partly because wildlife agencies enforced regulations in remote areas where Indigenous hunters had little access to legal help.

Where hunting rights did reach the courts, results varied. In State v. Arthur (1953), the Idaho Supreme Court held that a Nez Perce man could rely on the 1855 treaty’s hunting provision to hunt on open and unclaimed land outside the reservation in defiance of state game law, a rare early affirmation of treaty hunting rights against a state. In other places, hunters faced prosecution under state laws that treated all residents the same. In the Great Lakes region, Anishinaabe nations, including the Ojibwe of Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota, had made treaties in 1836, 1837, 1842, and 1854 under which they ceded land but retained, in the understanding of many communities, rights to hunt, fish, and gather in the ceded territory. States regulated deer hunting, commercial fishing in the Great Lakes, and wild rice harvesting, and conflicts occurred as Ojibwe harvesters sought to maintain practices that state law restricted. These conflicts would produce major litigation in the 1970s and 1980s, but their roots lay in the postwar regulatory expansion.

The Menominee case shows how treaty rights could survive the loss of federal recognition. When the Menominee Nation of Wisconsin was terminated in 1961, as examined in Chapter 104, the state sought to apply its fish and game laws to Menominee hunters and fishers on lands that had formerly been the reservation. In Menominee Tribe v. United States (1968), the Supreme Court held that the termination statute did not abrogate the tribe’s hunting and fishing rights under the Treaty of Wolf River (1854). The Court’s reasoning rested on the principle that Congress should not be presumed to have abolished treaty rights without clear language. The decision reinforced the point, central to this chapter, that treaty rights could outlast policy changes that were meant to end the special relationship between a tribe and the federal government.

On the Plains, treaties such as the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) contained provisions about hunting on unceded lands as long as game remained. Whether these provisions continued to have force after statehood and the extinguishment of the buffalo economy was a question that arose in later decades and cannot be assumed to have the same answer for every nation. Crow, Lakota, Cheyenne, and other Plains nations had different treaties, different territorial histories, and different relationships with state wildlife agencies. This chapter therefore avoids treating hunting rights as uniform. Instead, the central point is that each right depended on the particular treaty language, the nation’s history, and the jurisdiction in which it was asserted.

For Indigenous households, hunting, trapping, and gathering were not leisure or sport. In northern regions, trapping connected families to the fur economy, and hunting supplied meat that was costly or unavailable in local stores. Gathering plants such as camas, huckleberries, wild rice, and medicinal species supported both food security and ceremonial practice. When state regulation closed a gathering area, set a hunting season incompatible with community practice, or limited access to lands now under private or public ownership, it could disrupt economic and cultural life. The loss was compounded by the fact that access to land was often as important as the legal status of the right. A treaty might guarantee the privilege of hunting on open and unclaimed land, but as land passed into private ownership, fenced ranches, and managed national forests, the amount of land that was both open and accessible shrank.

Section 106.8

First Nations Treaty Harvesting Rights in Canada

In Canada, the treaties that most directly protected hunting, trapping, and fishing were the numbered treaties negotiated between 1871 and 1921 and several earlier agreements. The first seven numbered treaties, concluded between 1871 and 1877, covered much of the southern prairies and contained provisions that preserved the right of First Nations to hunt and fish on unoccupied Crown land, generally subject to government regulation. Treaty 8, signed in 1899 and covering northern Alberta, northeastern British Columbia, northwestern Saskatchewan, and part of the Northwest Territories, promised that the First Nations parties would have the right to pursue their usual vocations of hunting, trapping, and fishing, subject to government regulations and excepting lands taken up for settlement, mining, lumbering, trading, or other purposes. First Nations accounts of Treaty 8 emphasize that commissioners assured them that their way of life would not be interfered with, an understanding that did not always match later government practice.

Two features of these provisions shaped later conflict. First, the treaties usually made harvesting rights subject to government regulation and to the taking up of land. This language gave governments an argument that harvesting rights were limited and could shrink as development advanced. Second, the treaties were made with the Crown in right of Canada, but as the prairie provinces took control of natural resources, wildlife law came under provincial authority. In 1930, the federal government and the governments of Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta concluded the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements, which transferred ownership of Crown lands and resources to the provinces. These agreements, later confirmed as part of the constitution of Canada, contained a provision to ensure that the transfer did not deprive Indians of their ability to hunt, trap, and fish for food. The provision assured First Nations of the right, which the province “assured,” to hunt, trap, and fish game and fish for food at all seasons of the year on unoccupied Crown lands and other lands to which they had a right of access.

The effect of the 1930 agreements was double-edged. On one hand, they secured a constitutional guarantee that First Nations could hunt, trap, and fish for food regardless of provincial game seasons, which was a significant protection. On the other, they confirmed provincial control over wildlife and limited the guarantee to food, a limitation that raised disputes over commercial and ceremonial uses and over whether provinces could impose conservation restrictions. Provincial governments expanded licensing and management systems, including registered trapping areas and seasons for game, and enforcement officers sometimes charged Indigenous hunters under provincial statutes, leaving the courts to determine how far treaty protections extended.

Several cases in the early 1960s illustrate the range of outcomes. In Sikyea v. The Queen (1964), the Supreme Court of Canada upheld the conviction of Michael Sikyea, a Dene man in the Northwest Territories, for shooting a duck out of season in violation of the federal Migratory Birds Convention Act, holding that this federal legislation took precedence over his treaty right to hunt. The decision showed that treaty harvesting rights could be overridden by valid federal statutes, particularly those implementing international agreements. In contrast, R. v. White and Bob, decided by the British Columbia Court of Appeal in 1964 and affirmed by the Supreme Court of Canada in 1965, upheld a treaty protection for Indigenous hunters in a provincial setting.

The White and Bob case concerned Clifford White and David Bob, who were charged under British Columbia’s Game Act for possessing deer killed out of season on Vancouver Island. They defended themselves by relying on an agreement made in 1854 between the Crown colony of Vancouver Island, represented by Governor James Douglas, and the Snuneymuxw, the people of Nanaimo. Under the Douglas treaties, the Indigenous parties surrendered land in exchange for payment, while retaining village sites and enclosed fields and the liberty to hunt over unoccupied lands and carry on their fisheries as formerly. The key legal question was whether this 1854 document counted as a “treaty” within the meaning of the Indian Act, which provided that provincial laws applied to Indians subject to the terms of any treaty. The British Columbia Court of Appeal held that it did, and that the Game Act therefore did not apply to the defendants to the extent that it conflicted with the treaty. The Supreme Court of Canada upheld that ruling in 1965. The decision did not settle every question about Indigenous rights in British Columbia, where most of the province was not covered by treaties, but it confirmed that provincial game laws could not simply override a treaty hunting right.

Taken together, the Canadian cases show a legal landscape in which treaty rights could be upheld against provincial law but yield to federal law. They also show that First Nations hunters and trappers faced a structural problem similar to that of Indigenous fishers in the United States: they were typically defendants in criminal prosecutions, not parties to a process that recognized their governments as co-managers of the resource. The expansion of Indigenous political organization in Canada in the 1960s, including provincial and national associations, gave these concerns a wider audience, but the legal protection of harvesting rights remained uncertain and dependent on case-by-case litigation.

Treaty Food-Harvesting Rights and “Unoccupied Crown Lands”

Definition. In the prairie provinces, the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 provided that Indians had the right to hunt, trap, and fish game and fish for food at all seasons on unoccupied Crown lands and on other lands to which they had a right of access. “Unoccupied” generally meant land not put to a visible use inconsistent with hunting, such as settled or developed land.

Historical context. The provision responded to a concern that transferring resources to provincial control would expose First Nations hunters to provincial game laws that their treaties had never contemplated. It preserved a core of protection while confirming provincial authority over wildlife management.

Why it matters. The terms “for food” and “unoccupied” defined the scope of the right and became the basis for disputes about commercial harvesting and access to private or developed land.

Potential misunderstanding. The guarantee was not a general right to harvest wildlife for any purpose or on any land. It was limited by purpose and by the status of the land, and it operated within a system of provincial regulation.

Section 106.9

The Seneca Nation and the Kinzua Dam

The Seneca Nation of Indians is one of the Haudenosaunee nations whose homeland lay in what is now western New York. After the American Revolution and a series of land cessions in the 1790s, the Seneca retained a group of reservations along the Allegheny River and elsewhere. The Treaty of Canandaigua (1794), negotiated by Timothy Pickering on behalf of the United States, acknowledged the lands of the Six Nations and promised that the United States would not claim them or disturb the Seneca and their friends in what the text calls the “free use and enjoyment” of those lands. The treaty also provided for annual payments in goods, which were commemorated in the practice of delivering treaty cloth to Haudenosaunee communities and remain an annual observance at Canandaigua. For the Seneca, this treaty was not an antique document. It was the continuing foundation of their relationship with the United States and the guarantee of the territory on which Seneca families, longhouses, farms, and cemeteries stood.

The Allegheny River also lay in the path of federal flood-control planning. After catastrophic Ohio Valley floods, particularly the Pittsburgh flood of 1936, Congress authorized flood-control works across the Ohio River basin, and by the 1950s the Army Corps of Engineers had advanced a plan for a dam on the Allegheny River near Kinzua, Pennsylvania. The dam’s reservoir would back water into New York, covering a substantial part of the Seneca Nation’s Allegany Reservation, including the best bottomland, home sites, roads, schools, and the Cold Spring Longhouse, and would also flood the Cornplanter tract in Pennsylvania. The Seneca Nation opposed the project from the start, arguing that the treaty prohibited it and that an alternative was available. In 1955 the Nation invited Arthur E. Morgan, an engineer and former chairman of the Tennessee Valley Authority, to evaluate the project, and Morgan proposed an alternative based on a dam at Conewango Creek and related measures that would have avoided flooding Seneca land. The Corps rejected the alternative, arguing that it would not provide the same flood protection for Pittsburgh at a comparable cost. Historians still debate the technical merits of the competing plans, though the documentary record shows that the Corps did not treat the treaty as a decisive constraint.

Legal action followed. In Seneca Nation of Indians v. Brucker, a federal appeals court in the District of Columbia in 1958 ruled against the Seneca, holding that Congress had the power to take Seneca land for the project even if doing so conflicted with the 1794 treaty, and the Supreme Court declined to review the case in 1959. This reasoning followed the logic of Lone Wolf, discussed in Section 106.1: a treaty was binding law, but Congress could, by clear action, override it. The Seneca’s legal arguments therefore failed, and the Nation turned to political appeals. In 1961, the Seneca asked President John F. Kennedy to halt the project. His administration declined to stop it, citing the public interest in flood control, though it encouraged efforts to reduce harm to the Seneca. Construction proceeded and the Corps began work on the dam in the early 1960s.

The consequences were substantial. The reservoir flooded roughly nine to ten thousand acres of the Allegany Reservation, a large share of the reservation’s land and its most productive riverfront, and about six hundred Seneca people, from roughly 130 families, were required to leave their homes. Many were relocated to new housing communities at Jimersontown and Steamburg, built away from the river. Cemeteries were moved, and the community’s traditional gathering and ceremonial places were disrupted. The Seneca Nation’s lands were divided, and the loss was not merely material. The river valley had been a setting for family life, subsistence, and community memory. Survivors and descendants have emphasized the social shock of displacement, including the loss of a close-knit riverside community and changes to economic and civic life.

In 1964 Congress enacted Public Law 88-533, providing approximately $15 million in total compensation and rehabilitation funding to the Seneca Nation. That total comprised $3 million for land, improvements, relocation, and related direct losses, and $12 million for a wider community rehabilitation program. The statute also required federal officials to submit a proposal for withdrawal of federal supervision over the Nation by August 31, 1967, linking compensation to the termination-era policy atmosphere discussed in Chapter 104; it did not itself immediately terminate the Nation. Hearings and correspondence addressed the human consequences and sought remedies, but the legislation did not reverse the taking. Kinzua Dam was completed in 1965 and dedicated the following year. The dispute attracted national attention, including Peter La Farge’s song about the destruction of Seneca lands recorded by Johnny Cash in 1964.

The Kinzua case shows how a treaty could be honored in some respects and set aside in others. The 1794 treaty was acknowledged as binding, yet Congress and the courts held that it did not prohibit a taking for a public project if Congress authorized it and provided compensation. The Seneca argued that this reduced a treaty to a promise that held only until a government found it inconvenient. The government answered that Congress had the constitutional power to decide how to balance the interests of the nation, including the Seneca, against the benefits of flood control. Students should keep distinct three levels of the dispute: the Nation’s treaty claim, the government’s statutory and constitutional powers, and the judicial rulings that determined which prevailed.

Section 106.10

The Missouri River Dams and Indigenous Land Loss

The largest single program of river development to affect Indigenous nations in the postwar period was the Pick–Sloan Missouri Basin Program. After catastrophic Missouri River floods in the early 1940s, the Army Corps of Engineers (the Pick Plan, emphasizing flood control and navigation) and the Bureau of Reclamation (the Sloan Plan, emphasizing irrigation and power) proposed competing approaches. Congress merged them in the Flood Control Act of 1944. The combined program included a system of large main-stem dams, among them Garrison in North Dakota, Oahe, Big Bend, and Fort Randall in South Dakota, and Gavins Point on the Nebraska–South Dakota border. These dams produced electricity and flood control for regions downstream, but the large reservoirs behind them covered river bottomlands that were the most fertile and sheltered lands in the northern Plains. Many of these lands lay within Indigenous reservations.

The Fort Berthold Reservation in North Dakota, home to the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara peoples, was among the hardest hit. These nations had lived along the Missouri for centuries in earth-lodge villages, farming corn, beans, and squash in the river bottoms, hunting on the plains, and trading widely. The reservation, shaped by nineteenth-century treaties and agreements, remained centered on the river valley. The bottomlands provided farmland, timber, wild fruit, firewood, and winter shelter for livestock. The reservation’s agency town, Elbowoods, contained the agency headquarters, a hospital, schools, churches, and other institutions. Most of the reservation’s population lived in the valley.

When Garrison Dam was authorized, tribal leaders understood that it would flood the heart of the reservation. They protested, negotiated, and traveled to Washington to testify against the project and seek protection and adequate compensation. Congress’s July 31, 1947 appropriation (Public Law 80-296) earmarked $5,105,625 for acquiring Fort Berthold lands and related losses, conditional on further negotiation and congressional approval. In May 1948 tribal chairman George Gillette signed the Garrison Dam Agreement under intense pressure, as documented in a Bureau of Indian Affairs photograph dated May 20, 1948. Congress then enacted the Taking Act, Public Law 81-437, on October 29, 1949, vesting specified tribal lands in the United States and providing an additional $7.5 million, bringing the statutory compensation package to about $12.6 million. The dates and the two monetary components must be kept separate: the initial appropriation was made in 1947, the agreement was signed in 1948, and the taking legislation followed in 1949. Gillette’s distress at the signing is documented in historical accounts, but an image or recollection cannot independently establish every participant’s motives.

The reservoir, later named Lake Sakakawea, flooded more than 150,000 acres of Fort Berthold land, with estimates differing depending on how land is categorized. The loss was concentrated in the river bottoms, which meant that the nations lost their best agricultural land, timber, and sheltered settlement areas. More than three hundred families, probably over a thousand people, were relocated, and Elbowoods and other river communities were abandoned. The rising water split the reservation into separate parts, complicating travel between communities and access to services. The tribes were resettled on higher, more exposed lands that were less suited to farming and ranching, and the move disrupted kinship networks, local institutions, and the community’s economic base. The loss of the agency town also affected health care, schooling, and tribal governance.

Compensation became a continuing controversy. The original $5,105,625 allocation and the additional $7.5 million provided with the 1949 Taking Act were widely regarded by tribal leaders as inadequate in relation to the economic value of the land and the destruction of the river-bottom communities that depended upon it. Together these provisions amounted to approximately $12,605,625; presenting only the initial $5.1 million as the entire 1949 package would be misleading. Later reviews, including the Garrison Unit Joint Tribal Advisory Committee process in the 1980s, recommended further relief, and Congress subsequently enacted additional compensation measures. Those later actions registered the inadequacy of the original arrangements but could not restore the flooded towns, farms, and community networks.

Historians distinguish Garrison from other parts of Pick–Sloan. The Garrison project affected Fort Berthold, while Oahe, Big Bend, and Fort Randall flooded portions of the Standing Rock, Cheyenne River, Lower Brule, Crow Creek, Yankton, and other Sioux reservations, and each nation negotiated its own losses and compensation. Total Indigenous acreage lost across the program was very large, but figures vary, and this chapter does not give a single total. What the Missouri case demonstrates, alongside Kinzua, is that postwar river development could extinguish the practical value of reserved land even where the land was not formally taken for the purpose of ending a treaty. Indigenous nations lost land through the exercise of federal condemnation power rather than through treaty abrogation, and the line between the two was less clear to the families affected than to the officials who drew it.

Section 106.11

Reserved Water Rights and the Colorado River

In the arid West, land without water is of little use for agriculture, and the legal doctrines that allocate water have shaped the fate of Indigenous nations as profoundly as land policy. Western states adopted a system known as prior appropriation, under which the first person to put water to beneficial use acquires a right with priority over later users. Under this principle, water rights depend on actual use and the date of that use, which favored settlers and corporations who developed irrigation systems quickly. Indigenous nations, many of which had been confined to reservations with little capital for irrigation, were at a disadvantage if their water rights were measured only by this system.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Winters v. United States (1908) provided a different principle. The case arose from the Fort Belknap Reservation in Montana, home to Gros Ventre and Assiniboine people, established by an agreement in 1888. Upstream non-Indian settlers had diverted water from the Milk River, leaving too little for the reservation. The United States sued on behalf of the tribes. In an opinion by Justice McKenna, the Court held that the agreement that created the reservation had impliedly reserved enough water to make the reservation livable and productive, and that later state-law claims could not defeat it. The practical consequences were important: the right dated from the creation of the reservation, so it had priority over later users, and it did not depend on the nation having already used the water. The doctrine also meant that a reservation’s right to water could be asserted even if the tribe had not yet developed irrigation.

For decades, the Winters doctrine was largely dormant in practice. The federal government, through the Bureau of Reclamation, built irrigation projects across the West, generally for non-Indian farmers, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs, which was responsible for protecting tribal water, was in a different department with limited resources. The Secretary of the Interior thus oversaw both the agency building competing water projects and the agency responsible for tribal interests, a structural conflict that critics have emphasized. Cases such as the Ahtanum Creek litigation in Washington, in which the Ninth Circuit in 1956 recognized the Yakama Nation’s prior right to water under its treaty, showed that tribes could win protection, but such cases were exceptions.

The most important postwar test of Winters came on the lower Colorado River. The river’s water had been divided among the basin states by the Colorado River Compact of 1922 and the Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928, which authorized Hoover Dam and allocated water among California, Arizona, and Nevada. The Compact contained a clause stating that it did not affect obligations to Indians, but it did not quantify tribal rights. When Arizona sued California in 1952 over allocation of the lower basin’s water, the United States intervened to assert claims on behalf of Indian reservations along the river. The Supreme Court appointed a special master, Simon Rifkind, who held extensive hearings and recommended that the reservations’ rights be measured by the amount of water necessary to irrigate all the practicably irrigable acreage on the reservations.

In Arizona v. California (1963), the Supreme Court largely accepted the special master’s approach. It confirmed that the Winters doctrine applied to reservations created by executive order and by statute and not only by treaty. It held that the quantity of water reserved was meant to satisfy the future as well as the present needs of the reservations, and it adopted practicably irrigable acreage as the measure of the quantity. It also held that the priority date of each right was the date the reservation was established, which gave tribal claims priority over many non-Indian users. The decree awarded roughly nine hundred thousand acre-feet of water annually to the five reservations before the Court: the Chemehuevi, Cocopah, Colorado River, Fort Mojave, and Quechan reservations. The decision was a major victory for tribal water rights and became the foundation for later claims, discussed in Chapter 120.

Students should note the limits of this ruling. It addressed the five reservations along the lower Colorado River that were before the Court, not every tribal water claim in the United States. It also did not itself deliver water to farms. The nations had to build and finance irrigation systems, and the water might be used by others if the tribes lacked the resources to develop it. The decision therefore confirmed a legal right without guaranteeing that it would translate into economic development. It also reflected the conflict between tribal water rights and state and non-Indian interests, which has continued through later litigation and negotiated settlements.

Historical Vocabulary
Reserved Water Rights (the Winters Doctrine)

Definition. The principle, drawn from Winters v. United States (1908), that when the United States set aside a reservation, it also impliedly reserved enough water to fulfill the purposes of the reservation.

Historical context. The doctrine developed in the arid West, where state water law rewarded first use. Reserved rights carry the priority date of the reservation’s creation, and they are not lost by nonuse.

Why it matters. The doctrine gave tribes a legal basis for claiming water that predated later settlers, and it made water an element of Indigenous sovereignty and economic development.

Potential misunderstanding. The right exists in law, but it does not automatically produce a measurable quantity or the infrastructure to use the water. Quantification required litigation or negotiation, and early decisions applied only to the specific reservations before the courts.

Section 106.12

Environmental Change and the Practical Exercise of Rights

The cases in the previous sections concern rights that governments formally recognized or formally overrode. A different problem arose when rights remained legally intact but the physical conditions needed to exercise them were destroyed. Rivers can be dammed, fish runs depleted, and habitats degraded without any government ever declaring that a treaty right has ended. In such cases, the right on paper remained, while the practice that gave it meaning became difficult or impossible.

The most dramatic example in this period was the loss of Celilo Falls. Located on the Columbia River near The Dalles, Oregon, Celilo was one of the great fishing and trading places of western North America. Fishers used dip nets and platforms to harvest salmon as they struggled up the rapids, and people from many nations gathered at the falls to fish, trade, and hold ceremonies. The Yakama, Warm Springs, Umatilla, and Nez Perce all had treaty rights there, which the Supreme Court had recognized in Winans. When The Dalles Dam was completed in March 1957, the rising reservoir submerged the falls in a matter of hours. The federal government compensated the Columbia River tribes with a settlement of roughly twenty-seven million dollars and later provided replacement fishing sites, but the cash and the new sites could not replace the place itself. Many who watched the falls disappear understood the event as the end of a world.

Celilo was one episode in a larger pattern of Columbia River development. Bonneville Dam (1938) interrupted the river with fish ladders designed to help salmon pass, and Grand Coulee Dam (1942), built without fish passage, eliminated access to the upper river and affected Colville and Spokane fisheries. Other dams followed, flooding traditional fishing places and altering the river’s flow, temperature, and sediment. Hatcheries and fish ladders mitigated some harm but could not fully replace natural spawning habitat. In Puget Sound, the problem was different but related: logging, agriculture, urban growth, pollution from mills, and dams on rivers such as the Nisqually reduced spawning habitat, while the state’s management system focused chiefly on regulating harvest rather than protecting habitat.

These changes created a paradox for treaty rights. The treaties protected the right to take fish, but the fish depended on conditions the treaties did not expressly regulate. A government could therefore respect the right on paper while authorizing projects that diminished the resource. Indigenous communities understood this and began to articulate the point that a right to fish required fish, a theme that would become more prominent in later decades. In the period covered by this chapter, the argument was more often expressed through protest and testimony than through litigation, because courts had not yet developed doctrines that protected habitat as an aspect of treaty rights.

The environmental problem also raised questions of political authority. Who had the power to manage rivers, fisheries, and watersheds in ways that protected treaty rights? State agencies managed harvest, federal agencies built dams, and tribes, which had often managed fisheries through their own law and practice before the treaties, were largely excluded from the decision-making. The argument that Indigenous nations should have a role in management was therefore tied to their sovereignty. Control over the resource was a matter of political power as well as ecology. This theme links the defense of treaty rights in the postwar decades to later developments in tribal fisheries management, environmental knowledge, and co-management, which later chapters examine.

Section 106.13

Everyday Resistance and the Continued Exercise of Treaty Rights

Before there were lawsuits, petitions, or organized demonstrations, there were families who simply kept fishing, hunting, and gathering. In many Indigenous communities, treaty rights were not abstract legal categories that people debated in courtrooms. They were activities that fed households, brought income, and connected people to places and to each other. Continuing these activities in the face of regulation was therefore ordinary and political at the same time. When a Nisqually family set a net, a Seneca family held a ceremony in a relocated longhouse, or a Cree hunter went out for game despite a provincial game warden’s warning, each act affirmed that the right continued to exist, whether or not the person framed it as defiance.

Oral histories and memoirs, including those of participants in Northwest fishing communities, describe a world in which knowledge about rivers and fish was passed from one generation to the next through practice. Children learned from parents and grandparents where fish ran at different times of year, how to read the river and the tides, how to make and repair nets, how to handle and preserve the catch, and how to share it with relatives and neighbors. Elders held knowledge of specific fishing sites and their histories, including the places that families regarded as theirs by long use. This knowledge was not only technical. It carried ethical teaching about respect for the fish, obligations to share, and the relationship between people and the rivers they depended upon. When state regulation restricted fishing, it interfered with this system of transmission as well as with food supply.

Continued practice often meant adapting to enforcement. Families learned where wardens patrolled, chose times and places to fish, and sometimes fished at night or in out-of-the-way locations. Not all adaptation took the form of evasion. Some families fished openly, accepting the risk of arrest, while others stopped fishing to avoid losing equipment they could not afford to replace. These choices reflected different circumstances and different judgments about risk, and they were also affected by the reality that prosecution could mean fines, jail time, and the seizure of boats and nets that represented a family’s capital. The historical record of these everyday choices is thinner than the record of litigation, because most were never written down by officials and survive mainly in memory. Historians therefore rely on oral histories, memoirs, tribal records, and court files, taking care to recognize that these sources reflect particular perspectives and later reflection.

The same pattern appears outside the Northwest. Seneca families displaced by Kinzua rebuilt community institutions, including the relocated longhouse, to maintain ceremony and governance in a new setting. The Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara re-established community life on the uplands after the flooding of the river valley, adapting farming, ranching, and social organization to a transformed landscape. Cree, Dene, and other First Nations hunters in Canada continued to hunt and trap for food, sometimes challenging game regulations in court and sometimes simply continuing practices their treaties and families had long sustained. In each case, continuity depended on the work of people who kept the practice alive and passed it on.

Recognizing everyday resistance helps correct a common impression that Indigenous political action began only with organized protest. In fact, much of the political work of the period consisted of the daily maintenance of rights through use. Courts and governments often noticed these rights only when they were challenged in prosecutions, but the people exercising them understood their activity as part of a continuous tradition. The later fish-ins were, in this sense, a deliberate public form of something that had never stopped.

  • Fish, game, and plants remained central to food supplies, household economies, and ceremony
  • Knowledge about rivers, seasons, and harvest was still passed from elders to younger generations
  • Communities continued to understand treaty rights as obligations owed by governments and not as privileges
  • Kinship networks organized fishing, sharing, and mutual support

Pattern of change: Change was gradual and uneven, accelerating after 1945 with postwar development and state regulation. Families directly engaged in harvesting experienced it most acutely, while state agencies and urban consumers experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that treaty-rights activism grew out of existing practice and was not created from nothing by the national movements of later years.

Section 106.14

Tribal Governments, Petitions, and Legal Organization

While families defended treaty rights through practice, tribal governments worked through the institutions available to them. In the United States, many tribes had adopted constitutions and councils under the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, as Chapter 97 explains, and these governments became the primary vehicles through which treaty claims were presented to federal officials. Councils passed resolutions asserting treaty rights, wrote to members of Congress and federal agencies, testified at public hearings, and, when they could, retained attorneys. These activities required resources that many tribes lacked, and they often had to operate under federal supervision. Contracts with attorneys, for example, generally required approval from the Secretary of the Interior, which gave the federal government oversight over how tribes pursued claims, even claims directed against federal action.

The Seneca Nation offers an example of a government mobilizing multiple strategies at once. Its council opposed the Kinzua project through lobbying, public statements, engagement with engineering experts such as Arthur Morgan, and litigation. When the legal route failed, it turned to political appeals to the president and Congress, and it negotiated the terms of relocation and compensation. The Fort Berthold tribal council likewise sent delegations to Washington, testified before congressional committees, and negotiated the terms of the taking of its land. Though they could not prevent the dams, these governments shaped the settlements that followed and created a record of protest that later claims drew upon.

Another channel was the Indian Claims Commission, created by Congress in 1946. The Commission allowed tribes to file claims for lands taken without adequate compensation and other grievances, and it led tribes to assemble historical, anthropological, and legal evidence about their territories and treaty history. The process was frustrating in important ways, since it generally provided money and not land, and some tribes resisted the underlying assumption that historic injuries could be resolved by a cash award. Nevertheless, claims preparation produced research and legal expertise, and it encouraged communities to document their histories of land use and treaty negotiation. Some of that documentation later became evidence in treaty-rights litigation.

Intertribal cooperation also grew. Regional organizations such as the Affiliated Tribes of Northwest Indians, founded in 1953, brought together tribal leaders to discuss common problems, including fishing, termination, and federal policy. The National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, provided a national forum, though its institutional history belongs to Chapter 107. These organizations allowed tribes to share legal strategies, coordinate testimony, and respond to proposed federal legislation. They also helped build a network of leaders who knew one another, an asset that proved significant when the political environment shifted in the late 1960s.

In Canada, First Nations faced a more restrictive legal environment. For a period, amendments to the Indian Act in 1927 prohibited raising funds or retaining lawyers to pursue land claims against the government, a restriction that remained in force until the Act was revised in 1951. This provision limited the ability of First Nations to organize legal challenges. Even after its repeal, band councils under the Indian Act had little independent funding, and many treaty issues were taken up by regional organizations such as the Indian Association of Alberta, founded in 1939. First Nations also pressed their case through petitions, delegations to Ottawa, and, increasingly, test cases in criminal courts like White and Bob. Status Indians were not given the right to vote in federal elections until 1960, which meant that First Nations people had limited formal political leverage in the same period.

Across both countries, Indigenous leaders combined formal strategies and community mobilization, though the balance varied. Some leaders preferred negotiation and litigation, which could secure legal precedent and avoid the risk of arrest. Others, particularly younger activists, concluded that legal arguments alone had not protected their communities and that public demonstrations were needed. These approaches were not always in agreement, and tribal councils and activists sometimes disagreed about tactics. They nevertheless shared an understanding that treaty rights had to be defended actively and that governments would not necessarily honor them without pressure.

Section 106.15

The Early Fish-Ins and Frank’s Landing

By the early 1960s, a new generation of Northwest Indigenous activists had become convinced that litigation in the state courts was not protecting treaty fishing. Billy Frank Jr. of the Nisqually Tribe, born in 1931, had been arrested repeatedly for fishing, and by his own later account the arrests began in his teens. His family lived at what became known as Frank’s Landing on the Nisqually River, on land that had come to the family through allotment, which meant that the site lay outside the reservation boundary but within the area the Nisqually regarded as their own. Billy Frank’s father and other relatives fished from the landing, and it became a gathering place for fishers and supporters. The Nisqually Tribe’s treaty claim to fish at usual and accustomed places on the river was clear, and the state’s enforcement was equally persistent.

Indigenous leadership and allied organizations broadened the visibility of the campaign. The National Indian Youth Council (NIYC) helped organize early 1964 fish-ins, while the Survival of American Indians Association (SAIA), with figures including Janet McCloud and Hank Adams, built continuing local support and publicity. At a publicly planned fish-in on March 2, 1964, on the Puyallup River near Tacoma, Puyallup leader Robert (Bob) Satiacum fished with actor Marlon Brando and Episcopal minister John Yaryan; Brando and Yaryan were arrested and quickly released. This specific event was not at Frank’s Landing, which lies on the Nisqually River. Comedian and civil-rights advocate Dick Gregory joined later fishing-rights demonstrations. Celebrities attracted press attention, but Indigenous fishers, families, and organizers—not outside visitors—supplied the campaign’s aims, labor, and continuity.

The fish-ins had clear and limited objectives. Their immediate purpose was to affirm the right of Indigenous people to fish with nets at usual and accustomed places without state licenses or restrictions, and to challenge the state’s argument that conservation required prohibiting those practices. They aimed to generate court cases that could test the state’s position, to expose the practical consequences of enforcement, and to build public understanding that treaty rights were binding obligations. They were also designed to demonstrate that Indigenous people, not only lawyers and officials, were prepared to defend those rights. Participants used the language of treaty law and, for some, the language of civil rights, though the claim was grounded in the legal status of Indigenous nations and not in equal treatment as citizens.

Enforcement confrontations were part of the strategy and part of the risk. Arrests, fines, and equipment seizures imposed real costs, and fishers sometimes faced hostility from non-Indigenous commercial and sport fishers, who feared that treaty fishing would deplete the runs. State officials, for their part, saw themselves as enforcing laws designed to conserve a shared resource. The tension was heightened by the fact that fish-ins were intentionally public and confrontational. The relationship between local leadership and wider support was also complex: the Frank family and other fishers provided the practical and moral center, while outside supporters supplied publicity, legal assistance, and money, and sometimes created disagreements about direction and tactics.

This chapter treats the early fish-ins as part of a longer defense of treaty rights, not as the beginning of it. The Frank family and other Nisqually and Puyallup fishers had been asserting their right for decades. What changed in the 1960s was the scale of publicity and the willingness of activists to link local fishing to a broader political argument about treaty obligations. The most intense confrontations, including the violent raids and the full chronology of the Fish Wars, occurred later and are discussed in Chapter 113, as is the federal court decision that transformed the legal landscape in 1974.

Section 106.16

Courtroom Conflict and the Limits of Legal Victories

While fishers confronted wardens on the river, treaty claims moved through the courts. The most important Supreme Court decision of the 1960s on Northwest fishing was Puyallup Tribe v. Department of Game (1968). The case arose from Washington State’s prohibition on net fishing in the Puyallup River, which the Puyallup Tribe argued violated the Treaty of Medicine Creek. The Washington Supreme Court had held that the state could regulate and prohibit net fishing for conservation. The United States Supreme Court reviewed that ruling and, in an opinion by Justice William O. Douglas, set out a more nuanced standard.

The Court acknowledged that the treaty right to fish “in common with” other citizens did not eliminate every possibility of state conservation regulation. Drawing on Tulee, it held that off-reservation treaty fishing could be subject to regulations of the time and manner of fishing that were necessary for conservation and applied without discrimination. Crucially, however, Puyallup I did not finally invalidate Washington’s prohibition of steelhead net fishing. The Court distinguished the general principle from the unresolved factual question of whether particular restrictions were necessary and nondiscriminatory, and it remanded the litigation for further proceedings. The precise effect of a ban on the tribe’s net fishery therefore remained contested after the 1968 ruling.

From the Puyallup Tribe’s perspective, the decision reaffirmed the continuing legal force of a treaty fishing right while leaving crucial questions unanswered. On remand, the Washington courts sustained a restriction on net fishing for steelhead, and the controversy returned to the U.S. Supreme Court. It was in Department of Game v. Puyallup Tribe, 414 U.S. 44 (1973), commonly called Puyallup II, that the Court rejected the complete prohibition on the treaty-protected steelhead net fishery as discriminatory and required accommodation with other fishing interests. The 1973 decision must not be attributed to the 1968 Court. This brief clarification provides necessary legal continuity; the larger regional Fish Wars and United States v. Washington (1974) remain assigned to Chapter 113.

The Puyallup decision illustrates a larger point about legal victories. A judicial principle does not enforce itself. Agencies had to adopt regulations consistent with it, wardens had to apply it, trial courts had to interpret it, and tribes had to monitor compliance and file new cases when it was not honored. Many Indigenous fishers continued to experience arrest and seizure after favorable rulings, and the costs of litigation were borne by tribes and individuals with few resources. The same was true elsewhere: the Seneca lost in court and gained compensation through Congress; the Colorado River tribes won recognition of water rights that required further investment and negotiation to use; Canadian hunters won a precedent in White and Bob but lost in other contexts. In each case, the formal legal outcome shaped the possibilities of action without determining them.

This is why litigation and organizing remained intertwined. Courtroom success depended on evidence, legal counsel, and a political environment in which officials took court decisions seriously. Organizing, including the fish-ins, brought public attention to enforcement practices and created pressure on governments to comply. Together they formed a pattern in which each strategy reinforced and sometimes compensated for the limits of the other.

Section 106.17

Regional Differences in the Struggle for Rights

The preceding sections have moved among several regions, and it is now necessary to ask what they share and where they differ. Treaty-rights struggles were not a single movement with a common program. They arose from different treaties, in different ecological settings, under different legal systems, and with different political resources. Comparing them helps explain why the same underlying question, how to honor a treaty when a government also regulates the resource, produced such varied conflicts.

In the Pacific Northwest, the central issue was access to a mobile, renewable resource, salmon and steelhead, that crossed jurisdictional lines and was harvested by several competing groups. The treaties used a distinctive formula, the right of taking fish at usual and accustomed places in common with other citizens, and the conflict concerned its meaning and the extent of state regulation. The conflict took the form of arrests, test cases, and fish-ins, and it was shaped by the existence of reservations that were small and by the fact that much fishing occurred outside them. Because the resource was shared, any recognition of Indigenous fishing implied a claim on other users, a feature that made the conflict politically intense.

The Seneca case concerned land, not a mobile resource. The 1794 treaty protected possession and use of specific territory, and the threat came from a federal infrastructure project that physically removed the land from use. The legal issue was the extent of Congress’s power to take land despite a treaty, and the political process centered on the federal government and Congress. Unlike the Northwest fishers, the Seneca had a formal constitutional government, the Seneca Nation, with a recognized relationship with the United States and a history of diplomacy reaching back to the eighteenth century. The Fort Berthold case shared the structure of a federal taking for a dam but involved different treaties and agreements and a different compensation history. The Colorado River case differed again. It involved the interpretation of rights implied by the creation of reservations, in a setting where the primary adversaries were states and non-Indian water users, and where the federal government’s role as both trustee and water developer was central.

In Canada, the legal setting changed the nature of the struggle. The numbered treaties and the Douglas treaties contained harvesting provisions, but the allocation of authority between Parliament and the provinces, the subordination of treaty rights to federal statutes in cases such as Sikyea, and the narrow food-harvesting guarantee of the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements shaped the arguments. First Nations had fewer formal political tools than their counterparts in the United States, and treaty rights were most often asserted defensively in criminal prosecutions. Great Lakes Anishinaabe communities, whose territories and treaties straddled the Canada–United States border, experienced both systems, a reminder that Indigenous geography did not follow national boundaries, as Chapter 1 emphasized.

Despite these differences, patterns recur. In every region, governments justified restrictions on treaty rights by appeal to a public interest, whether conservation, flood control, irrigation, or provincial wildlife management. In every region, Indigenous nations insisted that the treaties were binding commitments and that governments were bound to protect them. And in every region, courts played an important but limited role, recognizing rights in principle while leaving the practical conditions of their exercise to other branches of government. These shared features explain why Indigenous leaders in different regions increasingly saw their struggles as related, even when the legal details differed.

Section 106.18

Families, Women, Elders, and the Social Meaning of Treaty Rights

Public accounts of treaty-rights conflict often focus on court cases and confrontations, which can obscure the social world that sustained the rights themselves. A treaty right was not exercised by an abstract rights-holder. It was exercised through households and kin networks that organized labor, shared food, transmitted knowledge, and bore the consequences of enforcement. Understanding this social dimension helps explain why the defense of treaty rights mattered so deeply to communities and why it endured despite hardship.

Fishing, hunting, and gathering required coordinated work. In fishing communities, men often handled boats and nets, while women commonly processed, smoked, dried, and preserved fish and managed distribution within the household and community, though practices varied among nations and families and the evidence does not support a single model. In gathering economies, women’s knowledge of plants, their habitats, and their seasonal availability was central, and in many northern and prairie communities women worked in trapping economies, preparing hides and furs and supporting household production. These tasks were not peripheral. The food and income they generated often determined whether a family could remain on its homeland.

Elders played a central role as holders of knowledge and memory. They knew where fish ran, which sites belonged to which families, how treaty negotiations were remembered in the community, and what protocols governed harvest and sharing. Their testimony was also important to legal efforts, since claims about usual and accustomed places or historical use often depended on community memory as well as documents. When elders spoke at hearings, assisted researchers, or taught younger relatives, they linked the legal defense of rights to the cultural continuity that the rights protected.

Women also played prominent roles in the political defense of treaty rights. In the Northwest, women such as Janet McCloud and Maiselle Bridges were among those who organized support for fishing-rights activism, provided food and shelter for gatherings, and participated in protests, and their work reflects a broader pattern in which women’s labor sustained activism even where public leadership was more often attributed to men. Historians have only begun to document these contributions fully, because the written record of the period often privileged officials and male spokespersons. Oral histories and community memory are therefore important sources for recovering the roles of women and of intergenerational networks.

For families, the consequences of conflict were intensely personal. A seized net could mean the loss of a season’s income; an arrest could separate a wage earner from the household; displacement from a flooded valley could scatter kin who had lived near one another for generations. The defense of treaty rights was therefore bound up with questions of survival, dignity, and continuity. It was also bound up with the question of whether younger people would be able to remain in their communities, learn from elders, and carry on practices that gave their nations their distinctive character, an issue that connects directly to the urbanization and relocation processes discussed in Chapter 105.

Everyday Life and Social History

Treaty Rights in the Household

A treaty right is easy to imagine as a sentence in a legal document. In daily life, it took the form of work. Families prepared nets and boats before the runs, waited for the tides and seasons, cleaned and preserved the catch, and distributed fish to relatives, neighbors, and elders who could not fish themselves. Food sharing reinforced kinship ties and community obligations, and fish provided meals as well as cash from sales. The knowledge required to do this well was learned by participation, and children often began helping at a young age.

The same everyday logic applied to hunting, gathering, and farming. Seneca families on the Allegany Reservation farmed river bottomland and held ceremonies in the longhouse; Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara families grew gardens and cut timber in the Missouri valley; First Nations families in the north depended on moose, caribou, and fur-bearing animals for food and income. When governments restricted or removed these resources, they interfered with household economies, education, and ceremonial life all at once. Historians reconstruct these experiences from oral histories, memoirs, agency reports, and tribal records, remembering that official sources often describe households from the outside.

Section 106.19

The Historical Debate over Treaty Obligations and Public Regulation

The conflicts examined in this chapter raise an enduring question about how governments reconcile treaty commitments with their responsibility to regulate resources and plan development for the public. Four sets of arguments competed in the period, and historians evaluate them by examining what each claimed, what evidence supported it, and whose interests it served.

State conservation arguments held that fish and wildlife were public resources that had to be managed by a single authority for the benefit of all citizens, and that regulations must apply to everyone to be effective. This argument had real force. Many fish populations were declining, and unregulated harvest could contribute to the problem. Yet it also had weaknesses. State management systems were shaped by commercial and sport interests, often did not address the habitat destruction that did much of the damage, and had a record of treating Indigenous gear and practices as illegitimate. The argument also assumed that conservation could be separated from allocation, when in practice decisions about who could fish and how were decisions about who would receive a share of the resource.

Federal development arguments held that large projects such as the Kinzua and Garrison dams served national purposes, including flood control, hydroelectric power, and regional economic growth, and that these purposes justified the use of eminent domain. These arguments reflected a postwar belief in large-scale engineering as a source of public benefit. Their weakness was that costs and benefits were unevenly distributed. Indigenous communities bore a disproportionate share of the burden, often without consultation, while the benefits flowed to regions and populations far away. Federal agencies also had a legal obligation of trust toward tribes, which was in tension with their promotion of projects that harmed tribal interests.

Tribal sovereignty arguments held that Indigenous nations retained inherent powers, including the power to regulate their own members’ harvest and to protect their territories, and that treaties confirmed rather than created these powers. Treaty-based arguments held that treaties were binding agreements whose terms governments were obliged to honor, and that the courts had recognized this principle in cases such as Winans. These arguments were supported by the text of the treaties, by judicial precedent, and by Indigenous testimony about how the agreements were understood, though their practical force was limited by Congress’s claimed power to override treaties and by the lack of effective enforcement.

Historians do not treat these arguments as equivalent merely because they were competing. The treaties were legally binding agreements, recognized as such by the Constitution and by courts, while conservation and development arguments were policy justifications that governments advanced for particular actions. A conservation regulation might be legitimate if it met the standard of necessity articulated in Tulee and Puyallup, but the fact that a regulation served a public purpose did not automatically make it consistent with a treaty. At the same time, historians recognize that real ecological problems existed and that sincere officials sought to address them. The analytical task is to examine the evidence for each claim and to ask whether governments applied the burden of conservation or development equitably.

Historical Debate

Treaty Obligations and Public Regulation: Where Should the Burden Fall?

The question: When public purposes such as conservation, flood control, or irrigation conflicted with treaty rights, should governments have treated the treaty as a limit on their power, or as one consideration to be balanced against the broader public interest?

Interpretation A — Treaties as binding limits: Treaties were agreements between sovereign parties, and a government that benefited from the land cessions they contained was obliged to honor the rights the Indigenous parties retained. Public purposes could justify regulation only within the limits the treaties allowed.
Interpretation B — Treaties within the public interest framework: Some officials and jurists argued that governments had to be able to adapt to changing circumstances, including population growth and resource depletion, and that treaty rights, like other rights, were subject to reasonable regulation and, in the case of Congress, to modification.

Evidence: Treaty texts, congressional statutes, court decisions, agency reports, and testimony all inform the debate. The legal record supports both that treaties were binding and that Congress claimed authority to override them, as Lone Wolf and the Seneca litigation illustrate.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and burden of proof more than chronology: who must justify a restriction, what counts as necessity, and whether compensation can substitute for a protected right.

Why it matters: How historians understand this debate affects whether they describe postwar developments as the exercise of lawful public authority, as breaches of treaty obligations, or as both, depending on the case. It also affects how they interpret the later growth of co-management and consultation.

Section 106.20

Treaty-Rights Activism and the Foundations of a New Political Era

By the late 1960s, the struggle over treaty rights had produced a body of results that were partial but significant. The courts had affirmed that treaty fishing rights could be exercised off reservation, that states could not impose license fees on treaty fishing, and that conservation regulation had to be reasonable, necessary, and nondiscriminatory. They had confirmed the existence of reserved water rights for the Colorado River reservations and recognized treaty hunting protections in particular cases. At the same time, governments had flooded Seneca and Fort Berthold land, closed Celilo Falls under the waters of The Dalles Dam, and continued to enforce restrictive regulations. Legal victories existed, but they had not secured practical protection, and the costs of obtaining them had been borne largely by the nations themselves.

These experiences contributed to a growing understanding among Indigenous leaders that the defense of treaty rights required sustained political action as well as litigation. Activists in the Northwest, Seneca leaders, tribal officials from the Plains and the Southwest, and First Nations leaders in Canada were beginning to recognize that they faced similar problems and could learn from each other. Intertribal organizations provided venues for sharing information, and the publicity surrounding the fish-ins and the Kinzua and Garrison dams brought the issue to a broader public. The experience of arrest, litigation, and negotiation also produced a cohort of leaders and advisers, including Billy Frank Jr. and Hank Adams, who would play important roles in later movements.

It is important not to overstate the connection. Treaty-rights activism was not merely a prelude to Red Power, and it should not be read as an inevitable step toward it. It had its own origins in treaty relationships and community practices, its own legal traditions, and its own institutions, and it continued to develop in directions that did not always align with the later national movement. Nor was it simply a response to termination and relocation, although those policies increased the urgency of the defense of treaty rights by suggesting that the federal government was moving away from its obligations. The activism of the 1950s and 1960s was shaped by a combination of long-standing treaty commitments, postwar development, state regulation, and the initiative of Indigenous communities.

The chapter has also shown that the struggle over treaty rights was a struggle over political authority. Whether a state could license treaty fishing, whether Congress could override a treaty to build a dam, whether a reservation carried a right to water, and whether a provincial wildlife law could apply to a treaty hunter were all questions about who had power to define and limit Indigenous rights. Indigenous nations answered that the power to define those rights lay in the treaties themselves and in their own sovereignty. This claim would become more prominent in the political movements of the next decade, and it connects the legal arguments of the 1950s and 1960s to the demands for self-determination that characterize the later period.

Chapter 107 turns to the national Indigenous organizations that provided institutional frameworks for intertribal cooperation, and Chapter 108 examines the beginnings of Red Power as a broader political movement. The fuller story of the Pacific Northwest Fish Wars and the 1974 Boldt decision is the subject of Chapter 113, and the later development of water rights is addressed in Chapter 120. This chapter ends at the threshold of those developments, with the recognition that Indigenous political life in the postwar decades was already vigorous, organized, and engaged in the defense of treaty obligations before the better-known mobilizations of the late 1960s and 1970s took shape.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that treaty rights were continuing legal and political obligations, not historical privileges, and that Indigenous nations defended them actively between the 1940s and the late 1960s. Treaties were agreements between political communities, and under the reserved-rights reasoning developed in United States v. Winans, rights that a nation did not give up remained with it. That reasoning explains how a right to fish, hunt, or use water could survive large land cessions.

The legal foundations were laid in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The Stevens treaties of 1854–1856 secured fishing at usual and accustomed places for many Northwest nations. Winans (1905) confirmed off-reservation access, Winters (1908) extended reserved-rights reasoning to water, and Tulee (1942) rejected a state license fee while recognizing a limited conservation power. These decisions were important but left open the meaning of “in common with,” the scope of state regulation, and the practical question of enforcement.

Postwar development placed new pressure on these rights. Dams, expanded commercial and sport fisheries, urban and industrial growth, and more elaborate state regulation intensified older disputes. Federal Indian policy, including the Indian Claims Commission and the termination policy, reinforced the impression that treaty relationships were diminishing. Authority was divided among federal, state, provincial, and tribal governments, so that Indigenous nations often confronted several governments with competing objectives, and the federal government was at once trustee, developer, and litigator.

Harvesting rights were defended in several settings. In the Pacific Northwest, states restricted Indigenous net fishing in the name of conservation, and fishers faced arrests and seizures. Hunting, trapping, and gathering rights were also contested, as in the Nez Perce and Menominee cases, though the rights varied by treaty and region. In Canada, the numbered treaties, the Douglas treaties, and the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 produced a distinct framework in which treaty harvesting could prevail against provincial law, as in White and Bob, but yield to federal statutes, as in Sikyea.

Land and water cases showed that treaty rights could be burdened without being formally abolished. The Seneca Nation lost much of the Allegany Reservation to the Kinzua Dam despite the Treaty of Canandaigua, because courts held that Congress could authorize the taking. The Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara lost their river bottomlands to the Garrison Dam under the Pick–Sloan program. On the Colorado River, Arizona v. California confirmed reserved water rights for five reservations, a major legal success whose practical value depended on further development.

Environmental change complicated every legal question. The flooding of Celilo Falls in 1957 and the broader transformation of the Columbia River showed that a right to fish could remain legally intact while the fishery itself was destroyed. This insight tied the defense of treaty rights to questions of habitat, management authority, and sovereignty.

Indigenous resistance took many forms. Everyday practice, in which families kept fishing, hunting, and gathering, was both ordinary and political. Tribal governments used councils, petitions, hearings, and attorneys, while regional organizations and the Indian Claims Commission process built expertise. In the 1960s, the fish-ins at Frank’s Landing and elsewhere added direct action, supported by Billy Frank Jr., Hank Adams, Janet McCloud, and others, though not all tribal officials agreed with this approach.

The legal record of the decade, culminating in Puyallup Tribe v. Department of Game (1968), showed both the possibilities and limits of litigation. The Court recognized state conservation power but bounded it with requirements of reasonableness, necessity, and nondiscrimination, and it left key questions to later cases. A favorable principle did not enforce itself, so litigation and organizing remained intertwined.

Regional comparison revealed shared patterns beneath different circumstances: governments justified restrictions by appeal to public interest, Indigenous nations insisted on treaty obligations, and courts recognized rights without ensuring their exercise. Families, women, and elders sustained treaty rights through labor, knowledge, and organization. Historians evaluating the debate between treaty obligations and public regulation do not treat the arguments as equivalent, since treaties were binding legal agreements, while recognizing that real conservation and development concerns existed.

The chapter concludes that treaty-rights activism was an established part of Indigenous political life before the national mobilizations of the late 1960s and 1970s. It had its own foundations, institutions, and leaders, and it contributed to the political environment examined in Chapters 107 and 108 without being reducible to them.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Treaty right
A legal entitlement created or confirmed by a treaty between an Indigenous nation and a colonial or settler government.
Reserved-rights doctrine
The principle that a treaty is a grant of rights from the Indigenous nation to the government, so that rights not surrendered remain with the nation.
Usual and accustomed places
Treaty phrase protecting fishing at sites a nation had traditionally used, including places beyond reservation boundaries.
In common with
Treaty phrase giving Indigenous fishers access alongside other citizens, interpreted differently by states and tribes.
Usufructuary right
A right to use and take the produce of land or waters without owning them.
Abrogation
Action by Congress or another government that ends or overrides a treaty provision.
Plenary power
The broad authority over Indian affairs claimed by Congress, notably in Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903).
Canons of treaty construction
Interpretive rules under which ambiguous treaty terms are read as the Indigenous signatories would have understood them.
Conservation necessity
Standard permitting limited state regulation of treaty harvest only when reasonable, necessary to protect the resource, and nondiscriminatory.
Trust responsibility
The federal obligation, arising from treaties, statutes, and case law, to protect tribal lands, resources, and rights.
Public Law 280
A 1953 statute extending some state civil and criminal jurisdiction over Indian country, with a savings clause for treaty hunting and fishing rights.
Termination
The federal policy of the 1940s–1960s of ending recognition of certain tribes’ special federal status, treated in Chapter 104.
Indian Claims Commission
A U.S. body established in 1946 to hear tribal claims against the United States, generally awarding compensation rather than land.
Eminent domain / condemnation
The power of a government to take private or tribal property for public use, normally with compensation.
Treaty of Canandaigua
1794 treaty between the United States and the Six Nations acknowledging Haudenosaunee lands, cited by the Seneca against the Kinzua Dam.
Stevens treaties
Treaties negotiated in 1854–1856 by Isaac Stevens in Washington Territory and neighboring regions, most containing fishing clauses.
Pick–Sloan Plan
The Missouri River basin program authorized by the Flood Control Act of 1944, combining Army Corps and Bureau of Reclamation proposals.
Winters doctrine
The principle from Winters v. United States (1908) that creating a reservation impliedly reserves enough water to fulfill its purposes.
Prior appropriation
Western water law system in which the earliest beneficial user holds priority over later users.
Practicably irrigable acreage
Standard used in Arizona v. California to measure reserved water by the reservation’s irrigable land.
Numbered treaties
Eleven treaties between the Crown and First Nations concluded between 1871 and 1921.
Douglas treaties
Agreements made in the early 1850s between the Crown colony of Vancouver Island and several Indigenous communities.
Natural Resources Transfer Agreements
1930 agreements transferring Crown resources to Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta while preserving Indian hunting, trapping, and fishing for food.
Food-harvesting right
The right, as stated in the 1930 agreements, to hunt, trap, and fish for food on unoccupied Crown lands and other lands to which Indians had access.
Fish-in
A public act of fishing in violation of state regulations, undertaken to assert treaty rights and test their enforcement.
Survival of American Indians Association
Organization formed in 1964 that supported fishing-rights activism in Washington State.
Frank’s Landing
Site on the Nisqually River associated with the Frank family and the first organized fish-in in 1964.
Federal supremacy
The constitutional principle that treaties are the supreme law of the land, placing them above conflicting state law.
Co-management
An arrangement in which tribal and government agencies jointly manage a resource, developed more fully after the period of this chapter.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that Indigenous political life in the postwar decades was not defined only by the federal policies of termination and relocation. Alongside those policies, Indigenous nations were defending agreements that predated them, using methods that ranged from everyday fishing to Supreme Court litigation. The treaties at issue were not relics. They were living claims about who had the right to govern rivers, fish, game, land, and water.

The chapter also reveals a persistent feature of settler-state history: the gap between recognition and enforcement. Governments acknowledged that treaties were binding while reserving powers that could undermine them, and courts affirmed rights while leaving the conditions of their exercise to agencies with other priorities. Understanding this gap helps explain why Indigenous leaders increasingly turned to organized political action, and why questions about management authority, habitat, and consultation became central to later struggles.

Many of the themes introduced here continue in later chapters. The fisheries conflicts of the 1960s lead to the Fish Wars and the federal court decision of 1974 in Chapter 113. The water-rights principle confirmed in 1963 becomes the foundation for the extensive claims and settlements of Chapter 120. The experience of displacement and legal struggle also informs the organizational developments of Chapter 107 and the political movements of Chapter 108, as well as the later sovereignty-era chapters in Volume X.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook’s larger argument that Indigenous nations are continuing political communities. Treaty-rights activism was an assertion of that continuity: a claim that agreements made in the nineteenth century, or the eighteenth, still bound governments in the twentieth. The question with which the chapter opened, what a treaty guarantee means when the government responsible for it also regulates the resource, was not settled in these years. It remained a central question of Indigenous history and of the history of North America.
