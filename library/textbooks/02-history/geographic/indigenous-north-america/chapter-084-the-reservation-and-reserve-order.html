Chapter Opening

A treaty can be read in an afternoon, and a survey plan can be taken in at a glance. Yet the lines drawn in such documents shaped where Indigenous families could live, what they could eat, whom they could visit, and who could speak for them for generations. This chapter is about what happened after the lines were drawn.

Two kinds of documents stand at the center of this chapter. The first is a treaty or statute: a text, negotiated or imposed, declaring that a defined area of land is set apart for the use of a particular Indigenous nation. The second is a survey plan: a map, produced by government surveyors, converting a homeland known through rivers, hunting circuits, seasonal camps, and kinship ties into a bounded parcel with measured edges. Taken together, these documents mark the moment when governments in the United States and Canada began to treat Indigenous territory as something that could be listed, divided, and administered. The result was the American Indian reservation and the Canadian First Nation reserve.

It would be easy to read these institutions as the simple aftermath of military defeat, the place where Indigenous peoples were sent when the wars described in Chapters 73 through 80 ended. This chapter argues that such a reading is incomplete. Many reservations and reserves were created through negotiation, and many Indigenous leaders worked deliberately to secure land that would remain theirs. Others were created through coercion, relocation, or the withholding of food. In every case the creation of a boundary was only the first step. What followed was a continuing system of administration through which federal governments tried to regulate land use, food supply, movement, work, law, schooling, religion, and political authority.

The central claim of this chapter is that reservations and reserves performed two functions that were often in tension. On one hand, they represented land that governments had promised, in treaties and statutes, to set aside for Indigenous communities. Indigenous leaders understood those promises as obligations and invoked them repeatedly. On the other hand, the same lands became instruments through which governments restricted mobility, controlled resources, supervised households, and asserted administrative authority over peoples who had recently governed themselves. This dual character, a promise of land and a mechanism of control, runs through the whole chapter and explains why historians continue to disagree about how these institutions should be characterized.

The chapter is comparative. The United States and Canada developed different legal frameworks, different administrative structures, and different treaty histories, even though officials in the two countries watched each other, shared assumptions about "civilization" and settlement, and dealt with Indigenous peoples whose homelands straddled the international boundary. American reservations were created by treaty, statute, and executive order, and American law developed the concept of a federal trust relationship with tribal nations. Canadian reserves were generally lands set apart by treaty or other Crown processes and held by the Crown for the use and benefit of a First Nation, under the federal authority conferred in 1867. Students should resist treating the two systems as interchangeable, and should equally resist treating either as uniform within its own borders.

The chronological focus is roughly 1850 to 1900. The period opens with the Robinson Treaties in what is now Ontario and the first Fort Laramie Treaty on the northern Plains. It runs through Canadian Confederation, the end of formal treaty-making in the United States, the negotiation of Canada's Numbered Treaties, the Canadian pass system after the North-West Resistance of 1885, the Dawes Act of 1887, and Canada's peasant-farming policy of 1889. It closes in the 1890s, when land reduction, administrative intervention, and Indigenous political advocacy were all intensifying. Later reversals and modern legal consequences appear in later chapters, and this chapter touches them only where necessary.

The evidence for this history is unusually uneven. Treaty texts, statutes, departmental regulations, annual reports, agents' correspondence, and survey plans survive in abundance, but they were produced by the governments doing the administering. Indigenous perspectives survive in recorded council speeches, petitions, delegation testimony, oral histories, and the scholarship of Indigenous communities and historians, but often filtered through interpreters and clerks. This chapter therefore pays continuous attention to who produced each source and what it cannot tell us.

This chapter builds on Chapter 81, which traced the destruction of the buffalo economy, Chapter 82, which examined Canada's Numbered Treaties, and Chapter 83, which examined the Red River and North-West Resistances. It prepares the ground for Chapter 85, which examines the Indian Act and the statutory machinery of Canadian colonial administration. Its contribution to Volume VII is to show that conquest was not completed when treaties were signed or wars ended. It continued through the daily operation of institutions that regulated land, food, movement, work, and authority, and it was met, throughout, by Indigenous communities that defended their land, kept their governments and ceremonies alive, found new livelihoods, and challenged what governments did.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the American Indian reservation from the Canadian First Nation reserve in terms of legal origin, land tenure, and administrative structure, and explain why neither was identical to a nation's traditional homeland.
  2. Trace the development of reservation policy in the United States from removal through the treaties of the 1850s and 1860s, the Peace Policy, and the end of formal treaty-making in 1871.
  3. Explain how Canadian reserves emerged from the Robinson Treaties, Confederation, the acquisition of Rupert's Land, and the Numbered Treaties, and how survey and land-selection processes translated treaty promises into reserve geography.
  4. Analyze the roles of Indian agents, departmental officials, the United States Army, and the North-West Mounted Police in administering and enforcing the two systems.
  5. Assess how the loss of independent subsistence and the use of rations created administrative dependency, and how officials sometimes used food for political and territorial ends.
  6. Evaluate government farming and economic programs, and explain how Indigenous initiative in agriculture, ranching, trade, and wage labor complicates the idea that economic change was merely imposed.
  7. Describe the Canadian pass system, explain why it lacked general statutory authority, and distinguish it from American mobility restrictions and from specific legal provisions.
  8. Compare the Dawes Act of 1887 with Canada's peasant-farming policy of 1889, identifying shared assumptions without conflating two different land-tenure and administrative programs.
  9. Interpret how governments tried to reshape Indigenous leadership, law, religion, and schooling, and how Indigenous communities preserved governance and cultural practice.
  10. Explain how reservation and reserve boundaries remained vulnerable to reduction and encroachment after their creation, using the Great Sioux Reservation and British Columbia as case studies.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of reservations and reserves as protected homelands, instruments of colonial control, or institutions that performed both functions under unequal conditions.
  12. Analyze primary sources, including treaties, statutes, administrative correspondence, survey plans, and recorded Indigenous statements, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did governments transform extensive Indigenous homelands into bounded, administered parcels, and what was gained and lost for Indigenous nations in that transformation?
  2. In what ways did the legal foundations of American reservations and Canadian reserves differ, and how did those differences shape administration?
  3. Why is it historically misleading to treat reservations and reserves only as the outcome of military defeat?

  6. How did Indigenous leaders and councils respond to administrative interference, and what strategies did they use to keep political authority alive?

  8. Why were reservation and reserve boundaries so vulnerable to later reduction, and who benefited from that vulnerability?
  9. How should historians weigh the protective and controlling functions of reservations and reserves against each other, and what evidence bears on the question?

Historical Context

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the governments that would build the reservation and reserve systems already had long experience of treating Indigenous peoples as political entities with distinct territorial claims. In British North America, the Royal Proclamation of 1763 had declared that Indigenous lands not ceded to or purchased by the Crown were reserved for the peoples who lived on them, and that private individuals could not buy them directly. The Proclamation became a foundational reference point for Crown-Indigenous relations, even though its application varied widely. The Indian Department that administered relations with Indigenous allies originated as a military and diplomatic institution of the British Empire, and it was transferred to the control of the Province of Canada in 1860, a step that began to shift its character from alliance management toward the administration of settled communities.

In the United States, the framework had developed through treaty-making, the Trade and Intercourse Acts, and the removal policy examined in Chapters 65 and 66. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the removals of the 1830s and 1840s had moved many southeastern nations west of the Mississippi and established the idea of a "permanent Indian frontier." That idea collapsed quickly. The annexation of Texas, the Oregon migration, the war with Mexico described in Chapter 69, the California gold rush described in Chapter 70, and the growth of overland trails meant that by 1850 the lands once imagined as a permanent Indigenous domain were crossed by settlers and soldiers. The Supreme Court's decisions of the early 1830s had also described tribal nations as "domestic dependent nations" holding a relationship to the federal government resembling that of a ward to a guardian, a formulation that later underpinned the doctrine of federal trust responsibility.

The environmental background mattered as much as the legal one. Across the Plains, Indigenous economies relied heavily on bison, and the contraction of the herds between the 1860s and the early 1880s, treated in Chapter 81, removed the material basis for mobile hunting societies. The southern herd collapsed first, and the northern herd followed within a few years. The timing varied by region, and so did the severity of its effects, but its combined consequence was that governments could increasingly offer food as a substitute for subsistence that Indigenous peoples could no longer secure independently. That shift made administrative control of food one of the most powerful tools of the period.

Settler expansion supplied the political pressure. Railways, mining frontiers, ranching, and homesteading all demanded land that Indigenous peoples occupied or used. In the United States, the Pacific railway projects and the Homestead Act of 1862 encouraged western settlement. In Canada, the acquisition of Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory in 1870 and the decision to build a transcontinental railway made the prairies a national project, and the Dominion government sought treaties that would clear title for agricultural settlement. Governments in both countries described these aims in the language of progress and "civilization," and often described reservations and reserves as stages in a transition from hunting to farming, from mobility to settlement, and from tribal government to individual citizenship.

Students should keep several cautions in mind. First, the reservation and reserve systems did not appear all at once. They developed through overlapping decisions, over decades, by different officials with different priorities. Second, they did not operate uniformly: a Pueblo village on lands confirmed under earlier Spanish and Mexican grants, a Cree community on a treaty reserve in Saskatchewan, and a Coast Salish community in British Columbia dealt with very different legal arrangements. Third, many Indigenous peoples lived outside reservations and reserves altogether, or within arrangements that bore little resemblance to the Plains model. And fourth, Indigenous nations were not passive in the face of these changes. They negotiated treaty terms, selected reserve sites, adapted economically, petitioned governments, and kept their own institutions alive under pressure.

Division I · Section 84.1

Indigenous Homelands, Territorial Sovereignty, and the Meaning of Reservation and Reserve

Indigenous nations occupied, governed, and maintained relationships with their territories long before any government designated parcels of land as reservations or reserves. A homeland was not simply an area on a map. It was a set of relationships among a people, the places they used across the seasons, the other peoples with whom they shared, contested, or traded access to resources, and the spiritual and historical meanings attached to particular landscapes. Lakota bands moved between river valleys, the Black Hills, and bison ranges according to season and opportunity. Cree and Saulteaux communities of the parkland and plains combined bison hunting, fishing, gathering, and trade across a wide region. Diné families moved between farming sites, grazing areas, and ceremonial landmarks in the Southwest. In none of these cases did political authority depend on a surveyed boundary, and in many cases territories overlapped or were shared by agreement.

Indigenous political authority over territory was exercised through institutions that differed enormously from one nation to another: councils of headmen, hereditary or achieved leadership, military or warrior societies that regulated hunts, clan and kin systems that allocated use rights, and diplomatic relationships with neighbors. Boundaries existed, but they were often understood as zones of use and relationship rather than fixed lines, and they could shift through alliance, warfare, or negotiation. When governments proposed treaties that described territory through surveyed boundaries and fixed acreage, they were asking Indigenous nations to translate one understanding of land into another. That translation is itself a historical problem, because Indigenous negotiators and government commissioners did not always mean the same thing by the same words.

The words "reservation" and "reserve" came to describe the outcome of that translation, but they described different legal creations. In the United States, a reservation is land set apart for Indigenous nations under federal law. Reservations were established through treaties, through acts of Congress, through executive orders issued by presidents, and through other federal actions. Many treaty-based reservations were understood by Indigenous negotiators, and later by courts, as portions of larger territories that the nation had retained rather than land the government had granted, a distinction that shaped later arguments about rights reserved in treaties. Title to many reservation lands was held by the United States in trust for the tribe, while other lands came to be held in individual allotments, and some tracts passed out of Indigenous ownership altogether.

In Canada, a reserve is land set apart for the use and benefit of a First Nation. Reserves arose through treaties, Crown decisions, colonial and provincial measures, and administrative processes, and by the time of the Indian Act of 1876 the legal definition treated a reserve as a tract set apart by treaty or otherwise for a band's use and benefit and vested in the Crown. The Constitution Act of 1867 (then the British North America Act) had given the federal Parliament exclusive legislative authority over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians," so that reserves were federal responsibilities even where provinces controlled other Crown lands. As a result, in Canada the reserve was typically Crown land held for a band, while in the United States a reservation could include tribal trust land, allotted land, and, later, land owned in fee by non-Indigenous people within reservation boundaries.

A reservation or reserve was also not necessarily identical to a nation's entire traditional homeland. In most cases it was a fraction. A Lakota nation that had once used a vast area of the northern Plains found itself, after 1868, associated with a large but defined reservation and a separate unceded hunting territory, and within two decades with several smaller reservations. A Cree community that had ranged across hundreds of kilometers might be allotted a reserve measured in square miles. Some communities, like the Diné after 1868, returned to a portion of their homeland. Others were relocated to territories far from their origins. Still others were never given reserves at all. The relationship between homeland and reserved land therefore has to be established case by case.

Historical Vocabulary
Reservation, Reserve, Homeland, and Federal Trust Responsibility

Reservation (United States) refers to land set apart by treaty, statute, or executive order for the use of an Indigenous nation or nations. Reservations varied greatly in size, legal history, and land tenure. The word "reserved" in treaty language sometimes signaled that the nation was retaining part of its former territory.

Reserve (Canada) refers to land set apart for the use and benefit of a First Nation, generally held by the Crown. The term appears in treaties, in colonial and federal legislation, and in the Indian Act. It is the standard Canadian term; "reservation" is not the Canadian legal term.

Homeland is a historical and cultural term, not a legal one. It describes the territory with which a people has long-standing relationships of use, governance, and meaning. Its extent is usually larger than any reservation or reserve, and may be documented through oral tradition, archaeology, treaties, and records of seasonal use.

Federal trust responsibility is a doctrine in American law holding that the United States has obligations of protection toward tribal nations and their lands, arising from treaties, statutes, and the course of dealing between the government and tribes. Its meaning and enforceability have been debated. Potential misunderstanding: students should not assume that "trust" meant that governments consistently acted in Indigenous interests; the doctrine has often coexisted with policies that reduced Indigenous land and authority. Canada developed a different vocabulary built around the honour of the Crown, treaty obligations, and fiduciary duty, concepts elaborated mostly in the twentieth century and later.

Division I · Section 84.2

The United States Reservation System: Removal, Treaty-Making, and Concentration

The American reservation system grew out of earlier federal practices, particularly removal. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 had authorized the exchange of eastern lands for territory west of the Mississippi, and the removals of the 1830s examined in Chapters 65 and 66 established a model in which the federal government relocated entire nations to designated lands under the supervision of federal agents. The agency that oversaw these relations, the Office of Indian Affairs, had originated in the War Department and was transferred to the new Department of the Interior in 1849. That transfer symbolized a gradual shift from treating Indigenous nations primarily as diplomatic and military counterparts toward treating them as populations to be administered, though the Army remained deeply involved in Indian affairs for decades.

The idea of a "permanent Indian frontier" west of the settled states did not survive the 1840s. Overland migration to Oregon and California, the acquisition of vast territories after the war with Mexico, and the gold rush meant that the lands set aside for removed nations were crossed by wagon roads and eyed by settlers. By the early 1850s federal officials, including Commissioner of Indian Affairs Luke Lea, were advocating the concentration of Indigenous peoples on limited, defined tracts. The logic was practical and ideological at once: smaller reservations would free land for settlement and transport corridors, would make Indigenous populations easier to supervise, and, officials argued, would allow Indigenous people to learn farming and "civilization" under federal tutelage.

Treaty-making in the 1850s and 1860s reflected these changing aims. The first Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851, negotiated at Horse Creek with Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, and other nations, did not create reservations in the later sense. It defined the territories of the signatory nations, secured safe passage for emigrants on the Platte River road, authorized forts, and promised annuities. The Senate, in ratifying it, reduced the promised duration of annuities from fifty years to ten, with the possibility of a five-year extension. A decade and a half later the situation had changed. After conflicts in Minnesota, Colorado, and Wyoming, treated in Chapters 73 through 75, Congress established an Indian Peace Commission in 1867, whose members negotiated treaties intended to locate nations on defined reservations. The Medicine Lodge treaties of October 1867 with the Kiowa, Comanche, Plains Apache, Southern Cheyenne, and Arapaho created reservations in Indian Territory, while preserving hunting rights south of the Arkansas River for as long as bison remained. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, examined in Section 84.5, applied the same approach to the northern Plains.

President Ulysses S. Grant's Peace Policy, announced at the beginning of his administration in 1869, added new institutions and a new moral vocabulary. Congress created the Board of Indian Commissioners in 1869, a body of civilian philanthropists to oversee Indian affairs. In 1870 Congress barred active-duty army officers from holding civil offices, and the administration began allowing Christian denominations to nominate agents for particular agencies. Quakers were the best-known participants, but Methodists, Episcopalians, Presbyterians, Catholics, and others also received agencies, and disputes over the allocation among denominations were frequent. The policy's stated aim was to replace corruption with moral oversight, to pacify Indigenous nations through humane treatment, and to hasten their assimilation through farming, schooling, and Christianity. Reservation administration was thus joined to missionary purpose, and the line between government function and church activity was blurred.

The Peace Policy did not end violence. The Marias River massacre of Piegan Blackfeet in Montana in January 1870 occurred in its first year, and warfare continued through the 1870s, culminating in the conflicts described in Chapters 77 through 80. Nor did church nomination eliminate disputes within agencies or between denominations and the government, and by the late 1870s the arrangement had largely faded. But its legacy endured. It established the expectation that reservation administration should pursue cultural transformation as well as supervision, and it deepened the involvement of missionaries and reform organizations in Indian policy.

In March 1871, Congress added a provision to an Indian appropriations act declaring that no Indigenous nation or tribe within the United States would thereafter be acknowledged as an independent nation with which the United States could contract by treaty, while expressly preserving obligations under treaties already ratified. The immediate impetus was a struggle between the House of Representatives, which had no role in treaty ratification, and the Senate, which did. The consequences were profound but often misunderstood. The provision did not abrogate existing treaties, and the United States continued to negotiate "agreements" with Indigenous nations that were ratified by both houses of Congress, including the 1877 Black Hills agreement discussed in Section 84.17. But it did signal a shift toward governing Indigenous affairs by statute and administrative regulation rather than diplomacy between nations, a shift reinforced by later legislation and by Supreme Court decisions asserting broad federal power over tribal affairs.

Reservation administration thus became a central component of American federal Indian policy. By the 1870s, the reservation was no longer simply a place where treaty obligations were fulfilled. It was the principal institutional setting through which the federal government pursued pacification, settlement, and assimilation, three objectives that were compatible in some respects and contradictory in others. Pacification could be served by feeding people and avoiding confrontation. Settlement required that Indigenous people be kept out of the way of expanding communities. Assimilation required intrusive intervention in households, religion, and governance. Officials and reformers weighed these aims differently, and Indigenous communities experienced the resulting contradictions directly.

Division I · Section 84.3

The Canadian Reserve System: Crown Policy, Treaties, and Confederation

Canadian reserves developed along a different path. Before Confederation, colonial governments in the territories that became Canada set aside lands for Indigenous communities through a variety of measures. In Upper and Lower Canada, reserves existed from the French and British periods onward, and an 1851 statute in the Province of Canada authorized the setting apart of tracts for Indigenous people in Lower Canada. In the Maritimes, colonial administrations assigned small tracts and appointed commissioners to oversee them. On Vancouver Island, Governor James Douglas negotiated a series of agreements between 1850 and 1854 with Indigenous communities, but the process ended before most of the colony was covered, and in the following decades British Columbia developed a policy of reserve allocation without comprehensive treaties.

The Robinson Treaties of 1850 mark a turning point in the development of the reserve system in central Canada. Negotiated by William Benjamin Robinson with Anishinaabe leaders north of Lakes Huron and Superior, in part in response to Indigenous protests over mining developments on their lands, the treaties ceded large territories in exchange for one-time payments, annual annuities, rights to continue hunting and fishing on unoccupied ceded land, and reserves. In important cases, the chiefs themselves identified the tracts they wished to retain. Subsequent treaty-making in Ontario and then on the prairies drew on this model: land cession, perpetual annuities, and the setting aside of reserves in exchange for the opening of the remainder to settlement and development.

Confederation in 1867 established the constitutional framework. Section 91(24) of the British North America Act gave the federal Parliament exclusive authority over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians." The Indian Department, which had been subject to provincial control since 1860, became a federal responsibility. Provinces, however, controlled most other Crown lands, and the dispute over the relationship between provincial and federal power over Indigenous land was to be a persistent source of friction. In 1888 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, in the St. Catherine's Milling case, held that Indigenous title in the Ontario territory covered by Treaty 3 was a burden on the underlying Crown title and that the province, not the Dominion, benefited from the surrender of that title. The decision illustrated how questions about reserve lands and ceded lands could become contests between levels of government, with First Nations as parties whose own views were rarely decisive.

The acquisition of Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory by Canada in 1870, discussed in connection with the Red River Resistance in Chapter 83, opened the prairies to Dominion treaty-making. Between 1871 and 1877 the Crown concluded Treaties 1 through 7 with First Nations of present-day Manitoba, northwestern Ontario, Saskatchewan, and Alberta, as examined in Chapter 82. These agreements did not all establish the same reserve entitlement. Treaties 1 and 2 generally promised 160 acres for each family of five; Treaties 3, 4, 6, and 7 generally provided one square mile (640 acres) per family of five. Treaty 5 (1875) generally provided 160 acres per family of five, with a special 100-acre-per-family-of-five arrangement for the Norway House families who moved to the Fisher River locality. Treaty 8 (1899) generally offered bands reserve lands at one square mile per family of five—equivalent to 128 acres per person—or, as a distinct option for individuals or families preferring to live apart from a band reserve, 160 acres per person in severalty. The variations were legally consequential: officials had to translate each negotiated provision into particular surveyed lands, while First Nations continued to assert their understanding of the promises and the Crown pursued territorial access for settlement, agriculture, and transportation.

The first generation of reserve administration under the Numbered Treaties was largely a story of implementation. The treaties promised reserves, but the details of location, boundaries, and acreage had to be determined after the negotiations, by surveyors, Indian Department officials, and chiefs and headmen. Delays between treaty signing and reserve survey were common, sometimes lasting years, and during those years settlers moved in, railway routes were selected, and the pressures that would later affect reserves took shape. The treaties' economic promises, including agricultural implements, livestock, seed, and instruction, created further expectations that shaped the later administration of reserves, though the delivery of those goods was often inadequate.

Reserves also existed outside the Numbered Treaty system. In eastern Canada, many reserves had been established before Confederation under colonial authorities, and their histories were governed by earlier legislation and practice. In British Columbia, which joined Confederation in 1871 under terms providing that the Dominion would assume charge of Indians and pursue a policy as liberal as that of the colonial government, reserves were laid out without the sweeping treaties of the prairies, except in the northeast corner of the province, covered by Treaty 8 in 1899. The result was a set of reserve geographies and disputes distinct from those of the prairies, to which Section 84.17 returns.

Cause and Effect

How Canadian Expansion Turned Treaty Land Promises into a Reserve System

Long-term causes

The Crown's long-standing practice, rooted in the Royal Proclamation of 1763, of obtaining Indigenous land through negotiated agreements; the model of earlier treaties such as the Robinson Treaties; and Indigenous nations' own interest in securing protected land bases.

Short-term causes

Confederation and the transfer of Rupert's Land; the government's determination to open the prairies to agricultural settlement and the transcontinental railway; and concern over the decline of the bison and the future livelihoods of Plains nations.

Immediate trigger and context

The 1871–1877 negotiations of Treaties 1 through 7, in which First Nations leaders pressed for assistance, farming support, and secure land in exchange for access to territory.

Immediate effects

Surveys, land selection, and the creation of reserves held by the Crown; the appointment of agents and farm instructors; and a framework within which treaty obligations and government control were both exercised.

Long-term consequences

A system in which promised land and administrative restriction were bound together, and in which treaty promises remained a continuing basis for Indigenous claims against government policy.

Historical complexity

Treaty outcomes depended on Indigenous diplomacy as well as Crown objectives; settlement pressure, constitutional arrangements, and the bison crisis all interacted. No single factor explains the system.

Division I · Section 84.4

Two Systems of Territorial Control: A Comparative Institutional Framework

The reservation and reserve systems shared a family resemblance but rested on different foundations. In the United States, the Constitution gave Congress power to regulate commerce with Indian tribes and gave the president and Senate the power to make treaties. Tribal nations were recognized in early Supreme Court decisions as distinct political communities, in the words of one famous opinion "domestic dependent nations," with a relationship to the federal government unlike that of foreign states or ordinary citizens. In Canada, the constitutional framework was federal authority over Indians and lands reserved for the Indians, exercised through Parliament, a federal department, and, in the prairies, treaties between the Crown and First Nations. The Crown's honour and treaty promises were central to First Nations' understanding of the relationship, though Canadian law was slower than American law to articulate enforceable doctrines.

Differences in land tenure were substantial. In the United States, reservation land might be held in tribal trust, allotted to individuals under later legislation, or lost to non-Indigenous owners within reservation boundaries. In Canada, reserve land was generally Crown land set apart for a band's use and benefit, with Indigenous occupants holding rights of possession recognized under the Indian Act's later framework, and with surrender of reserve land requiring formal processes. Neither pattern was fixed. Both were shaped by statute and administration, and both became subjects of dispute as governments sought to change the terms on which Indigenous people held land.

The two governments also shared objectives. Both wished to enable settler expansion by clearing title and opening land to agriculture, railways, and mining. Both sought to concentrate Indigenous populations on defined territories, limit access to wider lands, regulate economic life, and encourage assimilation through farming, schooling, and Christianization. Both relied on agents to supervise reservation and reserve communities, and both used food, annuities, and administrative recognition as tools of influence. Officials in each country were aware of the other's practices, and Canadian officials in particular drew comparisons with American policy, sometimes to claim that their own approach had avoided American-style warfare, a claim that the Indigenous experience of coercion complicates.

Implementation varied significantly by nation, region, and period. Some communities, like the Navajo after 1868, were able to return to a portion of their homeland, while others, like the Ponca or Northern Cheyenne in the 1870s, were forced to move long distances and then struggled to return. Some Canadian First Nations negotiated reserves near traditional sites with reliable resources, while others found their selected sites contested or were pressured to relocate. In the United States, executive-order reservations could be altered by presidential action, whereas treaty reservations carried a different legal weight. The comparative table below presents a conceptual guide to differences that this chapter examines in detail. It is not a substitute for attention to variation within each country.

Division II · Section 84.5

The Creation of American Reservations: Treaties, Military Pressure, and Relocation

The United States concentrated Indigenous communities on reservations through several mechanisms, often in combination: negotiated treaties, congressional acts, executive orders, military campaigns, the withholding of food, and relocation of agencies. This section uses selected cases rather than recounting every reservation's founding. Two cases illuminate the range. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 and the Great Sioux Reservation show a large reservation negotiated after a Native military success. The Navajo return from Bosque Redondo in 1868 shows how a defeated and confined people secured a return to part of their homeland.

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 followed Red Cloud's War, described in Chapter 75, in which Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors forced the United States to abandon forts along the Bozeman Trail. The Peace Commission negotiated with various Lakota bands over several months, and the treaty was signed at different times by different leaders, with Red Cloud signing in November only after the forts had been abandoned. The treaty set aside as the Great Sioux Reservation a large area that included all of present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River, and it provided that the area was to be set apart for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation of the Lakota and other signatory Indians. It also recognized an additional area, north of the North Platte River and east of the summit of the Bighorn Mountains, as unceded Indian territory in which no white person would be permitted to settle or pass through without Indian consent, and it preserved hunting rights south of the Platte and along the Republican River so long as bison ranged in numbers sufficient to justify the chase.

The treaty combined these territorial provisions with an agenda of change. It provided for agency buildings, farming districts on the reservation, and, under Article VI, the selection by a farming head of family of up to 320 acres within the reservation; an adult not heading a family could select up to 80 acres. Article VI also described a distinct 160-acre homestead-patent pathway on certain qualifying lands outside the specified reservation, which must not be confused with its internal 320-acre selection. The later Dawes Act of 1887 likewise used a different 160-acre allotment rule for heads of families. Other articles addressed annual distributions of goods and clothing, rations during transition, and schooling for children aged six to sixteen, with teachers to be provided by the government. Article XII required the consent of at least three-fourths of adult male Lakota occupying or interested in the reservation for the specified future land cessions. Thus the written treaty combined a territorial guarantee with federal plans for changed livelihoods and education. Lakota negotiators and U.S. commissioners did not necessarily interpret these provisions alike, and disputes over oral understandings and the English-language text continued.

The contrast with the Navajo case is instructive. The Diné had been subjected to the campaigns described in Chapter 71 and, from 1863 onward, imprisoned with Mescalero Apache at Bosque Redondo on the Pecos River, where disease, poor water, failed crops, and shortages caused great suffering. In 1868, General William T. Sherman and Samuel Tappan negotiated a treaty at Fort Sumner with Navajo leaders including Barboncito, who argued for a return to the country between the sacred mountains. The treaty of June 1, 1868, established a reservation on part of the Diné homeland, in present-day Arizona and New Mexico, and the Navajo began the journey home later that month. The initial reservation covered several million acres but was substantially smaller than the area the Diné had used, and later executive orders enlarged it. The treaty also promised schools, agricultural implements, and annual goods, and committed the Navajo to a program of farming and education.

The Navajo return differs in an important respect from relocations to unfamiliar territory. To go back to a portion of one's homeland, among familiar landmarks and resources, preserved continuities of religion, livelihood, and kinship that were disrupted when nations were concentrated far from home. The Northern Cheyenne offer the opposite example. After the wars of 1876–77, many Northern Cheyenne were moved to Indian Territory, where they suffered from disease and inadequate rations. In 1878 a group led by Dull Knife and Little Wolf left in an attempt to return north, resulting in the Fort Robinson tragedy of January 1879. Over the following years, the Northern Cheyenne gained a reservation on the Tongue River in southeastern Montana, established by executive order in 1884, a partial return to their homeland won through persistence. These examples show that the location of a reservation relative to a homeland mattered greatly for how Indigenous communities experienced confinement.

Military enforcement was part of the process. Troops were stationed at forts near agencies, escorted relocations, rounded up groups that had left their designated areas, and pursued those who refused to settle. The Army's role was not uniform, and the Peace Policy attempted to limit it, but the possibility of military force underlay negotiations. Pressure also came from the disappearing food supplies described in Chapter 81. As bison herds declined, communities that might otherwise have resisted relocation faced hunger, and agencies became the place where food was available. Agencies were also relocated for administrative and political reasons. The Red Cloud Agency, for instance, was moved several times in the 1870s, to the dissatisfaction of Red Cloud's people, and eventually became the Pine Ridge Agency in 1878. Each movement of an agency affected where people camped and how they were supplied.

Finally, there was often a gap between negotiated boundaries and subsequent government decisions. Treaty-defined boundaries could be revised by agreements, statutes, or executive orders, and the executive-order reservation became a flexible instrument by which presidents created, enlarged, or reduced reservations without the participation of Congress or the affected nation. The Great Sioux Reservation itself was reduced in 1877 and further subdivided under a statute passed in 1889 and made effective in 1890, as discussed in Section 84.17. The creation of a reservation, then, did not settle the question of territory. It set the terms of a continuing contest.

Division II · Section 84.6

The Creation of Canadian Reserves: Treaty Promises, Surveys, and Land Selection

Signing a treaty did not create a reserve. In the Canadian prairies, treaties promised reserves in general terms, and the actual selection, survey, and confirmation of particular tracts took place afterward through a process that involved chiefs and headmen, Indian Department officials, and Dominion Lands surveyors. This process translated treaty language into geography, and it was in the translation that many conflicts arose. The treaties set formulas, such as one square mile for each family of five, but they left room for debate over where the land should be, how it should be shaped, and how much of the available resources it would include.

Indigenous leaders approached reserve selection with their own priorities. Many sought locations that protected access to resources central to their economies: river or lake frontage for fishing and water, timber for fuel and building, hay land and pasture for horses and cattle, and, increasingly, arable land for farming. Others wished to remain near burial grounds, ceremonial sites, or areas associated with family histories. Some leaders, including Ahtahkakoop and Mistawasis in the Treaty 6 area, selected lands where they intended to pursue agriculture seriously, while others delayed selection in hope of improved treaty terms or because they continued to depend on the hunt. Mistahimaskwa (Big Bear) refused for several years to accept Treaty 6 and signed only in 1882, in the aftermath of the buffalo's disappearance from the region, and he did not select a reserve before he was drawn into the events of 1885. The pace and shape of reserve selection thus reflected differing Indigenous strategies as well as government timetables.

Government priorities were different. The Department of the Interior, which administered both Dominion lands and Indian affairs, wished to survey agricultural land in orderly townships and to keep reserves from interfering with settlement and railway planning. Officials preferred to locate reserves in compact blocks, to keep them away from railway lines, where possible, and to avoid fragmenting the township grid. They also sometimes promoted the consolidation of small bands onto fewer, larger reserves, or the location of reserves near agencies and farm instructors, which they argued would facilitate supervision. In practice these preferences meant that reserve locations were negotiated between Indigenous preferences and government convenience, and the outcomes were uneven.

The delay between treaty and survey could be considerable. In some areas surveys were completed within a few years, while in others reserves remained unsurveyed for a decade or more, leaving communities uncertain of their boundaries and exposed to settler encroachment. The Canadian Pacific Railway, completed across the prairies in the early 1880s, crossed some reserves, notably in the Treaty 7 area, and railway land grants shaped the pattern of nearby settlement. The creation of towns and agricultural districts near reserves created disputes over access to rivers, timber, pasture, and fishing places, and over the boundaries themselves, which were drawn by surveyors who might not have visited the ground or consulted Indigenous residents about their use of the land.

On the prairies, Cree, Saulteaux, Assiniboine, and Blackfoot nations selected reserves of differing size and character. Cree and Saulteaux leaders in the Treaty 4 area, around the Qu'Appelle Valley and the Touchwood Hills, chose reserves along river valleys and in parkland, and Treaty 6 leaders along the North Saskatchewan and Battle Rivers did likewise. The Blackfoot Confederacy nations under Treaty 7, the Siksika, Kainai, and Piikani, together with the Tsuut'ina and Stoney Nakoda, selected reserves along the Bow, Belly, and other rivers. Reserve selection could therefore lead to large differences between nations, depending on the quality of land, the presence of nearby settlements, and the leaders' bargaining position.

The treatment of reserves outside the Numbered Treaty area was different. In British Columbia, with a few exceptions, no comprehensive treaties were made, and reserves were laid out through a series of colonial and provincial measures. In the colonial period, Governor Douglas had allocated reserves in the 1850s and 1860s, often in accordance with Indigenous preferences, but after Douglas's retirement, Joseph Trutch, as Commissioner of Lands and Works, took a far more restrictive approach, denied that Indigenous people held title to land, and reduced previously allotted reserves. After British Columbia joined Confederation, a Joint Indian Reserve Commission, with representatives of the Dominion and the province, was established in 1876 to settle reserve allotments. It was replaced in 1880 by a single commissioner, Peter O'Reilly, who served for nearly two decades. The commissioners laid out many small reserves, often in accordance with a fisheries-oriented or village-based logic that recognized specific sites rather than extensive territory, and Indigenous nations such as the Nisga'a, Tsimshian, and Secwepemc protested that the reserves were inadequate and that their title had never been extinguished.

Division II · Section 84.7

Indian Agents, Federal Departments, and the Administrative Chain of Command

The agent was the face of the government for most Indigenous people on reservations and reserves. Understanding the administrative chain that ran from Washington or Ottawa to the agency office is essential to understanding how reservation and reserve policy worked, because policy as written in statutes and regulations was filtered through the judgment, priorities, honesty, and prejudices of local officials who had wide discretion and little supervision.

In the United States, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs headed the Office of Indian Affairs within the Department of the Interior, answering to the Secretary of the Interior. Below the commissioner were regional superintendencies, which were gradually abolished in the 1870s, and local agencies, each headed by an agent responsible for one or several reservations. Agents were appointed by the president, with Senate confirmation in many periods, and their selection was often shaped by political patronage, then, under the Peace Policy, by church nomination, and later again by political appointment. The agency staff included clerks, farmers, blacksmiths, carpenters, teachers, physicians, and interpreters, as well as, from 1878, Indian police. Agents supervised the issue of annuity goods and rations, managed agency farms and stock, reported on the conduct and conditions of the people, and handled relations between the agency and the surrounding non-Indigenous population.

The agent's discretion was substantial. The federal government's size and communications meant that agents on remote reservations made decisions about rations, school attendance, leadership recognition, and the treatment of customs with limited oversight. Corruption was a recurring problem. Contractors who supplied agencies with beef, flour, and other goods sometimes delivered short weights or inferior quality, and agents sometimes colluded with them. Commissioners' reports and congressional investigations documented abuses, and reformers used them to argue for changes, but the structure of dependence made agents powerful. Strong-willed agents, such as Valentine McGillycuddy at Pine Ridge, clashed with leaders such as Red Cloud over who should wield authority, and the conflicts reveal the contest between agency control and Indigenous leadership.

In Canada, the equivalent hierarchy ran from the Superintendent General of Indian Affairs, a post held for part of the 1880s by Prime Minister John A. Macdonald, through the Deputy Superintendent General to regional commissioners and local Indian agents. A separate Department of Indian Affairs was created in 1880, and in the North-West Territories an Indian Commissioner, a post held by Edgar Dewdney from 1879 and then by Hayter Reed from 1888, supervised the western agencies. Farm instructors, appointed to teach agriculture under treaty promises, worked under the agents, as did interpreters, clerks, and, in some places, medical officers. Treaty 6 included a promise of a medicine chest and of assistance in the event of famine or pestilence, and the implementation of those provisions was left largely to local officials.

Library and Archives Canada identifies agents as officials who controlled important aspects of farming, finance, governance, and movement. They distributed supplies, determined who was eligible for rations, forwarded petitions and complaints, reported on community conditions, communicated orders from Ottawa, supervised the work of farm instructors, influenced school attendance, and dealt with chiefs and councillors. The agent's recommendations carried weight in decisions about the recognition of chiefs, the approval of reserve land transactions, and the issue of passes. Some agents were sympathetic to Indigenous complaints, forwarded them to headquarters, and advocated for better supplies. Others were indifferent or hostile, and the wide variation in individual conduct shaped each community's experience.

Both systems placed local officials in a position of tension. They were expected to fulfill treaty promises and to administer government policy, but also to economize, keep order, and promote assimilation. Their reports shaped what higher officials knew, and they therefore also shaped the archival record historians use. Agents' reports often described Indigenous communities in terms of progress or backwardness, compliance or resistance, and the historian must read them with attention to the author's interests. At the same time, Indigenous councils and leaders sought to retain authority and to hold officials to treaty promises, using petitions, delegations, and sometimes confrontation, as discussed in Sections 84.13 and 84.18.

Historical Vocabulary
Indian Agent, Agency, Superintendent, and Administrative Dependency

Indian agent refers to a government official stationed at or responsible for a reservation or reserve (or group of them) who administered federal policy locally. The title was used in both the United States and Canada, although duties and legal powers differed.

Agency refers both to the administrative office and, in the United States, to the geographical area under the agent's supervision. In Canada, the term applied to groupings of bands under one agent.

Superintendent in this period had different meanings. In the United States, regional superintendencies existed for part of the nineteenth century and were gradually abolished; later, "superintendent" was used for school and agency heads. In Canada, "Superintendent General" was the minister heading the Indian Department.

Administrative dependency is a modern analytical term describing a condition in which a community's access to food, supplies, movement, or services came to depend on the decisions of government officials. Potential misunderstanding: the term should not suggest that Indigenous communities were passive or lacked initiative. Dependency was produced by specific policies and historical circumstances, and communities continued to organize, bargain, and adapt within it.

Division II · Section 84.8

Military Forts, Mounted Police, and the Enforcement of Territorial Boundaries

The reservation and reserve systems rested not only on law and administration but also on the possibility of coercion. Governments in both countries maintained armed institutions that supported territorial restriction, responded to conflict, and underpinned administrative authority. Yet it is important to avoid exaggeration. Not every reservation was constantly occupied by soldiers, and not every Canadian reserve was surrounded by police. The coercive framework operated mainly in the background, with periodic concentrations of force at moments of crisis, and its effect lay as much in what Indigenous people expected could happen as in constant presence.

In the United States, the Army was the principal armed institution in Indian affairs through much of the nineteenth century. Even after the Peace Policy sought to limit its role, soldiers escorted relocations, pursued groups that left designated areas, guarded agencies in times of tension, and fought the campaigns described in Chapters 73 through 80. Forts situated near agencies, such as Fort Robinson near the Red Cloud Agency in Nebraska and Fort Yates near Standing Rock, provided a base for rapid response and a visible reminder of federal power. The Army also administered some agencies directly in periods of conflict. In the 1870s the military role in dealing with the Lakota expanded after the war of 1876–77, when troops disarmed and dismounted bands that surrendered, depriving them of horses and weapons.

Military involvement also had legal limits. The Army's authority to use force against Indigenous people derived from particular circumstances, such as treaty enforcement, response to hostilities, or the removal of unauthorized persons from Indian country, and officers sometimes acted beyond clear legal authority. The Posse Comitatus Act of 1878, limiting the use of the Army in domestic law enforcement, did not prevent its use in Indian affairs, which were treated as a separate sphere. Disputes arose over whether the Army or the Interior Department should control Indian policy, and the debate over transferring the Office of Indian Affairs back to the War Department recurred in Congress in the 1870s without success.

In Canada, the North-West Mounted Police, established in 1873 and sent west in 1874, was the principal armed presence. The force was a federal police organization created to establish Canadian authority on the prairies, to suppress the whisky trade, and to maintain order as treaty-making and settlement proceeded. Police posts, such as Fort Walsh in the Cypress Hills and Fort Macleod in the Blackfoot country, became centers of Dominion authority, and police officers assisted treaty commissioners, accompanied ration distributions, escorted agents, and reported on Indigenous communities. The relationship between the police and the Indian Department was close, and police officers sometimes served as intermediaries, observers, and enforcers. Commissioner Dewdney relied on police to supervise the movement of Cree out of the Cypress Hills in the early 1880s.

The North-West Resistance of 1885, examined in Chapter 83, transformed the relationship between the police and Indigenous communities. After the events at Duck Lake, Frog Lake, and Batoche, authorities treated prairie First Nations with increased suspicion. The Dominion government trialed and punished leaders associated with the events, including Poundmaker and Mistahimaskwa, who were sentenced to imprisonment, and eight Cree and Assiniboine men were hanged at Battleford in November 1885. The government also increased the police presence on the prairies and tightened administrative controls on reserves, including through the pass system discussed in Section 84.11. The police thus became a more visible enforcement arm of reserve administration, although the legal basis for many of their actions was limited.

It is important to distinguish the legal powers of these institutions from informal or unauthorized practices. A police officer could arrest a person for specific offenses under the Criminal Code or the Indian Act, such as supplying liquor, but he did not have statutory authority to require a First Nations resident to produce a pass. Similarly, an American commanding officer could enforce a treaty provision against trespass but might exceed his authority in disciplining a community. Such gaps between formal power and practice are a theme of this chapter, and they illustrate how colonial administration operated through a mixture of law, regulation, discretion, and intimidation. Understanding the combination is essential to understanding how much force was actually used, how often, and where.

Division III · Section 84.9

Rations, Food Distribution, and the Politics of Dependency

Food distribution became the most important mechanism of everyday administrative control in the Plains reservation and reserve systems. It did not begin as a deliberate strategy of dependency, but as a response to the collapse of the subsistence economies that had supported Plains communities. The causes of that collapse, treated in Chapter 81, included commercial hide hunting, military policy that tolerated or encouraged the slaughter of bison, railway expansion, and the pressure of drought and settlement. By the late 1870s in the American Plains and the early 1880s in the Canadian prairies, many Plains communities could no longer rely on bison for their food, and war, displacement, and the confinement of hunting territories had eliminated other options. The governments that had asked them to settle on reservations and reserves now controlled access to food.

In the United States, rations were issued under treaty provisions and appropriations legislation. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty promised rations of meat and flour for a period of four years to Lakota who settled on the reservation, and later agreements and acts of Congress extended and modified the provision. Annuities, in the form of goods and sometimes cash, were distributed under treaty terms for fixed periods. Beef was issued on the hoof at many Plains agencies, and "beef issues" were events at which cattle were driven out and shot or chased in a manner that echoed the hunt, a practice that became a subject of photographs and ethnographic description. John C. H. Grabill's 1887 photograph of a beef issue at Pine Ridge, held by the Amon Carter Museum of American Art, offers a visual record of the practice. Students should distinguish what such a photograph shows, namely the scene, the crowd, and the animals, from what cannot be established without agency records, such as the quantities issued, the quality of the beef, who was entitled to receive it, or how the families regarded the arrangement.

Rations were supposed to be issued on a schedule, typically weekly or biweekly, based on lists of families prepared by the agent. The agent's census was therefore a crucial administrative tool. Counting households determined how much food an agency received, and the accuracy of the count affected whether people had enough. Congress, as it appropriated funds, and the Indian Office, as it distributed them, often reduced ration amounts, citing economy, and agents sometimes padded or understated census numbers. Reductions in the late 1870s and 1880s, especially at the Lakota agencies, were severe in some years and led to hunger, illness, and protest. In 1876, the United States had gone further, attaching to an Indian appropriation act a condition that Lakota rations would be withheld unless the nation ceded the Black Hills and the unceded territory, an episode examined in Section 84.17. The measure showed that food could be used explicitly as a tool to obtain land.

In Canada, treaty obligations regarding food were less explicit than in the United States. Treaties 1 through 5 generally did not guarantee regular rations, though the treaties provided annuities and agricultural assistance. Treaty 6 in 1876 added a clause promising assistance in cases of famine or pestilence, reflecting the anxieties of Cree leaders about the future. In practice, the Canadian government began issuing relief rations in the late 1870s when the bison disappeared, and by the early 1880s many prairie communities depended on them. Officials, however, were reluctant to accept a permanent obligation, and they justified reductions by saying that rations demoralized the recipients. The result was an administrative practice that provided food in emergencies but sought to restrict it as soon as possible, and that often tied it to labor or to compliance.

The historical evidence concerning Canada's Plains Cree demonstrates how withholding food could serve territorial and political objectives. In the late 1870s, groups of Cree and others gathered in the Cypress Hills, in the southwest of the present province of Saskatchewan, where bison lingered longest and where many hoped to select reserves in a single area. The Dominion government opposed such a concentration, which it feared would encourage Cree to remain near the United States border, near the Blackfoot, and out of the zones of planned agricultural settlement. Commissioner Dewdney and his officials used ration policy to induce the Cree to disperse to reserves in the north, withholding food from those who remained in the Cypress Hills and issuing it to those who moved. Historians have documented this policy and its effects: hunger, illness, the abandonment of preferred locations, and the weakening of political leaders such as Piapot who tried to maintain a united position. Fort Walsh, which had been a center for the Cypress Hills population, was closed in 1883 and the community broken up.

Officials in both countries also used rations to encourage compliance in other ways. They made issues conditional on attendance at roll call, on sending children to school, on abstaining from ceremonies the agent disapproved of, or on performing labor on agency projects. Rations were withheld as punishment or as pressure on political leaders. Such practices varied from agent to agent and were not always authorized by higher officials, but they were widely documented in correspondence, and they show how a basic need could be converted into leverage. Indigenous communities responded in many ways: by supplementing rations with hunting, fishing, and gathering where possible, by cultivating gardens, by engaging in wage labor and trade, by sharing food through kinship networks, and by protesting inadequate supplies through delegations and petitions. Dependence on rations did not mean that people lacked resourcefulness. It meant that resourcefulness operated within a system in which a government controlled the staple food.

Cause and Effect

From the Loss of Independent Subsistence to Administrative Control over Food

Long-term causes

Commercial exploitation of bison; the expansion of railways and settlement; treaty frameworks that anticipated a transition from hunting to farming.

Short-term causes

The collapse of regional bison herds in the late 1870s and early 1880s, restrictions on hunting ranges, and warfare and displacement that disrupted other food sources.

Immediate trigger and context

Starving or near-starving communities turning to treaty and agency supplies, and governments deciding how much to issue and on what conditions.

Immediate effects

Dependence on weekly or biweekly issues; census and roll-call practices; use of rations to relocate communities or press for land and compliance.

Long-term consequences

A pattern of administrative leverage over daily life, and a durable basis for Indigenous arguments that governments owed food as treaty or moral obligations.

Historical complexity

Ration policy reflected economy as well as control; local agents varied; Indigenous communities supplemented rations and bargained over their terms. Hunger was both a cause and a consequence of other policies.
Division III · Section 84.10

Farming, Livestock, Wage Labor, and the Restructuring of Indigenous Economies

Government officials in both countries regarded agriculture as the foundation of "civilization" and the path to self-sufficiency, and they sought to replace hunting, mobile pastoralism, and established trade networks with sedentary farming. Treaty promises, appropriations, and agency programs reflected this goal. They supplied plows, seed, livestock, and tools, hired farm instructors, established agency farms, and, in the United States, encouraged the allotment of individual holdings. But the policy confronted a series of practical and structural problems, and it coexisted with Indigenous economic initiatives that did not fit the government's model.

The practical difficulties were considerable. Much of the northern Plains and the Canadian prairies is semiarid, with short growing seasons, frost, hail, and recurrent drought, and many reservation and reserve lands were chosen without regard to agricultural suitability. Farming also required capital: draft animals, implements, seed, fencing, and housing. Treaty promises of such supplies were often delivered late, in small quantities, or of poor quality, and agents sometimes reported that people lacked the tools to farm effectively while simultaneously blaming them for failure. Farm instructors, often appointed through patronage with little agricultural experience, could be ineffective. Distances to markets were great, and transport costs and low prices reduced returns. Under such conditions, farming could not provide a living for many families in the early years, which is one reason ration dependence persisted.

Communities differed in their agricultural experience. Many nations in the Southwest and on the upper Missouri, including Pueblo peoples, the Diné, the O'odham, the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara, had long agricultural traditions, and they were not being introduced to farming but to government-directed forms of it. Others, including Plains hunting nations such as the Lakota and the Blackfoot, had relied primarily on the hunt and faced the need to develop new skills and to adapt to an unfamiliar way of making a living while grieving the loss of the old one. Some communities had a mixed heritage. Plains Cree with Parkland connections, for instance, had grown gardens and had long engaged in the fur trade, and some leaders, as in the Treaty 6 area, enthusiastically took up farming, in some cases before the treaties were signed.

Livestock offered an alternative. In many parts of the Plains and Southwest, ranching was better suited to the environment than field agriculture, and Indigenous communities that already kept horses adapted readily to cattle. The Diné developed extensive sheep herds in the decades after 1868, using flocks that the government distributed on their return and that became central to their economy and, increasingly, to weaving and trade. Lakota families on some reservations accumulated cattle, and Blackfoot and other Treaty 7 communities in Alberta began ranching, though they faced competition from settler ranchers for grazing land and water. Such developments were Indigenous initiatives as well as government-supported programs, and they show that communities pursued economic strategies appropriate to their circumstances.

Wage labor and trade supplemented both. Indigenous workers served as freighters, haying crews, scouts, police, and agency laborers, cut and sold firewood and hay, and sold crafts. On the prairies, First Nations farmers sold grain and vegetables, supplied hay and wood to nearby towns, and worked as farm laborers, and in British Columbia, Indigenous workers were prominent in fishing, canning, logging, and hop picking. In the United States, communities sold cattle, hay, and wood to agencies and neighboring settlers. These activities were not simply supplements to subsistence. They reflected participation in the regional economies that were growing around reservations and reserves, and they sometimes produced significant incomes. They also created tensions with officials, who sought to control the terms under which Indigenous people traded and worked.

The government's economic policy thus both created opportunities and restricted autonomy. It supplied tools and instruction, but it also set limits on the scale and form of production, regulated sales, and sought to direct how households organized their labor. The Canadian case, examined in more detail in Section 84.16, shows how policies adopted in the 1880s and 1889 restricted the use of machinery and limited First Nations farmers' participation in the market, undermining the success many had achieved. In the United States, the allotment policy sought to make each family an individual farm unit, regardless of local conditions. In both cases, the policy combined support for farming with restrictions on its form, and the result was an economic world in which Indigenous initiative persisted within narrow limits.

Division III · Section 84.11

Mobility, Travel Restrictions, and the Canadian Pass System

Control over movement was among the most consequential forms of administrative power, because mobility had been basic to Plains and many other Indigenous economies and political relationships. Hunting, trading, visiting relatives, attending ceremonies, gathering plants, and diplomacy all depended on travel. Governments in both countries sought to limit that travel, for reasons that combined security, economy, settlement, and cultural policy. This section distinguishes carefully between the American and Canadian cases, because the nature of the restrictions, and their legal basis, differed.

American Practice: Treaty, Agency, and Local Regulation

In the United States, there was no single national rule requiring reservation residents to remain on their reservations. Restrictions arose from treaty terms, from the circumstances of particular agreements, from military orders, and from agency practice, and they varied considerably. Some treaties preserved off-reservation hunting rights, as in the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty's provisions on hunting south of the Platte and in the unceded territory, and Medicine Lodge's provisions south of the Arkansas, so long as game remained. As bison disappeared, these rights lost practical force. Officials issued regulations requiring Indigenous people to obtain permission to leave their reservations for particular purposes, and agents commonly asked for notice of travel, especially for large gatherings. Army officers treated groups found off reservations without permission as potentially hostile, and several of the wars of the 1870s began or escalated over such departures, as in the Nez Perce flight examined in Chapter 78. Enforcement was uneven. Many individuals and families traveled to visit relatives, work for wages, or attend ceremonies, sometimes with the agent's permission and sometimes without it, and agents differed in their willingness to restrict them. Local rules about hunting, and the use of ration eligibility as a means of discouraging absences, were more important than any general law.

The Canadian Pass System

Canada's pass system was introduced after the North-West Resistance of 1885. During the Resistance, several Cree and Assiniboine bands in the Battleford and Fort Pitt districts were associated by officials with the violence, though many First Nations, including prominent Treaty 6 leaders, did not take part and in some cases assisted the authorities. In its aftermath, Indian Department officials, led by Hayter Reed, then Assistant Indian Commissioner, sought to prevent communication between reserves, to reduce the possibility of future collective action, and to supervise movement. Indian agents were instructed to issue written permits, or passes, to reserve residents who wished to leave their reserves, specifying the purpose, destination, and duration of the journey. Those who traveled without passes were liable to be challenged by agents, farm instructors, or the North-West Mounted Police, and, in some cases, to be told to return to their reserves.

The pass system was used for a range of purposes. It restricted travel to other reserves, discouraged attendance at ceremonies and gatherings, limited visits to towns, and was sometimes used to regulate trade and the sale of produce. It was also connected to the Department's wider efforts to control Indigenous economic activity, since a pass could be refused to someone wishing to sell goods or to seek wage work. Enforcement varied by place and over time. Some agents issued passes freely, while others were strict, and in some regions police officers took an active role while elsewhere they ignored the practice. Indigenous people sometimes traveled without passes, and officials noted resistance, evasion, and protest. Evidence from this period shows that the system never operated as a uniform, continuous barrier, yet it affected the lives of many people and became a symbol of administrative control. It lingered in various forms into the twentieth century.

A crucial historical fact about the pass system is that it did not have a general statutory basis authorizing officials to require First Nations residents to remain on reserves. Parliament never enacted a law requiring Indians to obtain a pass to leave their reserves. The Indian Act contained provisions on other subjects, such as restrictions on the sale of liquor, the control of reserve land, and, later, ceremonies, and it contained provisions that gave agents powers in some matters. But those provisions were not a legal foundation for a general pass requirement. Departmental correspondence shows that officials were aware that the system lacked statutory authority. Scholarship, including the entry on Hayter Reed in the Dictionary of Canadian Biography and the work of historians of the prairie west, establishes that officials acknowledged this and nevertheless continued to rely on the practice, using the agent's control of rations, supplies, and favors, as well as persuasion and the threat of police action, to secure compliance. The system is thus an example of colonial administration proceeding through practices that exceeded or lacked clear legal authority.

Students must not confuse this administrative practice with other forms of regulation. Specific laws did govern certain activities, such as the supply of liquor to Indians, the sale of reserve produce under certain amendments, and, from 1884, certain ceremonies. American reservations, similarly, were subject to particular regulations about trespass, trade licensing, and hunting. These were different instruments. The pass system was not a law, and it was not equivalent to the American reservation regulations. It was a Canadian administrative practice developed after 1885, and its history shows how officials could exercise control through a combination of discretion, intimidation, and the leverage that dependence provided.

Division III · Section 84.12

Family Life, Gender, Household Labor, and Community Institutions

Beyond the offices of agents and commissioners, reservation and reserve life took place in households. Families built houses, planted gardens, raised children, prepared food, traded, cared for the elderly, held ceremonies, and sustained kinship obligations, all within the constraints of the administrative system. The sources for daily life are limited, because agents' reports focus on policy concerns and Indigenous families rarely appear as individuals, but historians draw on photographs, oral histories, missionary and teachers' accounts, ethnographic records, and community histories to reconstruct them.

Household economies changed. In Plains communities, the hunt had organized much of the division of labor, with men hunting and warfare, and women processing hides and meat, preparing food, making clothing and tipis, and managing camp life. When the hunt ended, rations and farming reorganized this work. Women continued to prepare food, now often from flour, beef, and other issued goods, and they planted gardens, gathered wild foods, and tended livestock. Men took up farming, freighting, ranching, and wage work, and in some communities, men's agricultural labor conflicted with earlier understandings of work. Women's skills in processing hides, sewing, and beadwork remained vital, and crafts became a source of income through sale to travelers and visitors. In communities with prior agricultural traditions, such as many Pueblo, Diné, and Missouri River nations, women's roles in farming and food processing were more continuous.

Administrative recognition of households could change gender roles and family authority. Agents typically listed a male head of household for the ration roll, issued implements and seed to men, and in the United States allotted land to male heads of families. These practices privileged male authority in ways that did not always correspond to Indigenous understandings, especially in matrilineal and matrilocal societies where women held significant rights over land, houses, and children. Officials also attempted to regulate marriage, discouraging polygyny and customary marriage, and to introduce legal marriage under church or state forms. At the same time, communities adapted. Extended kin networks continued to share food and resources, women kept roles in ceremonial life, and family relationships persisted across administrative categories.

Housing changed as well. Governments and missionaries encouraged log houses and frame dwellings in place of tipis, lodges, and other traditional structures, and many families built houses, though often with inadequate materials. Poor ventilation, crowding, and damp contributed to tuberculosis and other illnesses that were serious causes of death in many communities. Health care was scanty, with agency physicians few and overworked, and medicine chests promised in some treaties were small. Care of children and elders was shaped by these conditions, and by the growing involvement of schools, treated in Section 84.15, which removed children from households for part of the year or longer. Elders remained central bearers of language, history, ceremony, and knowledge, and families continued to hold ceremonies, many of them in the face of official discouragement.

Communities also built institutions. Churches, schools, councils, dance and ceremonial societies, benevolent associations, and trading networks all structured community life. Local trading networks distributed goods, horses, and foodstuffs through kinship and alliance, sometimes over long distances and across the international boundary. Indigenous people maintained connections with relatives on other reservations and reserves, travelled to visit them, and gathered for ceremonies where officials permitted or turned a blind eye, and sometimes in secret. People who lived on a reservation or reserve differed in their experience from those who maintained ties with relatives elsewhere, who combined residence with periodic travel, and from those who lived off reservations altogether.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Documented Community: Ahtahkakoop's People and Farming under Treaty 6

Household-level detail is rare in the surviving record, so this feature uses a documented community case rather than an invented family. Ahtahkakoop (Star Blanket) was a Plains Cree headman who participated in the negotiation of Treaty 6 in 1876 and led his community in selecting a reserve near Sandy Lake, in present-day Saskatchewan. Together with Mistawasis, another Treaty 6 leader, he committed his community to farming at an early stage, a choice recorded in departmental reports, in the scholarship of Sarah Carter, and in the biography of Ahtahkakoop by Deanna Christensen.

Historians use this community to illustrate several general patterns. Leaders such as Ahtahkakoop pressed the government for the implements, oxen, seed, and instruction promised in the treaty, and they engaged farm instructors and built houses. Households combined gardening and field crops with hunting where possible, fishing, gathering, and wage work, and relied on rations in lean years. Women and men adjusted their work to the new combinations, with fieldwork, cattle tending, and house building added to existing responsibilities. At the same time, the community faced difficulties common to the region: delays and shortages in the delivery of promised supplies, frost and drought, and policies that, as the 1880s progressed, limited the scale and efficiency of reserve farming. The case shows commitment to farming in the face of obstacles, and it illustrates that Indigenous communities pursued agriculture by choice and strategy as well as under government pressure.

Evidentiary caution: Most evidence comes from departmental and missionary records and from later scholarship, not from first-person accounts by community members of the time. The reconstruction therefore describes community-level patterns more reliably than individual experience.

Division IV · Section 84.13

Indigenous Leadership, Councils, and the Contest over Local Government

Reservation and reserve administration profoundly affected Indigenous political authority. Before confinement, Indigenous nations governed themselves through institutions suited to their circumstances: hereditary leaders, leaders who earned their position through achievement and generosity, councils of elders and headmen, military and ceremonial societies, clan and kin systems, and women's roles in selecting and advising leaders. Reservations and reserves brought these institutions into contact with an administrative apparatus that recognized some forms of leadership and not others, and that sought, at various times, to restructure Indigenous government.

Officials in both countries tended to deal with leaders they could identify as spokesmen. During treaty negotiations, commissioners often named "chiefs" and "headmen" and treated them as the holders of authority to sign. This practice could distort Indigenous political structures, since some societies distributed authority among many leaders, required consensus, or depended on societies and councils that did not correspond to the single "head chief" officials preferred. Agents then used recognition as a tool. They issued rations or supplies through chosen leaders, appointed or promoted men who were cooperative, and sidelined or sought to undermine those who resisted. Annuity payments, in some cases, were distributed through leaders, and officials sometimes conferred or withheld medals and other signs of status. The result was often a contest between government-recognized authority and continuing Indigenous political traditions.

In the United States, such conflicts were common. At Pine Ridge, Red Cloud's long conflict with Agent McGillycuddy illustrated the contest over who held authority, with the agent attempting to bypass Red Cloud by supporting rivals and the Oglala leader insisting on his role as spokesman for the nation. Agents in many places promoted "progressive" leaders and Indian police officers as an alternative leadership, while traditional leaders and societies maintained their influence. Councils continued to meet, sometimes informally, and used delegations to Washington to bypass local agents. In the Southwest, Pueblo governments maintained their own civil and religious structures, including councils and officers whose authority came from long-standing practice, which agents tried at times to influence. The Diné maintained local leadership networks organized around headmen and kin groups, and officials struggled to identify centralized authority that did not exist.

In Canada, officials likewise sought to determine who the chiefs were and to deal with them as band representatives. The Indian Act framework, examined in Chapter 85, was moving toward elective band councils and the replacement of hereditary leadership, but the history of that legislation belongs to the next chapter. For the period of this chapter, what matters is the practical effect of administrative interference. Agents recommended the recognition or deposition of chiefs, and the Department sometimes confirmed leaders who were cooperative and ignored those who were not. After 1885, officials who disliked the political independence of some leaders sought to weaken them, and Indigenous leaders who had been prominent in Treaty negotiations sometimes saw their influence reduced when they were imprisoned, deposed, or isolated. Yet leaders such as Ahtahkakoop, Mistawasis, and Piapot continued to represent their communities, and many reserves maintained traditional forms of consultation alongside the government-recognized structures.

It is helpful to distinguish Indigenous decision-making from the forms of band or tribal administration preferred by federal departments. Indigenous consensus building, in which councils discussed matters until agreement was reached, differed from the formal votes and majority rule that officials favored. The roles of women, of military societies, and of ritual specialists in political life were often invisible in official records, which concentrate on male leaders. The continuing importance of such institutions is documented in ethnography, oral history, and later community records, and it helps explain why communities retained cohesion under pressure. Recognized authority and continuing Indigenous political tradition coexisted, sometimes uneasily, and communities used government-recognized institutions when it served them while maintaining their own forms of authority.

Division IV · Section 84.14

Police, Agency Courts, Punishment, and the Restriction of Indigenous Authority

Reservation and reserve administration included mechanisms of law enforcement and discipline that displaced or sought to displace Indigenous legal institutions. In the United States, the federal government created agency-level policing and courts in the late 1870s and early 1880s and then extended federal criminal jurisdiction. In Canada, authority rested with agents, the North-West Mounted Police, and the Department's administrative powers, with reference to the Indian Act.

Federally organized Indian police were authorized by appropriation in 1878 and spread across the agencies. Members were Indigenous men recruited and paid by the agency, charged with maintaining order, protecting agency property, carrying messages, enforcing agency rules, preventing the introduction of liquor, and, sometimes, arresting individuals who resisted agency policy. They offered stable wages in an impoverished economy and a position of status, and some officers served with commitment to their communities as they understood them. Their role was nevertheless controversial, because they could be used to enforce policies that many in the community resented, and because they sometimes carried out arrests of leaders or the removal of children to school. The killing of Sitting Bull in December 1890 by Indian police is discussed in connection with Chapters 89 and 90 and shows the tensions inherent in this institution.

In 1883, Secretary of the Interior Henry M. Teller approved regulations, issued by Commissioner of Indian Affairs Hiram Price, establishing Courts of Indian Offenses at agencies. The courts consisted of Indigenous judges appointed by the agent, and they heard cases involving offenses defined by the regulations. The rules were explicit about cultural objectives. They prohibited the "old heathenish dances," as the regulations characterized certain ceremonies, and the practices of medicine men, plural marriage, and other customs that officials classified as obstacles to assimilation. The regulations are preserved in contemporary government documents, and they show that the courts were designed not simply to maintain order but to enforce cultural change. The courts operated unevenly and without explicit statutory authorization at the outset, and they were not established at every agency, but they were an important means of extending agency control.

The federal government also expanded its criminal jurisdiction. In 1883, in Ex parte Crow Dog, the Supreme Court held that federal courts lacked jurisdiction over the killing of one Brulé Lakota, Spotted Tail, by another, Crow Dog, within the reservation, because the matter had been handled under Lakota law. Congress responded in 1885 with the Major Crimes Act, extending federal jurisdiction over specified serious offenses by Indians in Indian country, and the Supreme Court upheld congressional power to do so in United States v. Kagama in 1886, asserting broad federal authority over Indigenous peoples. These developments, discussed further in Chapter 118, show how federal law extended into Indigenous communities and narrowed the sphere of Indigenous legal authority.

The federal effort to suppress ceremonies went beyond the courts. Agents and missionaries discouraged or banned the Sun Dance and other ceremonies on many reservations, sometimes through the ration system, the courts, or direct intervention, and the Indian Office regulations of the period reflected the view that ceremonial gatherings encouraged "idleness" and traditionalism. Enforcement varied. Some ceremonies went underground, others were modified, and some agents allowed them to continue. Communities preserved ceremonies through adaptation and secrecy.

In Canada, law enforcement was less formally organized at the agency level. Indian agents had powers under the Indian Act, including, in some circumstances, acting as justices of the peace, and the North-West Mounted Police enforced criminal law and particular Indian Act provisions, such as those on liquor. In 1884, Parliament amended the Indian Act to prohibit the potlatch and the tamanawas dance, aimed at ceremonial practices on the Pacific coast, and amendments in 1895 broadened the prohibition and extended it to certain dances on the prairies. The legislative history belongs to Chapter 85. For present purposes it is important that federal administration and policing intersected in the effort to suppress ceremonies, and that, as in the United States, enforcement was uneven and communities found ways to continue their practices.

Indigenous legal institutions did not simply disappear when federal authorities attempted to replace them. Communities continued to resolve disputes through councils, elders, and kin-based mechanisms, to hold customary marriages and adoptions, and to apply their own norms regarding compensation, restitution, and reconciliation. In the Crow Dog case itself, the resolution had been reached through the established Lakota practice of restitution and reconciliation, which the Supreme Court recognized. The persistence of such systems in the face of imposed courts underlines the continuity of Indigenous governance, and later chapters trace how tribal courts and Indigenous legal orders reemerged.

Division IV · Section 84.15

Missionaries, Schools, Religion, and the Cultural Objectives of Administration

Reservation and reserve administration was never solely about land, food, and order. Governments and churches shared the belief that Indigenous peoples should be transformed, and they used the institutions of confinement as platforms for that purpose. Missionaries, schools, and government farming programs were intended to reshape household and ceremonial life. This section outlines those cultural objectives and their relationship to administration, without treating the detailed history of institutional schooling, which belongs to the later chapters on boarding and residential schools.

In the United States, missionary involvement was formalized under the Peace Policy, as noted above, with denominations running agencies and operating schools. Even after the church-nomination system faded, missionaries remained prominent on many reservations, building churches, running mission schools supported by government contracts, and providing medical and relief services. Government-supported schools proliferated. Day schools and agency boarding schools operated on reservations, and from 1879 the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania established a model of off-reservation boarding education, discussed in Chapter 87. Treaty provisions, such as the education article of the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty, supplied a legal basis for compulsory schooling, though in practice attendance depended on agents' pressure, ration leverage, and community response.

In Canada, missionaries of the Anglican, Roman Catholic, Methodist, and Presbyterian churches were active in the prairies and British Columbia and were, in many cases, the principal providers of schooling. The government's policy of supporting church-run schools was reinforced after 1879, when Nicholas Flood Davin's report on industrial schools in the United States recommended that Canada adopt a similar approach. In the early 1880s, the government authorized industrial schools in the North-West Territories, including at Battleford, Qu'Appelle, and High River, to be run by churches with federal funding, and the system expanded thereafter. Day schools on reserves also existed. The institutional arrangements differed from those in the United States, in that the Canadian system relied on churches as operators under government contracts, while American schools included direct federal operation along with church contract schools. Both systems nonetheless pursued similar goals of instruction in English, Christianity, and agricultural and domestic skills.

Cultural objectives were closely connected to economic and administrative ones. Farming programs aimed to produce a sedentary, household-based farming population. Missionaries and teachers sought to change family structure, gender roles, marriage practices, and ceremonial life. Language policy was central. Schools generally insisted on English or, in Quebec, French, and they discouraged or punished the use of Indigenous languages, a practice that weakened the transmission of language and knowledge from elders to children. Schools were also sites where Indigenous intergenerational instruction, in which children learned from grandparents, relatives, and communities through observation, story, and apprenticeship, was displaced by classroom education and, in boarding settings, by separation from family.

Indigenous responses ranged widely. Some families chose to send children to school, seeing literacy and English as necessary tools for dealing with governments and markets, and some Indigenous leaders requested schools as a treaty right. Others refused or resisted, hid children, or withdrew them. Many people accepted Christianity selectively, blending it with older practices, or maintained ceremonies alongside church membership, a process of adaptation examined in Chapter 54. Elders and families preserved languages and ceremonies at home, and communities kept their own forms of education. The cultural objectives of administration thus met a mixture of participation, selective adoption, and refusal, and the outcomes varied by community and by generation. Chapters 87 and 88 examine the schooling systems and their consequences in detail.

Division IV · Section 84.16

Land Allotment, the Dawes Act, and Canada's Restrictive Farming Policies

By the 1880s, a powerful current of opinion in the United States held that communal landholding was the central obstacle to Indigenous "civilization." Reformers associated with groups such as the Indian Rights Association and the Lake Mohonk Conferences, many of them sincere in their belief that they were helping, argued that individual ownership of farms would teach thrift and self-reliance and would bring Indigenous people into American society as citizens. Western settlers and railway and land interests supported allotment for a different reason: after each family received its share, the remaining reservation land could be declared surplus and opened to non-Indigenous settlement. These two currents, reform and land hunger, produced the General Allotment Act, signed on February 8, 1887, and commonly called the Dawes Act after Senator Henry L. Dawes of Massachusetts.

The act authorized the president to survey reservations and to allot land to individual Indians. The original scheme provided 160 acres to the head of a family, 80 acres to a single person over eighteen and to orphans under eighteen, and 40 acres to other single persons under eighteen. Allotments were to be held in trust by the United States for twenty-five years, during which time the land could not be sold or taxed, after which the allottee would receive a patent in fee. Allottees were to become citizens of the United States, and the act contemplated that they would come under state and territorial law. Once allotments were made on a reservation, the government could negotiate with the tribe for the purchase of the remaining land, which would be opened to settlement, with proceeds placed in funds for the tribe's benefit. The act did not apply to some nations, including the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole of Indian Territory, and to certain other groups, and its scope was later extended by amendment.

Allotment should be distinguished from reservation creation. The Dawes Act did not create new reservations. It sought to transform land tenure within or associated with existing reservations, dissolving collective, tribal ownership into individual parcels and thereby also dissolving the collective land base that supported tribal government. The statute's operation was gradual. Allotment proceeded reservation by reservation over decades, through the work of special agents and surveyors, and its effects depended on local circumstances. Where allotments were made on land unsuited to farming, where families lacked equipment and capital, and where leasing to non-Indigenous farmers and ranchers became the main source of income, the policy left many allottees poor and dependent. Heirship, as allotted land passed to multiple heirs, created fragmented ownership that made use difficult. A widely cited estimate places Indian-held land at approximately 138 million acres in 1887 and about 48 million by 1934; other federal histories use different endpoints and definitions, so these numbers should be treated as approximate indicators rather than an independently reconciled series. Losses arose from multiple laws and practices, not the Dawes Act alone. Chapter 86 examines the evidence and consequences in depth.

Historical Vocabulary
Allotment, Severalty, Trust Land, and Peasant-Farming Policy

Allotment refers to the division of communally held reservation land into individual parcels assigned to Indigenous persons. Severalty is the nineteenth-century term for individual landholding, as opposed to communal or tribal holding, and "land in severalty" was the policy goal in the language of reformers.

Trust land in this context refers to land held by the United States for the benefit of a tribe or an individual allottee, with restrictions on sale and taxation. Allotments were held in trust for a specified period, after which the allottee was supposed to receive full ownership, a transition that exposed many holders to sale, mortgage, or tax loss.

Surplus land was the term for reservation land remaining after allotment, which the government could purchase and open to settlers.

Peasant-farming policy refers to the policy introduced in 1889 by Hayter Reed in Canada, which required prairie reserve farmers to cultivate small plots with hand tools and limited their use of machinery. The term "peasant" was Reed's own and reflects the assumption that Indigenous farmers should begin at a subsistence scale. Potential misunderstanding: the Dawes Act and peasant-farming policy were not the same program. The first was a United States statute altering land tenure, while the second was a Canadian administrative policy governing how reserve farming was conducted.

Canada's late-nineteenth-century policies reshaped reserve agriculture through different means. Canada did not enact a general allotment law of the Dawes type for prairie reserves. The reserves remained Crown land set apart for bands, though the Indian Act provided for individual "location tickets" conferring possession of particular parcels on band members, and officials experimented with them. The central instrument of the period was an administrative policy. By the late 1870s and early 1880s, many prairie First Nations farmers had made notable progress, with Treaty 4 and 6 communities increasing acreage and, in some cases, producing surpluses for sale. Indian Department officials had also established "home farms," government-run farms intended to demonstrate agriculture and provide employment, which had mixed success. But in the later 1880s, Hayter Reed, who became Indian Commissioner in 1888, argued that Indigenous farmers were proceeding too fast and should not adopt modern methods until they had mastered hand cultivation. In 1889 he introduced what became known as the peasant-farming policy.

The policy restricted agricultural machinery, requiring farmers to use hand tools for planting, harvesting, threshing, and grinding, instead of mowers, binders, and threshing machines. It limited the size of the plots each farmer cultivated and encouraged individual family plots over larger, communal fields. Other administrative measures, including restrictions on the sale of produce, particularly the permit requirements that applied to reserve produce in the prairie region, meant that First Nations farmers needed an agent's approval to sell their crops. Reed justified the policy on several grounds: that Indigenous people needed to learn farming gradually, that mechanization would make them dependent on equipment and debt, that large-scale operations competed unfairly with settlers, and that smaller plots would better suit supervision. Historians, notably Sarah Carter in Lost Harvests, have shown that the policy disadvantaged First Nations farmers, who had been adopting machinery and expanding production, and that it undermined the economic gains many had achieved. The results included reduced production, continued reliance on rations, and a long-lasting grievance.

The comparison between the two policies concerns their objectives, economic consequences, and administrative assumptions, not their legal form. Both aimed at agricultural transformation of Indigenous economies and both imagined that Indigenous people would become small farmers on individual holdings. Both assumed that officials knew better than Indigenous communities how land should be used, and both hampered Indigenous commercial initiative, the Dawes Act through exposure to land loss and the Canadian policy through restrictions on production. But they were different instruments. The Dawes Act was a statute that changed land title and opened surplus land to non-Indigenous purchase. Canada's policy was a departmental directive, not a transfer of ownership, and the reserves remained Crown-held. It would be inaccurate to describe the Canadian policy as an allotment act or to say that Canada replicated American allotment. Chapter 85 will examine the legislative framework that structured Canadian administration, and Chapter 86 will examine the Dawes Act and its consequences further.

Division V · Section 84.17

Land Reduction, Encroachment, and the Opening of Indigenous Territories

The establishment of a reservation or reserve did not secure the promised land against future dispossession. Boundaries were vulnerable to alteration through federal demands for additional land, allotment and surplus-land sales, unauthorized settlement, railway and mining interests, and the growth of nearby towns. In many cases, within a generation of its creation, a reservation or reserve was smaller, divided, or otherwise changed from what was originally set aside.

The Black Hills and the Great Sioux Reservation offer a focused example. The Black Hills lay within the reservation established by the 1868 treaty and held great spiritual significance for the Lakota. In 1874, an expedition under Lieutenant Colonel George A. Custer entered the Hills, in violation of the treaty, and reported the presence of gold, prompting a rush of prospectors that the Army at first attempted to restrain and then ceased to prevent. The government attempted to purchase the Hills, but Lakota leaders refused. In 1876, as war was under way, Congress attached to an Indian appropriation act a provision that rations would be withheld until the Lakota ceded the Black Hills and the unceded territory to the west. A commission obtained signatures from a number of leaders under duress, but not the three-fourths of adult males required by the treaty, and Congress ratified the resulting agreement in February 1877. The taking of the Black Hills remained a central Lakota grievance. The Supreme Court in 1980, in a decision examined in later chapters, held that the taking had required just compensation, and the Lakota nations have continued to decline the monetary award.

The remaining reservation was further divided under a contested legal process. After an earlier commission failed to secure the required Lakota assent in 1888, Congress passed the Sioux Act on March 2, 1889, setting out separate reservations and proposed relinquishment of remaining lands, conditioned on consent under the treaty threshold. A commission including General George Crook then sought signatures in 1889, using promises and pressure that became controversial. President Benjamin Harrison proclaimed the act effective and opened designated former reservation lands to settlement on February 10, 1890. The resulting reservations included Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Cheyenne River, Standing Rock, Lower Brule, and Crow Creek, while large areas became available for non-Indigenous settlement. Ration reductions and economic hardship around 1889–1890 intensified the crisis that formed the background to the Ghost Dance movement and the events at Wounded Knee, examined in Chapters 89 and 90. The statutory authorization, commission assent, and presidential proclamation were distinct stages; they should not be collapsed into a single act of division in 1889.

Elsewhere in the United States, reductions took many forms. Executive-order reservations were reduced by presidential order, often following pressure from settlers or railways. Treaty reservations were reduced by agreements and acts. Allotment and surplus-land sales opened tracts on many reservations in the late 1880s and 1890s. Unauthorized settlement and trespass, mining claims, and the leasing of land to non-Indigenous ranchers took further tolls, and the federal government was often unwilling or unable to remove trespassers.

In Canada, the vulnerability of reserves arose in different ways. Settlers pressed for reserve land that was fertile or well located near rivers and railway lines, and local newspapers and politicians argued that Indigenous people held more land than they could use. Disputed surveys left reserve boundaries uncertain. The Indian Act provided that reserve land could be surrendered to the Crown, with the consent of the band as defined in the act, so that it could be sold or leased, and the Department encouraged surrenders in various places, especially after the 1890s as settlement increased. The procedures for obtaining consent were open to manipulation, and the pressure on communities with limited alternatives could be great. The Department also relocated some reserves, arguing that certain locations were unsuited to agriculture or were wanted for other purposes. The details of the surrender provisions belong to Chapter 85, but the practical result was that reserve land could be reduced through administrative action as well as through external encroachment.

British Columbia presented distinctive controversies. Because the province had no comprehensive treaties, the extent of Indigenous land rights was the subject of continued dispute. The reduction of reserves in the 1860s under Trutch, the small size of reserves laid out by the commissions of the 1870s and 1880s, and the refusal of the provincial government to recognize Aboriginal title provoked protest. In 1887, Nisga'a and Tsimshian chiefs traveled to Victoria to press their claim, and they were rebuffed. Indigenous nations in British Columbia continued to petition, form organizations, and assert title in the decades that followed. The province and the Dominion disagreed about the proper size of reserves and about responsibility, and the Indian land question in British Columbia was not resolved by the end of the century.

Railway expansion, agriculture, mining, and the growth of nearby towns increased pressure everywhere. The Canadian Pacific Railway crossed prairie reserves, the Northern Pacific, Union Pacific, and later lines crossed Plains reservations, and railway grants created blocks of settler land around them. Towns grew near agencies and reserves, creating markets and neighbors with interests in Indigenous land and labor. Mining claims in the Black Hills, Idaho, and British Columbia, and agricultural expansion across the prairies and the Great Plains, intensified the competition for resources. The cumulative effect was that the reservations and reserves of 1900 were often considerably smaller than those of 1870, and that their boundaries were the product of continuing contest as much as original agreement.

Division V · Section 84.18

Indigenous Petitions, Diplomacy, Resistance, and Local Political Initiatives

Indigenous political agency is the thread that connects the preceding sections. Indigenous people did not wait for governments to decide their fate. They petitioned, sent delegations, wrote letters, made speeches, hired lawyers where possible, organized councils, and used treaty texts as the basis of claims. They also used the institutions that governments created, including schools, agency councils, and courts, when it was strategically useful, while continuing to maintain their own forms of authority. The sources for this activity are partial, but they are rich enough to show a sustained pattern of political engagement.

In the United States, Lakota leaders were among the most prominent critics of agency administration and demanders of treaty compliance. Red Cloud traveled to Washington in 1870, met with President Grant and Secretary of the Interior Jacob D. Cox, spoke in New York, and presented his understanding of the 1868 treaty and of government promises. Spotted Tail, Sitting Bull, and many others used councils and delegations as well. Leaders from other nations similarly traveled to Washington, wrote to officials, and sought the intervention of reform groups. The Ponca leader Standing Bear, after the forced removal of the Ponca to Indian Territory, won the 1879 federal court ruling that an Indian was a person within the meaning of habeas corpus law, and spoke on a lecture tour in the East. Such efforts show that Indigenous leaders engaged American public opinion, courts, and Congress, with mixed but real results.

In Canada, First Nations leaders pressed treaty claims through petitions to the Governor General and the Department, through meetings with commissioners, and through direct complaints to agents. Plains Cree leaders, particularly Mistahimaskwa, Poundmaker, Piapot, and others, argued that treaty promises of assistance were not being honored, that the reserve lands offered were inadequate, and that ration cuts caused hunger. Treaty 6 chiefs referred to the famine clause and to their understanding of what the Crown had promised. Leaders also criticized restrictions on farming, the sale of produce, and movement. In British Columbia, Indigenous nations organized, sent delegations to Victoria and Ottawa, and, in later decades, to London, to assert title. The Six Nations of the Grand River and other communities in Ontario made petitions on land and governance as well. These actions indicate that treaty claims and criticisms of administration were consistent elements of First Nations politics.

Communities also used government-recognized institutions strategically. Chiefs worked with agents when it brought supplies or support, accepted government-supplied positions such as police or interpreter when it offered wages and influence, sent children to school when families saw benefits, and used the agents' reports to put grievances on the record. At the same time they maintained councils, ceremonial leadership, and kin-based authority outside the official structure. The pattern was one of selective engagement rather than simple acceptance or rejection.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Treaty Diplomacy to Political Advocacy within the Reservation and Reserve Systems

What Changed

  • Indigenous leaders who had negotiated as heads of nations increasingly addressed governments as petitioners within systems the governments controlled
  • In the United States, the end of treaty-making in 1871 shifted advocacy toward Congress, courts, reform organizations, and public opinion
  • In Canada, the Indian Act framework and administrative controls narrowed the formal channels for political expression
  • New instruments, including delegations, newspapers, and legal representation, supplemented older diplomatic forms

What Stayed the Same

  • Treaty texts and oral understandings of treaties remained central to Indigenous claims
  • Councils, ceremonial leadership, and kin-based authority continued to operate beside official structures
  • Indigenous leaders continued to travel, speak, and write to governments and the public
  • The claim to land and to a measure of self-government persisted

Timing and pattern: Change was gradual and uneven, differing by nation and country. People most directly affected were leaders and communities whose diplomatic status was reduced, while continuity was strongest in kinship, ceremony, and treaty memory. Why the pattern matters: It shows that advocacy under the reservation and reserve systems was not a break with earlier politics but an adaptation of it, laying groundwork for the organizations and legal campaigns examined in later volumes.

Division V · Section 84.19

Regional Differences: Plains Reservations, Canadian Prairie Reserves, and Other Indigenous Territories

A single model did not apply everywhere, and students should avoid assuming that the Plains experience represents the continent. Comparing three settings, Lakota communities on the Northern Plains, First Nations communities on the Canadian prairies, and selected communities in British Columbia and the American Southwest, illustrates the variety of arrangements.

The Lakota reservations of the Dakotas occupied semiarid grassland with a short growing season. Following the 1868 treaty and the 1889–1890 division process, the Lakota lived on six reservations (including Standing Rock, shared with Dakota), with their economies based on rations, small-scale farming and stockraising, wage labor, and leasing, and with the agency, school, and church as central institutions. Treaty obligations were substantial, and the memory of the Black Hills taking shaped political life. The Army's presence was proximate, and the reservations were geographically isolated, with limited markets. Prairie First Nations in Canada, in the Treaty 4, 6, and 7 areas, held Crown reserves of smaller scale under treaties that promised implements, livestock, and assistance, and that were administered by agents and farm instructors under distinctive policies such as the pass system. Their reserves were closer to emerging railway towns and settler farms, with markets that provided opportunity and competition.

British Columbia differed again. The coastal and interior nations had varied economies, based on salmon fishing, marine resources, hunting, gathering, and, increasingly, wage labor in canneries, logging, and agriculture. They received small reserves without comprehensive treaties, and their reserves were often located at village sites, fishing stations, and resource locations, but were frequently too small to support whole communities. Questions of title and fishing rights remained unresolved, and Indigenous nations pressed claims through political organization. In the American Southwest, many Pueblo communities held land under Spanish and Mexican grants recognized by the United States, not under reservation treaties, and they maintained long-standing agriculture, village government, and ceremonial life, with the agent's influence exerted through schools and the Indian Office. The Diné after 1868 had a large reservation suited to herding. Apache nations, by contrast, were concentrated in the 1870s and 1880s on reservations such as San Carlos in Arizona, where bands from different areas were placed together, an arrangement that fueled the tensions and conflicts discussed in Chapters 79 and 80.

Cross-border kinship, trade, and movement complicated all of these arrangements. The Blackfoot-speaking peoples were divided by the international boundary, with the Siksika, Kainai, and Piikani in Alberta and the Blackfeet in Montana, and families maintained relationships across the line. After the Little Bighorn, Sitting Bull and a group of Lakota went to Canada, where they stayed near Wood Mountain until hunger and the disappearance of the buffalo led them to return and surrender to American authorities in 1881. Plains Cree and Métis hunters traveled between Canada and Montana, and in 1896 and 1897 American and Canadian authorities cooperated in expelling Cree to Canada, though some had lived in Montana for years. The 1883–84 winter was devastating for the Blackfeet in Montana, with hundreds dying of starvation, and estimates of the death toll cluster around 600. Such events show that the border mattered, as a barrier to some forms of movement and a line that created different administrative regimes, but also that Indigenous kinship and political relationships continued across it.

It is also vital to remember that many Indigenous peoples lived outside reservations and reserves, or in arrangements that differed significantly from Plains patterns. Some nations in Canada's north, such as Dene, Inuit, and many Cree communities in the subarctic, had not yet entered treaties, and the Treaty 8 negotiations of 1899 were still in the future. In the United States, nations in California, the Great Basin, and the Pacific Northwest lived under varied arrangements, including small rancherias, executive-order reservations, and no recognized land at all. Individual Indigenous people lived in towns and cities and worked as laborers. Reservations and reserves were therefore important but not universal institutions.

Division V · Section 84.20

The Reservation and Reserve Order: Conquest, Administration, and Indigenous Continuity

The governments of the United States and Canada pursued similar objectives through different legal frameworks. Both sought to enable settler expansion by clearing title and concentrating Indigenous populations, to regulate economic life, and to promote assimilation. The United States relied on treaties until 1871 and thereafter on statutes, agreements, executive orders, and administrative regulations, within a doctrine of trust and federal power. Canada relied on the Crown's treaty relationship, on federal constitutional authority over Indians and reserved lands, and on departmental administration. In the United States, military force was more directly involved in the creation of reservations, while in Canada the North-West Mounted Police and the dependence created by food policy played larger roles, though the Dominion was by no means free of coercion, as the events of 1885 and their aftermath show.

The mechanisms of control were comparable in many respects. Agents supervised daily life. Rations were used as a lever, as seen in the Black Hills agreement of 1876–77 and in the Cypress Hills policy. Farming programs shaped the economy, whether through the allotment of land in the United States or the restriction of reserve production in Canada. Mobility was regulated through agency practice in the United States and the pass system in Canada, the latter lacking statutory authority. Local government was influenced by recognition and interference, law enforcement was extended through Indian police, agency courts, the Major Crimes Act, and the North-West Mounted Police, schools and missions pursued cultural change, and land was reduced through division, surplus sales, surrenders, and encroachment.

The reservation and reserve order should be understood as more than the aftermath of military defeat. Many reservations and reserves were created through negotiation, and some wars were won by Indigenous nations in ways that shaped treaty terms, as at Fort Laramie. Defeat and coercion were often present, but the order was sustained mainly through a continuing administrative system, through which governments attempted to organize Indigenous political and economic life, day by day and household by household. That system produced patterns of dependency, restricted initiative, and imposed policies of cultural change. It was also a field in which Indigenous people exercised agency, and in which treaty promises and legal arguments remained alive.

Indigenous nations persisted. They kept their languages and ceremonies, sometimes in secret, preserved councils and kin-based governance, developed farms, herds, and wage livelihoods, petitioned and traveled, and refused to accept that their land and rights had been extinguished. These continuities explain why later movements for self-determination, treaty enforcement, and land claims, examined in Volumes IX and X, could build on foundations that had never disappeared. The reservation and reserve order of 1900 was neither a complete success from the viewpoint of its architects nor an end to Indigenous political life.

Historical Debate

Were Reservations and Reserves Primarily Protected Homelands, Instruments of Colonial Control, or Institutions That Performed Both Functions under Unequal Conditions?

The question: Historians and Indigenous scholars disagree about how best to characterize reservations and reserves. The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the basic facts, which are documented in treaties, statutes, and administrative records.

Interpretation A — Protected homelands: Some scholars and Indigenous communities emphasize that reservations and reserves were land that nations retained or secured through negotiation, often against great pressure. They remain the base of Indigenous land, community, language, and governance today, and treaty guarantees are the foundation of modern claims. On this view, to describe them primarily as instruments of control understates Indigenous achievement in preserving a territorial base.
Interpretation B — Instruments of colonial control: Other scholars emphasize that reservations and reserves were created to clear land for settlement and to supervise and transform Indigenous peoples, and that restrictions on movement, economy, law, religion, and schooling were integral to their operation. The loss of land after creation and the use of food as leverage support this view.
Interpretation C — Both functions under unequal conditions: A third approach, adopted in this chapter, holds that the two functions coexisted and could not be separated. The same land that sustained communities was the setting for administrative control, and the same treaties that justified restriction also supplied the basis for Indigenous claims. The balance varied by place, period, and policy.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on treaty texts and negotiation records, statutes, agents' reports, survey plans, ration records, petitions, oral histories, and studies such as Sarah Carter's of prairie farming. They weigh the same evidence differently.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and emphasis, and, to some extent, terminology (for instance, whether metaphors such as "prison" are appropriate, a question this chapter answers by emphasizing actual enforcement in each case). It also concerns the relative weight of Indigenous agency and government power.

Why it matters: How historians characterize the institutions shapes how modern treaty rights, land claims, and responsibility for historical policy are understood.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the American reservation system and the Canadian reserve system as comparative institutions of territorial confinement, federal administration, economic dependence, and Indigenous survival. Its central argument is that reservations and reserves performed two competing functions. They were land that governments had promised to set aside for Indigenous nations, and they were instruments through which those governments restricted mobility, controlled resources, and asserted administrative authority. The chapter has argued that neither function can be understood without the other.

The formation of the two systems followed different paths. In the United States, reservation policy grew out of removal and the treaty-making of the 1850s and 1860s, including the Fort Laramie and Medicine Lodge treaties, and was shaped by Grant's Peace Policy, which joined reservation administration to missionary purpose. In 1871 Congress ended formal treaty-making while preserving existing obligations, shifting governance toward statutes, agreements, and administrative regulation. In Canada, the Robinson Treaties of 1850, Confederation in 1867, the acquisition of Rupert's Land in 1870, and the Numbered Treaties of the 1870s created expectations that lands would be set aside for First Nations while the Crown pursued broader access for settlement.

The creation of particular reservations and reserves varied. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 created the Great Sioux Reservation and an unceded hunting territory after a Lakota military success, while the Navajo treaty of the same year allowed the Diné to return to part of their homeland. In Canada, treaty promises were translated into reserves through surveys and land selection, a process shaped by Indigenous preferences, government convenience, and delay, and in British Columbia reserves were laid out without comprehensive treaties and in smaller parcels. In all cases, a reservation or reserve was a portion of a larger homeland, and its location relative to that homeland shaped how communities experienced confinement.

Administration depended on agents, who distributed supplies, reported on communities, and exercised wide discretion, and on departments in Washington and Ottawa that depended on those reports. Armed institutions, the United States Army and the North-West Mounted Police, supported the system, though not every reservation was occupied or every reserve surrounded. The legal powers of these institutions differed from practices they sometimes adopted informally, a distinction that is central to the history of the Canadian pass system.

The loss of independent subsistence, driven by the collapse of the bison economy, made food distribution a central mechanism of control. Rations met genuine needs, but governments also used them to induce relocation, enforce compliance, and obtain land, as in the 1876 rider on Lakota rations and the Cypress Hills policy toward the Plains Cree. Farming programs, livestock, and wage labor reshaped Indigenous economies. Many communities embraced agriculture and ranching and developed trade and labor, but poor land, scarce equipment, and restrictive policies limited their success.

Mobility restrictions took different forms. In the United States they arose from treaty terms, military orders, and agency practice, while in Canada the pass system emerged after 1885 as an administrative practice without general statutory authority, which officials knew and nonetheless continued. The chapter has insisted on distinguishing these practices from specific laws, and on recognizing that enforcement varied by place and over time.

Daily life, governance, and culture were all affected. Households reorganized work and gender roles, built homes and gardens, and maintained kinship and ceremony under pressure. Agents and officials recognized some leaders and sought to undermine others, but councils, hereditary and community authority, and kin-based institutions persisted. Federal Indian police, the Courts of Indian Offenses, the Major Crimes Act, and Indian Act provisions targeted both order and cultural practice, and missionaries and schools pursued assimilation, met by selective participation, refusal, and preservation of language and ceremony.

Land policy transformed the systems late in the century. The Dawes Act of 1887 sought to dissolve communal tenure through individual allotments and the sale of surplus land, while Canada's peasant-farming policy of 1889 restricted reserve agriculture through administrative rules. The two policies shared assumptions about transforming Indigenous economies but were legally and structurally different, and the chapter has insisted that they not be conflated. Boundaries also proved vulnerable. The taking of the Black Hills, the division of the Great Sioux Reservation, surrenders and relocations in Canada, and the contested reserve question in British Columbia all showed that creation of a boundary did not secure the land.

Indigenous peoples responded with petitions, delegations, councils, legal arguments, and the strategic use of institutions while keeping their own authority, as illustrated by Red Cloud's 1870 statement and by the advocacy of Cree, Nisga'a, and other leaders. Regional and cross-border variation, from Lakota reservations to Canadian prairie reserves, British Columbia, the Pueblo world, and Apache concentration, shows that no single model applied and that many peoples lived outside reservations and reserves.

The chapter's contribution to Volume VII is to show that conquest continued after treaties and wars through the daily operation of administrative institutions that regulated land, food, movement, work, and political authority. The debate among historians over whether reservations and reserves were protected homelands, instruments of colonial control, or both is best resolved by recognizing both functions and the unequal conditions in which they operated. Chapter 85 will examine the Indian Act and the statutory machinery behind a major part of the Canadian system.

Key Terms

Reservation
In the United States, land set apart for Indigenous nations by treaty, statute, or executive order, with varying tenure including tribal trust and, later, allotted land.
Reserve
In Canada, land set apart for the use and benefit of a First Nation, generally held by the Crown.
Homeland
The territory with which a people has long-standing relationships of use, governance, and meaning, usually larger than any reservation or reserve.
Federal trust responsibility
A doctrine in United States law that the federal government has obligations of protection toward tribal nations and their lands, arising from treaties, statutes, and dealings.
Indian agent
A government official who administered federal policy locally on a reservation or reserve, distributing supplies and reporting to higher authorities.
Agency
The administrative office and area under an Indian agent's supervision.
Administrative dependency
A condition in which a community's access to food, supplies, or movement depended on official decisions, produced by specific policies and circumstances.
Annuity
A treaty-based periodic payment, in goods or money, owed to Indigenous nations in exchange for land.
Ration
Food issued by government agencies to Indigenous communities, under treaty provisions, appropriations, or relief policy.
Beef issue
The distribution of cattle on the hoof to Plains reservation families, often by driving animals out to be killed.
Peace Policy
President Grant's approach beginning in 1869, combining civilian oversight and church nomination of agents with pacification and assimilation goals.
Board of Indian Commissioners
A civilian oversight body created in 1869 to advise on Indian affairs and purchasing.
1871 treaty-making provision
A congressional measure ending recognition of tribes as parties to future treaties, while preserving existing treaty obligations.
Executive-order reservation
A reservation established or altered by presidential order, without a treaty or act of Congress.
Unceded territory
Land recognized in a treaty as Indigenous land not surrendered, such as the unceded area in the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty.
Numbered Treaties
Eleven treaties between the Crown and First Nations (1871–1921), including reserve provisions and annuities.
Pass system
A Canadian administrative practice, developed after 1885, of requiring written permission for reserve residents to leave reserves, lacking general statutory authority.
Farm instructor
A Canadian Indian Department employee appointed to teach agriculture on reserves, in accordance with treaty promises.
Home farm
A government-operated farm on or near a reserve intended to demonstrate farming and employ Indigenous workers.
Peasant-farming policy
Hayter Reed's 1889 policy restricting prairie reserve farmers to small plots and hand methods.
Dawes Act
The General Allotment Act of February 8, 1887, authorizing allotment of reservation land to individuals and the sale of surplus land.
Allotment
The division of communal reservation land into individual parcels assigned to Indigenous persons.
Severalty
Nineteenth-century term for individual landholding as opposed to communal tenure.
Surplus land
Reservation land remaining after allotment, available for purchase and settlement.
Trust period
The time, twenty-five years under the Dawes Act, during which allotted land was held by the United States and could not be sold or taxed.
Courts of Indian Offenses
Agency-level courts established in 1883 with regulations that targeted certain ceremonies and customs.
Major Crimes Act
An 1885 statute extending federal jurisdiction over specified serious offenses committed by Indians in Indian country.
Land surrender
In Canada, the transfer of reserve land to the Crown, requiring band consent as defined in law, so that it could be sold or leased.
Joint Indian Reserve Commission
A federal-provincial body (1876–1878) that allotted reserves in British Columbia.
North-West Mounted Police
A federal police force, created in 1873, that supported treaty-making, administration, and order on the prairies.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how colonial authority operated after the great wars and treaty councils were over. The campaigns of Volume VII ended with territory taken, but control of that territory, and of the people living on its remnants, had to be exercised daily. The reservation and reserve order was the setting in which that exercise took place, through agents, ration lists, survey lines, passes, courts, schools, and statutes. Understanding it requires attention to ordinary administrative detail, because that detail is where policy touched lives.

The comparison between the United States and Canada illuminates both. Similar objectives were pursued under different legal frameworks, with different mixes of force, treaty obligation, and administrative discretion. The comparison also warns against easy generalization: the Dawes Act and the peasant-farming policy, the American reservation and the Canadian reserve, and the Plains and British Columbia were not equivalents. Seeing the differences clarifies what was distinctive in each national system and what was shared.

The chapter's deeper lesson concerns Indigenous persistence. Under constraints that were often severe, communities kept their governance, ceremonies, languages, and treaty memory alive, adapted economically, and pressed claims through petition and delegation. Those continuities shaped the later twentieth century, when Indigenous nations used treaty texts and organized advocacy to challenge termination, assert self-determination, and pursue land claims, topics that Volumes IX and X examine.

Chapter 85 now turns to the Indian Act and the legislative machinery of Canadian colonial administration. Readers who carry forward the understanding that administration depended on both law and practice, and that Indigenous communities contested both, will be well equipped to examine how statutory authority structured the system described here.
