Indigenous Intellectuals, Writers, and Political Leaders

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1879, in a federal courtroom in Omaha, Nebraska, a young Omaha woman translated for a Ponca chief whose right to be treated as a legal person was being argued before a judge. The case, the translation, and the lecture tour that followed show Indigenous voices entering a modern public sphere on terms they did not control, and often reshaping those terms anyway.

The proceeding was Standing Bear v. Crook. Standing Bear and a group of Ponca had left Indian Territory, where the United States had relocated their nation in 1877, to bury a son in the homeland on the Niobrara River. The army arrested them, and a lawyer's petition for a writ of habeas corpus challenged the detention. Judge Elmer Dundy ruled in the spring of 1879 that an Indian was a "person" within the meaning of that writ and could not be held without lawful authority. The ruling mattered legally, but historians of this chapter's subject have an additional reason to remember the case. Standing Bear spoke through an interpreter, Susette La Flesche (Inshta Theamba, "Bright Eyes"), the Omaha daughter of a prominent family. Together with the newspaper editor Thomas Henry Tibbles, whom she married in 1881, she and Standing Bear then carried the Ponca case to audiences in eastern cities. Her work as translator, speaker, and writer is one of several beginnings for the story this chapter tells.

This chapter asks how Indigenous peoples became influential participants in the modern public sphere between roughly 1880 and 1930, with essential precedents drawn from earlier in the nineteenth century. By "public sphere" we mean the shared arenas in which opinions are formed and policy is argued: newspapers, books, magazines, lecture halls, churches, scholarly societies, museums, petitions, conventions, and the correspondence networks that linked them. Indigenous writers edited newspapers, published autobiographies, poetry, fiction, and ethnography, performed on lecture platforms, carried out research in federal and university institutions, drafted petitions to Parliament and to the Crown, and founded associations that claimed to speak for Indigenous people at a national scale. Their work took place in the same decades in which the United States pursued allotment and boarding-school education (Chapters 86 to 88) and Canada administered First Nations under the Indian Act (Chapter 85).

The chapter's central argument is that Indigenous intellectual and political leadership did not originate in colonial schools or in Western institutions. Indigenous nations already possessed rich traditions of oratory, diplomacy, legal reasoning, historical memory, ritual knowledge, and political deliberation, examined in earlier volumes and in Chapter 1. What changed after conquest were the institutions through which that knowledge could circulate and the audiences it could reach. Indigenous leaders selectively adapted new forms of communication, such as the newspaper, the printed book, the lecture platform, the scholarly monograph, and the formal association, to defend existing nations, preserve knowledge, correct misrepresentation, and argue for competing visions of the future. The adaptation involved real innovation, but it also involved continuity of purpose.

That argument needs two qualifications. First, literacy in English or French should not be mistaken for intelligence or political sophistication, and its absence should not be read as their absence. Many of the most consequential Indigenous political actors of the period, including council members, ceremonial leaders, and community spokespeople who never published a word, exercised their authority through speech, kinship, and local institutions. Second, the people discussed here were not interchangeable. Sarah Winnemucca, Carlos Montezuma, Laura Cornelius Kellogg, Charles Eastman, Zitkala-Ša, Francis La Flesche, E. Pauline Johnson, Alexander Posey, Arthur C. Parker, the chiefs who signed a memorial to the Prime Minister of Canada, and the Tlingit and Tsimshian founders of the Alaska Native Brotherhood disagreed about sovereignty, citizenship, assimilation, religion, tribal institutions, and the role of the federal government. Those disagreements are part of the history, not obstacles to it.

The chapter's evidence is itself an object of study. The surviving record includes books and periodicals produced by Indigenous authors, but also speeches transcribed by non-Indigenous secretaries, memoirs shaped by editors, lectures reported by hostile or sympathetic journalists, ethnographic texts published under joint names, petitions drafted with the help of intermediaries, and archival correspondence. Each form raises questions of authorship, translation, editing, audience, and power. For example, Winnemucca's memoir was edited by Mary Peabody Mann, the Omaha scholar Francis La Flesche worked within the Bureau of American Ethnology under a more famous collaborator, and the 1910 memorial from Interior Salish chiefs to Prime Minister Wilfrid Laurier was prepared with the help of the ethnologist James Teit. Historians must keep both the individual Indigenous voices and the mediating structures in view.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the intellectual foundations of Indigenous public life: knowledge systems that preceded colonial schooling, the roles of language and translation, the uses made of literacy, and the infrastructure of print and platform. Division II turns to journalism and literature, from Winnemucca and Susette La Flesche Tibbles to E. Pauline Johnson and Alexander Posey. Division III addresses autobiography, scholarship, and the institutions of knowledge through Zitkala-Ša, Charles Eastman, Francis La Flesche, and Arthur C. Parker. Division IV moves to organization: the Society of American Indians, the competing programs of Carlos Montezuma and Laura Cornelius Kellogg, First Nations petitions and the League of Indians of Canada, and Alaska Native organizing. Division V synthesizes the subject through women's intellectual leadership, Indigenous-language writing in Greenland and Mexico, the question of Indigenous modernity, and an assessment of the situation by about 1930.

The chapter keeps to its assigned boundaries. Schooling and family separation belong to Chapters 87 and 88, economic adaptation to Chapter 91, citizenship and legal membership to Chapter 93, the First World War to Chapter 94, organized reform campaigns to Chapter 95, and conditions on the eve of the New Deal to Chapter 96. Detailed First Nations, Alaska Native, and Inuit political histories are the responsibility of Chapters 98 through 100. The chapter's task is to show how Indigenous writers, scholars, editors, and organizers created the voices, institutions, and arguments on which those later histories depend.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Indigenous intellectual and political leadership in the period c. 1880–1930 is better understood as an adaptation of existing traditions than as a product of colonial schooling.
  2. Describe the knowledge systems, including oral history, diplomacy, ceremony, law, and environmental knowledge, that preceded and continued alongside Indigenous participation in print culture.
  3. Analyze how translation, interpretation, and editorial mediation shaped the surviving record of Indigenous testimony.
  4. Distinguish between the assimilationist purposes of mission and boarding schools and the uses Indigenous graduates made of their education.
  5. Trace the infrastructure of the Indigenous public sphere: newspapers, presses, lecture circuits, churches, associations, and correspondence networks, and the economic constraints on each.
  6. Compare the strategies of Sarah Winnemucca, Susette La Flesche Tibbles, E. Pauline Johnson, Alexander Posey, and Zitkala-Ša in addressing audiences that were largely non-Indigenous.
  7. Evaluate the contributions of Charles Eastman, Francis La Flesche, and Arthur C. Parker to Indigenous scholarship and to the question of who held authority over Indigenous knowledge.
  8. Explain the origins, aims, membership, and internal disagreements of the Society of American Indians.
  9. Contrast the political programs of Carlos Montezuma and Laura Cornelius Kellogg, and explain why reformers who condemned colonial administration could envision different institutional futures.
  10. Describe Indigenous political petitioning and organizing in Canada and Alaska, including the 1910 Memorial to Sir Wilfrid Laurier, the League of Indians of Canada, and the Alaska Native Brotherhood.
  11. Assess Indigenous-language writing in Greenland and the evidentiary problems of recovering community-authored documents in Mexico, without treating these regions as a single literary movement.
  12. Evaluate the historiographical question of whether Western education and national publishing institutions primarily empowered or primarily constrained Indigenous intellectual leadership.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What distinguishes Indigenous intellectual leadership from the mere presence of Indigenous individuals inside non-Indigenous institutions?
  2. How did translation and editorial mediation expand Indigenous audiences while also altering or obscuring what Indigenous speakers said?
  3. In what ways did Indigenous writers and scholars turn education acquired in assimilationist institutions toward purposes those institutions did not intend?
  4. Why did access to a newspaper, publisher, or lecture platform not guarantee control over how Indigenous people were represented?
  5. How did gender, region, language, religion, and nation shape the opportunities and constraints that Indigenous public figures faced?
  6. Why could Indigenous reformers agree that colonial administration was unjust yet disagree about the institutional future they sought?
  7. What does the Indigenous scholar's position inside federal and museum institutions reveal about professional authority and recognition?
  8. How did petitions, conventions, constitutions, and associations adapt older Indigenous political practices to new settings?
  9. What does the study of Greenlandic and Mexican Indigenous-language evidence add to a history otherwise centered on English-language writers?
  10. What forms of Indigenous intellectual authority persisted largely outside print, and how can historians recognize them?

Historical Context

By 1880 the military phase of the continental conquest was drawing to a close in the United States, though not everywhere and not definitively, and settler states had constructed administrative systems of unprecedented reach. In the United States, the reservation system, treaty annulment, the Dawes Act (1887), and the expansion of off-reservation boarding schools such as Carlisle (opened 1879) reorganized Indigenous land tenure and childhood. In Canada, the Indian Act of 1876 consolidated earlier legislation and gave the Department of Indian Affairs sweeping authority over band governance, status, and movement, while the numbered treaties and the reserve system settled the Prairies for agricultural colonization. In Mexico, liberal land reforms of the nineteenth century and the policies of the Porfirian period (1876–1911) pressed on communal landholding, and Indigenous communities responded through litigation, petitions, and, in the revolutionary decade after 1910, mobilization. In Greenland, Danish administration and the Lutheran mission shaped a society that had its own printing, schooling, and hunting economy. These histories are treated in Chapters 84 to 91 and 141.

The same decades saw changes in the technology and economy of communication. Railroads and steamship lines connected western and northern regions to eastern cities. Cheaper printing, rural postal service, and the telegraph expanded the circulation of newspapers and magazines. Lecture circuits, including the Chautauqua and lyceum systems, created paying audiences for speakers on moral and social questions. Reform organizations such as the Indian Rights Association (founded 1882) and the Women's National Indian Association, together with annual gatherings of "friends of the Indian," created a market for Indigenous testimony and a set of expectations about what such testimony should say. Museums, universities, and the Bureau of American Ethnology (established 1879) created new institutions in which Indigenous knowledge was collected, classified, and published. Each of these developments opened opportunities for Indigenous people and each imposed conditions.

The people who used these opportunities were not newcomers to public argument. The nineteenth century already had Indigenous authors and editors: William Apess (Pequot), whose autobiography A Son of the Forest appeared in 1829; Elias Boudinot, editor of the Cherokee Phoenix (1828–1834), discussed in Chapter 64; the Ojibwe and Mississauga writers George Copway and Peter Jones; and Ely S. Parker (Seneca), who assisted Lewis Henry Morgan's pioneering study of the Haudenosaunee and later served as Commissioner of Indian Affairs. Older still were the councils, treaty conferences, and wampum diplomacy examined in Chapters 55 and 56, in which Indigenous orators addressed colonial delegations with great skill. The period after 1880 expanded the forms available rather than inaugurating Indigenous public speech.

Historians who study this period must resist two simplifications. One treats Indigenous writers as "assimilated" individuals whose achievements merely demonstrate the success of colonial education. The other treats them as isolated heroes of resistance. Both flatten the evidence. Most of the individuals discussed here retained strong ties to their nations, worked within institutions they sometimes criticized, and held views that shifted over time. Their writing frequently addressed multiple audiences at once: federal officials, reform networks, general readers, their own communities, and other Indigenous people elsewhere. Reading it well requires attention to genre, audience, publisher, and context.

Indigenous Knowledge, Education, and the Modern Public Sphere

Intellectual foundations, literacy, communication, and political expression.

Section 92.1

Indigenous Intellectual Life after Conquest: Continuity and New Forms of Leadership

Any account of Indigenous writers and political leaders must begin by recognizing that Indigenous nations were not intellectually empty before the arrival of schools and printing presses. Every nation discussed in this chapter maintained systems for creating, testing, preserving, and transmitting knowledge. Dakota, Omaha, Osage, and Haudenosaunee communities passed on historical narratives, genealogies, and ceremonial procedures through designated specialists. Coast Salish and Interior Salish communities remembered territory through named places, family histories, and stories that carried legal and moral weight. Muscogee and Cherokee councils debated treaties and law in town and national settings. Inuit and Kalaallit hunters transmitted environmental knowledge through apprenticeship and narrative. Treaty protocol, wampum, winter counts, and ceremonial recitation (Chapter 1) were means of keeping and interpreting historical memory. These traditions were taught, challenged, corrected, and applied, which are the activities historians associate with intellectual life anywhere.

Conquest and colonial administration changed the institutions through which this knowledge circulated, often severely. Removal, epidemic disease, confinement to reservations and reserves, bans on ceremonies, restrictions on movement, the replacement of hereditary or ceremonial councils by agency-approved bodies, and the schooling of children away from home all disrupted the settings in which knowledge had been taught. In some communities, specialized knowledge was endangered when elders died or when ceremonies went underground. Yet the traditions were not eliminated. Knowledge keepers continued to teach in families and societies, councils adapted to new constraints, and religious specialists reshaped practice under pressure, as the Ghost Dance and the Native American Church (Chapter 89 and later chapters) illustrate. The change was not from "having knowledge" to "lacking it," but from one set of institutional arrangements to another, with unequal power over which arrangements could operate openly.

Against this background, a new type of figure became prominent between about 1880 and 1930: the Indigenous public intellectual. The term needs a careful definition, which this chapter supplies in a Historical Vocabulary box below. In brief, it refers to Indigenous people who addressed broad audiences through writing, speaking, scholarship, journalism, performance, or organization, and who claimed authority to interpret Indigenous history, culture, or politics for wider publics. They included writers, editors, lecturers, teachers, interpreters, physicians, ethnologists, clergy, lawyers, artists, and organizers. Many held more than one role. Eastman was a physician, author, and lecturer. Zitkala-Ša was a violinist, teacher, author, editor, and political organizer. Parker was an archaeologist, museum director, and editor. Montezuma was a physician, publisher, and political critic.

The difference between intellectual leadership and mere participation is central. An Indigenous student who completed a school curriculum or a clerk who worked in a federal agency took part in non-Indigenous institutions without necessarily shaping public understanding or setting a political agenda. Leadership, as used here, means that the person interpreted, argued, or organized on the basis of Indigenous knowledge, community responsibility, or political claims, and thereby altered what a public could say or do. By that measure, a Dakota teacher who corrected a misrepresentation in a national magazine, an Omaha ethnologist who recorded ritual knowledge with attention to its structure, and a group of Interior Salish chiefs who submitted a collective memorial to a Prime Minister were all acting as intellectual leaders, though in very different registers. Equally, a Cherokee council member or a Pueblo religious leader who never wrote for publication might exercise intellectual authority of a different but historically equal weight.

The relationship between continuity and innovation runs through the chapter. When Indigenous writers adopted the newspaper, they often did so for purposes close to those of council speech: to inform the community, to hold leaders accountable, to correct falsehoods, and to address neighbors. When they adopted ethnography, they did so in part to preserve knowledge that older institutions were struggling to transmit. When they founded associations, they drew on practices of intertribal diplomacy and on models of confederacy, as Laura Cornelius Kellogg explicitly argued in terms of the Haudenosaunee. Innovation was real, since no Indigenous society had previously issued a printed daily or organized a national convention that included delegates from distant nations. But the innovations served continuing communities with their own purposes.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous Public Intellectual

Definition. An Indigenous person who addresses a broad public, whether through writing, speaking, scholarship, journalism, performance, or organization, and who claims or is granted authority to interpret Indigenous histories, cultures, or political claims.

Historical context. The category became visible in the decades after 1880 because new media and institutions made such interpretation possible on a larger scale. It overlaps with older roles such as orator, council speaker, historian, and religious specialist but is not identical to them.

Why it matters. The term allows historians to describe leadership that took place outside tribal councils without implying that councils or oral specialists lacked intellectual authority.

Potential misunderstanding. The label does not mean that these figures spoke for all Indigenous people. Many were criticized within their own communities, and they frequently disagreed with one another.

Section 92.2

Oral Traditions, Indigenous Languages, Translation, and Historical Authority

Oral tradition remained a living source of historical authority throughout the period, and one of the most important features of Indigenous writing between 1880 and 1930 is how often it drew on spoken knowledge. Zitkala-Ša's retellings of Dakota stories, Pauline Johnson's use of narratives told to her by Squamish people, Francis La Flesche's recording of ceremonial recitations, and Arthur Parker's gathering of Seneca stories all began in oral performance. Oral and written knowledge were not inherently competing systems. A narrative might be told in a lodge, recorded in a notebook, published in a magazine, and recited again in a community gathering, with each version serving a distinct audience. Some communities developed their own writing systems or adopted others for community purposes: Cherokee syllabary literacy, discussed in Chapter 64, and later syllabic writing among Cree and Inuit communities in Canada, for example, supported religious, personal, and political communication in Indigenous languages.

Translation was the hinge on which much public Indigenous expression turned. A speech given in Lakota, Omaha, Paiute, or Salish had to be rendered into English to reach most officials, newspaper readers, and reformers. Interpreters, whether employed by agents, military officers, missionaries, courts, or Indigenous communities, thus occupied positions of great influence. Susette La Flesche's work in Omaha in 1879 was a prominent example, but the interpreters who served at treaty councils, agency meetings, and legal proceedings across the continent often exercised discretion over emphasis, tone, and meaning. Sarah Winnemucca worked as an interpreter for the army and for Indian agents before she became a public lecturer, and her knowledge of the obligations and risks of the role informed the way she presented herself to audiences. The interpreter might be a trusted intermediary, a target of suspicion in her or his own community, or both.

Translation could expand an audience while changing meaning. Grammatical structures, metaphors, and rhetorical conventions do not map neatly across languages, and a translator working under time pressure may reduce a speech to a summary. Speakers sometimes composed with the translator in mind, shortening sentences or choosing images likely to be understood. The next step in the chain, the transcription and publication of a translated speech, introduced further shaping by secretaries, editors, and reporters. The Black Hawk autobiography of 1833, dictated through an interpreter and edited by a local newspaperman, is an early example of a pattern in which an Indigenous account reached print through several intermediaries. Similar chains shaped the way many testimonies in this chapter's period survive. Historians must therefore ask not only what a source says but who translated it, who wrote it down, who edited it, and who published it.

Bilingual writers and knowledge holders also served as intermediaries in the other direction. Indigenous translators read and explained newspapers, legal documents, and agency regulations to people who did not read English, and in some communities they decided what information to circulate. They accompanied delegations, wrote letters, kept records for councils, and helped prepare petitions. When an Indigenous organization sent a written memorial to a government, the final text frequently depended on bilingual drafters who knew both the community's concerns and the conventions of official English. In British Columbia, the Interior Salish memorial of 1910, discussed in Section 92.15, is a clear case. The competence required, an understanding of two political cultures as well as two languages, should be counted as a form of intellectual work.

The difficulties facing historians are substantial. Much Indigenous testimony from this period survives only in translation, and the original-language performance is often lost. Reporters sometimes rendered Indigenous speech in stereotyped "Indian English," which obscures both content and the speaker's actual command of English. Ethnographers recorded ceremonial and linguistic material in texts and phonetic notation that may preserve detail while omitting the performance context in which the material gained authority. Moreover, some knowledge was not meant for publication, and Indigenous communities placed limits, then and now, on who could hear, record, or retell certain material. These issues anticipate later debates about repatriation, data sovereignty, and Indigenous control of knowledge (Chapters 125 and 145). For the period considered here, the practical lesson is that the voice in a published source is rarely unmediated. Reading Indigenous testimony responsibly means recognizing both the person who spoke and the structure that carried the words to the page.

Section 92.3

Colonial Education, Literacy, and the Emergence of Indigenous Writers

Chapters 87 and 88 examine mission, boarding, residential, and day schools as institutions of assimilation, and they address the experiences of children and families. This section asks a different question: what did some of the people who passed through those institutions do with the knowledge they acquired? The answer is varied. Many former students returned to their communities and contributed their skills in ways that were not necessarily visible in print, serving as clerks, translators, teachers, farmers, tradespeople, and community members. A smaller number built careers as writers, editors, physicians, scholars, lawyers, clergy, and organizers. They are the subject of this chapter, but it is important not to mistake them for the typical product of the schools or to assume that the schools' intentions explain their careers.

The assimilationist purposes of the schools were explicit. Their founders sought to replace Indigenous languages, religions, and kinship practices with English, Christianity, individual landholding, and wage labor. That aim did not determine the use students made of what they learned. Literacy, rhetorical training, music, medicine, and law could be used to defend Indigenous nations, to criticize federal policy, to record Indigenous knowledge, and to challenge the schools themselves. Zitkala-Ša's 1900 essays, discussed in Section 92.9, subjected the culture of mission and boarding schools to sharp criticism in the pages of a prominent literary magazine. Charles Eastman's educational path through mission, college, and medical school equipped him to write for national audiences about Dakota life. Francis La Flesche's recollection of a Presbyterian mission school, The Middle Five, mixed humor with observation in a way that treated the boys' own culture as the subject, not a problem to be cured. These graduates did not reject education as such. They often valued it, but on terms different from those of the institutions that provided it.

Their opportunities were constrained by unequal access. Advanced education was available to few Indigenous students. Most schooling ended in the primary grades and emphasized manual and domestic labor over academic preparation, so the combinations of schooling, scholarship funds, patrons, and family encouragement that produced a physician such as Eastman or Montezuma were unusual. Montezuma, who as a child had been taken from his Yavapai community in Arizona and raised among non-Indigenous people, attended the University of Illinois and a Chicago medical college. Eastman completed Dartmouth College and Boston University's medical school. Parker, whose Seneca family had a tradition of public service, entered museum work in New York. Such trajectories depended on patrons and chance as well as ability, and they were exceptional.

Racial prejudice shaped even the most successful careers. Indigenous professionals frequently found themselves employed by the very agency whose policies they criticized, assigned to positions that reflected assumptions about suitability, or denied promotion and authority. Women faced the additional barriers of gender. Zitkala-Ša had to negotiate with publishers, school officials, and reform groups that expected her to fit a particular script. Francis La Flesche, who by the early twentieth century was an authority on Omaha and Osage ceremony, worked for decades in a federal bureau without the formal academic degrees that would have given him standard professional rank, even as he earned a law degree at night. Meanwhile, no Indigenous-controlled universities or professional schools existed in the United States or Canada in this period, so Indigenous scholars worked inside institutions that did not belong to them. Tribal colleges, treated in Chapter 124, belong to a later era.

Finally, the institutions of Indigenous literacy included more than government and mission schools. Communities valued literacy for their own reasons, and some nations ran their own schools. The Cherokee and Choctaw national school systems in Indian Territory, which predated allotment, produced generations of educated citizens, and the Cherokee Nation's own newspaper and bilingual publications sustained a literate public. Dakota-language materials produced at Santee, Cree and Inuit syllabic literacy, and Kalaallit schooling in Greenland also show that literacy could be tied to community religion, administration, and identity rather than only to assimilation. Reading and writing, in other words, entered Indigenous societies through multiple channels and served multiple purposes. This is another reason to treat the writers of 1880 to 1930 as participants in a broader and older history of Indigenous literacy.

Section 92.4

Newspapers, Publishing Networks, Public Lectures, and Indigenous Political Communication

An Indigenous public sphere required material infrastructure. Newspapers needed presses, type, paper, ink, skilled labor, postage, and subscribers. Books required publishers willing to print them, editors who might alter them, and distribution networks that carried them to booksellers and libraries. Lectures required halls, sponsors, tickets, and transportation. Associations needed meeting places, mailing lists, and funds for travel and correspondence. In the decades after 1880 these resources became more accessible than before, yet remained largely in non-Indigenous hands.

Printing capacity expanded in the late nineteenth century. Small-town newspapers proliferated across the American West and Canada, and job printing shops made it possible to produce pamphlets and circulars cheaply. Government and mission schools operated printing programs in which students learned the trade, and student newspapers such as the Carlisle school's publications and the Indian Leader at Haskell circulated widely, though they were controlled by school officials and reflected institutional agendas. Independent Indigenous periodicals were fewer and more precarious. In Indian Territory, the Cherokee and Muscogee nations supported national newspapers; in Greenland, the mission and administration supported a press in the capital; and in Canada, short-lived Indigenous papers tried to reach Six Nations and other First Nations readers. A periodical's life usually depended on a single editor's energy and finances.

The economics of publication constrained Indigenous voices. Subscriptions were limited, since many potential Indigenous readers were poor, dispersed, or unable to read English. Advertising revenue, which sustained many small papers, was thin in communities with little commercial base. Editors often relied on patrons, church or reform organizations, government printing contracts, or their own pockets. Dependence of this kind shaped content, since a paper supported by a reform society was unlikely to criticize it, and a paper that relied on territorial business advertisers could not ignore their interests. Books carried similar pressures. Publishers expected authors to meet market demand for narratives of "Indian life," and editors and reviewers shaped manuscripts toward those expectations.

Lectures offered a different route to an audience. The lyceum and Chautauqua circuits sent speakers to towns across the United States and Canada, and the reform movement of the 1880s created demand for Indigenous speakers in churches, parlors, and meeting halls. Winnemucca, Susette La Flesche Tibbles, Pauline Johnson, Eastman, and Zitkala-Ša all spoke to mostly non-Indigenous audiences, and they did so in settings where entertainment, moral instruction, and political argument overlapped. A lecture platform conferred visibility and income but also required the speaker to conform to audience expectations, which often included stereotypes. Some speakers wore Indigenous clothing, either by choice or because audiences expected it; others dressed in the fashion of their hosts. Neither choice was without political implications, and several figures in this chapter adjusted their presentation to different settings.

Access to a platform did not equal control of political representation. A newspaper editor who published a letter from an Indigenous leader might edit it, headline it, or surround it with commentary. A reform society that sponsored a tour might shape its message. Government officials who attended a lecture might dismiss it as exaggeration. In many cases the audience that mattered most politically, such as Congress, the Department of Indian Affairs, or a provincial government, was the one least responsive to Indigenous arguments. Moreover, Indigenous speakers faced the problem that non-Indigenous audiences frequently treated them as representatives of all "Indians," ignoring differences among nations, regions, and political views. The Indigenous public sphere therefore developed in an environment of unequal power, in which the ability to speak was valuable, but the ability to be heard on one's own terms was limited.

Nor was the public sphere confined to print and platform. Churches, especially Episcopal, Presbyterian, Methodist, and Catholic congregations, hosted gatherings and sometimes employed Indigenous clergy who became community leaders. Letters passed among individuals who coordinated across great distances. Councils and conferences, such as the annual reform meetings and later the conventions of Indigenous organizations, drew delegates from many communities. These networks mattered because they allowed Indigenous people to hear one another, not only to address outsiders. The development of intertribal contact, in which a Dakota physician in Minnesota corresponded with a Seneca museum director in New York and an Oneida organizer in Wisconsin, was one of the most consequential aspects of the period. It laid the basis for the organizations discussed in Division IV.

Cause and Effect

How Literacy, Transportation, Printing, and Political Networks Expanded Indigenous Communication

Long-term causes

Older Indigenous traditions of oratory and diplomacy; nineteenth-century Indigenous literacies (Cherokee syllabary, mission literacy, school systems in Indian Territory); and long experience of intertribal and colonial diplomacy.

Short-term causes

Expansion of railroads, mail service, and cheap printing; growth of reform organizations and lecture circuits; creation of federal and mission schools; and the emergence of museums and the Bureau of American Ethnology.

Allotment and the reorganization of reservation life; the Ponca case and other publicized cases; and the need to respond to policy that directly affected land, education, and governance.

Section 92.5

Indigenous Newspapers, Editors, and the Politics of Representation

Indigenous journalism did not begin in 1880. The Cherokee Phoenix, first issued in 1828 under the editorship of Elias Boudinot and published in English and in the Cherokee syllabary, set an enduring precedent for a periodical controlled by a nation and addressed both to its citizens and to outsiders; its history belongs to Chapter 64 and is mentioned here only as a predecessor. After removal, the Cherokee Nation's later paper, the Cherokee Advocate, appeared at Tahlequah in the 1840s and, after an interruption, through the early 1900s, and other nations in Indian Territory supported newspapers of their own. These were not marginal publications. They carried laws, council proceedings, notices, editorials, and news, and they connected a dispersed citizenry. They were also political actors. In the last decades of the nineteenth century they argued over allotment, the Dawes Commission, and the future of the national governments of the Five Tribes.

By the period of this chapter, Indigenous journalism operated in several distinct settings, and the differences among them are important. First were national or tribal papers tied to tribal governments, such as the Cherokee paper. Second were independent commercial or personal papers edited by Indigenous individuals in small towns, of which the Muscogee-area Indian Journal at Eufaula, under Alexander Posey, is a well-known example. Third were school and mission publications, including the student newspapers of Carlisle, Haskell, and other government boarding schools, and the Dakota-and-English Iapi Oaye (The Word Carrier), issued from the Santee mission from 1871. These were edited by teachers or missionaries, sometimes with Indigenous contributors, and their message was shaped by institutional priorities. Fourth were activist periodicals edited by Indigenous reformers, notably the journal and magazine of the Society of American Indians (Section 92.13) and Carlos Montezuma's Wassaja (Section 92.14). In Canada, short-lived papers aimed at Six Nations and other First Nations readers appeared in the 1880s, and Indigenous writers also contributed to mainstream newspapers.

The distinction between independent Indigenous publications and government-school papers matters for evidence. A student paper at a federal school generally presented the school's successes, printed students' compositions that praised their training, and omitted conflicts. Students and staff nonetheless sometimes used these pages for more complicated purposes, such as printing news of alumni, community events, or language. By contrast, an independent paper could criticize the agent, the territorial government, or federal policy, but it faced financial fragility and the risk of retaliation. Historians who read Indigenous newspapers must therefore ask who owned the press, who paid for the paper, and who the intended readers were.

A newspaper mattered for several reasons. It was an instrument of political education, giving readers information on laws, treaties, and court decisions that might otherwise reach them only through an agent. It was a vehicle of criticism, in which an editor could hold leaders, whether tribal or federal, to account. It was a means of community communication, connecting families and settlements across distance. And it was an instrument of public advocacy, making arguments to non-Indigenous readers who might never otherwise encounter an Indigenous perspective. These functions overlapped, and a single paper might do all four. In the politics of representation, the newspaper offered a place to contest stereotypes and misrepresentations printed in the mainstream press, though the power to do so was limited by the paper's small circulation.

Two figures illustrate how differently journalism could be used. Alexander Posey, treated fully in Section 92.8, used humor, dialect, and satire in the pages of a small Muscogee-area newspaper to comment on the politics of allotment and statehood. His audience was primarily regional, and his weapon was the invented voice of a traditionalist commentator. Carlos Montezuma, discussed in Section 92.14, edited a monthly newsletter from Chicago that attacked the Bureau of Indian Affairs without compromise. His audience was national, and his weapon was direct argument. One writer worked in the idiom of local community debate and the other in the idiom of national policy critique. Both belonged to the same larger development, in which Indigenous people claimed the press as a place to speak for themselves.

Finally, the politics of representation extended beyond Indigenous-owned papers to the way Indigenous people appeared in non-Indigenous press and publishing. Mainstream newspapers frequently printed stereotyped accounts, treating Indigenous leaders as romantic remnants or as obstacles to progress. Indigenous writers responded by writing letters to editors, publishing rebuttals, and placing articles in magazines. The difficulty was that they were responding to an established discourse in which Indigenous voices carried less credibility than those of officials and "experts." Overcoming that credibility gap was one of the central tasks of the writers in this chapter, and the strategies they adopted, humor, scholarly evidence, personal testimony, literary skill, and collective petition, varied with their circumstances.

Section 92.6

Sarah Winnemucca, Susette La Flesche Tibbles, and the Politics of Public Testimony

Sarah Winnemucca

Sarah Winnemucca, whose Northern Paiute name is usually given as Thocmetony, was born around 1844 in the Great Basin, in the region that is now northwestern Nevada. Her grandfather, known as Truckee, and her father, Winnemucca, were leaders among Northern Paiute bands, and she grew up in a period in which emigrant traffic, mining, and settlement transformed her people's lands. She learned English and Spanish and became a skilled interpreter. She worked as an interpreter and intermediary at military posts and Indian agencies in Nevada and Oregon, and during the Bannock War of 1878 she served the army as a scout and interpreter. Her loyalties and her motives in that conflict have been debated, but the evidence shows that she was trying to protect Paiute people from violence and starvation. After the war, many Paiute people who had lived at the Malheur Reservation in Oregon were forced to relocate to the Yakama Reservation in Washington Territory, a journey and exile that she denounced.

Winnemucca became a public speaker in San Francisco in 1879 and then in the East, where reformers received her as a witness to the injustice of federal Indian policy. She lectured in Boston and elsewhere in 1883 and 1884. Her book, Life Among the Piutes: Their Wrongs and Claims, appeared in 1883, edited by Mary Peabody Mann, widow of the educator Horace Mann, with the support of Mann's sister Elizabeth Palmer Peabody. The book combines memoir, ethnography, and political indictment. It describes Northern Paiute social life, recounts violent encounters with emigrants and soldiers, narrates her own role as interpreter, and accuses specific agents of dishonesty and neglect. She argued for fair treatment of the Paiute and sought land for them, and she used the book and her lectures to press the case. She also founded a school for Paiute children at Lovelock, Nevada, in 1884, supported by Peabody and other patrons and closed in 1887, an effort that shows her commitment to Indigenous education on terms she could influence. She died in 1891 in Montana.

The mediation in her case is documented and important. Mann edited the manuscript, corrected spelling and grammar, and contributed to its organization, and she added a preface for a reform audience. Historians have therefore asked how much of the text's phrasing, ordering, and emphasis reflects Winnemucca and how much her editor and her intended readers. The book nevertheless reflects an author with command of the material, a clear political purpose, and a willingness to confront powerful people. Her critics included agents and some army officers who disputed her accounts, and her authority was also questioned by people within the Paiute community and by those who viewed her as too close to the army. Her contemporaries thus saw her in sharply different ways, and historians have continued to debate her legacy.

Susette La Flesche Tibbles

Susette La Flesche (Inshta Theamba, "Bright Eyes") was born in 1854 on the Omaha reservation in Nebraska and was a daughter of Joseph La Flesche, a leading Omaha figure who supported education and agricultural change. Her siblings included Francis La Flesche, discussed in Section 92.11, and Susan La Flesche Picotte, who became a physician. Susette attended the Presbyterian mission school on the reservation and later a school in New Jersey, and she taught at the Omaha agency school. Her command of English made her a natural interpreter.

The Ponca campaign made her a national figure. The Ponca had been removed from their Niobrara homeland to Indian Territory in 1877, with severe loss of life, and in 1879 Standing Bear's party, having returned north, was arrested. Thomas Henry Tibbles, an editor of the Omaha Daily Herald, publicized the case. Attorneys took up the habeas corpus petition, and Judge Dundy ruled in the Ponca's favor. Susette La Flesche interpreted for Standing Bear in court. In the months after the case she and Standing Bear, with Tibbles, toured eastern cities to raise support, and she spoke to large audiences in her own right. Her presence on the platform as a young, educated Omaha woman who could explain the Ponca case in her own words was a powerful rhetorical fact, and many contemporaries commented on it. In 1886 and 1887 she lectured in Britain.

She was also a writer. A novel by Tibbles, Ploughed Under (1881), appeared under her Omaha name as author, while Tibbles stood behind the text, an arrangement that illustrates how authorship and credit were negotiated in reform publishing. She wrote articles and illustrated books. She also took part in debates within her own community. Like some members of her family she was at first sympathetic to allotment, which she and others hoped would protect Omaha landholding, and she worked with the ethnologist Alice Cunningham Fletcher, who administered Omaha allotment. The outcomes of allotment, examined in Chapter 86, led many Indigenous people to reassess such hopes. Susette died in 1903.

Possibilities and Constraints

Taken together, the two women show both what public testimony could do and where it was limited. It gave them visibility, income, and access to influential people, and it enabled them to challenge official accounts directly. It helped build reform support, which could lead to concrete results: the Ponca case produced a precedent and a campaign that eventually restored part of the Ponca homeland to some members of the nation. But the audiences who welcomed them often wanted a particular narrative, one of suffering that could be cured by assimilation, citizenship, and education. Such audiences might applaud a speaker's eloquence while ignoring the political claims she made. Reformers sometimes treated the speakers as symbols of what "civilization" could achieve. The speakers adapted. They told painful truths, used humor and rhetoric, named specific wrongdoers, and shaped their presentations for different crowds, but they could not control the way listeners and reviewers used them.

Section 92.7

E. Pauline Johnson and the Emergence of Indigenous Literary Performance

Emily Pauline Johnson, who used the Mohawk name Tekahionwake in her public career, was born in 1861 at Chiefswood, on the Six Nations of the Grand River reserve in Ontario. Her father, George H. M. Johnson, was a Mohawk chief who served as an interpreter and official; her mother, Emily Howells Johnson, was English-born. The family's household placed her at the intersection of Haudenosaunee community life and the literary culture of Anglo-Canadian society. She began publishing poems in the 1880s, and in January 1892 she performed in Toronto, beginning a stage career that took her across Canada, to the United States, and to Britain.

Her performances took a distinctive form. She recited her poems, many of which addressed Indigenous subjects, and she organized her programs in two parts, appearing in an invented Indigenous-style costume in the first and in contemporary evening dress in the second. The structure invites interpretation. It allowed her to meet an audience's expectations of the "Indian" while also presenting herself as a modern, cultivated woman, and it enabled her to claim two identities in one evening. She toured for years with a collaborator and endured the strains of travel, venues of uneven quality, and the commercial pressures of a touring career. Her stage persona was thus a negotiated construction rather than a transparent representation of Mohawk culture, and historians caution against reading her costume or programs as evidence of Haudenosaunee practice generally.

Her books include The White Wampum (1895), published in London, and Canadian Born (1903), with later collections such as Flint and Feather (1912) appearing late in her career. Her poetry combined contemporary literary forms, including conventional meter and imagery drawn from British and Canadian verse, with Indigenous subject matter and expressions of pride in her Mohawk heritage. Some poems addressed the treatment of Indigenous people and the position of women, and others celebrated landscape and canoe travel. Critics have debated how far she transcended or reproduced the conventions of her time. A fair assessment recognizes both: she worked within the genres her publishers and audiences expected while using them to assert Indigenous presence in Canadian literature.

Her later work involved collaboration with Squamish knowledge holders. After settling in Vancouver, she became acquainted with Chief Joe Capilano (Sa7plek), whom she had met in London in 1906 when he was there with a delegation. Capilano and other Squamish people told her narratives, and she wrote them for the Vancouver newspaper The Province and then gathered them in Legends of Vancouver, published in 1911. The book thus reflects Squamish stories, as told and as shaped by Johnson's literary style and her readers' expectations. The collaboration illustrates a general feature of Indigenous-authored literature in this period: published narratives often resulted from cooperation among several people, with Indigenous knowledge holders as sources and the credited author as an interpreter. Whether Johnson's renderings adequately represent the stories as Squamish people understood them, and how Squamish people now regard the book, are questions that require attention to Squamish sources and are part of continuing discussion.

Johnson died in Vancouver in 1913, and her memorial in Stanley Park was dedicated in 1922. Her significance for this chapter lies in three areas. She showed that an Indigenous woman could build a professional career as a poet and performer. She demonstrated the commercial and cultural pressures such a career faced. And she exemplified the problem of representation: audiences saw "the Indian poetess," a role that was both a platform and a constraint. Her work should not be treated as a straightforward record of Mohawk or Haudenosaunee tradition. Her own Mohawk and English family background, her Canadian nationalism, her literary training, and her performance career all shaped what she wrote. The Six Nations political and cultural life of her time, including the debates over governance at Grand River, belongs to other chapters and should be consulted for a fuller picture.

Section 92.8

Alexander Posey, Muscogee Journalism, and Political Satire

Alexander Lawrence Posey was born in 1873 near Eufaula in the Creek (Muscogee) Nation, Indian Territory, to a Muscogee mother and a father of white ancestry. He grew up in a community that spoke Muscogee and English and in a nation whose government retained real authority. He attended Bacone Indian University (now Bacone College) near Muskogee, where he began writing poetry and developed an interest in journalism. In the 1890s he held positions in Muscogee national government and as a school administrator, including at the national orphan asylum, and he served in the national legislature. These roles gave him close knowledge of Muscogee politics at a time when the Dawes Commission was negotiating the end of the nation's collective landholding and government.

Posey's early reputation rested on poetry, much of it about the landscape and about nature, in the contemporary literary style, and he published in regional papers. In 1902 he became editor of the Indian Journal, published at Eufaula, and used it for political commentary. His most significant work was the series of "Fus Fixico Letters," which began to appear in 1902. They were written in the voice of Fus Fixico, a full-blood Muscogee traditionalist who addressed letters to the editor and relayed conversations among his friends, including a character named Hotgun, in a comic English with Muscogee-inflected phrasing. The letters ran for several years and were reprinted in other newspapers. Their invented voice allowed Posey to comment on political leaders, federal policy, the Dawes Commission, Muscogee politics, the pressure for a single state of Oklahoma, and President Theodore Roosevelt, all with an ironic detachment that a signed editorial might not have permitted.

The satire raises questions of interpretation. The dialect and humor risk reproducing stereotypes, and some critics have argued that Posey's use of a broken English voice played to settler audiences. Others emphasize that the form allowed him to articulate a community perspective, to expose the self-interest of politicians, and to ridicule federal officials. Both readings find support in the texts. The letters show Posey's command of political detail and his skill with irony. They also show that he treated Muscogee politics, not only federal policy, as a legitimate target, criticizing leaders in his own nation as well as outsiders.

Posey's own positions were complicated and should not be reduced to a label. He was not a simple accommodationist, and he was not an unqualified resister. He worked as a translator and then fieldworker for the Dawes Commission beginning in 1904, even while his political satire criticized the commission’s methods and the fraud surrounding land transfers. The distinction between paid commission employment and endorsement of its policies is essential: his record cannot be reduced to simple approval of allotment. He apparently accepted that allotment and statehood were likely, while attacking their abuses and the opportunism of politicians who profited from them. His treatment of full-blood traditionalists in the letters mixes sympathy and gentle mockery. Such ambiguity is characteristic of a writer who lived inside the institutions he criticized and who wrote for a mixed audience of Muscogee, other Indian Territory readers, and non-Indigenous Oklahomans. Historians recognize in him a participant in the political debates of his nation, not merely a commentator on them.

Posey drowned in 1908 in the North Canadian River, at about thirty-four years of age. His widow, Minnie Posey, published a collection of his poems in 1910, and scholars later gathered the Fus Fixico letters in modern editions. For this chapter, Posey's importance is twofold. First, he shows how Indigenous journalism could use literary voice to criticize colonial authorities and community leaders alike, addressing debates inside the nation as well as the larger political struggle over its dissolution. Second, he illustrates the way humor and dialect could be a political tool, ambiguous in its effects but unmistakably Indigenous in its engagement with local politics. The statehood of Oklahoma in 1907 and the allotment era in which he wrote are treated in Chapters 86 and 91.

Section 92.9

Zitkala-Ša: Indigenous Autobiography, Education, and Cultural Criticism

Zitkala-Ša (Red Bird), the name adopted by Gertrude Simmons Bonnin, was born in 1876 on the Yankton Reservation in South Dakota, to a Yankton Dakota mother and a father of European ancestry who was largely absent from her upbringing. At about eight she left home for White's Manual Labor Institute, a Quaker mission boarding school in Indiana, an experience she later described in prose that remains among the best-known accounts of Indigenous schooling. She later attended Earlham College and studied violin at the New England Conservatory in Boston. She taught at Carlisle Indian School from 1897 to 1899, and her relationship with the school's founder, Richard Henry Pratt, deteriorated as she came to question the institution's methods and premises. The experience of being educated, of working inside a school system, and then of criticizing it from outside runs through her writing.

Her literary reputation began with three autobiographical essays in The Atlantic Monthly in January, February, and March 1900, titled "Impressions of an Indian Childhood," "The School Days of an Indian Girl," and "An Indian Teacher Among Indians." They narrate a childhood in a Dakota household, the departure for school, the rituals of the boarding institution, and a return that left her feeling estranged from both worlds. The essays are carefully composed. She uses a child's perspective to expose the school's regimentation without explicit polemic, and she describes a Dakota mother's attention and a daughter's longing in language that gives readers access to Dakota life as a moral and emotional world. She also uses irony, measuring the school's promises of "civilization" against its practices. Her publication in a leading magazine was itself noteworthy, since the Atlantic was read by the educated reform public that supported assimilationist schooling.

She followed these with Old Indian Legends (1901), a retelling of Dakota stories, and with further essays and stories, including "Why I Am a Pagan" and others in the Atlantic and in Harper's early in the 1900s. Many were collected in American Indian Stories (1921), published in Washington, D.C. The volume mixes autobiographical writing, short fiction, and political essays. In it she criticizes missionary condescension, the severing of children from families, and the hypocrisy of institutions that claimed to uplift Indigenous people while treating them as inferior. At the same time she does not reject literacy, art, or Christianity wholesale. She presents Dakota spiritual and ethical traditions as sources of dignity, and she adapts Euro-American literary forms to Dakota subject matter.

Her work extended to music. With the Utah-based composer and teacher William F. Hanson she created The Sun Dance Opera, first performed in Vernal, Utah, in 1913, which drew on Ute and Dakota material and reflected collaboration across cultures, though its representation of ceremony has been discussed critically. She lived at the Uintah and Ouray Reservation in Utah for many years with her husband, Raymond Bonnin, a Yankton Dakota who worked in the Indian Service. Her activism, including her work with the Society of American Indians and her founding of the National Council of American Indians in 1926, is treated in Sections 92.13 and 92.17, and her role as a critic of federal and corporate abuses in the 1920s belongs in part to Chapter 95. This section concentrates on her writing.

Her literary accomplishment lies in the tension she sustains. The institutions that shaped her education gave her the English and the platform she used, yet her work challenges their cultural assumptions. She describes the value of her Dakota upbringing and the injuries of school with an equanimity that makes the critique more telling. Her stories portray Indigenous characters with interior lives, which was uncommon in popular fiction. Historians and literary scholars have asked how to read her relationship to Christianity, to assimilation, and to Dakota tradition, and the evidence supports a nuanced answer: she was neither an uncritical product of mission education nor a simple rejecter of it. She was a writer who used what she learned to speak for Indigenous dignity, and who changed her emphasis as political conditions changed.

Historical Vocabulary
Self-Representation

Definition. The practice by which a people or an individual presents their own history, identity, and claims in their own voice, rather than being described by outsiders.

Historical context. Between 1880 and 1930, Indigenous writers sought to counter stereotypes in popular literature, scholarship, and journalism. The achievement was real but partial, because publishers, editors, translators, and audiences mediated the results.

Why it matters. The concept highlights the contest over who has authority to define Indigenous people and distinguishes Indigenous-authored accounts from accounts written about them.

Potential misunderstanding. Self-representation does not guarantee unmediated authenticity or consensus within a community. Individual authors spoke from specific positions and often differed from one another.

Section 92.10

Charles Alexander Eastman and the Indigenous Interpretation of Modern America

Charles Alexander Eastman was born in 1858 in Minnesota into a Santee Dakota family and was given the name Hakadah, later Ohiyesa. The Dakota War of 1862 (Chapter 73) separated him from his father, and he was raised by his grandmother and an uncle, with whom he lived in Canada. In 1873 his father, who had been imprisoned after the war and had become a Christian, returned and took him to a homestead in Dakota Territory, where he began formal schooling. He attended the Santee Normal Training School, Beloit College, Knox College, and Dartmouth College, graduating from Dartmouth in 1887, and then earned a medical degree from Boston University in 1890. His education was supported by missionaries and patrons and followed a path almost unique among Indigenous students of his generation.

In late 1890 Eastman became the physician at the Pine Ridge agency in South Dakota, arriving shortly before the killing of Lakota people at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890, examined in Chapter 90. He treated survivors brought to the agency, and in the days after a blizzard he took part in a search of the battlefield for the wounded. His own accounts, written later, are a source for the aftermath, though like all retrospective testimony they should be read alongside other evidence. In 1891 he married Elaine Goodale, a New England writer and reformer who worked in Indian education, and who assisted in editing and publishing his early books. He resigned from Pine Ridge in 1893 after disagreements with agency officials, and he later worked for the YMCA and in other positions.

His literary career took off after 1900. Indian Boyhood (1902) recounted his early years among the Dakota, presenting a picture of childhood education, kinship, and the natural world. The Soul of the Indian (1911) offered an interpretation of Native religious thought for a general readership, stressing continuity between Indigenous and Christian ethical concerns. From the Deep Woods to Civilization (1916) narrated his path from Dakota childhood through schooling and medicine to public life, with commentary on federal policy and reform. His other writings included collections of tales and works for younger readers. He lectured widely and took part in national discussions of Indian policy. He also assisted the federal project, in the early twentieth century, of assigning family names to Sioux people for allotment and inheritance records, a task that put him in the position of implementing policy while he criticized parts of it.

Eastman's writing was shaped by his audience. Readers and publishers wanted accounts of "the vanishing Indian," and they responded to the combination of authenticity and refinement that he offered. He often emphasized similarities between Dakota ethics and Christian teaching and the moral soundness of traditional life, and he presented Indigenous people as capable of full participation in modern society. Some modern critics see in this a degree of accommodation to assimilationist assumptions, since he accepted the language of "civilization" and allotment. Others point out that he also defended Dakota culture and criticized fraud, agency corruption, and the failures of federal policy. The best reading acknowledges both features, along with the reality that his editors and his wife influenced the shape of his books.

In the Society of American Indians, which he helped found, Eastman was a prominent figure, though he later withdrew from active leadership as the organization's disagreements deepened. His career shows an Indigenous intellectual interpreting Dakota knowledge to national audiences while working within the expectations of reform institutions, churches, and publishers. It also shows that the same figure could serve as a federal employee, a critic of federal policy, a defender of Indigenous spirituality, and a popular author, without any of these roles canceling the others.

Section 92.11

Francis La Flesche and the Rise of Indigenous Anthropology

Francis La Flesche, born in 1857, was a member of one of the most prominent Omaha families, son of Joseph La Flesche and half-brother of Susette La Flesche Tibbles and Susan La Flesche Picotte. Raised on the Omaha reservation in Nebraska, he attended the Presbyterian mission school at Bellevue, an experience he later recalled in The Middle Five. In 1881 he took a position in the Office of Indian Affairs in Washington, working as a clerk, and over the next years he studied law at night, earning degrees in the early 1890s. His career was shaped by an association that began in the 1880s with the ethnologist Alice Cunningham Fletcher, whom he met when she worked among the Omaha.

The partnership with Fletcher was productive and unequal. Fletcher, a non-Indigenous researcher with institutional connections to Harvard's Peabody Museum and the Smithsonian, relied on La Flesche's knowledge of Omaha language, ritual, and social life, as well as his access to elders. La Flesche in turn gained a role in research that his formal status might not otherwise have afforded. Together they produced works on Omaha music and social organization, culminating in The Omaha Tribe, a large monograph published in the Bureau of American Ethnology's annual report in 1911. The title page credited Fletcher first, though La Flesche's contribution was substantial, and scholars have since given his role fuller recognition. They also worked on the allotment of land among the Omaha and, later, the Nez Perce, assignments that placed both in the machinery of federal policy.

The Omaha Sacred Pole, known as Umon'hon'ti, illustrates the complexities of this collaboration. In 1888, with the agreement of its Omaha keeper and of others who were concerned for its preservation as the community changed, Fletcher and La Flesche arranged for the Pole to be deposited in the Peabody Museum at Harvard. The decision reflected Omaha leaders' fears about the future and the confidence they placed in La Flesche. The Pole remained in the museum for a century and was returned to the Omaha in 1989, in a repatriation that anticipated the principles later written into federal law (Chapter 125). The episode shows that Indigenous scholars could be agents in the movement of objects to museums, and that later communities have evaluated those decisions in light of changing circumstances.

In 1910 La Flesche joined the Bureau of American Ethnology as an ethnologist, a notable appointment for an Indigenous scholar. From that position he began an extended project among the Osage in Oklahoma, working with ritual specialists who held, recited, and explained the Osage rites. He recorded texts in Osage, translated them, and published a series of monographs on Osage ceremonies in the bureau's annual reports from the 1910s into the late 1920s. In 1932 the bureau issued his Dictionary of the Osage Language. The work preserved ceremonial and linguistic material of great depth, and it is still consulted by Osage people and by scholars. It was possible because Osage ritual authorities chose to share knowledge with him, in part from concern that the traditions might be lost, and in part from trust in an Indigenous colleague. He retired in 1929 and died in 1932.

His position inside the bureau shows how Indigenous expertise functioned within institutions that limited recognition. La Flesche had unmatched knowledge, yet he held no doctoral degree and worked in a setting that gave senior authority to others. Indigenous ethnologists of his generation faced similar conditions. J. N. B. Hewitt, a Tuscarora scholar, worked at the bureau from 1886 and contributed major studies of Iroquoian languages and traditions. William Jones, a Meskwaki (Fox) scholar, earned a doctorate under Franz Boas at Columbia in 1904 and did research on Algonquian languages before his death in the Philippines in 1909. These men demonstrate that an Indigenous presence in ethnology existed from early in the discipline's history, though it remained small and its authority contested. La Flesche's work also raises questions that later generations of Indigenous scholars and communities would debate: who owns recorded ceremonial knowledge, how it should be stored and accessed, and what obligations researchers owe to the communities that provided it (Chapter 145).

Everyday Life and Human Experience

A Documented Working Life: Francis La Flesche's Osage Research

La Flesche's research among the Osage, carried out after he joined the Bureau of American Ethnology in 1910 and published across the following two decades, is a well-documented example of the daily work of an Indigenous scholar. The enterprise depended on relationships rather than institutions alone. Osage ritual authorities, who held responsibility for particular rites, recited and explained long ceremonial texts, and La Flesche transcribed the language, rendered translations, and added explanatory notes. The process required time, patience, and the willingness of the knowledge holders, who weighed the risks and benefits of sharing material that had been transmitted within their own societies. Because La Flesche was Indigenous, spoke an Omaha language closely related to Osage, and understood protocol, he could conduct the work in ways an outsider could not.

The work also placed him at a point of tension. He was a federal employee whose research was published by a government bureau, yet the knowledge he recorded belonged to communities whose religion and ceremonies had been restricted by federal policy. The records he created preserved material that might otherwise have been lost, and they now serve Osage communities seeking to revive and study their traditions. But the same records raise questions about consent, ownership, and access that later generations continue to address. Historians reconstructing his daily work rely on his published monographs, his correspondence, and the bureau's records, which show the outline of his activity but seldom the inner life of the Osage speakers who made it possible.

Section 92.12

Arthur C. Parker, Indigenous Historians, Museums, and Scholarly Authority

Arthur Caswell Parker was born in 1881 on the Cattaraugus Reservation in New York to a Seneca family with a record of public service; his great-uncle Ely S. Parker had assisted Lewis Henry Morgan's Haudenosaunee research in the 1840s and 1850s and later served as Commissioner of Indian Affairs. Arthur worked first as an assistant in museum and archaeological research and in 1904 joined the New York State Museum in Albany. In 1906 he was appointed State Archaeologist. He conducted excavations across New York, wrote reports and monographs, and developed expertise in Iroquoian material culture and history. In 1924 he became director of the Rochester Municipal Museum, which grew into the Rochester Museum of Arts and Sciences, and he held that position until the 1940s.

His scholarship combined archaeology, ethnology, and history. The Constitution of the Five Nations (1916), published as a bulletin of the New York State Museum, presented an English-language version of the oral tradition of the founding of the Haudenosaunee confederacy and the Great Law, using text provided by Seneca and other Haudenosaunee knowledge holders and by earlier scholars. Seneca Myths and Folk Tales (1923) collected narratives for publication, and An Analytical History of the Seneca Indians and works on New York archaeology placed Haudenosaunee history in a long local sequence. His writings addressed both scholarly and public audiences and sought to correct popular stereotypes. He also engaged contemporary questions about museum display and about the treatment of Indigenous remains and sacred objects, though the norms of his era differed from those of later decades.

Parker's career exemplifies a distinction central to this chapter: the difference between Indigenous knowledge being collected by outside researchers and Indigenous scholars participating in interpretation, publication, and institutional decision-making. When non-Indigenous ethnologists recorded stories and ceremonies, they usually kept authorship, control of the manuscripts, and the right to decide on publication. Indigenous scholars like Parker, Hewitt, and La Flesche held a different position. They could shape questions, determine how knowledge was framed, and sometimes control what was published and how communities were credited. Yet their institutions still owned the collections, and the structures of museum and bureau employment limited their autonomy. An Indigenous museum director in the 1920s directed collections that included objects removed from Indigenous communities, and the principles of consent and repatriation that later reshaped museum practice were not yet in force.

Questions of attribution accompanied these issues. Published ethnographic works did not always name the community knowledge holders who supplied narratives, or they named them as "informants" in footnotes while crediting the scholar as author. Parker was more attentive than many contemporaries to acknowledging Haudenosaunee sources, but the conventions of the day still privileged the scholar's name. Scholarly representations also had public effects. Museum exhibits, school textbooks, and popular books shaped public understanding of Indigenous history, whether by presenting Indigenous people as vanished or as living participants in modern society. Indigenous scholars worked to emphasize continuity and complexity, and their influence can be traced in the way later exhibits and publications evolved, though the process was slow.

Parker's political involvement links this section to Division IV. He was among the organizers of the Society of American Indians, serving as secretary-treasurer and editor of its early journal, and later in other leading roles. His position as a state employee and respected scholar gave him credibility but also tied him to institutions whose purposes sometimes conflicted with the positions of more radical reformers. The next division examines how the intellectual networks described here, among writers, scholars, and professionals, became the basis of formal organizations that claimed a national voice. A younger generation of Indigenous scholars, such as the Yankton Dakota ethnologist and linguist Ella Cara Deloria, who began working with Franz Boas in the late 1920s, would build on the foundation that Parker, La Flesche, Hewitt, and Jones had laid.

Section 92.13

The Society of American Indians and the Emergence of National Indigenous Organization

The Society of American Indians (SAI), founded in October 1911 at a conference at Ohio State University in Columbus, was the first national organization in the United States led by Indigenous people from many nations. Its founding drew on networks formed in the preceding decades among graduates of mission and boarding schools, Indigenous professionals, clergy, and employees of the Indian Service, many of whom had met at reform gatherings or corresponded about shared concerns. A sociologist at Ohio State, Fayette A. McKenzie, helped convene the first meeting, and the organization's initial structure included Indigenous "active" members and non-Indigenous "associate" members who could support but not govern. Sherman Coolidge, an Arapaho Episcopal priest, became its first president.

Those involved in the founding and early years included Arthur C. Parker, Sherman Coolidge, Carlos Montezuma, Charles Eastman, Laura Cornelius Kellogg, Charles Dagenett, Thomas Sloan, and, later, Zitkala-Ša and Henry Roe Cloud, among others. The members were mostly educated professionals, and they came from nations in the Plains, Great Lakes, Northeast, Southwest, and Indian Territory. They proposed to promote the advancement of Indigenous people, to provide a forum for discussion, to encourage education and citizenship, to bring Indigenous views to the public, and to improve the administration of Indian affairs. Their annual conferences brought together members, supporters, and officials, and they issued resolutions on education, land, legal status, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs.

The Society's publications were integral to its work. The Quarterly Journal of the Society of American Indians, which began publication in 1913 under Parker's editorship, carried conference proceedings, essays, and news. In 1916 it was succeeded by the American Indian Magazine, a more varied periodical, which Zitkala-Ša edited in 1918 and 1919. These journals were Indigenous-edited vehicles for debating policy, circulating biographies, and presenting Indigenous achievements to non-Indigenous readers. They also reveal the Society's internal arguments. Contributors disagreed about education, allotment, citizenship, and the role of the federal Indian administration. The pages are an important source for the Indigenous intellectual history of the period, though they largely reflect the views of a particular educated and often Christian segment of Indigenous societies.

The Society was internally diverse and persistently divided. Some members, including Montezuma, believed the Bureau of Indian Affairs should be abolished and its functions ended. Others, including those who worked for the Bureau or who thought reform was possible from within, favored changing the institution rather than eliminating it. Disagreements over the participation of Indian Service employees as officers, over the policies of the Society's leadership, and over specific issues such as the use of peyote in religious practice, which a number of members opposed while others defended, strained the group. Members also differed in their relationships to their home communities: some were deeply involved in tribal affairs, while others lived mostly in cities. The conflicts of the mid-1910s, including Montezuma's separation from the leadership after the 1916 conference, were personal as well as political.

The Society's accomplishments were real but limited. It provided a model of intertribal organization, a platform for Indigenous voices at the national level, and a network that outlasted the organization itself. It did not command a mass membership, it struggled financially, and it did not secure its main policy goals. By the early 1920s it had faded, and its successors included Zitkala-Ša's National Council of American Indians (1926), discussed in Section 92.17, and, in later decades, the National Congress of American Indians (Chapter 107). Historians continue to evaluate the Society's significance: some emphasize its unrepresentative membership and its acceptance of assimilationist premises; others stress that it created institutions and habits of organization that later activists used. The evidence supports both statements.

Historical Vocabulary
Political Advocacy

Definition. The organized effort to influence public opinion or government policy through speech, writing, petitions, lobbying, litigation, or association.

Historical context. Between 1880 and 1930, Indigenous advocates used lectures, periodicals, petitions, conventions, and associations. Many nations had long practiced diplomacy and delegation, and the new forms adapted those traditions to settler-state institutions.

Why it matters. The term includes activity by people with no formal office, and it helps explain how Indigenous people influenced policy debates even when excluded from voting or legislative power.

Potential misunderstanding. Advocacy was not the same as representation. Individuals and organizations frequently claimed to speak for more people than they could demonstrably represent, and Indigenous governments and councils held their own political authority.

Cause and Effect

Why a National Indigenous Organization Emerged in 1911

Long-term causes

Traditions of intertribal and colonial diplomacy; nineteenth-century pan-Indigenous movements; the effects of allotment and administrative control that touched many nations at once.

Short-term causes

A growing group of educated Indigenous professionals; school alumni networks; reform conferences where Indigenous participants met; and expanded print and rail connections.

Immediate trigger

An invitation to convene at Ohio State University in 1911, supported by sympathetic non-Indigenous academics and by Indigenous organizers.

Effects

A national forum and journal; a model of intertribal organization; and persistent disagreements that foreshadowed later debates over reform and sovereignty.

Historical complexity: The Society did not arise from a single event or leader. It combined older Indigenous political practices with new educational and communication conditions, and its internal divisions reflected real differences in experience and aim.

Section 92.14

Carlos Montezuma, Laura Cornelius Kellogg, and Competing Visions of Indigenous Independence

Carlos Montezuma (Wassaja) was born about 1866 in what is now Arizona, into a Yavapai community. As a child he was captured during a raid by Pima people and was sold to a photographer, Carlo Gentile, who gave him the name Montezuma and took him east. He was later raised by a Baptist minister's family and educated at the University of Illinois and at a Chicago medical college, receiving his medical degree in 1889. He worked as a physician in the Indian Service at several agencies and at Carlisle, where he became a friend of Richard Henry Pratt, then built a private practice in Chicago. He remained connected to his Yavapai relatives, whom he visited in Arizona, and he later campaigned against schemes to remove the Fort McDowell Yavapai from their lands.

Montezuma's political position hardened into a single demand: end the Bureau of Indian Affairs. He argued that the Bureau kept Indigenous people in dependence, that reservations functioned as confinements, and that federal paternalism was incompatible with freedom and individual responsibility. He believed that Indigenous people should enter American society as citizens and individuals. His criticism was ferocious and personal, and he attacked officials and fellow reformers who disagreed. In 1916 he launched Wassaja: Freedom's Signal for the Indians, a monthly newsletter that he wrote and published from Chicago, bearing his Yavapai name. It carried editorials, reports, and commentary on federal policy until shortly before his death in January 1923. He died at Fort McDowell, having returned to his community in his last illness.

Laura Cornelius Kellogg, an Oneida born in 1880 and raised in Wisconsin, followed a different course. She attended Stockbridge schools, studied at Barnard College and elsewhere, and became an early member and speaker of the Society of American Indians. She also criticized the Bureau and the effects of allotment, but she turned toward building Indigenous institutions rather than dissolving them. Her book Our Democracy and the American Indian (1920) argued that the Haudenosaunee Confederacy offered a model of democratic government, and she proposed that Indigenous communities reorganize on their own terms, with economic self-sufficiency and community-controlled institutions. She promoted a plan, known as the Lolomi Plan, for community development and cooperative economic organization, and she worked on Oneida land and claims issues in New York and Wisconsin. Her program emphasized collective, community-based independence.

The contrast between the two is instructive. Montezuma's vision was individualist and integrationist in its institutional ambition: remove the Bureau, treat Indigenous people as citizens, and allow individuals to make their way. Kellogg's was communal and nation-centered: strengthen Indigenous institutions, build economic independence, and treat Indigenous communities as self-governing political bodies. Both condemned colonial administration as unjust. Both appealed to Indigenous capacity and to democratic ideals. Yet they disagreed fundamentally about what "independence" meant and about which institutions should survive. One imagined a future in which the legal category "Indian" and the reservation would disappear; the other imagined communities continuing as distinct polities with governing institutions of their own.

These disagreements illustrate why Indigenous reformers cannot be treated as a single movement. They shared opposition to arbitrary federal authority, but their reasoning rested on different premises about land, community, citizenship, and tradition. Montezuma's experience as a child removed from his community and his career in the non-Indigenous world informed his emphasis on individual advancement. Kellogg's Haudenosaunee connections and her interest in Oneida land claims shaped her community-centered approach. The debate continued through the twentieth century and into the era of termination and self-determination (Chapters 104 and 114).

Section 92.15

First Nations Political Leadership, Public Petitions, and Canadian Advocacy

In Canada, Indigenous political leadership faced a framework quite different from that of the United States. The Indian Act (Chapter 85) gave the Department of Indian Affairs broad control over band governance, and subsequent amendments restricted movement and organization. On the Prairies, an informal pass system limited travel off reserves after 1885, and later legislation constrained fundraising for the pursuit of land claims, a development addressed in Chapter 98. In British Columbia, where few treaties had been made, Indigenous nations pressed the unresolved question of Aboriginal title. In this setting, petitions, delegations, and memorials became principal instruments of public leadership.

British Columbia's Indigenous leaders developed a tradition of travel and petition. In 1887, Nisga'a and Tsimshian chiefs journeyed to Victoria to press their claims to land and to request a treaty, and they were rebuffed by provincial authorities. In 1906, Joe Capilano (Sa7plek), a Squamish leader, traveled to London with other representatives and was received by King Edward VII, who referred the matter back to the Canadian government. The delegation also made contact with sympathetic organizations in Britain. Capilano was a respected spokesperson in Vancouver, where he spoke for his community's land concerns and cooperated with writers such as Pauline Johnson. He died in 1910. In 1913 the Nisga'a Land Committee petitioned the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, taking the claim to the highest imperial legal body. These actions demonstrate a continuing strategy of appealing beyond provincial authorities to the Crown and Parliament.

The 1910 Memorial to Sir Wilfrid Laurier is the most fully documented of these efforts. In August 1910, when Laurier visited Kamloops during a western tour, chiefs representing the Shuswap (Secwepemc), Okanagan (Syilx), and Thompson (Nlaka'pamux, then often called Couteau) peoples presented him with a written memorial. It asserted that the Interior Salish peoples had never ceded their lands and criticized provincial policy and the inadequacy of reserves. It used an image of hosts and guests: the chiefs described their ancestors as having welcomed newcomers, who gradually took over the house. The memorial asked the federal government to recognize Indigenous title and to settle the land question. The text was prepared with the assistance of James Teit, a Shetland-born ethnologist who lived in the Nicola Valley, was married to a Thompson woman, and served as secretary and intermediary. The chiefs, not Teit, were the document's collective authors and political principals, and Teit's role was that of scribe, translator, and organizer. Laurier's government did not initiate a treaty process, and the land question remained unresolved, but the memorial became a landmark of Indigenous argumentation in Canada.

The memorial also illustrates how written argument adapted older practices. Delegations and speeches addressed to leaders of other nations had long been part of Indigenous politics, and the chiefs now used a printed text to address the Prime Minister in an idiom he could recognize while grounding the claim in their own history and law. The document preserved the form of a council address while employing the vocabulary of rights, title, and treaty. The role of Teit also shows the interplay of Indigenous leadership and non-Indigenous assistance, which could strengthen a text's reach but also complicates the historian's ability to separate the chiefs' words from the secretary's phrasing.

Eastern and Ontario leaders organized differently. Frederick Ogilvie Loft (Onondeyoh), a Mohawk from Six Nations of the Grand River who had served in the Canadian Expeditionary Force in the First World War, founded the League of Indians of Canada in 1919, seeking a national voice for First Nations on issues of governance, treaty rights, and administrative control. The League held conventions in Ontario and in the West and encountered obstacles, including departmental suspicion and the difficulty of communication across vast distances. Its internal history, its regional branches, and the broader pattern of interwar First Nations organizing belong to Chapter 98. For this chapter, Loft marks the point at which Canadian Indigenous organizing began to take national form, parallel to, though distinct from, developments in the United States.

Section 92.16

Alaska Native Leadership, Indigenous Associations, and Regional Political Networks

In southeastern Alaska, Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian leaders created an enduring form of political association. The Alaska Native Brotherhood (ANB) emerged from meetings in Juneau in 1912 among Indigenous men connected with Sitka, Juneau, Wrangell, Klawock, Angoon, and other communities. A further Juneau meeting in 1913 helped establish constitutional arrangements, elected leadership, and a network of local camps or chapters; Sitka Camp No. 1 became a central institutional base. The Brotherhood drew on school-educated members and Indigenous political traditions rather than belonging simply to a school or missionary organization. The ANB’s own Manual of Ceremonies lists no elected grand president for 1912 and identifies Peter Simpson, a Tsimshian leader associated with Sitka Camp No. 1, as grand president beginning in 1913. The dates therefore distinguish 1912 organizing from 1913 elected grand leadership. William Paul, a Tlingit lawyer and later legislator, rose to prominence in subsequent years rather than serving as a member of the original founding cohort.

The Brotherhood developed an organizational language that could reach different communities. It adopted a constitution, held conventions, kept minutes, elected officers, passed resolutions, and sent letters and petitions to territorial and federal officials. Its local camps linked villages across a marine geography in which boat travel could determine who could attend a meeting and when an announcement arrived. Leaders appealed for schooling, civil rights, suffrage, and fair treatment in public accommodations while also debating the role of Indigenous cultures and institutions. The organization’s newspaper The Alaska Fisherman, published from 1923 to 1932, extended its communications beyond any single convention. It should not be confused with the later Voice of Brotherhood, which began publication decades afterward. Public speeches and printed resolutions were new media for older responsibilities: deliberating about the community’s future, negotiating with outsiders, and keeping relationships among families and villages.

Indigenous women helped develop a parallel organizational tradition through the Alaska Native Sisterhood (ANS), but its founding chronology is not settled by a single accepted date. Josephine Ukas’s retrospective account, discussed by the Sealaska Heritage Institute, recalls organizing meetings at Wrangell in autumn 1914. The U.S. National Library of Medicine dates establishment to 1915 and places organizing activity at Haines; Alaska’s own historical chronology also records the first ANS convention in 1915 at Sitka. These sources need not describe exactly the same institutional milestone: informal local organization, establishment of a camp, and a wider convention could occur at different times and places. Because the early officer records are incomplete and later recollections diverge, the identities and sequence of the first grand officers must not be asserted as settled. What is clear is that Alaska Native women organized alongside and in partnership with Brotherhood members, developing public, educational, welfare, and civil-rights initiatives.

The Brotherhood's agenda included challenges to segregation in schools and public accommodations, claims to fishing rights as canneries and non-Native fishing interests expanded, and arguments for citizenship and voting rights, whose legal history belongs to Chapters 93 and 99. The later statutory and land settlements of Alaska, including the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (Chapter 115), lie beyond this chapter. Here the emphasis is on the intellectual and organizational emergence: a group of Native leaders who used public speaking, constitutions, conventions, correspondence, and petitions to articulate demands, and who constructed institutions that endured. Tlingit people also participated in scholarship and museum work in this period; Louis Shotridge, a Tlingit researcher from Klukwan, worked with the University of Pennsylvania Museum from the early 1910s, conducted collection and documentation work, and received a formal assistant-curator appointment in 1922. The Penn Museum’s archival finding aids distinguish his earlier research and collecting from that later curatorial title. His record raises questions similar to those discussed in Sections 92.11 and 92.12 about scholarly credit, community knowledge, and museum authority.

Section 92.17

Indigenous Women as Writers, Organizers, and Public Intellectuals

Women appear throughout this chapter: as interpreters, lecturers, poets, memoirists, editors, teachers, organizers, and critics. This section synthesizes their contributions and asks what was distinctive about their positions. The five most prominent figures, Sarah Winnemucca, Susette La Flesche Tibbles, E. Pauline Johnson, Zitkala-Ša, and Laura Cornelius Kellogg, worked in different decades, nations, and genres, and their careers should be compared without being merged.

Their strategies differed. Winnemucca built her public career on testimony and indictment, using the authority of an eyewitness and interpreter to accuse officials and to plead for land. Susette La Flesche Tibbles used the platform created by a legal case and a publicity campaign, speaking as an interpreter and advocate, and she collaborated with her husband's journalism. Johnson used performance and poetry, shaping an identity that held audiences' attention while asserting Indigenous presence. Zitkala-Ša employed literary autobiography and fiction, then moved to organization and political lobbying. Kellogg wrote political argument and community plans, focusing on institutions. Each adapted to the opportunities available. Taken together, they show that women's public leadership took multiple forms, and that none of these forms was a mere substitute for men's political authority.

Gender shaped the barriers they faced. In publishing and lecturing, women were expected to embody certain roles: the suffering victim, the exotic performer, the gracious educated example. Sponsors and editors, mostly non-Indigenous reformers, often supervised their work. Winnemucca's reliance on the Peabody sisters, Johnson's dependence on touring managers and booking agents, and Zitkala-Ša's negotiations with publishers and school officials illustrate the dependence on patrons. Professional institutions offered few openings. Women who sought careers in medicine, law, and scholarship, such as Susan La Flesche Picotte, who earned a medical degree in 1889 and practiced among the Omaha, and Marie Bottineau Baldwin, a Turtle Mountain Ojibwe woman who worked in Washington and obtained a law degree, made their way against both racial and gender discrimination. Angel De Cora, a Ho-Chunk artist who taught art at Carlisle, belongs to the same generation. Within reform networks, women were frequently admitted as members but given less authority.

Their intellectual work was entangled with kinship and community responsibility. Many of these women understood their public roles as extensions of obligations to family and nation. Winnemucca's lectures aimed at the survival of her people and were linked to her family's long role as intermediaries. Susette La Flesche's activity arose from her family's leadership among the Omaha and her ties to siblings who became a scholar and a physician. Zitkala-Ša's activism was carried out in partnership with her husband and in relation to Dakota communities, and Kellogg's plans were rooted in Oneida concerns. Women's organizations such as the Alaska Native Sisterhood in Alaska show another dimension: women established institutions that managed community welfare and political advocacy in their own right. Treating women's intellectual work as separate from family and community can miss the way Indigenous women often understood leadership as embedded in relationships.

Zitkala-Ša's trajectory illustrates how literary work could lead to organizational leadership. After her early publications and her years in Utah, she moved to Washington, D.C., in 1917 and became secretary of the Society of American Indians and editor of the American Indian Magazine. When the Society faded, she and Raymond Bonnin founded the National Council of American Indians in 1926, with the goal of uniting Indigenous people for political purposes, and she served as its president for the rest of her life. Her work with this organization and with reform groups in the 1920s, including investigations of exploitation in Oklahoma, is considered in Chapter 95. The National Council of American Indians should not be confused with the later National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944 (Chapter 107). In the 1920s her leadership demonstrated that an Indigenous woman could direct a national political organization, an achievement that cannot be separated from the work of the earlier generation, from Winnemucca's testimony to Johnson's stage career, that had made Indigenous women's public voices familiar.

Section 92.18

Indigenous-Language Literature, Greenlandic Intellectuals, and Continental Diversity

An account confined to English-language writers would misrepresent the continent. Indigenous peoples wrote and published in their own languages, and in many places the language of intellectual life was not English. This section extends the chapter to Greenland and Mexico, and it does so with an important caution: these regions did not form a single Indigenous literary movement. Their colonial settings, educational systems, religious institutions, and political circumstances differed profoundly. The purpose is comparison, not synthesis.

Greenland

Greenlandic, or Kalaallisut, is an Inuit language with a literate tradition developed in close connection with the Lutheran mission and Danish administration. Samuel Kleinschmidt, a German missionary, published a Greenlandic grammar and established a standardized orthography in the middle of the nineteenth century, which remained in use until a spelling reform in 1973. A printing press operated at the Greenlandic capital, and the newspaper Atuagagdliutit, first issued in 1861 under the Danish administrator and scholar Hinrich Rink, printed Greenlandic contributions. A teacher-training seminary at Godthåb (Nuuk), founded in the 1840s, produced catechists and teachers who formed a Greenlandic educated class. Greenlandic writers recorded oral traditions: Aron of Kangeq, an artist and narrator, produced illustrations and texts that Rink drew upon for his published collections of traditional tales. The literature thus arose within a colonial system that made Greenlandic-language print possible and also constrained it.

By the early twentieth century a Greenlandic public voice was visible. Henrik Lund, a poet and artist, composed in 1912 the poem that became Greenland's national anthem. Danish administrative reforms in 1911 introduced consultative provincial councils. Mathias Storch, a Greenlandic writer, published Singnagtugaq in Copenhagen in 1914, generally identified as the first novel in Greenlandic; the spelling Sinnattugaq appears in later bibliographical and orthographic forms. Knud Rasmussen’s Danish translation, En Grønlænders drøm, followed in 1915. The novel imagines Greenland’s future from within Greenlandic society and addresses questions of modernization, education, political representation, and Greenlanders’ control of their own institutions. The work shows that Greenlandic intellectuals were engaging with the same issues of modernity, education, and identity that occupied writers elsewhere, but in Greenlandic, for Greenlandic readers, and under Danish colonial administration. The relationship between writing in an Indigenous language, translation, mission education, and political thought was thus distinctive: Greenlandic literacy was widespread and church-linked, and political claims often emerged through consultative bodies and the press rather than through petitions to an imperial center. The later political history of Greenland is treated in Chapters 100 and 148.

Mexico

In Mexico, Indigenous-language intellectual traditions had colonial roots. Maya communities in Yucatán preserved and recopied prophetic and historical texts, known as the books of Chilam Balam, across centuries, and communities in central Mexico kept colonial-era titles and land records that they used in legal disputes. In the nineteenth century, the Caste War of Yucatán, which began in 1847, produced a Maya polity centered at Chan Santa Cruz whose leaders corresponded with neighboring authorities, including those of British Honduras, in documents that mixed Spanish and Maya contexts. In the revolutionary era after 1910, villages in Morelos and elsewhere argued for the restoration of lands on the basis of long-held community titles, and Article 27 of the Constitution of 1917 created procedures for land restitution and distribution through petitions and the ejido system. In Yucatán, reforms under governors in the late 1910s and early 1920s, including leagues of resistance associated with Felipe Carrillo Puerto, who spoke Maya, involved Maya-speaking communities in political organization.

The difficulties facing historians here are real. Community-authored documents, when they exist, are often found in municipal, judicial, or agrarian archives where they are classified by legal category, not by the authorship or ethnicity of those who wrote them. Petitions were frequently drafted by local scribes, lawyers, or officials, and thus reflect mediation. National policy after the Revolution emphasized the identity of the peasant, or campesino, which can obscure Indigenous authorship and community identity in the records. Intellectuals writing about Indigenous peoples, such as Manuel Gamio, whose book Forjando patria appeared in 1916, belonged to the movement known as indigenismo, in which non-Indigenous or mestizo thinkers sought to define Indigenous people's place in the nation. Tracing Indigenous-authored voices requires searching for community titles, petitions, letters, and oral histories, and it is an area of ongoing research. Other Indigenous peoples of northern Mexico, including the Yaqui and Rarámuri, appear in Chapter 141.

Elsewhere on the continent, Indigenous-language writing continued in several forms: the Cherokee syllabary in the Southeast and Indian Territory, bilingual Dakota publications at Santee, Cree and Inuit syllabic literacy in Canada, and Haudenosaunee ritual and political texts preserved in the Longhouse tradition. These examples caution against assuming that Indigenous intellectual life moved only toward English. They also show that the questions this chapter has raised, about mediation, translation, and control of knowledge, appeared in many languages and legal settings. The future of Indigenous languages in education and public life is taken up in Chapter 123.

Section 92.19

Indigenous Modernity: Assimilation, Autonomy, Reform, and Competing Political Futures

How did Indigenous intellectuals understand modernity? The question has no single answer. The figures in this chapter lived in a world of railroads, newspapers, universities, and electoral politics, and they had to decide how to relate to it. They disagreed about education, citizenship, tribal institutions, cultural survival, and political independence, and their disagreements shaped the debates of the following decades. Understanding those disagreements prevents the error of treating Indigenous intellectuals as a bloc.

Consider the range. Montezuma urged the abolition of the Bureau and the integration of Indigenous people as individual citizens, rejecting reservation life as confinement. Kellogg advocated community institutions, economic independence, and a political structure drawing on Haudenosaunee precedent. Eastman defended the moral and spiritual integrity of Dakota life while accepting that adaptation to modern society was necessary, and he served the government as well as criticizing it. Zitkala-Ša criticized assimilationist schooling and later organized politically for reform and for the defense of Indigenous rights. Parker pursued scholarly institution-building and organized for reform within the system. Posey exposed the abuses of allotment while participating in its implementation. Winnemucca and Susette La Flesche Tibbles asked for protection and land, and, like some others of their era, at times saw education and individual landholding as protective measures. Capilano and the Interior Salish chiefs insisted on recognition of Indigenous title and treaty-making. Alaska Native Brotherhood leaders sought civil equality and organizational strength. Each offered a distinct answer to the question of what a good Indigenous future would look like.

Participation in national institutions did not necessarily mean abandoning Indigenous identity. A Seneca archaeologist directing a museum, a Dakota physician serving at an agency, and an Omaha ethnologist in a federal bureau all remained members of their nations and often worked to protect knowledge and rights. Likewise, criticizing federal or Canadian institutions did not always mean rejecting every modern political structure. Montezuma attacked the Bureau but embraced citizenship; Kellogg attacked allotment but built plans for modern economic organizations. Many Indigenous intellectuals saw modern tools, such as literacy, law, medicine, and associations, as available instruments, to be used for purposes they defined. At the same time, critics within Indigenous communities questioned whether those who worked within non-Indigenous institutions could represent community interests, and the criticism was sometimes warranted.

The legal history of citizenship, including the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 in the United States and the status provisions of Canadian law, belongs to Chapter 93 and must be distinguished from the intellectual disagreements described here. Intellectuals debated what citizenship should mean and whether to seek it; the law determined what status individuals and nations in fact held, and in many cases without consultation. Similarly, the organized reform campaigns of the 1920s are treated in Chapter 95. The present section is concerned with the range of ideas, not with legislative outcomes.

Historical Debate

Did Western Education and National Publishing Institutions Empower or Constrain Indigenous Intellectual Leadership?

The question: Historians and literary scholars have long asked whether the schools, publishers, universities, and museums through which Indigenous intellectuals gained skills and audiences primarily enabled their leadership or primarily confined it within colonial expectations. The question is both historical and historiographical, because it bears on how the achievements of these figures should be interpreted.

Interpretation A — Empowerment: Education provided literacy, professional credentials, and access to platforms that Indigenous people used to defend their nations, to preserve knowledge, and to criticize policy. Many writers turned their training against the institutions that gave it, and national publishing allowed Indigenous voices to reach influential audiences. On this view, the new institutions were tools that skilled Indigenous people adapted to their own purposes.
Interpretation B — Constraint: Schools and publishers set the terms of what could be said and by whom. Editors shaped manuscripts, audiences expected stereotypes, patrons controlled funding, and professional institutions limited authority and recognition. On this view, the opportunities were real but conditional, and they channeled Indigenous leadership into forms acceptable to colonial institutions, sometimes at the cost of community accountability.
Interpretation C — Variation: Many historians argue that the question cannot be answered in general. The balance of empowerment and constraint differed by individual, institution, nation, gender, and decade. A figure like La Flesche may have gained influence while being constrained by institutional rank, whereas a speaker such as Winnemucca may have gained visibility but lost control over her message.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on the same sources: published works and their editorial histories, correspondence among authors and publishers, institutional employment records, reviews, and the records of Indigenous organizations. Comparison of what individuals wrote with what they were able to publish is especially informative.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and emphasis more than basic facts. Historians differ about how much autonomy individuals had, how to weigh editorial mediation, and whether participation in non-Indigenous institutions implied accommodation.

Why it matters: The answer affects how Indigenous intellectual history is related to the history of assimilation. If institutions primarily empowered, Indigenous intellectual life appears as adaptive and agentive; if they primarily constrained, it appears as resistance under pressure. The evidence supports a more differentiated account in which both processes operated.

Section 92.20

Indigenous Intellectual Leadership by 1930: Public Voice, Institutional Change, and Continuity

By about 1930, Indigenous writers, educators, scholars, editors, and organizers had established an increasingly diverse presence in newspapers, books, academic and museum institutions, political associations, and public debate. The change since 1880 was substantial. A reading public that had once heard Indigenous voices mainly through interpreters at treaty councils or through sympathetic reformers now had access to Indigenous-authored autobiographies, poetry, scholarship, and political argument. Ethnographic and archaeological institutions included Indigenous professionals. National and regional organizations existed in the United States, Canada, and Alaska. Indigenous-language print culture persisted in Greenland and elsewhere. Later in the 1920s other voices were joining them: Luther Standing Bear (Lakota), a Carlisle graduate, published My People the Sioux in 1928; Will Rogers, a Cherokee citizen, reached millions through his newspaper column and radio and stage work, though he seldom spoke as an Indigenous political representative; and Ella Cara Deloria was beginning work as a linguist and ethnologist.

The achievements deserve careful assessment. Indigenous intellectuals corrected misrepresentations, preserved knowledge, created institutions, and articulated arguments that have remained foundational. They also created habits of intertribal communication that later movements would use. Their limitations were equally evident. They lacked control of major publishing and educational institutions, they depended on patrons, and they often struggled financially. Their organizations were small and divided. Their educated, often Christian and English-speaking, membership did not always reflect the views of their communities. Federal and provincial governments often disregarded their arguments. Evaluating the era requires keeping these facts together without reducing the figures to either triumph or tragedy.

Equally important is what continued outside the new institutions. Indigenous languages remained the working languages of many communities. Oral traditions continued to be told and taught, and ceremonial leaders retained authority even where ceremonies were restricted. Kinship networks organized economic and social life. Local councils, band governments, and village and clan authorities made decisions that affected daily life more directly than did any national association. Interior Salish chiefs, Muscogee council members, Osage ritual authorities, Haudenosaunee council leaders, and Tlingit clan leaders exercised intellectual and political authority without publishing a word. The published record underrepresents them, which is a reason for historians to read it with caution and to supplement it with community-based sources and oral history.

The developments described here laid foundations for later struggles. The questions debated by Montezuma, Kellogg, Parker, and Zitkala-Ša, including the status of Indigenous people as citizens, their service in military forces, the reform of federal administration, and the structure of Indigenous governance, would be addressed in Chapter 93 on citizenship, Chapter 94 on the First World War, Chapter 95 on reform movements, and Chapter 97 on the Indian Reorganization Act. The networks and organizations explored here fed into the interwar First Nations politics of Chapter 98 and the Alaska Native and Inuit histories of Chapters 99 and 100. The questions about knowledge, authorship, and community control that arose in scholarship and museums anticipated the debates over repatriation and data sovereignty in Chapters 125 and 145. And the literary tradition that began with Winnemucca, Johnson, Zitkala-Ša, and Posey would continue in the cultural resurgence described in Chapter 126. Mediation of Indigenous testimony also persisted; Black Elk Speaks (1932), produced through an interpreter and a non-Indigenous writer, appeared just after the period considered here.

  • Indigenous professionals entered museums, bureaus, medicine, law, and journalism
  • National and regional associations brought together people from different nations
  • Petitions, memorials, constitutions, and conventions became familiar forms of political communication

What Remained Continuous

  • Oral tradition, ceremonial knowledge, and community-based authority persisted
  • Indigenous languages remained central in many communities and in Greenland's print culture
  • Claims to land, nationhood, and self-government continued under new forms
  • Intellectual leadership remained tied to kinship and community obligation

Timing and pace: Change was gradual and uneven, accelerating after 1900 with the expansion of organizations and publications. Who experienced change most directly: educated professionals, writers, and urban or institution-based leaders. Who experienced greater continuity: community members and leaders whose authority rested on language, ceremony, and local institutions. Why it matters: The pattern shows innovation and continuity operating together, and it cautions against equating public visibility with the whole of Indigenous intellectual life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Indigenous peoples became influential participants in the modern public sphere between about 1880 and 1930 by adapting new forms of communication and organization to purposes rooted in existing nations and traditions. Newspapers, books, lectures, scholarship, associations, and petitions were new instruments, but the intellectual and political leadership that used them did not begin with colonial schools. Oratory, diplomacy, oral history, ceremonial teaching, and legal reasoning long predated these forms and continued alongside them.

Division I established the foundations. Conquest and administration disrupted but did not eliminate Indigenous knowledge systems, and a new figure, the Indigenous public intellectual, appeared. Oral and written knowledge were not rival systems, but translation and editorial mediation shaped the record that survives, so historians must ask who translated, edited, and published each text. Graduates of mission and boarding schools sometimes turned their education against the assumptions of the institutions that provided it, though access was limited and prejudice was pervasive. The infrastructure of presses, rail lines, lecture circuits, and correspondence created opportunities, but economic dependence and audience expectations limited control over representation.

Division II traced journalism and literature. Indigenous periodicals ranged from tribal national papers to school publications and independent editors' papers, and each served different purposes. Sarah Winnemucca and Susette La Flesche Tibbles used testimony and interpretation to address largely non-Indigenous audiences, gaining visibility while facing editorial and audience constraints. E. Pauline Johnson built a literary and performance career that negotiated Indigenous identity, commercial expectations, and collaboration with Squamish knowledge holders. Alexander Posey used satire and the Fus Fixico letters to comment on allotment and statehood, criticizing both colonial authorities and Muscogee leaders in a politically complex position.

Division III examined autobiography and scholarship. Zitkala-Ša's essays and stories turned a literary education toward critique of boarding schools and defense of Dakota dignity. Charles Eastman interpreted Dakota life for national readers while serving in federal and reform institutions. Francis La Flesche, working with Alice Fletcher and later alone at the Bureau of American Ethnology, produced major studies of Omaha and Osage ceremony, while his position illustrated the limits on Indigenous scholarly authority. Arthur Parker and other Indigenous scholars, including J. N. B. Hewitt and William Jones, expanded Indigenous participation in archaeology, ethnology, and museums, raising questions about attribution, ownership, and the difference between being a source and being an interpreter.

Division IV turned to organization. The Society of American Indians, founded in 1911, was the first national intertribal organization in the United States led by Indigenous people, and it published a journal and magazine, held conferences, and struggled with internal division over the Bureau of Indian Affairs, peyote, and the role of federal employees. Carlos Montezuma urged abolition of the Bureau and integration of individuals as citizens, while Laura Cornelius Kellogg promoted community institutions and economic independence on Haudenosaunee-inspired lines. In Canada, delegations and petitions, including the 1906 Squamish delegation to London and the 1910 Memorial to Sir Wilfrid Laurier by Interior Salish chiefs, expressed collective claims to land and title, and Frederick Loft founded the League of Indians of Canada in 1919. In Alaska, the Alaska Native Brotherhood, begun in 1912, combined civil-rights advocacy with parliamentary forms and community organization.

Division V synthesized and broadened. Women's contributions were comparable but distinct in strategy and constraint, shaped by gender barriers in publishing, professions, and reform networks, and intertwined with kinship and community responsibility. Zitkala-Ša's move from literature to organization culminated in the National Council of American Indians in 1926. Indigenous-language writing in Greenland, notably by Henrik Lund and Mathias Storch, and the evidentiary challenges of recovering Indigenous-authored documents in Mexico showed that the continental picture cannot be reduced to English-language writers or to a single literary movement.

The chapter's principal debate concerned whether Western education and national publishing institutions empowered or constrained Indigenous leadership. The evidence supports a differentiated answer: for each figure, institutions both opened and limited possibilities, and the balance varied by person, place, gender, and decade. Intellectual disagreements about modernity, citizenship, tribal institutions, and independence were deep and should be distinguished from the legal history of citizenship, which is treated in Chapter 93.

Continuities were as important as changes. Indigenous languages, oral traditions, kinship networks, local councils, and community authorities continued to carry intellectual and political authority, much of it undocumented in print. The published record therefore represents a particular, often educated, segment of Indigenous societies, and historians must read it alongside community-based sources.

The causes of the developments were multiple. Long-term traditions of oratory and diplomacy, nineteenth-century Indigenous literacies, expanding transportation and printing, the growth of reform and scholarly institutions, and the pressures of allotment, schooling, and administration all contributed. No single factor, whether schooling, technology, or reform patronage, explains the emergence of Indigenous public leadership, and the people involved were agents who chose among available tools.

By about 1930, Indigenous writers, scholars, editors, and organizers had established a diverse and enduring presence in public life. Their achievements and limitations laid foundations for later struggles over citizenship, military service, reform, and governance, and for later movements for repatriation, knowledge sovereignty, language revitalization, and cultural resurgence. They did not merely respond to conquest; they produced new interpretations, literary traditions, arguments, organizations, and institutions that shaped modern North American public life.

Key Terms

Public sphere
The shared arenas, such as newspapers, lectures, meetings, and associations, in which opinion is formed and public issues are debated.
Indigenous public intellectual
An Indigenous person who addresses a broad public through writing, speaking, scholarship, performance, or organization and claims authority to interpret Indigenous histories and politics.
Self-representation
Presentation of a people's own history and claims in their own voice, typically mediated in this period by editors, publishers, and translators.
Political advocacy
Organized effort to influence opinion or policy through speech, writing, petition, association, or litigation.
Editorial mediation
Shaping of a text by an editor, translator, or secretary, which may alter wording, arrangement, or emphasis.
Interpreter
A person who renders spoken language between communities, often exercising discretion over emphasis and meaning.
Oral tradition
Knowledge transmitted through speech, narrative, and ceremony, serving as a source of historical and legal authority.
Syllabics
Writing systems in which characters represent syllables, such as the Cherokee syllabary and the syllabics used for Cree and Inuit languages.
Lyceum and Chautauqua
Lecture and entertainment circuits that carried speakers to towns across North America and created audiences for Indigenous lecturers.
Ethnology
The comparative study of peoples and cultures, practiced in this period in institutions such as the Bureau of American Ethnology.
Bureau of American Ethnology
A Smithsonian research bureau, established in 1879, that employed and published the work of Indigenous scholars including Francis La Flesche and J. N. B. Hewitt.
Memorial (petition)
A formal written statement of claims and requests addressed to a government, such as the 1910 Interior Salish memorial to Prime Minister Laurier.
Intertribal organization
An association drawing members from multiple nations, such as the Society of American Indians.
Society of American Indians
National organization founded in 1911 that published a journal and magazine and debated policy until it faded in the early 1920s.
Allotment
Division of communal Indigenous land into individual parcels, a policy debated by many Indigenous intellectuals (Chapter 86).
Assimilation
Government and missionary policy aimed at replacing Indigenous languages, religions, and institutions with those of the settler society.
Survivance
A term used by scholars to describe active Indigenous continuity and presence rather than mere survival or victimhood.
Satire
Use of irony and humor to criticize persons or policies, as in Alexander Posey's Fus Fixico letters.
Dialect writing
Rendering of speech in nonstandard spelling or syntax, which could be used for community voice or could reproduce stereotypes.
Indigenismo
A movement in Mexico, led largely by non-Indigenous and mestizo thinkers, to define Indigenous peoples' place in the national project.
Ejido
A form of land tenure in Mexico, established after the Revolution, in which rural communities held land under agrarian reform procedures.
Provenance
The documented history of a source's creation, ownership, and transmission, essential for judging authorship and authenticity.
Alaska Native Brotherhood
Organization begun in 1912 at Sitka by Tlingit, Tsimshian, and other Alaska Native leaders to pursue civil rights, education, and community organization.
League of Indians of Canada
National First Nations organization founded in 1919 by Frederick Loft.
National Council of American Indians
Organization founded in 1926 by Zitkala-Ša and Raymond Bonnin; distinct from the later National Congress of American Indians (1944).
Fus Fixico letters
Satirical letters written by Alexander Posey from 1902 in the voice of a Muscogee traditionalist commenting on allotment and statehood.
Habeas corpus
A legal writ requiring a detaining authority to justify a person's detention, used in Standing Bear v. Crook.
Landsråd
Consultative provincial councils introduced in Greenland by Danish administrative reform in 1911.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that Indigenous peoples did not enter the modern world only as subjects of policy. They entered it as writers, editors, scholars, lecturers, and organizers who argued about its terms. The figures discussed here faced unequal power, editorial control, and limited institutions, yet they produced works and organizations that changed what could be said in North American public life. Understanding them corrects the view that Indigenous history in this period consisted only of loss.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Colonial schooling, print capitalism, reform networks, and scholarly institutions created conditions that Indigenous people used for their own purposes, often in ways their creators did not intend. At the same time, the chapter demonstrates the persistence of intellectual authority outside those institutions, in languages, ceremonies, councils, and kinship. Both stories are needed. A history that follows only the published authors misses much of Indigenous intellectual life, and a history that ignores them misses a significant mode of Indigenous agency.

The developments traced here shaped what followed. The debates between integrationist and community-centered visions anticipated later disputes over citizenship, termination, reorganization, and self-determination. The networks of the Society of American Indians and the petitioning traditions of British Columbia and Alaska foreshadowed later national and international organizing. The questions about authorship, ownership, and mediation raised by ethnology and museum work foreshadowed modern debates over repatriation and Indigenous control of knowledge.

In the context of this textbook, Chapter 92 links the economic adaptation of Chapter 91 to the legal and political questions of Chapters 93 through 100. It shows how Indigenous nations, in a period of severe pressure, built new means of speaking, recording, and organizing while maintaining older ones. That combination of continuity and innovation is among the central themes of the volumes ahead, and it is why Indigenous intellectual history belongs at the center of the continental story.
