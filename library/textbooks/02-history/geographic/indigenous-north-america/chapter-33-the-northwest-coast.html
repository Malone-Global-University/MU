The Northwest Coast

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

By the middle of the eighteenth century, a traveler moving by canoe along the Pacific edge of North America, from the mountain-backed fjords of southeastern Alaska to the great estuary of the Columbia River, would have passed through the homelands of dozens of distinct political communities. Those communities spoke languages belonging to at least seven different language families or isolates, and they were joined to one another by marriage, trade, ceremony, diplomacy, and war.

That journey of well over a thousand kilometers would have followed channels, straits, and open ocean shorelines that were not wilderness but a densely inhabited, named, owned, and managed landscape. Winter villages of large cedar plank houses faced the water. Salmon streams, eulachon rivers, halibut banks, shellfish beaches, sea mammal rocks, berry grounds, and cedar stands were held by particular houses and lineages, whose rights were publicly remembered and periodically reaffirmed before witnesses. The sea was the principal road, and canoes carved from single logs were the means by which people visited relatives, brought goods to market, hunted whales, hosted feasts, and carried out raids.

This chapter examines the peoples who made this world: the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Nisga'a, and Gitxsan; the Haisla, Heiltsuk, Wuikinuxv, and Nuxalk of the central coast; the Kwakwaka'wakw; the Nuu-chah-nulth and Makah; the many Coast Salish nations; the Chinookan peoples of the lower Columbia; and neighboring maritime communities. Its central argument is stronger than the familiar observation that the coast was rich in resources. Northwest Coast societies transformed a productive but demanding maritime environment into interconnected political worlds through specialized ecological knowledge, storage, large permanent communities, sophisticated maritime and woodworking technologies, inherited rights, ranked social institutions, and extensive systems of trade, ceremony, diplomacy, and warfare. Salmon runs, cedar forests, shellfish beds, and sea lanes became politically significant because people managed access to them, organized labor, preserved surpluses, defended claims, and embedded resources within kinship and law.

The chapter has deliberate boundaries. The deep development of Northwest Coast marine economies, sedentary villages, and woodworking traditions belongs to Chapter 10, and this chapter builds on that foundation rather than repeating it. Indigenous California, including the Yurok, Karuk, and Hupa, belongs to Chapter 32 and appears here only where northern California connects to Northwest Coast exchange. The Subarctic and northern interior belong to Chapter 34, so Athabaskan and other interior peoples appear here as partners in coast-interior relationships. The Plateau and its Columbia River peoples are treated in Chapter 31. And the detailed history of nineteenth-century treaty making, warfare, land loss, and resistance in the Pacific Northwest belongs to Chapter 72. This chapter ends at the threshold of sustained European maritime intrusion rather than narrating what followed.

Historians reconstruct this world from several kinds of evidence, each with strengths and limits. Archaeology supplies village plans, shell middens, fish weirs, tools, and, in exceptional waterlogged sites, preserved cedar objects. Oral histories, such as the adaawk (also spelled adaawx) of the Gitxsan and Tsimshian and the clan histories of the Tlingit and Haida, preserve detailed accounts of origins, migrations, territories, and events, and are held and interpreted by those entitled to them. Linguistics traces relationships among languages. The earliest European and Russian documents, beginning in the 1740s and 1770s, record brief encounters. Much of the most detailed description comes from ethnographers working from the 1880s onward, including Franz Boas and his Kwakwaka'wakw collaborator George Hunt, and John Swanton among the Haida and Tlingit. These later records describe societies already transformed by epidemics, the maritime fur trade, missions, and colonial law, so using them to reconstruct the eighteenth century requires care.

Terminology also requires care. "Northwest Coast" is a scholarly construct, a culture area defined in part by shared features such as plank houses, salmon storage, and cedar technology. It was not an Indigenous political unit, and the people described here never formed a single nation. Many names in older literature are exonyms: "Kwakiutl" for the Kwakwaka'wakw, "Nootka" for the Nuu-chah-nulth, "Bella Coola" for the Nuxalk, and "Queen Charlotte Islands" for Haida Gwaii. This chapter uses current Indigenous names wherever they are well established and identifies older names where students will meet them in sources.

None of this is intended to present the pre-colonial coast as either an idyll of natural abundance or a story already overshadowed by what was coming. Its societies knew hunger when runs failed, practiced slavery, fought wars, and argued bitterly over rank and territory. They were also governed by sophisticated law, sustained complex economies, and produced art and ceremonial institutions of great intellectual depth. Understanding them as political and legal communities, rather than as picturesque survivals, is essential to understanding both their history and the sovereignty claims their descendants pursue today, examined in Volume X.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Northwest Coast as an Indigenous maritime world and explain why modern international and state boundaries do not describe its historical geography.
  2. Explain how archaeological, oral, linguistic, documentary, and ethnographic evidence together support a history of the coast, and identify the limits of later ethnography for reconstructing earlier centuries.
  3. Distinguish among language families, culture areas, and political communities, and explain why "Coast Salish," "Tsimshian," or "Kwakiutl" cannot be treated as single nations.
  4. Compare the political and social organization of the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshianic, Wakashan, Coast Salish, and Chinookan peoples.
  5. Analyze the role of storage, labor organization, technology, and resource rights in turning salmon, eulachon, shellfish, and sea mammals into the basis of ranked societies.
  6. Describe the technologies of cedar working and canoe building and explain why they should be understood as systems of knowledge rather than as natural consequences of abundant timber.
  7. Explain how houses, clans, lineages, and named titles organized ownership and use of territory, and how rank and slavery operated differently across the coast.
  8. Interpret the potlatch and related feasts as legal, political, economic, and ceremonial institutions rather than as generic gift-giving.
  9. Trace the trade routes, goods, and coast–interior relationships, including copper, dentalium, obsidian, and eulachon grease, that tied the coast to the Plateau and Subarctic.
  10. Assess evidence for warfare, captive-taking, and diplomacy on the coast while distinguishing deep-time evidence from nineteenth-century documentation.
  11. Evaluate the debate over whether salmon abundance explains Northwest Coast social complexity.
  12. Describe the situation of Northwest Coast nations at the threshold of sustained European maritime contact and avoid treating later colonial outcomes as inevitable.

Historical Context

Chapter 10 traced the long development of the Northwest Coast: human settlement along the Pacific edge at the end of the last Ice Age, the growth of marine economies, the appearance of permanent villages and shell middens, the emergence of woodworking, and evidence for increasing social differentiation. Students should bring three conclusions from that chapter. First, the coast was occupied for many millennia, and its societies changed continually. Second, the large plank-house villages and ranked institutions of the last few centuries were the product of long, regionally varied histories rather than a single moment of invention. Third, archaeological cultures cannot be assigned mechanically to modern nations, since the identities and languages of ancient communities must be argued from evidence.

The environment frames this history without determining it. The coast is a temperate rainforest shaped by heavy precipitation, steep mountains, drowned valleys, and immense tidal ranges. Fjords and island chains made travel by water easier than travel by land in many districts, and the Coast Mountains, Olympic Mountains, and Cascades separated the coast from the interior while rivers such as the Stikine, Skeena, Nass, Fraser, and Columbia cut through them. The same environment was demanding. Salmon, eulachon, and herring appeared in concentrated runs at particular seasons and varied from year to year and place to place. Storms, fog, strong currents, and the risk of earthquakes and tsunamis made the sea dangerous. Prosperity depended on knowledge of when and where resources would be available and on the ability to preserve them for the lean months.

Students should also understand the problem of the "ethnographic present." The most detailed accounts of houses, feasts, rank, and ritual were recorded between roughly 1880 and 1950, after smallpox and other epidemics, after decades of the maritime fur trade, and in some places after missionary and government campaigns against Indigenous ceremonies and law. This chapter therefore distinguishes, wherever possible, between what archaeology, oral history, and early documents support for the period before sustained colonialism and what later ethnography records. Where a practice is documented only late, the text says so.

Finally, this chapter treats Indigenous nations as sovereign political communities with their own legal systems, a theme introduced in Chapter 1. The houses, clans, chiefs, and ceremonial rights discussed below were not merely cultural customs. They were institutions of governance and property that regulated access to resources, adjudicated disputes, arranged marriages, and sustained relationships among communities. The same institutions continue, adapted, in many Northwest Coast nations today.

Section 33.1

The Northwest Coast as an Indigenous Maritime World

The Northwest Coast, as scholars use the term, extends from the northern Gulf of Alaska, where Tlingit and their Eyak neighbors lived near the Copper River delta, through the thousands of islands of southeastern Alaska and British Columbia, around the Salish Sea, and south through the Olympic Peninsula and the lower Columbia to the Oregon coast. Its southern edge is ambiguous. Peoples such as the Tillamook, Coos, and Tolowa shared features with the coast to the north and with the cultures treated in Chapter 32, and the boundary of the region is a scholarly convenience. Inland, it ends where the coastal mountains and rainforest give way to the Plateau and Subarctic, though trade and kinship crossed that line constantly.

The region's physical geography created a distinctive set of possibilities and risks. Offshore islands such as Haida Gwaii and Vancouver Island shelter inside waters, while the outer coasts face the full force of the Pacific. Glacial rivers, such as the Stikine, Taku, and Alsek, and rain-fed rivers, such as the Skeena, Nass, and Fraser, pierce the mountain barrier and carry salmon and eulachon far inland. Tides and currents in narrow channels can be treacherous or immensely productive, and the dense coniferous forest, dominated by western red cedar, yellow cedar, hemlock, spruce, and Douglas fir, provided building materials and fiber. Travel by water was often faster than travel overland, and the density of villages along channels reflects it. At the same time, the region lies on an active tectonic margin, with earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, and landslides that appear in oral histories and the geological record alike.

The modern boundaries that cut through this region were drawn long after its political geography was established. The Alaska–Canada boundary divides Tlingit and Haida communities, and the boundary through the Strait of Georgia and the Strait of Juan de Fuca separates relatives among the Coast Salish and between the Makah and Nuu-chah-nulth. The 49th parallel, drawn in 1846 through the continent, ran through the interior Salish world and ended in the middle of the Salish Sea. None of these lines organized the coast's earlier history. Marriage alliances, trade routes, ceremonial invitations, and warfare ran along the water, and a history organized by modern jurisdictions would obscure the networks that mattered most.

Historical Vocabulary
Culture Area and Ethnographic Present

Definition. A culture area is a scholarly grouping of peoples who shared a set of broadly similar features such as environment, economy, and material culture. The Northwest Coast culture area was defined by early twentieth-century anthropologists, who also developed the convention of the ethnographic present: describing a people in the present tense as they were when first recorded, as though that moment were a timeless baseline.

Historical context. The convention suited a method that treated each society as a stable system. Historians now recognize that the societies recorded by ethnographers had been profoundly changed by earlier events, including epidemics and the fur trade, and that Indigenous societies had always been changing.

Why it matters. A culture area is a tool for comparison, not a political reality, and the ethnographic present can obscure change. Both ideas help students see why this chapter distinguishes between evidence for the eve of colonialism and later documentation.

Potential misunderstanding. Shared features do not mean shared identity or political unity. Neighboring nations on the coast often differed in language, descent rules, and law, and sometimes fought each other.

Section 33.2

From the Ancient Northwest Coast to Historically Identifiable Nations

The transition from Chapter 10's archaeological record to the nations described in this chapter is neither simple nor uniform. Archaeologists can trace continuous occupation of many sites and districts, document changes in house forms, subsistence, tools, and burial, and in a few cases link particular sites to the oral histories and territories of present-day nations. What they cannot do, as Chapter 1 emphasized, is read language, descent rule, or political identity directly from material remains. A shell midden does not announce whether its makers spoke a Tsimshianic or Wakashan language, and a change in house style does not automatically signal migration or conquest. Historical claims about continuity therefore rest on converging evidence rather than on any single source.

Several kinds of evidence are especially important. Waterlogged "wet sites," such as Hoko River on the Olympic Peninsula, preserve basketry, cordage, and wooden fishing gear that usually decay, and the later village of Ozette, discussed in Section 33.10, preserved a remarkable range of everyday objects. Culturally modified trees, trees whose bark or planks were harvested in ways that left characteristic scars, document cedar use across the landscape. Fish weirs and stone traps in tidal flats and streams, and rock-walled clam gardens in the intertidal zone, record long-term investment in managing marine resources. Oral histories add territorial, genealogical, and event-based detail that archaeology cannot supply, and several have been corroborated by geological findings. Nisga'a accounts of a devastating eruption in the Nass valley and Nuu-chah-nulth and Huu-ay-aht accounts of a night-time wave destroying a village have both been connected by scholars to datable natural events, although the precise correspondences continue to be discussed.

Linguistic evidence also bears on the question. The coast is a zone of exceptional linguistic diversity, and the relationships among its languages suggest long histories of both divergence and contact. Shared features across unrelated languages, such as similar sound systems and borrowed words for technology and ceremony, point to centuries of interaction. Genetic and linguistic studies have been used to suggest migrations, but, as Chapter 1 cautioned, genetic ancestry, language, and political identity are different things, and the connections require careful argument.

The historical record is thus layered. For the centuries before the 1740s, archaeology and oral history are the main sources. From the 1740s, a small number of European and Russian written descriptions appear, usually brief, produced by outsiders, and focused on trade and navigation. From the late nineteenth century, ethnographers recorded detailed descriptions that often relied on elders' memories of earlier decades. This chapter treats the period immediately before sustained colonialism, roughly the eighteenth century, as the point where these sources converge and where the identifiable nations of the coast can be described with reasonable confidence, while recognizing that those nations had long, complex histories reaching back far before it.

Section 33.3

Languages, Nations, and Regional Diversity

The Northwest Coast is one of the most linguistically diverse regions in North America. As in Chapter 32, a crucial distinction governs this section: a language family is not a political nation. Related languages tell historians about ancient divergence and contact, not about shared government, loyalty, or identity. Closely related communities could be rivals, and speakers of unrelated languages could be close allies and relatives. Beyond language, the coast varied politically and socially. Northern peoples, the Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshianic nations, were organized in matrilineal descent groups, the clans and houses described in Section 33.15. The Wakashan-speaking peoples used a range of principles. Kwakwaka'wakw social groups combined descent through both parents' lines with inherited ranked positions, and the Nuu-chah-nulth organized around ranked houses and chiefs with territorial responsibilities. The Coast Salish used bilateral kinship, tracing relationships through both parents, and built networks across many villages through marriage. The Chinookan peoples had ranked societies in which marriage and wealth also carried weight. These differences affected how rights were held and transmitted, how alliances were formed, and how authority worked, and they warn against any single "Northwest Coast" model.

Scale of political organization also varied. In some regions, the key political units were individual houses and villages. In others, such as the Tlingit kwáan or the Tsimshian "tribes" that wintered together, several houses or clans formed larger territorial groups. Neither pattern produced states with centralized rulers. Authority rested with house heads and hereditary chiefs who held their positions through lineage, wealth, ceremonial validation, and the acknowledgment of their peers, and who could not command obedience in the manner of a king. This decentralized political form, combined with rich ceremonial and legal institutions, is one of the coast's defining features and one of the reasons European observers frequently misunderstood it.

Section 33.4

Tlingit Worlds of Southeast Alaska

The Tlingit (Lingít) occupied the archipelago and coastal strip of southeastern Alaska and extended inland along several major rivers and passes. Their society was divided into two moieties, usually called Raven (Yéil) and Eagle, with the Eagle moiety in some southern districts called Wolf, and into numerous matrilineal clans (naa) subdivided into named houses (hít). A person belonged to the clan and moiety of their mother and typically married a member of the opposite moiety. Larger territorial groupings, the kwáan, such as the Sheet'ká (Sitka) and Jilkaat (Chilkat) kwáan, brought neighboring clans together in shared regions, but they were not governments in the European sense. Authority belonged to house leaders and clan leaders whose standing depended on lineage, wealth, and their record of ceremonial and legal leadership.

Clans held a category of property called at.óow: crests, names, songs, regalia, stories, and, importantly, rights to land and resources. Salmon streams, halibut banks, sea mammal haulouts, berry grounds, and hunting territories were held by clans or houses, and the history by which a group came to hold a place, often a migration or encounter story, was recited publicly as part of the proof of ownership. Much of the Tlingit economy was seasonal. Spring brought herring spawn and eulachon in some districts, summer and fall brought salmon runs that were harvested and smoked in great quantity, and the cold months were devoted to ceremony, craft, and visiting. Sea otters, seals, sea lions, and fur seals were hunted from canoes, and mountain goats were hunted in the high country. Woodworking, basketry, and the weaving of the famous Chilkat blankets, a technique associated with the northern coast generally, were highly developed crafts.

Tlingit law and ceremony centered on the koo.éex', the feast or potlatch, discussed in Section 33.17. Following a death, the clan of the deceased was served and supported by the opposite moiety, who had specific obligations to carry out funerary work and receive payment, and who were later honored in a memorial feast. Housebuilding, the raising of memorial poles, the transfer of names, and the resolution of disputes were all validated through feasts. The prominent role of the opposite moiety illustrates an important principle: the clans were joined, not isolated, and many of their most important ceremonial obligations required cooperation across moiety lines.

The Tlingit were also major traders. Coastal groups such as the Chilkat and Chilkoot controlled passes leading inland toward the Yukon River system and exchanged coastal goods, including eulachon and other fish oil, dentalium and other shells, and later iron and trade items, for interior copper, furs, and hides with neighboring Athabaskan peoples such as the Tagish and Tutchone, whose histories belong to Chapter 34. Kinship and marriage with interior groups reinforced these ties. Russian vessels appeared in Tlingit territory in 1741, marking the earliest recorded European presence on this part of the northern coast; sustained Russian–Tlingit contact developed later, and the 1741 voyages stand at the end, not the beginning, of the history treated here.

Section 33.5

Haida Gwaii and the Haida World

Haida Gwaii, a group of islands about 100 kilometers off the mainland, was the homeland of the Haida, whose language is an isolate. In 2010 British Columbia formally renamed the islands from the colonial "Queen Charlotte Islands" to Haida Gwaii, reflecting long-standing Haida usage. The Haida were organized into two matrilineal moieties, Raven and Eagle, and into dozens of named lineages, each linked to one or more villages. Villages such as Skidegate and Old Massett remain important today, and the abandoned village of SGang Gwaay (Ninstints), with its standing mortuary poles, is a protected site within Gwaii Haanas. In the mid-eighteenth century Haida emigrants, the Kaigani, began moving into the southern Prince of Wales archipelago on the Alaskan side of Dixon Entrance, an area also used by Tlingit communities. Seasonal and exploratory use preceded permanent villages, so this was a gradual process, not a single migration on one date.

The Haida were renowned as maritime people. Their dugout canoes, carved from large cedar trees on the islands and in some cases traded to the mainland, were among the finest on the coast, and the Haida used them to cross Hecate Strait to the mainland and to travel along the whole length of the northern coast. Fishing for salmon, halibut, and herring, hunting sea mammals, and gathering shellfish and seaweed supplied most of their food. Because Haida Gwaii lacked certain mainland resources, such as eulachon runs of the Nass River and some kinds of fur, the Haida maintained close trading and marriage ties with the Tsimshian and Tlingit, sending canoes, carved goods, and dried foods to the mainland and receiving eulachon grease and other items in return.

Haida art and architecture are among the best known of the coast. Houses with elaborately carved front poles and interior house posts, memorial and mortuary poles, painted house fronts, and carved chests expressed the crests and histories of lineages. The earlier chronology of freestanding monumental poles is debated, and scholars generally believe that the scale and number of poles increased in the nineteenth century as metal tools and new sources of wealth became available, while earlier house posts, mortuary structures, and carved architectural elements existed long before. Haida carving was never decoration alone. It was a means of recording and asserting rights, marking where a house or lineage stood in relation to others.

Haida politics was decentralized and competitive. Village chiefs and house heads held rank through descent and demonstrated it through feasts, building, and the management of resource territories. Warfare, including long-distance raiding in large canoes, and the taking of captives were part of Haida life, and Haida war parties were feared along the coast. Documentation of the most distant raids, such as the Haida expeditions to Puget Sound reported in the nineteenth century, comes largely from after the period treated here and from circumstances shaped by new trade, so the chapter returns to this question in Section 33.19. The Haida were thus both a politically differentiated island world and a participant in the wider system of canoe travel, marriage, and trade that linked the entire northern coast.

Section 33.6

Tsimshian, Nisga'a, Gitxsan, and the Skeena–Nass Networks

The Skeena and Nass rivers, both flowing from the interior to the sea near the present Alaska border, formed the heart of a world shared by the Tsimshianic-speaking peoples. The Coast Tsimshian (Ts'msyen, speakers of Sm'algyax) lived on the lower Skeena and the nearby coast, including the Prince Rupert Harbour area whose ancient history is treated in Chapter 10. The Southern Tsimshian lived along the central coast to the south. The Nisga'a lived on the Nass, and the Gitxsan, whose name means "people of the Skeena," lived on the upper Skeena and its tributaries. Treating "Tsimshian" as a single coastal people obscures the fact that this was a continuum of related nations stretching from the sea to the interior, with strong ties to non-Tsimshianic neighbors such as the Haida, Tlingit, and Athabaskan-speaking Wet'suwet'en.

Tsimshianic societies were matrilineal and divided into four crest-based clans (or "phratries"), called in various spellings Raven, Eagle, Wolf, and Killer Whale. Each clan comprised many named houses (wilp), the principal landholding and political units. A house was a body of related people, living and ancestral, led by a hereditary chief (sm'oogyit in Sm'algyax; sim'oogit in Nisga'a; spellings vary), who held a ranked name and was custodian of the house's territories, crests, songs, and stories. The house's rights were validated in feasts, where chiefs took names, and the history of the house, its adaawk, was recited publicly as evidence of its claim. Because clans were spread across many villages and nations, they linked people across political boundaries, and a Gitxsan could find kin among the Nisga'a or Tsimshian of the same clan.

The economy was built around salmon and eulachon. The Nass River eulachon run in late winter and early spring was among the most important single resource events on the northern coast. People converged on the river from the coast and the interior to catch the small, oily fish, and to render them into grease that could be stored and traded. Salmon runs on the Skeena and Nass were harvested with weirs, traps, dip nets, and harpoons at specific fishing sites owned by houses. Control of the canyon sites on the Skeena, such as those near Kitselas, gave certain groups strategic positions in the traffic between coast and interior. The Tsimshian and Nisga'a served as intermediaries in the movement of eulachon grease, shell, and coastal goods upriver and of furs, hides, and other items down to the coast. These routes are often called grease trails and are discussed more fully in Section 33.18.

Oral history provides particularly rich evidence for the Nass valley. Nisga'a accounts preserve the memory of a catastrophic volcanic eruption in which a lava flow from the Tseax cone entered the valley and killed many people; recent research describes three destroyed villages and death tolls estimated at up to roughly 2,000, whereas older sources often name two villages. Radiocarbon and paleomagnetic work constrains the eruption to 1675–1778 CE at 95.4% probability, and Nisga'a accounts point to an eighteenth-century event, probably in summer; the often-cited "about 1700" is a conventional shorthand, not a known year. The Nisga'a account has become a frequently cited example of oral history preserving evidence of a datable natural event. Such accounts are not merely records of disaster. They also encode Nisga'a law and ethics, including lessons about respect for resources, and they are held by those with the right to tell them. Tsimshian communities also maintain extensive records of migrations, including movements from the interior to the coast and between river systems, that illustrate how the political geography of the region changed over time.

Section 33.7

Haisla, Heiltsuk, Nuxalk, and the Central Coast

Between the Tsimshian and the Kwakwaka'wakw lay the central coast of British Columbia, a labyrinth of fjords, islands, and rivers. It was home to the Haisla (Kitamaat) on Douglas Channel and the Kitlope, the Xai'xais and Kitasoo around Klemtu, the Heiltsuk (Bella Bella) on the islands around Milbanke Sound, the Wuikinuxv (Oweekeno) on Rivers Inlet and Owikeno Lake, and the Nuxalk (Bella Coola) at the head of the Bella Coola valley. The Haisla, Heiltsuk, Xai'xais, and Wuikinuxv speak Northern Wakashan languages, while the Nuxalk language belongs to the Salishan family, though it is separated geographically from the other Salish languages by hundreds of kilometers and has undergone considerable change. This central coast was an area of interaction in which languages, ceremonies, and marriage alliances crossed the boundaries of language families.

Central coast communities lived in cedar plank houses in winter villages and moved seasonally to fish camps. Eulachon, salmon, herring and herring spawn, halibut, shellfish, seaweed, and sea mammals were important foods, and the different rivers gave the region several distinct eulachon fisheries that supported valuable grease. The Heiltsuk and their neighbors built extensive canoe-landing and habitation sites and maintained intertidal resource sites, including clam gardens, while the Nuxalk of the Bella Coola valley combined coastal and riverine fisheries with trade into the interior. Social organization combined ranked houses and hereditary titles with lineage structures that varied among groups, and the area shows strong links to both the matrilineal north and the more flexible systems of the south.

Interaction with the interior was a central feature of the Nuxalk world. The Bella Coola valley provided one of the few relatively low routes across the Coast Mountains, and trails running east linked the Nuxalk with the Dakelh (Carrier) and Tsilhqot'in peoples of the interior, whose histories belong to Chapter 34. Eulachon grease went east along these "grease trails" in exchange for interior products, and kinship and ceremonial ties accompanied the goods. The route was later followed in 1793 by the Scottish trader Alexander Mackenzie, who reported being guided over trails already in regular use. This detail illustrates a recurring point: European explorers frequently traveled along existing Indigenous routes and depended on Indigenous knowledge and hospitality.

The central coast was also a zone of ceremonial exchange. The winter ceremonial systems of the Heiltsuk, Wuikinuxv, Nuxalk, and Kwakwaka'wakw share many elements, including dancing societies, masked performances, and ranked ceremonial positions. Scholars have long discussed the direction in which particular ceremonies spread, and Kwakwaka'wakw accounts and ethnographic records describe the acquisition of some ceremonial privileges from northern neighbors through marriage and exchange. Such transmission was not casual borrowing. Ceremonies and the rights to perform them were property, and they passed from one group to another through carefully negotiated marriages, purchases, and inheritance, which tied the political relationships of the coast together.

Section 33.8

Kwakwaka'wakw Worlds

The Kwakwaka'wakw, "those who speak Kwak'wala," lived on northern Vancouver Island, the adjacent islands, and the mainland inlets facing them. The older name "Kwakiutl" properly belonged to a single group, the Kwagu'ł of Fort Rupert, and its application to the whole population came through the work of early anthropologists. In fact, the Kwakwaka'wakw comprised many separate tribal-level groups, including the 'Namgis, Mamalilikulla, Dzawada'enuxw, Gwa'sala, 'Nakwaxda'xw, Tlowitsis, Da'naxda'xw, and others, each with its own territory, villages, history, and chiefs. These groups were linked by language, intermarriage, and ceremony, but also competed and sometimes fought. Calling them a single nation misrepresents their political organization.

Within each group, the basic social unit was a named, ranked house-group, conventionally called by the Kwak'wala term numaym in the ethnographic literature: a ranked corporate kin or house group, not simply a "clan." Boas retained the Indigenous term because descent labels such as "clan" or "gens" were misleading. A numaym held named positions, each with associated privileges, such as rights to specific names, songs, dances, crests, and resource sites. These positions were ranked and inherited, but the system was more flexible than a simple lineage descent system. Positions and privileges could pass through daughters and sons-in-law and could be transferred through marriage, so that rights and rank circulated among related groups. The ranked positions of the numaym also determined seating and speaking order at feasts, and chiefs were identified by their names and the privileges attached to them.

The Kwakwaka'wakw are best known for the winter ceremonial season, during which the ordinary social order was reorganized around ceremonial societies. During the winter months, people took on ceremonial names and statuses, and dancing societies performed dramatic initiations and transformations, which included masked dances, spectacular staging, and carefully controlled secrecy. Among the best known is the Hamatsa society, whose initiation dramatizes the taming of a possessed novice, and the transformation masks that open to reveal an inner face. These ceremonies, held in large houses before invited witnesses, were rooted in oral tradition and law, in the ownership of ceremonial privileges, and in the displays of wealth that validated rank. Descriptions in Boas's records, produced with George Hunt over decades, preserve many of these performances in detail, though they also reflect the circumstances of the late nineteenth century.

Kwakwaka'wakw economy rested on salmon, eulachon, herring and herring spawn, halibut, shellfish, sea mammals, and cedar. The abundant and varied marine environments of Queen Charlotte Strait and Johnstone Strait supported dense settlement, and clam gardens such as those documented on Quadra Island show long-term shaping of the intertidal zone. Trade ran north to the central coast, south to the Coast Salish, and west to the Nuu-chah-nulth, and coppers, discussed in Section 33.18, became prominent items of rank and exchange. Politically, relationships among Kwakwaka'wakw groups and with their neighbors involved alliance, rivalry, and warfare. The Lekwiltok (Laich-kwil-tach), a Kwak'wala-speaking group, began moving southward into Salish territories around the period of first European contact, but most of that expansion took place in the early and mid-nineteenth century, beyond this chapter's frame. Its causes remain discussed and may have involved new trade and weaponry.

Section 33.9

Nuu-chah-nulth and the West Coast of Vancouver Island

The Nuu-chah-nulth ("along the mountains") are a group of related nations on the west coast of Vancouver Island, from Barkley Sound northward to Kyuquot, who speak related Southern Wakashan languages. Older sources call them "Nootka," a name derived from a misunderstanding of a word spoken to Cook's party in 1778. Their country faces the open Pacific, with rugged inlets, exposed beaches, offshore banks, and heavy surf. Each nation, such as the Mowachaht, Hesquiaht, Ahousaht, Tla-o-qui-aht, Uchucklesaht, and Huu-ay-aht, held a territory and was usually led by hereditary chiefs (ha'wiih, singular ha'wilth), who held responsibility for specific territories (hahuuli) and for the welfare of their people. The Ditidaht of the southwestern coast speak a related language and shared much of this way of life.

Nuu-chah-nulth economies combined salmon and herring fisheries, halibut and other bottom-fish, shellfish, fur seals, sea otters, and, most famously, whales. Whaling was pursued in large cedar canoes by crews led by a harpooner of high rank, who prepared through days of ritual purification and abstinence, as part of an obligation that connected the success of the hunt to the welfare of the community. A harpoon with a mussel-shell or later iron blade was thrust into the whale, and sealskin floats attached to the line tired the animal until it could be killed and towed ashore. The right to hunt whales was usually held by chiefly families, and after a successful hunt the whale was divided according to rank, with specific portions assigned to the chief and the harpooner. The hunt was thus a communal, ritual, and political event, not simply an economic one.

Settlement and politics reflected the exposed coast. Villages of shed-roof plank houses were located in sheltered coves, and groups moved to summer sites on the outer coast or river mouths for fishing and sealing. Nuu-chah-nulth ha'wiih held authority within their territories and could form alliances with one another through marriage and ceremony, as in the winter ceremonies, which included a major dancing sequence associated with the Wolf ritual and sometimes the Shaman's dance. Larger alliances, which scholars sometimes call confederacies, formed among neighboring groups, but they were fluid and subject to rivalry. Warfare among Nuu-chah-nulth nations, and between them and their neighbors, was recorded in oral tradition and later documents, and the capture of fisheries and territories was one motive for conflict.

Yuquot ("where the winds blow from many directions"), the Mowachaht village at Nootka Sound, is the best-known Nuu-chah-nulth place in the historical record because Cook's expedition spent about a month there in 1778. European accounts describe a sophisticated and politically aware community that traded on its own terms, controlled access, and set the prices of goods. Oral history among the Nuu-chah-nulth also preserves the memory of the outer coast's geological upheavals. Huu-ay-aht oral accounts of a village destroyed by a wave on a winter night have been connected by researchers to the Cascadia earthquake and tsunami of January 26, 1700, although the identification involves interpretation and remains discussed. Evidence that coastal oral traditions preserve catastrophic events is strong; assigning any particular narrative to a specific date is a stronger claim. The example illustrates how oral history and geophysical research can illuminate each other when each is treated with care.

Section 33.10

Makah and the Southern Wakashan Borderlands

The Makah (qʷidiččaʔa·tx̌, the Makah endonym, often interpreted as "people of the cape" or "people who live near the rocks and seagulls") live at Cape Flattery, the northwestern tip of the Olympic Peninsula in present-day Washington. Their language belongs to the Southern Wakashan family, closely related to Nuu-chah-nulth and Ditidaht, although separated from them by the Strait of Juan de Fuca. They are thus a bridge between the Vancouver Island world and the Olympic Peninsula, where their neighbors included the Chimakuan-speaking Quileute and Hoh, and the Salishan-speaking Clallam and Quinault. The Makah participated in the kinship, ceremonial, and trade networks of the Nuu-chah-nulth, whom they often married, and in the regional economy of Cape Flattery's fishing banks.

The Makah economy centered on the sea. Halibut banks offshore were fished with specialized hooks and lines, and fur seals, sea lions, and sea otters were hunted from canoes. The Makah, like the Nuu-chah-nulth, Ditidaht, Quileute, and Hoh, were whalers, pursuing gray and humpback whales from canoes with harpoons and floats. Whaling gave status to the families that conducted it and provided oil, meat, and sinew, and it was embedded in ritual obligations, songs, and inherited privilege. Cape Flattery's position at the entrance to the Strait of Juan de Fuca gave Makah chiefs influence over the movement of canoes through one of the region's major waterways.

The Makah village of Ozette, on the outer coast, offers a unique window into this world. At some point in the past few centuries, a mudslide buried part of the village, preserving houses and their contents in wet clay that kept wood, basketry, cordage, and other perishable materials intact. The village was excavated from 1970 to 1981 as a collaborative project after erosion exposed the site and the Makah Tribe contacted Washington State University; the resulting collection, which reports commonly put at more than 50,000 objects, remained under Makah stewardship and is held at the Makah Cultural and Research Center in Neah Bay, which the Makah established and administer. The discovery is therefore both an archaeological resource and an example of descendant-community control of heritage, a theme treated in Chapter 125.

Section 33.11

Coast Salish Worlds

"Coast Salish" is a regional and linguistic label for the Salishan-speaking peoples of the Strait of Georgia, Puget Sound, the lower Fraser River, southeastern Vancouver Island, and neighboring waters, extending south to the Olympic Peninsula and, in the case of the Tillamook, the Oregon coast. The Salish Sea, a name adopted officially in the late twentieth century, now refers to the waters they lived beside. The Coast Salish speak many different languages, among them Halkomelem, Lushootseed, Straits Salish, Squamish, Sechelt, and Comox, not all of which are mutually intelligible. They did not form one nation. They lived in hundreds of villages, in a mosaic of communities that included the Musqueam, Stó:lō, Squamish, Saanich, Lummi, Suquamish, Duwamish, Puyallup, Skagit, and many others.

Coast Salish kinship was predominantly bilateral, tracing relatives through both parents, in contrast with the matrilineal north. A person could hold rights and claim kin in several communities, and marriages were often arranged across villages and even across language boundaries, producing wide networks of relatives. These networks governed access to resources, since people could fish, gather, or hunt in places where their relatives held rights. Social rank was real, and families of high status held valued names, resource sites, and ceremonial rights, but it operated through a web of kin ties rather than through clan or moiety divisions. Political authority was generally local, resting with respected leaders of extended families and villages, and the Coast Salish are often described as lacking the large consolidated chiefdoms found elsewhere, although this should not be read as lack of political sophistication.

The Coast Salish economy was extremely diverse. Salmon, particularly the great Fraser River runs, were caught with weirs, dip nets, and traps and dried in quantity. The Straits Salish groups, including the Saanich, Lummi, and Samish, used reef nets, a sophisticated system in which nets were suspended between canoes over artificial or natural reefs where salmon passed, an arrangement requiring skilled crews and recognized ownership of sites. Shellfish, clam gardens, herring, halibut, ducks taken in great nets, deer, and elk contributed to the diet. On the Garry oak meadows of southeastern Vancouver Island and the prairies of the Puget lowlands, people harvested camas bulbs, managed with fire and cultivation, and in the lower Columbia and Willamette valleys they gathered wapato, an aquatic tuber. Weavers made blankets from mountain goat wool, the hair of a specially kept wool dog, and plant fibers, using spindle whorls that were often carved with designs.

Houses were large cedar plank structures, in some cases extremely long, sheltering multiple households under a common roof. Winter ceremonial life included the spirit dances (often called syuwen or similar names in various languages), initiation-based ceremonies held in houses, together with naming ceremonies and feasts at which witnesses validated names, marriages, and rights. Coast Salish groups also built defensive earthworks and palisades, and they were both targets and participants in raiding. Defensive responses are visible in archaeological and oral evidence, and the Coast Salish were repeatedly threatened by canoe raiding from the north in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, although the intensity and causes of this raiding are debated. This internal diversity of Coast Salish communities, with their different languages, political histories, and local institutions, means that any statement about "the Coast Salish" must be qualified by place.

Section 33.12

Chinookan Peoples and the Lower Columbia

The Columbia River drains an enormous region of the interior and reaches the Pacific through a broad estuary. Its lower reaches were home to the Chinookan peoples, who spoke related languages and lived in many politically separate villages. Downstream near the mouth lived the Lower Chinook, Clatsop, Kathlamet, and Wahkiakum. Upstream, villages of the Cathlapotle, Multnomah, and Clackamas lined the river, the islands, and the Willamette confluence, and still farther up, the Wasco and Wishram lived at the Dalles and the Columbia gorge, where the river passes through narrow rapids. The Dalles and nearby Celilo Falls, which belong in part to the Plateau world discussed in Chapter 31, were among the greatest fishing and trading places of western North America.

The Chinookan economy centered on salmon, sturgeon, eulachon (locally called smelt), and, in the Willamette and lower Columbia, the starchy tuber wapato, harvested largely by women from marshy ponds and sloughs. Villages of plank houses stood along the banks, and canoes carried goods and people along the river and through the estuary. Chinookan societies were ranked, with prominent families holding wealth, and they practiced slavery, taking and trading captives. Infants of free families often had their heads gently flattened in the cradle, a mark of status shared with several neighboring peoples and later widely noted by European observers. The history of slavery among the Chinookans and neighboring peoples, and the trade in enslaved people that reached south into California and inland to the Plateau, is treated in Chapter 53.

The lower Columbia was a gateway for exchange between the coast and the interior. Dentalium and other shell from the coast, dried salmon, fish oil, wapato, and furs moved up the river, and obsidian, bison robes, horses (later), and interior products moved down. Chinookan traders and brokers occupied key positions, supplying and receiving goods across linguistic lines. A trade language, Chinuk Wawa, or Chinook Jargon, emerged in this milieu. Scholars continue to debate how much of it existed before the arrival of Europeans and how much developed in the fur trade, but most agree that it drew on Chinookan, Nuu-chah-nulth, and later English and French vocabulary and that it spread widely in the nineteenth century. The jargon is therefore a record of the region's multilingual exchange, not the sum of Chinookan culture.

The Chinookan peoples also illustrate the difficulty of describing political organization on the coast. Authority rested with village headmen and ranked family leaders, and some individuals gained wide influence through trade and marriage. Their position at the river mouth meant they could exercise control over access to the lower river, and they were among the first on the Columbia to encounter maritime fur traders in the 1790s, an event that lies beyond this chapter's scope. Like the Coast Salish, the Chinookans were embedded in multilingual networks, and their historical identity cannot be reduced to a single label or to the later "Chinook" name applied to many unrelated things.

Section 33.13

Salmon, Eulachon, Shellfish, Whales, and the Maritime Food Economy

The maritime food economy was the material foundation of Northwest Coast societies, but it must be understood as a system of knowledge, labor, and law, not as a simple gift of nature. Five species of Pacific salmon, chinook, sockeye, coho, chum, and pink, returned to rivers at different times of the year and in varying numbers. Fishers built weirs and traps across streams, used dip nets, harpoons, and gaffs at falls and canyons, and set reef nets in saltwater. Success depended on knowing when and where each run would arrive, which sites were most productive, and how to organize crews at the right moment. Because runs varied from year to year and from stream to stream, people also depended on relatives and trading partners elsewhere for security, which gave kinship and exchange a role in food supply.

Preservation was crucial. Most salmon was split, filleted, and dried or smoked on racks and in smokehouses for storage through the winter, an operation requiring large amounts of labor, skill, and wood. Eulachon, an oily smelt-like fish, were caught in nets and rendered into grease by letting the fish ripen and boiling them in boxes or pits, a process that produced a prized food, medicine, and trade good. Herring and herring spawn, collected on boughs and kelp, supplied early-spring food. Halibut was caught with hooks and lines from canoes on offshore banks and dried for storage. Shellfish, gathered in great quantity, were eaten fresh or dried, and in many places the beaches were modified into clam gardens, rock-walled terraces that made more productive habitat. Sea mammals, such as harbor seals, sea lions, sea otters, and fur seals, were hunted from canoes, and whales were hunted by the Nuu-chah-nulth, Makah, Ditidaht, Quileute, and Hoh, and their stranded carcasses were used by others.

Terrestrial resources also mattered. Deer, elk, and mountain goat were hunted, berries such as salal, huckleberry, and salmonberry were gathered and often dried in cakes, and roots such as camas, wapato, and fern rhizomes were dug and cooked. Plant management, including controlled burning of prairie and berry grounds, was practiced in many districts. Women generally did most of the processing and preservation, while labor was also contributed by slaves and by men in the heavier or more dangerous tasks, patterns that varied from group to group.

Historical Debate

Did Salmon Abundance Produce Northwest Coast Social Complexity?

The question: Northwest Coast societies were large, sedentary, ranked, and artistically sophisticated without agriculture, which made them important cases in discussions of hunter-gatherer complexity. Did the abundance of salmon and other resources cause these features, or were they produced by social, political, and technological developments that shaped how resources were used?

Interpretation A — Environmental abundance: Some scholars, especially in earlier generations, emphasized that predictable seasonal runs of salmon, once preserved by smoking and drying, supported dense populations and surplus. In this view the productivity of the coast made sedentary life and social ranking possible and, to some extent, likely.
Interpretation B — Social and political organization: Others argue that resources do not manage themselves. Weirs, traps, storage, and processing required organized labor and control over sites, and ranking, ownership, slavery, and feasting were institutions through which groups claimed and defended access. On this view, social complexity shaped the use of the environment as much as the reverse.
Interpretation C — Variability, risk, and regional history: A third approach stresses that resources were abundant but patchy and unpredictable from year to year, so that security required storage, exchange, kinship alliances, and rights over multiple sites. Regional variation in timing, such as the apparent earlier development of large villages in some districts, suggests that no single factor can explain all cases.

Evidence: Archaeological evidence includes the antiquity of weirs, houses, storage features, and shell middens, skeletal evidence for rank and violence in some contexts, and paleoenvironmental data on salmon abundance, which is difficult to measure. Ethnographic and oral sources describe resource ownership and labor, but they date from later periods.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation and interpretation: whether abundance was a necessary condition, a sufficient one, or only a background; and how much weight to give to demography, warfare, technology, and ideology. Disagreement also involves chronology, since the dates at which complex societies developed differ by region.

Why it matters: If abundance alone explains complexity, Indigenous achievements appear as automatic products of environment. If organized management, law, and technology were central, those achievements become the result of historical choices and institutions, which is the position this chapter adopts while acknowledging the role of the environment as a condition.

Student task: Identify at least two kinds of evidence that could help decide between Interpretations A and B, and explain the limits of each. Consider whether the interpretations are mutually exclusive.

Section 33.14

Cedar, Woodworking, Canoes, and Technological Systems

Western red cedar (Thuja plicata) and, in the north, yellow cedar were fundamental to Northwest Coast life, and the phrase "people of the cedar" is often used. The tree supplied plank boards, house posts, canoes, bentwood boxes, tools, fiber for clothing, rope, mats, and baskets, and was central to ceremonial regalia. But cedar was a resource only because of knowledge. Woodworkers knew which trees had straight grain and few knots, how to fell or select them with fire, stone and later iron tools, and how to split planks with wedges and mauls. Women stripped bark in long strips from living trees in a way that allowed the trees to survive, then processed it into soft fiber for capes, hats, mats, and rope. The resulting scarred trees, still visible in forests, are archaeological evidence for cedar use and, when dated, for the long history of the practice.

Canoes were the region's most important vehicles. A dugout canoe was carved from a single large cedar log, hollowed with adzes and fire, then softened with heated water and stones and spread to a wider beam. The sides were shaped by steaming, and thwarts were fitted to hold the shape. Different peoples developed distinct canoe forms suited to local waters: lightweight river canoes, "shovel-nosed" forms for sheltered waters, and large seagoing canoes with high bows for the open Pacific. The largest could carry dozens of people. Canoe building required specialists, was a mark of wealth and prestige, and involved ceremonial protocol. Canoes were also traded, with Haida and Nuu-chah-nulth canoes valued on the mainland.

Houses were built from vertical or horizontal split cedar planks attached to a frame of massive posts and beams. Plank houses were occupied by a house group and could be taken apart and moved, since the planks could be carried by canoe from winter to summer settlements. Boxes made by kerfing and steaming a single plank into four sides were used for storage, cooking, and burial, and watertight boxes could be used for boiling food with heated stones. Carving and painting, applied to houses, poles, boxes, canoes, masks, and tools, recorded crests and histories. The long-term development of these technologies is documented in Chapter 10, and the Ozette assemblage discussed above shows them in use.

The technological system extended beyond wood. Fishing required nets of nettle and cedar fiber, hooks of wood and bone, and traps and weirs of wood and stone. Whaling required harpoon heads of shell and bone, floats of sealskin, and strong line. Weaving included cedar bark, spruce root basketry, mountain goat wool, and, among the Coast Salish, wool dog hair. Metals, including native copper, were worked by hammering and cutting, and iron from drift or trade became available in the late period. These technologies were passed on by apprenticeship within families, and the knowledge of how to work cedar was inseparable from the spiritual and legal relationships that gave people the right to take it. Emphasizing technique and knowledge corrects the cliché that the coast's wealth derived from "natural abundance," since people created the means of using it.

Section 33.15

Houses, Villages, Kin Groups, and Territorial Rights

The plank house was both a building and a social institution. In many nations the word for "house" also named the group of people, living and ancestral, who belonged to it, together with its names, crests, stories, songs, and territories. A large house might shelter several related families under one roof, each with its own area around a hearth, while a chief's family occupied the position of highest rank. Winter villages stood in rows along beaches, with houses facing the water, and at some northern sites freestanding poles marked house fronts. In summer, households dispersed to camps near salmon streams, shellfish beaches, berry grounds, and sealing rocks, often carrying their cedar planks with them by canoe.

Historical Vocabulary
House, Clan, Moiety, and Crest

Definitions. A moiety is one of two halves into which a society is divided. A clan is a descent group whose members trace relationship to a common ancestor, usually through the mother in the northern coast societies. A house is a named, corporate group within a clan, holding property, names, and territory. A crest is an emblem, often an animal or supernatural being, acquired by a group through historical events and displayed on poles, houses, regalia, and in ceremony.

Historical context. The Tlingit and Haida used moieties, the Tsimshianic peoples four clans, and the Wakashan and Salish peoples other arrangements, including ranked house-groups and bilateral kin networks.

Why it matters. These groups determined who could use a fishing site or hold a name and who one could marry, and they linked communities across villages.

Potential misunderstanding. Crests are not "totems" in the sense of simple religious symbols, and houses are not simply residences. Both are property and legal claims.

Territorial rights were held by houses, lineages, or ranked family groups and were extremely specific. A house might own a section of river with named fishing sites, a stretch of beach, a reef net location, a halibut bank, a berry patch, a cedar stand, or a hunting area. Rights could include exclusive use, rights to harvest at particular times, or rights to receive shares from others who fished there. Rights were demonstrated by use, remembered through oral history, and publicly validated in feasts, where witnesses acknowledged the holder's claim. Because they could be shared with kin, visitors, and allies, rights formed a flexible system of recognized access, not a rigid map of fenced property. The resulting political geography was one of overlapping, negotiated claims, a fact later colonial surveyors and officials often failed to recognize.

Authority within the house and village was exercised by hereditary leaders, called by many names, including ha'wiih among the Nuu-chah-nulth and sm'oogyit among the Tsimshian, an office tied to the house group and its territories rather than a generic "tribal chief." Leaders were responsible for the house's resources, its people, and its ceremonial obligations, and they relied on advisors and speakers who remembered the house's history and managed its public ceremonies. Authority was constrained by the expectation of generosity and the need for the acknowledgment of peers, and a leader who failed to uphold the house's honor could lose support. Women often held important positions, including as holders of names and, in matrilineal societies, as the means through which house membership passed, although women's formal roles in public ceremony varied by nation.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Year in a Coastal Household

Life followed a seasonal round that varied by place but usually moved from winter villages to a series of resource camps and back. Late winter and spring brought herring spawn, eulachon in some rivers, and early plant foods. Summer and fall brought the major salmon runs and berry harvests, and the work of cutting, drying, and smoking fish occupied most members of a household. By winter, the stored food, the accumulated wealth of the year, and the return of kin to the village allowed the ceremonial season of feasting, naming, marriage, and dance.

Work was divided but not rigidly. Women commonly processed fish, gathered shellfish and plants, wove cedar-bark and spruce-root items, tended children, and managed household stores. Men commonly built canoes and houses, hunted sea mammals, fished at weirs and offshore, carved, and traveled for trade and war, although individual roles varied and many tasks were shared. Children learned through participation, accompanying relatives to fishing sites, practicing canoe handling, and listening to the stories that taught law and history. Young people of high rank went through ceremonies marking their transition to adulthood, and infants' heads might be shaped in the cradle among the Chinookan and some neighbors.

Many houses included enslaved people, captured or purchased, who performed labor and had no recognized family or rights within the house. The condition of commoners was also different from that of nobles, with differences in food, ceremonial access, and the ability to hold names. Because most detailed descriptions of everyday life come from the late nineteenth century and from the accounts of elders, historians must read them with awareness of the changes underway and of the gender and rank perspectives of those who spoke and those who recorded.

Section 33.16

Rank, Wealth, Slavery, and Social Difference

Northwest Coast societies were hierarchical, but hierarchy took different forms. Among the northern peoples, the Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshianic nations, rank was tied to descent within matrilineal houses and clans. House heads and their close relatives held high-ranking names, and others in the house held lower positions, but the distinctions were often described as differences within one kin group rather than as separate classes. In the Wakashan societies, ranking of names and positions was elaborate, with seating orders and ceremonial privileges tied to specific titles. Among the Coast Salish and Chinookan peoples, social status was expressed through families who held valued names, resources, and wealth, and descriptions often refer to upper-class and lower-class families, with variations by community. Anyone seeking to describe the coast's social structure must therefore specify which people they mean.

Wealth took many forms, including stores of food, canoes, blankets, shells, coppers, slaves, and the less tangible wealth of names, songs, and privileges. Wealth was valuable because it could be used to fulfill ceremonial obligations, support kin, and demonstrate competence. Rank and wealth were linked but not identical, since a high-ranking house with limited resources faced pressure, and a wealthy person without rank could not simply buy a high name. Marriage was an important strategy, and the marriages of children of rank were arranged carefully to bring together names, rights, and alliances, with payments exchanged to validate them. Such strategies enlarged the circle of relatives and also created obligations.

Slavery was a recognized institution on much of the coast. Enslaved people were typically captives taken in raids, or their descendants, or people purchased in trade, and the practice is documented among the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshianic peoples, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth, Coast Salish, and Chinookan peoples, among others. Enslaved persons performed labor, could be traded, and had no kin group rights within their owner's house, and in some places they were sometimes killed or freed at ceremonies, although accounts of such practices come largely from later sources and are discussed by scholars with caution. Estimates of the proportion of the population who were enslaved vary widely and are drawn mostly from the nineteenth century, when trade and warfare were changing, so it is difficult to say how common slavery was in earlier centuries. Chapter 53 examines Indigenous slavery across the continent, and students should treat the Northwest Coast case as one among many distinctive forms of captivity.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of Ranked, House-Based Societies

Long-term causes

Productive but seasonal and variable marine resources, a long history of sedentary settlement, the development of storage and processing technologies, and the elaboration of kin-based claims over fishing and gathering sites.

Short-term causes

Competition among houses for access to key sites and prestige, expanding trade networks, and intensifying alliance-making through marriage and ceremony in the centuries before sustained contact.

Immediate effects

Hereditary titles, ranked seating and privilege, consolidation of valuable resources in house hands, the use of captives for labor, and the growth of public ceremonial validation of rights.

Long-term consequences

Distinctive legal and political systems that later colonial governments misunderstood, along with art, ceremony, and law that remain central to modern nations' claims and cultural life.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced these societies. The mechanism connecting resources to rank ran through institutions: groups had to organize labor, control or share sites, store and distribute food, and publicly validate claims. Rank did not develop everywhere at the same time or in the same form, and it was contested within societies. The interpretation also remains debated, as the Historical Debate in Section 33.13 shows.

Section 33.17

Potlatch, Feasting, Titles, and the Public Validation of Rights

Of all Northwest Coast institutions, the potlatch is the most famous and the most misunderstood. In popular accounts it appears as a spectacle in which hosts destroy or give away wealth to gain prestige. Historians and Indigenous scholars now emphasize that feasts were far more than that. They were legal, political, economic, genealogical, and ceremonial institutions, held to mark funerals, naming, marriages, the raising of poles, the building of houses, the transfer of titles, and the settlement of disputes. By inviting guests of rank to witness an event and by distributing food and goods, hosts established publicly that a name had been transferred, that a marriage was legitimate, or that a house's claim to a territory was recognized. The witnesses, not a written record, preserved the transaction.

Historical Vocabulary
Potlatch and Feast

Definition. "Potlatch" is an English term, entering the language through Chinook Jargon and generally traced to a Nuu-chah-nulth word for giving, that refers to a range of ceremonial feasts at which hosts distribute food and gifts to invited guests. Local terms differ: Tlingit koo.éex' (also spelled ku.éex’), Haida wáahlaal, Tsimshian loolgit, and, specifically among the Kwakwaka'wakw, p̓a̱sa, glossed as "to flatten" under the weight of gifts. Elders often prefer their own terms to the generic English word.

Historical context. Practices differed by nation. The Tlingit emphasized the roles of opposite moieties, the Tsimshianic peoples feasts tied to house chiefs and clans, the Kwakwaka'wakw competitive displays tied to ranked positions, and the Coast Salish ceremonies of naming and witnessing across extended kin networks.

Why it matters. Feasts were the means by which law and property were made public and enforceable. They also redistributed food and goods and bound communities together.

Potential misunderstanding. The word suggests a single institution and a simple exchange of gifts for prestige. In fact feasts varied widely, were governed by detailed protocol, and in the late nineteenth century were targeted by colonial laws that interpreted them as wasteful or immoral.

Feasts also had economic functions. Preparing for a major feast could take years, as households accumulated food, canoes, blankets, and other goods, and the event redistributed wealth among the guests according to rank. The goods given were often later returned in other feasts, so that accounts remained open and long-term relationships were maintained. In the Kwakwaka'wakw system, coppers and large quantities of goods were displayed and given, and the rivalry among chiefs could be intense, including contests over rank in which goods were given away to humble a rival. The most dramatic examples recorded by ethnographers come from the late nineteenth century, when new wealth from trade and the population decline from epidemics had changed the context, and how far such rivalry operated in earlier centuries is discussed by scholars.

Titles and names were transferred at feasts. A person who inherited a name from a deceased relative had to take it publicly, with the appropriate feast, songs, and display of the privileges it carried. Marriage involved the exchange of valuable goods between families, and children received names at feasts that formally established their place. Feasts could also settle conflicts, since a payment given and accepted before witnesses closed a dispute that might otherwise lead to violence. Because guests were invited to witness and eat, they were also asked to remember, and their memories formed the community's record. When Canada's government, in 1885, amended the Indian Act to ban the potlatch, a law enforced for decades, it attacked an institution that was also a system of law and property, a subject treated in Chapter 85.

Multiple Perspectives

One Feast, Many Viewpoints

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns recorded by scholars and communities. They are not quotations from any named individual.

From the perspective of a host house chief: The feast is the means of fulfilling obligations to the dead, taking a name, and having the house's rights recognized. Years of preparation, the generosity of the house, and the correct performance of songs and protocol determine whether the claim stands.
From the perspective of an invited guest chief: Attendance and acceptance of gifts confirm the host's claims, and the guest is expected to remember what was announced and to answer in due course. Guests also assess the host's rank, and seating and the order of gifts show where they stand relative to others.
From the perspective of a commoner household member: The feast demands labor and contributions of food and goods, and brings the chance to take part in ceremony, to receive gifts, and to see one's relatives honored, though access to high names and privileges is limited by rank.
From the perspective of an enslaved person in the host household: The preparation and serving of the feast depend heavily on enslaved labor, while the enslaved person has no kin group rights in the proceedings and no part in the transfers of name and title. Surviving evidence for this perspective is scarce and mostly indirect.
From the perspective of a later colonial official (nineteenth century): Outside observers interpreted feasts as wasteful, pagan, or an obstacle to assimilation, often failing to recognize their legal and economic functions. This perspective belongs to the later period and is included to show how outsiders misread the institution.
Section 33.18

Trade, Canoe Highways, Copper, Dentalium, and Eulachon Grease

The Northwest Coast was a trading world long before European ships arrived. The coast's canoe routes, such as the Inside Passage and the Strait of Georgia, and the river and mountain routes inland, carried a wide range of goods. Foods such as dried salmon, halibut, herring roe, seaweed, berry cakes, and eulachon grease moved between districts with different resources. Canoes, cedar boxes, mountain goat wool blankets, and woven goods were traded between nations. From the interior came hides, furs, and goods from the Plateau and Subarctic, and from the coast flowed shell, oils, and finished goods inland. Exchange was often embedded in kinship and ceremony, with goods circulating as marriage payments, feast gifts, and trade between partners who maintained long-term relationships.

Several goods were especially significant. Eulachon grease, valued as food, medicine, and a trade item, traveled far into the interior along "grease trails," long overland routes from the Nass, the Skeena, and the Bella Coola valley. Dentalium, a slender tusk-shaped shell harvested off the west coast of Vancouver Island, was traded across the Plateau and far beyond, reaching communities in the Great Basin and Plains, and served as a valued form of wealth. Obsidian from sources in British Columbia was traded widely, and nephrite and other stones were used for tools. Native copper, obtained from northern sources, was worked into plates and ornaments. Shield-shaped coppers, important in northern and Kwakwaka'wakw ceremonial exchange, carried names and values tied to their histories. Scholars generally agree that native copper working and the importance of copper predate European contact, but they disagree about how far the scale of copper exchange and the values assigned to named coppers were enlarged by access to trade copper in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Coast–interior exchange was central. The Tlingit of Chilkat and Chilkoot controlled the passes toward the Yukon, the Tsimshian and Nisga'a traded up the Skeena and Nass, the Nuxalk traded from Bella Coola to the Dakelh and Tsilhqot'in, and the Chinookan peoples brokered goods between the lower Columbia and the Plateau. Interior partners, discussed in Chapters 31 and 34, were not passive recipients. They held their own rights to routes and fisheries, intermarried with coastal peoples, and maintained their own trading systems, some of which reached east toward the Plains. Middlemen groups gained wealth and influence from their position, and competition over such positions was one source of conflict.

In the continental perspective, the coast was a major node in an exchange system spanning the West, connecting to the networks discussed in Chapter 32 for California and those synthesized in Chapter 36. Shell, obsidian, and other materials moved across hundreds of kilometers, passing through many hands, and each exchange involved negotiation and relationship. The system was not driven by a single market or authority. It rested on kinship, diplomacy, and ceremony, and it provided channels along which ideas, ceremonies, and, later, European goods and diseases could travel ahead of Europeans themselves.

Section 33.19

Warfare, Raiding, Captivity, Diplomacy, and Alliance

Northwest Coast societies were not peaceful, isolated fishing villages. Warfare is documented in several lines of evidence. Archaeologists have found fortified sites on defensible rock outcrops in the Tsimshian region, defensive ditches and palisades among the Coast Salish, and skeletal evidence of violence in burials, some of it very old, as noted in Chapter 10. Oral histories describe raids, feuds, and battles, and early European observers recorded warriors wearing armor of wooden slats, hide, and helmets, especially among the Tlingit. Canoe warfare could cover long distances, and raiders took captives, plunder, and revenge.

The causes of conflict were varied. Raids might seek captives to be enslaved or ransomed, retaliation for a killing, control of valuable fishing and trading sites, or rank and honor. The principle of compensation was important, and a killing created obligations between kin groups that could be satisfied by payment or by retaliation, with feasts and gifts as the means of settlement. Because clans and houses were spread over many villages, a conflict between two members could involve distant relatives and rapidly widen. At the same time, the same networks provided means of peacemaking, including intermarriage, the exchange of high-ranking hostages, ceremonial peace-making in which payments were given and accepted before witnesses, and formal alliances among neighboring groups.

Students should be cautious about chronology. Much of the most vivid documentation of warfare dates from the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when firearms, new wealth, shifting populations, and the demand for captives and furs may have changed the scale and character of conflict. The Haida and Kwakwaka'wakw raids on Coast Salish communities, the main phases of Lekwiltok expansion, and Tlingit resistance to Russian settlement all belong largely to this period, and their relationship to earlier patterns is discussed by scholars. This chapter therefore distinguishes between the long-standing presence of conflict and negotiated peace, documented in archaeological and oral sources, and the intensification and specific events recorded after about 1780. Treaties, wars with colonial states, and the transformation of Indigenous warfare under colonial pressure belong to Chapter 72.

Diplomacy was as important as war. Chiefs from different nations visited, feasted, and married each other's relatives, and agreements about fishing and travel rights were negotiated and confirmed publicly. Marriage between the houses of different peoples, such as between Haida and Tlingit or between Nuu-chah-nulth and Makah, created enduring links, and in some districts regional alliances were maintained for generations. The coast thus functioned as a system of both conflict and cooperation, in which peace was an achievement that had to be renewed, not a default condition.

Section 33.20

Ceremony, Oral Tradition, Crest Art, and the Northwest Coast on the Eve of Sustained Colonialism

Ceremony, art, and oral tradition were the means by which Northwest Coast peoples recorded and enacted their history, law, and relationship to the world. Masks, carved poles, house fronts, painted screens, woven blankets, and carved boxes displayed crests acquired through historical events recounted in oral histories. Transformation narratives described how ancestors met supernatural beings, and the crests that resulted became hereditary property, displayed and protected by the group that held them. Origin traditions, such as the Haida accounts of Raven, Tsimshian adaawk, and Tlingit clan histories, recorded migrations, the founding of villages, and the establishment of claims. These were not decorative stories. They were formal accounts, told in appropriate settings by those entitled to tell them, and they functioned as evidence in feasts where rights were validated.

Art was also a record of relationships. Distinct regional styles, northern formline design among the Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshianic peoples, Kwakwaka'wakw masks, Coast Salish carved house posts and spindle whorls, and Nuu-chah-nulth ceremonial objects, were products of specialists whose work was commissioned and paid for in feasts. Some art traveled, as ceremonial privileges and the associated designs and songs were acquired through marriage and purchase, so that the art of one nation might appear in another, always with the right of use established. Sacred places, including mountains, rivers, and village sites, were bound into the same system of memory, and many remain central to modern nations.

By the middle of the eighteenth century, the Northwest Coast was a network of sovereign communities connected by marriage, trade, warfare, ceremony, and travel by sea. Each nation governed itself through house heads and hereditary chiefs, regulated resource use through inherited rights, and negotiated with neighbors through feasts and alliances. Population was large relative to most of the continent north of Mesoamerica, though estimates vary, and the societies of the coast were politically diverse. This was the world into which, from 1741 onward, Russian, Spanish, and British vessels began to arrive. The first encounters were brief and shaped by Indigenous terms, as seen at Yuquot and off Haida Gwaii, where chiefs controlled access and set conditions. Epidemic disease may have reached some parts of the coast before direct contact, a subject of continuing scholarly discussion treated in Chapter 40, and the maritime fur trade that began in the 1780s was to transform the coast (Chapter 46). Those changes, with the later treaty and land struggles of Chapter 72, belong to later chapters, and this chapter deliberately ends at their threshold, so that the coast is not read through the lens of what came afterward.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Houses, Names, and Waters from Antiquity to the Eve of Colonialism

What Changed

  • Village size, house form, and storage intensified over millennia, with regional timing varying
  • Trade networks expanded and the value and circulation of items such as copper and shell changed
  • Political relations among nations shifted through migrations, alliances, and conflicts, such as the mid-eighteenth-century Kaigani Haida settlement in southern Alaska and the early stages of Lekwiltok movement southward
  • Technologies such as metal tools gradually supplemented stone and bone, and the scale of carving and ceremonial display changed accordingly

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on salmon, shellfish, sea mammals, and cedar, and on the knowledge and labor needed to use them
  • The primacy of canoes and sea routes in travel, trade, and politics
  • Houses, hereditary names, and inherited rights as the basis of property and authority
  • Public ceremony and oral history as the means of validating claims

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual and uneven and was driven by Indigenous decisions and conditions, while core institutions remained stable enough to be recognizable across long periods. This helps explain why many of these institutions survived later pressure and why they form the basis of present-day claims.

Section 33.21

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This section gathers the chapter's review materials: a summary, key terms, people and places, chronology and geography exercises, source and review questions, writing exercises, a research project, and a closing reflection. Students should use them to test their understanding of how the Northwest Coast's societies worked and how historians know it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Northwest Coast societies transformed a productive but demanding maritime environment into interconnected political worlds through ecological knowledge, storage, large permanent communities, maritime and woodworking technology, inherited rights, ranked institutions, and networks of trade, ceremony, diplomacy, and warfare. Abundance alone does not explain these societies, since the resources of the coast became politically significant through management, labor, and law.

The coast is best understood as an Indigenous maritime world organized by water routes, not by modern borders. Its geography of islands, fjords, straits, and rivers made canoes the principal means of travel and linked peoples across what later became the Alaska–Canada boundary and the boundary through the Salish Sea.

Historians reconstruct this world from archaeology, including waterlogged sites such as Ozette, from oral histories that preserve territorial and event-based knowledge, from linguistics, from a small number of early European records, and from later ethnography. Because the most detailed descriptions date from after epidemics and the fur trade, the chapter has distinguished between evidence for the eve of colonialism and later documentation.

The peoples of the coast spoke languages of at least seven families or isolates, and language did not correspond to political nation. The Tlingit and Haida were organized in matrilineal moieties, clans, and houses. The Tsimshianic nations, the Nisga'a, Gitxsan, and Tsimshian, had four crest-based clans and houses, and the central coast peoples linked northern and southern systems. The Kwakwaka'wakw were many tribal-level groups with ranked house-groups and elaborate winter ceremonial systems, and the Nuu-chah-nulth and Makah combined whaling and chiefly territorial authority on the outer coast. The Coast Salish used bilateral kinship and marriage networks across many villages, and the Chinookan peoples of the lower Columbia brokered exchange between the coast and the interior.

The economy rested on salmon, eulachon, herring, halibut, shellfish, sea mammals, whales, and plants, and on technologies of cedar working, canoe building, and fiber work. Preserved foods and stored goods, organized labor, and resource rights allowed communities to support large populations, and the debate over salmon abundance showed that scholars continue to disagree about the relative weight of environment and social organization.

Houses were both buildings and corporate groups, holding names, crests, and territories. Rank, wealth, and slavery operated differently across the coast, and enslaved people performed labor without kin rights. The potlatch and related feasts were legal, political, and economic institutions that publicly validated names, marriages, titles, and territorial rights, and they cannot be reduced to displays of gift-giving.

Trade ran along canoe routes and inland trails, carrying eulachon grease, dentalium, copper, obsidian, and many other goods between the coast, the Plateau, and the Subarctic. Warfare, raiding, and captive-taking were part of coastal life, balanced by diplomacy, marriage alliances, and peace-making, though much of the detailed evidence dates from the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and requires caution about earlier periods.

Ceremony, oral tradition, and crest art recorded history and law, and the coast on the eve of sustained colonialism was a network of sovereign communities. The first Russian, Spanish, and British voyages, in 1741, 1774, and 1778, met communities that often set the terms of contact, and the chapter ends at the threshold of the maritime fur trade, without treating later outcomes as inevitable. The continuities of houses, names, rights, and ceremony explain why these institutions remain central to modern Northwest Coast nations.

Grease trail
An overland trade route carrying eulachon grease and other goods between the coast and the interior.
Chinuk Wawa (Chinook Jargon)
A trade language of the lower Columbia and beyond, whose precontact roots and later expansion remain debated.
Endonym and exonym
A people's own name for itself (such as Nuu-chah-nulth) versus a name used by outsiders (such as "Nootka").

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a coastal environment became the setting for complex political worlds without becoming their cause. The Northwest Coast demonstrates that sedentary, ranked, artistically sophisticated societies could develop and persist on the basis of fishing, hunting, gathering, and woodworking, and it shows that the institutions that made this possible, houses, hereditary rights, feasts, and trade, were works of law, labor, and knowledge, not accidents of abundance.

The chapter also reveals how much depends on evidence and its limits. The coast is documented by archaeology, oral history, linguistics, and a late and mediated ethnographic record, and the best accounts combine these without overstating any one. Examples such as the Nisga'a eruption account, the Nuu-chah-nulth tsunami tradition, and the Ozette village show what such combinations can achieve, and the debate over salmon shows where interpretation remains open.

Many of the institutions described here shaped later history. The legal and political importance of houses, names, and territories lies behind the resource claims, fisheries disputes, and land and treaty negotiations treated in Chapters 72, 113, and 117, and the attack on the potlatch in Chapter 85. The coast's role in continental exchange prepares the synthesis in Chapter 36, and the continuing importance of feasts, art, and oral history informs the cultural resurgence examined in Volume X.

The chapter has ended deliberately at a threshold. By the 1740s and 1770s, Northwest Coast nations were sovereign communities with their own histories, alliances, and conflicts. What came next, including epidemics, the fur trade, colonial settlement, and the legal struggles that followed, was shaped by Indigenous choices and resistance as well as by outside power. Understanding the coast before that moment makes it possible to see what was at stake and why the continuities that survived mattered.
