Chapter Opening

Alaska, 1942: Indigenous volunteers defended coastal homelands while Unangax̂ families were ordered from theirs.

In 1942 Indigenous North Americans found themselves on sharply different sides of decisions taken in the name of national defense. Alaska Native volunteers organized to watch northern coasts and report danger, while Unangax̂ families were ordered away from villages they had inhabited for generations. The same conflict that drew some people into military cooperation subjected others to forced displacement. Beginning with these simultaneous experiences makes the Second World War a history of Indigenous homelands and political authority as well as a history of overseas combat.

The Alaska Territorial Guard depended on local knowledge of waterways, seasonal ice, animals, and travel routes. Indigenous volunteers participated because they lived in and understood the regions their governments sought to defend. Yet American authorities also evacuated 881 Unangax̂ civilians from nine Aleutian communities into inadequate camps in Southeast Alaska, where many died. Attuans, meanwhile, endured captivity under Japanese forces. The contrast illustrates why one simple label, patriotic service or wartime victimization, cannot encompass Indigenous experience.

The scale of service elsewhere was considerable. The Smithsonian reports about 44,000 American Indian military personnel during the war, including hundreds of women. Veterans Affairs Canada records at least 3,000 First Nations members of the Canadian armed forces, while noting that Métis and Inuit totals were not consistently maintained. These are estimates derived from different definitions, not numbers that can be combined to calculate a precise continental rate of participation.

Indigenous soldiers fought and worked across North Africa, Italy, France, Germany, the Pacific islands, and the Aleutians. Women entered military auxiliaries, nursing, transport, and aviation support. Service records identify individuals and units; official decorations record selected battlefield acts; personal testimony often reveals the costs that administrative papers omit. Those evidentiary differences matter when a historian moves from celebrated names to the wider population whose service attracted little public notice.

Far from the battlefield, industrial production and military procurement changed patterns of work. Indigenous men and women entered shipyards, aircraft factories, farms, transport systems, and mining. Some workers moved to growing cities and formed new intertribal connections; others remained home to operate farms, sustain fisheries, and care for children and elders. These changes were uneven and had different consequences for families facing discrimination, limited housing, or the loss of wage work after demobilization.

Wartime political language stressed democracy, freedom, and national unity, but Indigenous nations still confronted treaty violations, unequal services, and restrictions on political rights. Some leaders supported recruitment while challenging compulsory service, land use, or the treatment of returning veterans. In 1944 delegates founded the National Congress of American Indians in Denver, extending an older tradition of organized advocacy. The relationship between wartime contribution and political equality was thus an unresolved issue inside the war itself.

This chapter examines that relationship across five divisions. It begins with recruitment and legal status, follows service in global campaigns, turns to the war in Alaska and the Arctic, reconstructs home-front economic change, and concludes with political organizing and demobilization in 1945. It does not invent thoughts or dialogue for historical participants. Primary records, community histories, and later scholarly or institutional investigations are distinguished according to their origins. Code talkers receive their detailed treatment in Chapter 102, and the longer postwar consequences in Chapter 103.

Historical Context

Before the war Indigenous nations within the United States operated under diverse governments and federal arrangements. New Deal reforms had created some new constitutional possibilities without resolving land losses or administrative control. Treaty relationships, tribal citizenship, subsistence and wage economies, schools, and local institutions shaped the resources with which communities responded to military mobilization.

In Canada First Nations under the Indian Act, Métis communities outside most reserve structures, and Inuit under northern administrative systems did not share the same political or legal position. Federal restrictions and the geographic isolation of some reserves affected access to paid employment and services. Earlier Indigenous military participation in the First World War influenced some families, but neither it nor citizenship resolved contested treaty rights.

Alaska Native communities occupied a rapidly changing territory whose military importance rose as the Pacific conflict expanded. Greenland remained a Danish colony, with its overseas connection disrupted by German occupation of Denmark in 1940. Colonial rules differed across these regions, and wartime projects were conducted through different systems of authority.

The chapter uses Indigenous names where identifiable and quotes historical outside designations only for documentary clarity. Wartime records often classify people by administrative status, not by their own nations; consequently published service totals should be interpreted as estimates. Historical context is provided only to explain the years 1939–1945, with the fuller prewar and postwar histories assigned to adjacent chapters.

Division 1 · Section 101.1

Indigenous North America on the Eve of the Second World War

The Second World War did not arrive in an Indigenous North America whose peoples were politically or economically alike. In the United States, federal policy in the 1930s had partially reversed the assault on tribal institutions associated with allotment, but the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 had not restored lands lost in preceding generations or created uniform prosperity. Some nations adopted constitutions under the new legislation, some refused those forms, and others continued governments whose authority rested on institutions older than the United States. Federal administrators remained influential over budgets, schooling, resource management, and decisions that tribal leaders regarded as their own. The Great Depression had exposed the vulnerability of rural employment and commodity prices. Yet tribal councils, ceremonial associations, kinship organizations, churches, and locally developed enterprises provided resources for collective response when the war came.

The political position of First Nations in Canada was different. The Indian Act gave federal officials extensive authority over reserve lands and administrative matters, while Indigenous nations continued to assert treaty promises and their own forms of governance. Status under Canadian legislation was not identical with citizenship or the ability to vote without restrictions; provincial and federal practices differed, and enfranchisement could entail the loss of Indian status. Many Métis communities, meanwhile, fell outside the reserve-based administrative system despite their distinct historical identity and political claims. An assessment of wartime recruitment that treats all these populations as a single group would conceal why Canadian conscription prompted objections framed in treaty language, why military enlistment data were incomplete, and why access to veterans' assistance later depended so much on administrative categories.

In Alaska, territorial institutions governed an immense region in which Indigenous communities had received highly unequal access to schools, health services, transportation, and political representation. The Alaska Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood had developed rights campaigns before the war and would continue them during it. Iñupiat, Yup'ik, Unangax̂, Tlingit, Athabascan, and other peoples did not share a single colonial experience. The maritime Aleutians were soon to become a battlefield; villages facing the Bering Sea would help watch for potential invasion; Southeast Alaska was tied more closely to canneries, shipping, and territorial politics. In Greenland, Kalaallit communities experienced Danish colonial administration, a regulated trading economy, and local councils, with different wartime consequences after Germany occupied Denmark in 1940. These distinctions are central rather than incidental to the continental narrative.

Indigenous communities also entered the conflict with substantial histories of military participation. During the First World War, American Indian and First Nations soldiers had enlisted even while their nations experienced restrictions on political authority. Families remembered service, injury, separation, and the difficulties of returning to civilian life. Some young people in 1939 or 1941 saw enlistment through that intergenerational experience; others assessed it against immediate economic needs or the threat to their homelands. The existence of military traditions did not erase opposition to conscription or concerns about federal encroachment. A treaty nation could value military service by individual members and still reject the proposition that an outside government possessed unrestricted authority over those members.

The economic geography of Indigenous North America before the war helps explain its different effects. Farming and ranching communities, fishing peoples, timber producers, wage laborers, artisans, and hunters were connected to broader markets on unequal terms. Reservation roads, remote northern transportation routes, and the location of industrial plants determined who could move quickly into wartime jobs. Family responsibilities, language, health, education, and discriminatory hiring also shaped opportunities. The war would not create all these conditions, but it would magnify them by drawing labor toward factories, enlisting working-age adults, expanding military spending, and placing strategic installations on Indigenous lands. By 1945 some families had entered unfamiliar urban economies, while others faced shortages or forced removal.

The chapter begins from this varied political landscape because a simple question—how many Indigenous people joined the armed forces—cannot explain the war's significance. Service totals depend on who counted, whether volunteers and draftees were distinguished, and whether officials recognized Métis or Inuit identity in their records. Military success must be set alongside the decisions of community governments and families, and economic opportunities must be compared with displacement. The central issue is how wartime mobilization intersected with sovereign relationships already under strain. Indigenous peoples neither entered the conflict from outside modern history nor ceased to be distinct political communities when they joined Allied forces.

Division 1 · Section 101.2

The Coming of War and Indigenous Responses

Canada declared war on Germany in September 1939 after parliamentary consideration, whereas the United States entered the global conflict after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 and subsequent declarations of war. The different dates created different rhythms of enlistment, recruitment, and domestic mobilization. Canadian First Nations volunteers appeared early in the war and entered an overseas military already engaged with the British Commonwealth. In the United States, Native men and women followed events in Europe and the Pacific before their government became a belligerent, and military registration began under the peacetime draft of 1940. Alaska's strategic importance was increasingly evident as relations with Japan deteriorated. No single declaration immediately transformed every Indigenous community, but each changed what governments asked from them.

Motives for service were multiple and cannot be reduced to either uncomplicated American or Canadian nationalism or universal resistance to colonial rule. Some recruits wanted to defend home territories against invasion, especially when the Pacific War reached Alaska. Some saw service as compatible with duties to their own nations and families; others were influenced by relatives who had fought in earlier conflicts or by the public standing awarded to service in their communities. Pay, food, clothing, training, and the prospect of leaving places with scarce employment mattered. For young women, auxiliary branches offered routes into skilled work and mobility that could be difficult to secure otherwise. Such motives could coexist within a single person's decision, and they changed as the war progressed.

Indigenous opposition to fascism also operated within a complicated political framework. The Allied language of freedom could resonate with people who understood the danger of military aggression but also remembered treaty violations, coerced schooling, and state discrimination. Some Indigenous leaders embraced wartime mobilization while arguing that their governments deserved recognition as nations with their own treaty relationships. Other communities objected to compulsory service or to the expectation that treaty partners should bear burdens while rights remained unfulfilled. Neither response was necessarily inconsistent: supporting soldiers, demanding fair treatment, and questioning a government's authority to conscript could all belong to the same political campaign. Historians must read Indigenous statements in their local context instead of dividing them neatly into loyal and disloyal camps.

Official propaganda often simplified these distinctions. Newspapers depicted Native recruits as evidence of national unity or celebrated a supposed ancient warrior instinct. Such representations could offer public recognition, but they could also substitute racial stereotypes for soldiers' training, decision-making, and individual abilities. The same press might praise an Indigenous soldier abroad while giving scant attention to discrimination faced by Native families at home. Ceremonial sendoffs, community fundraising, letters from overseas, and later veterans' testimony provide different ways of reconstructing what mobilization meant to participants. Published stories about heroic individuals need to be checked against military records rather than used as the whole history of a community's contribution.

The war affected communities that produced few uniformed personnel as well as those that sent many. Recruiting officers, rationing rules, transportation demands, local surveillance, construction contracts, and military purchases reached places far from the front. A household might have one person in uniform, another working in a cannery or defense plant, and younger relatives maintaining farming, trapping, or fishing activity. Northern communities could find themselves inside newly important strategic corridors despite having played little role in the diplomatic crises that led to the war. Greenland illustrates this especially clearly: occupation of Denmark severed ordinary colonial lines of communication and prompted major decisions about defense made by governments and diplomats outside the island.

The significance of 1939–1942 is therefore not simply the outbreak of fighting. It marks the beginning of extraordinary demands on Indigenous labor, mobility, and political relationships. Government agencies sought manpower and material; Native families assessed risks and obligations; leaders connected wartime service to enduring claims for rights. The years of mobilization set up a contradiction that runs through the chapter: Indigenous people could be indispensable to national defense yet marginalized when decisions concerned their own lands and communities. Understanding that contradiction requires attention to specific institutions and choices, not the assumption that service automatically produced either assimilation or equality.

Division 1 · Section 101.3

Recruitment, Enlistment, Conscription, and Sovereignty

Recruitment and conscription exposed differences between the legal relationships of Indigenous peoples and the states in which they lived. In the United States, the Selective Training and Service Act of September 1940 established the first peacetime national draft. Registration and later wartime mobilization applied within a country where federal citizenship had been extended broadly to American Indians in 1924, but tribal citizenship and sovereign political relationships continued to exist. Federal citizenship did not end discrimination in voting or public life, and it did not erase tribal claims to self-government. Military boards and recruiters encountered men whose circumstances varied by reservation, state, employment, family responsibility, and health. Statistics describing voluntary enlistment and drafted service should not be merged without checking the categories used by the original source.

Many American Indian men volunteered; others were inducted through the draft. Some saw enlistment as an opportunity to exercise choice over service branch or assignment, while induction carried different obligations. Tribal governments and local leaders sometimes held ceremonies for departing members or organized support for families. These public acts should not be mistaken for blanket endorsements of every federal military policy. The relationship between a citizen's duty to the United States and membership in a sovereign Native nation was not identical for all communities. Officers conducting recruitment often relied on familiar assumptions about Native men's supposed suitability for scouting, but induction standards were official military standards, not an expression of a collective Indigenous political obligation.

Canadian conscription followed a more complicated timeline. The National Resources Mobilization Act of 1940 allowed compulsory service for home defense and required people to register for national purposes; the Canadian government did not initially impose general overseas conscription. During the war, overseas volunteers remained essential to Canadian operations, and the pressure to send conscripts abroad became politically contentious. In late 1944 the government authorized deployment of some conscripts overseas amid a severe infantry replacement crisis. First Nations people and their leaders faced the additional question whether treaties or status under the Indian Act limited compulsory military obligations. These disputes were not rhetorical additions to an otherwise settled national policy; they affected decisions made by officials and registrars.

Historical Vocabulary

Conscription, Enlistment, and Treaty Obligation

Conscription is compulsory service imposed by government; volunteering is a person’s choice to enlist. Treaty relationships describe political obligations between governments that are not automatically cancelled by ordinary citizenship. Wartime laws, registration, induction and assignment should be distinguished. The principal misunderstanding is to infer from a service total how many volunteered, or to claim one national conscription rule applied in precisely the same way to every Indigenous community.

A Canadian Department of National Defence history records that in December 1944 the government determined that First Nations people were generally liable for service, but without a universal treaty-based exemption. Administrators recognized that particular treaty claims might justify different treatment and that some treaty-related objections specifically concerned compulsory overseas service. Early in 1945 Indian Affairs issued directions limiting call-ups for people in certain remote or linguistic circumstances. These administrative rules did not translate into a single uniform experience across Canada. Regional interpretation, uneven enforcement, and the distinction between voluntary and compulsory service mattered. The chapter should therefore avoid describing all First Nations men either as wholly exempt or as identically subject to conscription.

The problem of evidence is equally important. Canadian military records did not consistently preserve whether a recruit identified as Métis or Inuit, and the word 'Indian' in a particular document may reflect legal status rather than a comprehensive Indigenous identity. Records of the United States likewise use shifting ethnic classifications and may exclude people whose Indigenous ancestry or community membership was not recorded by military clerks. Enlistment statistics are valuable for establishing scale, but they cannot supply a complete picture of motives or of the participation of all Indigenous populations. A figure of at least 3,000 First Nations service members is a minimum for one group, not an estimate of every Indigenous Canadian participant.

Conscription could mobilize Indigenous political action. Leaders petitioned about treaty rights, administrators answered requests for clarification, and families had to assess economic consequences when a young adult was called for service. A parent might accept voluntary military work yet oppose enforced departure; an elder might distinguish defending the home continent from fighting overseas; a veteran might favor service while questioning unequal access to government programs. Those positions belong in a history of political choice, not merely in a legal footnote. By the end of the war, the question of military obligation had revealed how incomplete the relationship between Indigenous sovereignty, citizenship, and federal power remained.

Division 1 · Section 101.4

Training, Integration, and Discrimination

Military training brought recruits into institutions designed to standardize conduct, skill, and discipline. Basic instruction stressed drill, weapons handling, physical conditioning, teamwork, maintenance, and obedience within a chain of command. Indigenous volunteers and draftees entered training camps with widely varied experiences: some had worked as ranch hands, fishers, loggers, or mechanics, some had been students, and others had experience in town or city employment. Their success in service cannot be explained by ethnicity. Training, opportunity, leadership, personal aptitude, and the demands of a particular unit shaped performance in ways comparable to those affecting other recruits. The military nevertheless often assumed that Native soldiers possessed special instinctive abilities in woodland movement or reconnaissance.

The racial structure of wartime American forces was not uniform. American Indians generally served in integrated units with white personnel, unlike the systematic segregation imposed on many African American military formations, but that distinction must not be mistaken for an absence of discrimination. Indigenous soldiers could experience hostile language, pressure to conform to stereotypes, and the denial of recognition for individual identities. At the same time, shared danger and close cooperation within units sometimes produced camaraderie across civilian racial boundaries. What a soldier experienced depended on unit culture, superiors, region, and the battlefield. An account that declares the wartime military either uniformly egalitarian or invariably hostile would flatten this diversity.

Canadian forces also presented possibilities and constraints. In many cases First Nations personnel served alongside non-Indigenous Canadians, and military life offered pay, travel, training, and recognition unavailable at home. Yet the broader society was marked by legal restrictions, and Indigenous soldiers did not surrender those restrictions simply by putting on a uniform. Recruits could encounter misunderstanding of their home communities, pressure to assimilate, and unequal assumptions about competence. Stories of distinguished service should include the institutional setting in which those distinctions were earned and the ways veterans later described their experiences. Recognition in the field did not ensure that the same person's rights would be respected after discharge.

Service introduced unfamiliar places and new kinds of knowledge. Recruits from inland communities could train at coastal bases; men accustomed to seasonal subsistence might become vehicle operators or communications specialists; women recruited for auxiliary work could enter administrative and technical organizations. Letters, photographs, wartime newspapers, and oral interviews reveal adjustments to barracks life, food, language, social expectations, and military hierarchy. Those sources must be handled carefully. A celebratory newspaper story might omit boredom or discrimination; a personal recollection recorded many years later can preserve meaning while compressing the chronology. Military personnel files document postings and dates more reliably than feelings, but are less capable of explaining personal motivation.

One recurring stereotype cast Indigenous soldiers as 'natural warriors.' It sometimes appeared complimentary, especially when praising courage, endurance, or skill, but it placed racial explanation where a historian should analyze training and circumstance. It also narrowed the range of Indigenous participation by overlooking clerks, cooks, engineers, medics, mechanics, pilots, and women. Native service members were not all in combat arms, and those who were had no single experience of war. In addition, public imagery could appropriate ceremonial clothing or tribal symbols as decoration while ignoring the actual political organizations that sustained their communities. Studying how these representations circulated helps students distinguish Indigenous military history from the wartime mythology built around it.

The experience of training and military integration therefore has two consequences for the larger chapter. First, it explains how people from diverse nations became participants in enormous Allied systems that depended on coordinated specialization. Second, it reveals that military inclusion was conditional and uneven: discipline and shared service did not eliminate the political distinctions of Indigenous nations, and battlefield respect did not necessarily carry into civilian institutions. Later sections examine individual campaigns and the consequences of return, but the analytical point begins here. Military institutions could expand individual possibilities while reproducing, revising, or temporarily suspending the assumptions of the societies that created them.

Division 2 · Section 101.5

American Indian Soldiers in the European and Mediterranean Campaigns

The Allied campaigns in North Africa and Europe placed American Indian soldiers in some of the war's most demanding military settings. Indigenous recruits did not generally fight as separate ethnic units; they served in infantry divisions, artillery batteries, engineer formations, communications branches, supply services, and medical organizations. This breadth is important. The movement of an army depended not only on soldiers under fire but also on mechanics who maintained vehicles, drivers who carried fuel, medics who evacuated wounded personnel, and engineers who opened roads or bridged obstacles. Accounts dominated by a handful of decorations can inadvertently hide most Indigenous service. The historical task is to place individual achievements within the formations, commands, and logistical systems that made the campaigns possible.

The U.S. 45th Infantry Division offers a well-documented case. Known by its Thunderbird insignia, it included many American Indian soldiers and participated in the campaigns of Sicily and Italy before fighting in France and Germany. The presence of a Thunderbird symbol associated in varying ways with Indigenous cultures should not be treated as proof that the division was an Indigenous political institution. It was a United States Army formation with soldiers of different backgrounds, and its campaigns reflected Allied strategy and operational command. For Native soldiers, however, the division could provide a shared setting in which service, public recognition, cultural representation, and wartime sacrifice intersected. Individual service records remain necessary to determine who served in specific actions.

Ernest Childers, a member of the Muscogee (Creek) Nation, received the Medal of Honor for action near Oliveto, Italy, on September 22, 1943, while serving with the 180th Infantry Regiment, 45th Division. Official citations describe how he advanced under fire against enemy positions. His actions deserve attention as military decisions made amid particular tactical conditions, not as confirmation of a timeless racial trait. Childers's military life extended beyond one celebrated episode, and his later career illustrates the distinction between a battle citation and the experience of service as a whole. The award's importance is real, but the everyday risks faced by less publicly known Indigenous soldiers were equally part of the division's history.

Winter conditions, destroyed transportation, resistance in towns and forests, and casualties among replacements altered units over time. Division 2 · Section 101.6

Indigenous Soldiers in the Pacific War

The Pacific War demanded military operations across immense distances, and Indigenous Americans participated in a variety of Army, Navy, Marine Corps, and supporting roles. Island campaigns required amphibious landings, naval support, engineering, the movement of supplies, and sustained ground combat against Japanese forces. The geography made logistics decisive: food, ammunition, medical supplies, replacement personnel, and communications had to be moved among scattered bases and ships. Native service members encountered tropical conditions in some campaigns and exposed volcanic terrain in others. A history of their participation should therefore include the operational environments of the Solomon Islands, New Guinea, the Marianas, the Philippines, and Iwo Jima without claiming that every individual fought in all of these places.

Military service in the Pacific involved both direct fighting and less visible work. Engineers repaired airstrips and roads; sailors maintained ships and carried supplies; medical personnel treated wounded men and responded to infectious disease; communications specialists carried messages whose speed and security could affect entire units. Indigenous personnel served alongside other Americans under the command structures of the armed forces. Some came from communities with maritime experience, but military training and assigned duties determined their immediate responsibilities. Wartime writing occasionally described Native soldiers as exceptionally skilled at jungle warfare or tracking. That language should be analyzed as stereotyping unless a particular individual's experience is supported by evidence.

Ira Hayes, an Akimel O'odham Marine, became one of the most widely represented Indigenous service members of the war. On February 23, 1945, he was among the six men photographed by Joe Rosenthal raising a large American flag on Mount Suribachi during the Battle of Iwo Jima. The celebrated photograph records the second raising of a flag on that height that day, not the end of the battle, which continued with severe casualties. Hayes's identification, his home community, and the circumstances of the photograph should be separated from the simplified public narrative of instant victory. A photograph is powerful evidence for a precise event, but its later circulation can create meanings its subjects neither planned nor controlled.

Hayes's wartime visibility also illustrates the difference between public honor and the full experience of a person who served. Government publicity and bond campaigns drew on the image of the flag raising, while the men who had survived Iwo Jima remained connected to the losses of their unit. It is misleading to narrate Hayes's history solely through later difficulties or solely through celebratory symbolism. His service, the photograph's political uses, and the experience of returning to a country with unequal treatment of Native communities require distinct analysis. The broader point applies to others whose wartime identities were reduced to a single image or decoration.

Indigenous contributions to communications formed part of this theater, but Chapter 102 is devoted to code talkers and the organization of military language programs. Here it is enough to explain why accurate and secure communications mattered in fast-moving combat and why Native-language personnel were recruited for specialized assignments. Such programs did not encompass all Indigenous service, nor were they the only forms of Indigenous military knowledge. Limiting their discussion in this chapter preserves room for the next chapter to examine code construction, training, combat application, secrecy, and recognition from the standpoint of documentary evidence and language communities.

The Pacific theater also had direct consequences for Indigenous homelands. The northern Pacific campaign brought Japanese forces to the Aleutian Islands, while industrial expansion on the American West Coast attracted Indigenous civilian workers to shipyards and aircraft plants. These two developments belong to the same geography of war but affected communities differently. An overseas marine, a dockyard worker, a Guard volunteer on the Bering Sea coast, and an Unangax̂ family forcibly evacuated from its village all participated in wartime history. The Pacific War should therefore not be reduced to an overseas campaign separated from North American political and civilian life. Its strategic demands reshaped the continent as well as distant battlefields.

Division 2 · Section 101.7

First Nations, Métis, and Inuit Personnel in Canadian Service

First Nations, Métis, and Inuit individuals served in Canadian forces despite unequal legal status and, in many cases, discriminatory conditions at home. Veterans Affairs Canada documents at least 3,000 First Nations personnel during the Second World War, including women, while acknowledging that records do not consistently identify Métis, Inuit, and other Indigenous members. Such figures establish a minimum rather than a complete national total. Canadians entered the war in 1939, so Indigenous volunteers could join the expanding Army, Royal Canadian Navy, Royal Canadian Air Force, and associated organizations before American entry. Service was spread across occupations and units, and a complete history cannot be represented by a single celebrated combat regiment.

The Canadian Army fought in the Mediterranean and Northwest Europe. Indigenous soldiers were present among the forces that trained overseas, took part in operations in Italy, participated in the Normandy campaign and subsequent advance, and supported the military through transportation, engineering, medical, and supply duties. Some returned with decorations; others were wounded or killed without becoming nationally known. The conditions of service differed among branches and roles, but the shared institution of the armed forces could create relationships beyond home communities. Military records often show posting, rank, and disciplinary outcomes more clearly than Indigenous identity, making systematic research difficult. This limitation should be stated rather than filled by speculative totals.

Thomas George 'Tommy' Prince, an Anishinaabe/Ojibwe soldier from Manitoba, provides a detailed example. He served with the First Special Service Force, a joint Canadian-American unit often called the Devil's Brigade, and gained recognition for hazardous reconnaissance and observation. Near the Italian front in 1944, he maintained an observation position and communications under enemy pressure; official accounts describe how he repaired a damaged line while disguising his movement. The story shows technical skill, initiative, and risk in a particular military setting. Prince's later service in Korea belongs to another historical period and should not be imported into the 1939–1945 account to enlarge its wartime achievements artificially.

David Greyeyes, a Cree soldier from Saskatchewan, also served with distinction, providing another route into the diversity of Canadian military experience. Individual biographies are useful when the historian identifies what they can establish: dates, postings, awards, and reported experiences. They cannot by themselves supply statistics about every First Nations volunteer or explain all reactions to federal policy. Communities supported soldiers through letters, fundraising, and responsibilities carried by relatives who remained home. Indigenous political leaders could regard distinguished service as evidence that the state owed greater respect to treaty partners while continuing to oppose encroachments on local authority.

First Nations women's military participation is better documented in some branches than others, and the categories used in wartime statistics need explanation. Veterans Affairs Canada records at least seventy-two First Nations women among those who joined the armed forces, while the true scope of Indigenous women's work also included civilian employment, nursing, and community organization. Métis and Inuit service can be especially difficult to recover from personnel files that did not record identity in the same way as later organizations. The danger is not only undercounting but the creation of a narrative in which archival silence is mistaken for historical absence. Oral histories, family records, and community commemorations can expand the account, provided they are used with appropriate source criticism.

Military participation did not automatically confer equality after discharge. Canadian federal and provincial arrangements affecting voting rights, reserve administration, land, and veterans' benefits continued to shape lives differently from those of non-Indigenous personnel. Access to advice and paperwork, geographic distance from government offices, and the interpretation of benefit rules affected outcomes. Chapter 103 will examine the postwar period in detail. For this chapter, the essential wartime conclusion is that First Nations, Métis, and Inuit service was real, varied, and sometimes publicly acclaimed, but the relationship between participation and political equality remained unresolved at the end of the war.

Division 2 · Section 101.8

Indigenous Women in Uniform

Indigenous women's participation in the Second World War is often obscured when military history is narrated entirely through infantry units and battlefield decorations. Women joined auxiliary organizations, nursing services, clerical and communications branches, and wartime aviation programs. They also performed civilian industrial work and sustained households from which soldiers had departed. These were distinct forms of contribution, and the chapter should not classify all women in factories as military personnel. The opening of military opportunities was shaped by recruiting rules, gender expectations, prior education, and the racial assumptions of officers and the public. For some Indigenous women, service provided pay and mobility; for others, family needs or exclusion limited the available choices.

Minnie Spotted Wolf, a Blackfeet woman from Montana, joined the United States Marine Corps Women's Reserve in 1943. National Archives materials document her ranch upbringing and her service as a driver and heavy-equipment operator. Her experience challenges stereotypes that women entered the war only in clerical roles: vehicle and equipment work demanded training and responsibility. The press sometimes portrayed her as remarkable because of her Indigenous identity or her physical competence, but such reporting can exaggerate novelty while overlooking the skilled labor women performed as a group. Her biography should be interpreted alongside the broader development of women's branches and the constraints placed on their assignments.

Ola Mildred Rexroat, an Oglala Lakota woman, was the only Native American woman presently known to have served in the Women Airforce Service Pilots program. After training she was assigned to tow aerial targets for gunnery practice, an activity requiring precise flying near aircraft using live ammunition. The WASP program carried considerable danger, yet its women aviators occupied an ambiguous institutional position and did not receive the same wartime military status as male pilots. Rexroat's experience can illuminate aviation labor and the uneven recognition of women's service without treating her as a representative of all Native women or implying she flew combat missions. Records at the National Park Service and Air Force historical sites allow her wartime assignments to be distinguished from later recognition.

Division 3 · Section 101.9

The Alaska Territorial Guard and Indigenous Defense of the North

In 1942 the Japanese advance across the northern Pacific gave new urgency to the defense of Alaska's vast coastline. The Alaska Territorial Guard was organized after regular National Guard forces had been called to federal service, leaving remote communities far from large garrisons. Territorial officials and Army personnel sought a locally recruited force capable of observing movements, reporting unusual activity, and helping defend settlements. The Guard was neither a ceremonial organization nor a standard front-line combat division. Its distinctive importance came from the relationship between military surveillance and the knowledge of residents whose lives already depended on travel, weather judgment, and detailed familiarity with coastlines and river systems.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian reports more than 6,300 Alaska Native volunteers from 107 communities serving in the Territorial Guard. This figure is a documented institutional estimate, not a count of everyone who defended Alaska, and the terminology of the period often labeled portions of the force 'Eskimo Scouts.' A responsible textbook should explain the historical label while using the names of Iñupiat, Yup'ik, Athabascan, and other peoples wherever appropriate. Volunteers included people whose age, family responsibilities, or circumstances would have prevented them from entering the regular military. Women and elders contributed alongside younger men, and community participation gave the force a composition unlike that of ordinary American units.

Guard responsibilities included maintaining observation points, reporting aircraft or vessels, carrying messages, and supporting the security of settlements and transport routes. In northern and western Alaska, travel by boat or dog team depended on local expertise and the ability to judge ice, currents, weather, and seasonal hazards. Soldiers from distant bases could not acquire this knowledge simply by reading a chart. Indigenous guards brought skills developed through daily work and collective memory, but their usefulness also depended on organization, communication with military authorities, and access to suitable equipment. The force's limitations in weapons, supplies, and formal training should be acknowledged; military officials did not always provide adequate resources for the duties they asked volunteers to perform.

The term should not be read as identifying one people, excluding women, or implying all volunteers served in identical conditions.

The Guard provides a clear example of Indigenous agency operating within an outside military structure. Defending a village against a possible foreign attack could be understood as the defense of ancestral homeland rather than surrendering the right to govern it. Recordkeeping and recognition present historical problems. Rosters and individual service records were not always maintained to later standards, and some participants struggled to document service after the war. Wartime surveillance relied on those networks while potentially drawing them more closely into territorial government. The Guard thus belongs in both military and Indigenous political history. It illustrates how a colonial state depended upon the skills and voluntary labor of people over whose lands it asserted authority, and how Indigenous communities could use a wartime institution to defend a homeland understood on their own terms.

Division 3 · Section 101.10

The Japanese Occupation of Attu and Kiska

The Aleutian Islands form a chain across the northern Pacific, and in 1942 their location made them strategically important to Japan and the United States. The islands were also long-inhabited Unangax̂ homelands, not vacant stepping-stones for armies. In early June 1942 Japanese aircraft attacked the American base area at Dutch Harbor on Unalaska Island; Japanese forces then occupied Attu and Kiska farther west. The operations were connected to broader Pacific strategy, but local consequences cannot be understood by treating the islands simply as points on a military map. Fishing, maritime travel, Orthodox institutions, villages, and ancestral places were suddenly exposed to occupation, bombardment, and the decisions of competing militaries.

Attu's residents experienced a fate different from that of most other Unangax̂ communities. The National Park Service records that Japanese forces captured forty-two Attuan villagers and transported them to Japan in 1942. The villagers endured captivity and deprivation in Otaru on Hokkaido, and some died before the end of the war. Their experience was not an American evacuation to a supposedly safer place; it was imprisonment under an occupying power. The surviving Attuans eventually learned that their home village would not be restored for their return. Postwar American decisions about military use and rebuilding made the displacement permanent for them, despite their survival of captivity.

American forces undertook a costly campaign to retake Attu in May 1943. Severe weather, difficult terrain, limited visibility, and entrenched Japanese positions contributed to the intensity of fighting. The operation required naval transport, air and artillery support, and infantry advancing across terrain in which exposure and illness were major threats. Wartime reports and later military histories emphasize casualties and tactical decisions; an Indigenous history must also ask what destruction of the battlefield meant for the island's original community. The island's strategic importance to military planners did not automatically translate into a commitment to reconstruct Attuan civil life after the fighting.

Kiska presented a different military episode. Japanese forces withdrew from the island before the Allied landing in August 1943, and the attacking force discovered the principal occupation force gone. The episode reminds students that military intelligence, weather, and uncertainty shape operations as much as the intentions of commanders. The costly preparation and danger of landing did not produce the expected battle, but the war had already changed the islands through fortifications and military occupation. Kiska's history should not be collapsed into the Attu campaign even though both islands were occupied in June 1942 and both appear in narratives of the Aleutian campaign.

The Aleutian fighting affected civilians beyond Attu. American authorities evacuated Unangax̂ people from other communities, concentrating them in camps far from familiar environments. Some villages were occupied or used by military forces while residents were absent, and homes and property suffered from abandonment, vandalism, or military use. The existence of an external enemy furnished an official rationale for removal, but the conditions of confinement and the obstacles to return have to be evaluated separately from the military threat. A history that moves directly from the Japanese attack to Allied victory leaves this Indigenous civilian story unexplained.

A geography study should therefore distinguish Dutch Harbor, Attu, Kiska, the evacuated villages, and the Southeast Alaska confinement sites. Distances across water and differences in climate mattered both to soldiers and to families. Military records establish dates of attacks and landings; National Park Service research, Unangax̂ community history, photographs, and survivor testimony reveal the experience of occupation and forced movement. When those sources are compared, the Aleutian campaign emerges as more than a remote North American battlefield. It becomes a case in which global strategy intersected with the right of Indigenous communities to remain on, return to, and rebuild their homelands.

Division 3 · Section 101.11

The Forced Evacuation and Internment of Unangax̂ Communities

The forced removal of Unangax̂ communities in 1942 illustrates one of the starkest contradictions of American wartime policy. Following the attacks on the Aleutians, American military and civilian authorities evacuated 881 people from nine villages, according to the National Park Service. Officials described the action as protection from Japanese forces, but removal was hurried and the receiving institutions were inadequately prepared. The evacuated people did not choose the sites to which they were sent, and families could take only limited possessions. Their treatment is more accurately described as forced evacuation and confinement than as ordinary voluntary migration away from military danger. The word 'evacuation' records an official rationale; it does not by itself establish that the policy was humane or adequately administered.

Transport carried families to sites in Southeast Alaska whose landscape, climate, and food resources differed markedly from the Aleutians. Former cannery buildings, a disused mine site, and other facilities were used as camps, including sites associated with Funter Bay, Killisnoo, and Burnett Inlet. These structures were not designed for long-term family housing. Inadequate sanitation, crowding, damp conditions, insufficient heating, and poor medical provision created serious health risks. Islanders accustomed to different coastal environments could not readily reproduce customary subsistence economies at the camps. Government agencies that had ordered or facilitated removal were responsible for a population whose basic needs and freedom of movement they had disrupted.

Diseases such as tuberculosis and pneumonia spread in these conditions, and deaths affected families already separated from their homelands. The Aleutian Pribilof Islands Association reports that approximately eighty-two of the 881 people removed died during confinement. The exact number in different accounts depends on the scope and period counted, so the textbook should identify the source rather than present an apparently universal statistic. Surviving photographs show families, children, camp buildings, and transport conditions, but visual evidence cannot reveal every illness, administrative decision, or personal response. Oral testimony and later investigations are essential for understanding the consequences of confinement and the meaning of lost homeland.

Historical Vocabulary

Evacuation, Internment, and Captivity

Evacuation is an official removal often justified as protection; internment or confinement involves restricting people’s freedom at designated sites; captivity concerns detention by an occupying or hostile power. Unangax̂ residents sent by U.S. authorities to camps in Southeast Alaska must be distinguished from Attu residents taken by Japanese forces to Japan. The word “evacuation” alone does not establish adequate consent, care, or the right to return.

Unangax̂ people did not cease to act for their communities in confinement. Families organized care, shared scarce resources, maintained religious and kinship relationships, sought assistance, and tried to protect children and elders. Their capacity to do these things should not be used as an excuse for neglect by the authorities. Community action arose within constraints that the government itself had created. The presence of strong kinship and Orthodox religious institutions could help sustain collective life, but neither could replace adequate shelter, medical services, and safe access to familiar food. Historians should resist both a narrative of passive victimhood and one that celebrates resilience so strongly that responsibility for harmful policy disappears.

Return posed another set of difficulties. During the years of absence, houses, boats, tools, churches, and stored property in some villages were damaged, removed, or left to deteriorate. Some communities were not reestablished in their original form, and certain former villages remained unoccupied. Families returned to changed environments and had to rebuild households while coping with bereavement and economic loss. The separate case of Attu, whose inhabitants were taken to Japan, must not be counted within the same U.S. evacuation experience. Both histories involved wartime displacement, but their perpetrators, routes, confinement conditions, and postwar consequences were distinct.

Later federal investigations and Unangax̂ rights campaigns exposed the inadequacy and injustice of wartime policy. Such later findings are legitimate evidence for assessing the period, but their chronology should not be mistaken for actions taken in 1942. The relevant wartime documentation includes transport orders, Fish and Wildlife Service correspondence, camp administration records, and photographs. Division 3 · Section 101.12

Greenland, the Canadian Arctic, and Northern Homelands

The war transformed Greenland and northern Canada because the North Atlantic and Arctic became strategically important routes for aircraft, ships, weather information, and communications. Geography that colonial administrators had once treated as remote acquired immediate military value. In Greenland, the German occupation of Denmark in April 1940 severed ordinary communication with Copenhagen and complicated the island's relationship to Danish authority. Greenlandic local councils and Danish administrators faced decisions involving supply, trade, security, and the growing American presence. A history focused exclusively on formal diplomatic acts risks overlooking the Kalaallit communities whose labor, local knowledge, and everyday relationships to land were affected by those decisions.

On April 9, 1941, Danish minister Henrik Kauffmann signed an agreement with the United States concerning the defense of Greenland. The circumstances were unusual and politically disputed. Kauffmann asserted authority to act for a Denmark whose government was under German occupation, while officials in occupied Copenhagen protested the agreement and challenged his authority. The United States rejected that position as operating under German duress and proceeded with the arrangement. The signed agreement and the contemporary diplomatic correspondence survive in government archives. Their disagreement is evidence of competing claims concerning legitimate representation during occupation, not proof that Greenlandic communities had been consulted as sovereign participants in the negotiations.

American construction expanded the network of airfields, weather stations, radio facilities, ports, and supply installations. These supported transatlantic operations and provided information needed for navigation and forecasting. Work created some employment and altered commercial opportunities, but construction also brought new demands on land and transportation. Outside military personnel and supplies changed the scale of foreign presence in particular locations. The effects varied among Greenlandic communities; it would be inaccurate to assume that every coastal settlement was directly transformed in the same way. Local records and oral histories are needed to explain where installations affected hunting, employment, or access to traditional spaces.

Northern Canada's strategic geography also changed. Airfields, staging routes, meteorological stations, and military supply systems connected northern locations to continental defense and the movement of personnel. Construction could increase wage opportunities while drawing labor away from other activities, and new transportation systems could make a place more accessible to government agencies after the war. Yet wartime construction did not produce a uniform program of permanent settlement or modern social services across all Inuit territories. Chapter 100 addresses the later Cold War acceleration of administrative and military presence, including radar networks and the DEW Line. Here the focus belongs to the facilities and priorities that emerged during the war itself.

Kalaallit and Canadian Inuit people were not simply observers of imported strategic plans. Local workers, hunters, boat operators, interpreters, and families provided labor and knowledge that outside forces needed in the Arctic. Some could gain income or access to goods; others faced disrupted travel or pressure from the decisions of authorities distant from their communities. What mattered was not only whether a military base was built but who controlled the land, who made the decision, what compensation or consultation occurred, and what followed when the construction phase ended. Because these questions are not answered by military maps alone, researchers should compare official contracts and operational histories with locally grounded sources.

The wartime Arctic demonstrates how global war could expand state power without immediately resolving colonial political relationships. Denmark's occupied government, Greenland's colonial institutions, and the United States negotiated over defense in a setting with little direct Kalaallit control of the agreement. Canada made strategic decisions in territories where Inuit authority had its own histories and where federal administration varied by region. In both places, military development opened paths for subsequent economic and administrative change, but those later developments were contingent rather than inevitable. A careful narrative closes the wartime story in 1945 and recognizes the foundation it laid for the different postwar transformations studied elsewhere in the textbook.

Division 4 · Section 101.13

Reservation and Reserve Economies During Mobilization

Wartime production reached Indigenous communities through farms, livestock operations, timber cutting, mineral extraction, transport, and the sale of goods needed by expanding military and civilian populations. The effects were not uniform. Communities with access to markets, transportation, equipment, and credit could sometimes increase production; others faced shortages of labor and materials as young adults left for the armed forces or distant industry. Federal officials frequently described such production in terms of national need, but Indigenous households also evaluated whether it supported their own food security and local economy. A wartime price increase for a commodity could provide income while also making dependence on outside buyers more dangerous when demand later declined.

On American reservations, agricultural and ranching activity depended on the quality and legal status of land, local water availability, equipment, and access to transport. The legacy of allotment meant that landholding and leasing arrangements differed widely. The Indian New Deal had not eliminated these inequalities, and some tribal governments had to negotiate with federal agencies over use of resources. Wartime mobilization could put pressure on scarce trucks, fuel, and skilled workers, limiting the ability to plant or harvest even when prices were favorable. Some families combined wage work with farming; others relied on relatives to maintain crops and animals while a service member was away. These choices reflect household strategy, not automatic movement toward full-time industrial employment.

Indigenous fishing and timber economies also experienced wartime demand. Fishing crews, canneries, sawmills, and transportation systems were tied to food supply and military construction, but control over permits, contracts, or processing facilities often rested outside Native communities. Along Pacific coasts, access to fishing grounds remained linked to treaty and customary rights, and wartime production did not suspend disputes about those rights. In Alaska, fisheries and local transport existed in a region rapidly filling with military personnel and infrastructure. A Native worker might find a paid job in a coastal industry yet remain subject to discriminatory housing or employment practices. Economic participation therefore should not be equated with control of the industry in which the work occurred.

Division 4 · Section 101.14

Indigenous Workers in American Defense Industries

American wartime industrial mobilization drew workers toward factories, shipyards, arsenals, military depots, construction sites, and transportation centers. Indigenous men and women joined this movement from reservations and rural communities as manufacturers expanded production of aircraft, vessels, vehicles, clothing, and supplies. The National Museum of the American Indian describes an immense combined contribution through military and war-related employment, while the National Library of Medicine notes tens of thousands of American Indian industrial workers. Figures vary because some count all war work, others only defense industry, and workers could change jobs over the course of the war. A reliable chapter should attribute every statistic to its source and avoid adding overlapping categories as if they were separate populations.

The Pacific Coast was especially important. Aircraft plants in the Los Angeles region and Puget Sound, shipyards around San Francisco Bay and Seattle, and other defense employers needed large labor forces. Indigenous migrants arrived in cities whose housing markets and public services were already strained by wartime growth. A factory wage could exceed rural earnings, but the cost of lodging, segregation in some workplaces or neighborhoods, and unfamiliar employment arrangements shaped its value. Some employers trained workers in welding, machine operations, and assembly tasks; others offered only unstable or low-paid positions. The war opened real opportunities without guaranteeing equal access to promotions or reliable housing.

Workers did not enter an undifferentiated urban population. Families and individuals brought tribal affiliations, languages, religious practices, and personal networks into new surroundings. Relatives helped locate jobs or rooms, and migrants sometimes formed friendships with Indigenous people from other nations whom they might not previously have met. These intertribal relationships matter because they helped create social connections later important in urban Indigenous community life. Yet it would be anachronistic to describe every wartime migrant as part of the formal federal relocation programs of the 1950s. Wartime movement resulted largely from recruitment by employers, economic pressure, personal decisions, and military-related work; later federal relocation had a different institutional design and belongs in Chapter 105.

Division 4 · Section 101.15

Indigenous Labor and Wartime Economic Change in Canada

Indigenous labor contributed to Canada's wartime economy through farming, forestry, mining, transport, manufacturing, construction, and community-level work that sustained the military effort. The nature of that contribution varied by region and legal status. Prairie First Nations farmers operated under reserve administrative arrangements, while Métis families often encountered different access to land, credit, and government support. Communities in British Columbia might be connected to fisheries, forestry, or coastal industries, while northern workers participated in transport and resource economies shaped by distance and seasonal conditions. A single national wage statistic cannot describe this range, and the documentation is much stronger for some sectors than for others.

Wartime industry expanded in cities and industrial regions, creating opportunities for Indigenous workers who could relocate or commute. Employment might supply cash, training, and contact with unionized workforces, although access to jobs and accommodation was affected by racism and by employers' assumptions about Indigenous competence. Some workers had previous experience in non-Indigenous labor markets; others entered unfamiliar occupations as employers sought to expand output. Family networks were important in deciding whether to move and how to manage household needs. The mere existence of jobs did not mean people could leave a reserve or remote community without economic and social costs.

Agricultural production provided a different set of pressures. Military enlistment reduced available hands in some communities just when national demand for food rose. Families adjusted by redistributing work or increasing the responsibilities of women and older relatives. At the same time, federal regulation of reserve lands and the authority of local Indian agents could shape agricultural decision-making. Government accounts sometimes measured the success of Native farms against an administrative ideal of productivity rather than Indigenous priorities for food security or collective management. A historical assessment must distinguish increased market sales from an increase in community control of land and resources.

Métis participation deserves specific attention because Métis people were not uniformly classified in Indian Affairs reports. Métis workers could contribute to agriculture, logging, transportation, and wartime manufacturing while being excluded from data sets labeled 'Indian.' Some Métis men and women entered military service and civilian defense work, yet their identities might be recorded only through place of residence or occupation. An absence of Indigenous classification in a military or industrial ledger cannot establish an absence of Métis participation. Family histories and Métis community collections are valuable correctives, but they must be linked to verifiable individuals and circumstances rather than treated as interchangeable with First Nations statistics.

Inuit and northern Indigenous workers faced distinctive conditions. Limited industrial infrastructure and expensive transport meant that wartime jobs were often tied to shipping, local construction, weather stations, supply operations, or resource extraction rather than a large nearby aircraft factory. Communities could gain work from military supply systems while also experiencing pressure on travel, resource access, and family economies. The broad chronology of permanent settlement and Cold War administration, discussed in Chapter 100, should not be projected onto every northern community in 1939–1945. The wartime transformation was important partly because it laid groundwork for later, more extensive changes.

Comparison with the United States shows both common processes and important differences. Increased military production demanded labor and introduced workers to new institutions in both countries. But the Canadian Indian Act, provincial legal conditions, and distinct Métis and Inuit relationships shaped the distribution of opportunities and their political consequences. Records created by employers can document wages and jobs, while veterans' and workers' accounts explain everyday experience. Neither alone proves general equality or exploitation. The essential wartime conclusion is that Indigenous contributions were woven into Canada's civilian war economy, yet the rights and resources available to workers remained unequal and dependent on the institutions governing their communities.

Division 4 · Section 101.16

Women, Families, Agricultural Labor, and the Borderlands

Wartime mobilization changed family life as well as paid employment. When a household member enlisted or moved for industrial work, those who remained had to divide responsibilities anew. Women might manage farming, fishing preparation, household budgets, child care, care of elders, and correspondence with relatives overseas. Some entered paid employment, while others sustained unpaid labor on which the household depended. Children encountered changes in work and schooling; elders retained important roles in teaching skills and maintaining community obligations. A history of wartime production that counts only factory wages misses this economy of care and continuity. The burdens could be especially heavy when transportation was unreliable or government services were inadequate.

Indigenous households also participated in public wartime activities. Communities raised money, collected materials, supported departures and memorials, and maintained ceremonial or religious practices connected to service. Such actions did not mean that every member shared the same political views. A family could support a soldier while opposing a discriminatory local official; a tribal government could contribute to national defense while protesting the use of its land. Letters and oral interviews show that military life was sustained by communication with home communities, even when distance and censorship constrained correspondence. The effects of absence were not limited to anxiety; they could include changes in who managed resources and spoke for a household during critical decisions.

The United States–Mexico borderlands illustrate the risks of assigning Indigenous identity too broadly. The bilateral Bracero Program began in 1942 as an arrangement for Mexican contract labor in the United States, principally in agriculture and later in some other work. Many Mexican workers came from regions with Indigenous languages, communities, and histories, but program records did not consistently classify participants by Indigenous national or linguistic affiliation. It would be unsound to label all braceros Indigenous or invent an Indigenous proportion. The chapter can discuss how cross-border labor demand affected the wider economic environment while remaining explicit that the ethnic and community composition of the labor force requires specific evidence.

Everyday life also changed in communities that experienced direct military presence. In Alaska, rationing, transport requirements, military installations, and the loss of family members through evacuation reshaped households in ways unlike those of a West Coast factory worker. On a Canadian reserve, the departure of adult workers could affect crop production and elder care. Comparing these situations reveals the danger of treating all wartime households as though they followed a single path from rural subsistence to urban industrial modernity.

The useful unit of analysis is the relationship among state demand, household choices, and institutions controlling mobility. Some women and families gained independence through wages or training; others experienced greater economic burden, discrimination, or forced separation. After 1945 the return of soldiers and the decline of wartime employment did not restore every prewar arrangement. The chapter should present these outcomes without making claims of inevitable liberation or inevitable disintegration. Indigenous families managed a war economy while retaining political and cultural obligations older than the war, and their responses were essential to sustaining both military participation and community life.

Division 5 · Section 101.17

Wartime Federal Indian Policy and the Struggle for Rights

Wartime federal policy toward Indigenous peoples combined calls for national unity with administrative practices that had long restricted Native political authority. In the United States, the Indian New Deal had supported some tribal reorganization but left major questions concerning land, federal oversight, and economic resources unsettled. The war shifted government attention toward manpower, defense production, and strategic land use. For Indigenous nations, this meant new opportunities to demonstrate the importance of their communities while also creating new grounds for disputing federal decisions. A nation could send many of its citizens into uniform and still maintain that federal agencies lacked the authority to decide every question of tribal government or land use.

The language of wartime democracy created a particularly sharp contrast with Indigenous political conditions. American Indian citizens continued to experience exclusions from voting in some states and discrimination in public services; First Nations people in Canada confronted restrictions related to Indian Act status and voting. These arrangements were not identical, so they should not be summarized as one national system. Nevertheless, Indigenous advocates could reasonably ask why governments defending liberty abroad refused full respect for treaty obligations at home. Such criticism came from institutions and leaders with histories predating the war. Wartime military service made the contradiction visible, but it did not originate the underlying claims.

Recruitment and economic mobilization intersected with territorial questions. Military construction, training areas, transport routes, and resource production could create incentives to use land or labor without full consultation. It is important to identify a concrete project before asserting that a particular community's property was taken. The broader institutional relationship is clear without generalization: government agencies possessed extraordinary wartime authority, and tribal leaders sometimes had to negotiate with officials whose priorities focused on immediate military needs. The terms of employment, compensation, and the protection of local interests were politically significant. Wartime administration should be analyzed as a sequence of decisions rather than as an impersonal force called modernization.

In Canada, wartime conscription sharpened debates about treaties and government obligations. The same government that sought First Nations manpower continued to regulate reserve administration and treated Indigenous legal identity as a matter defined by statute. Some First Nations leaders defended the right of volunteers to serve while questioning compulsory military obligations under treaty relationships. Others connected service to later expectations of political participation and fair access to government programs. The evidence does not support imposing a single political position on all First Nations. Geography, treaty history, community leadership, and family experience shaped responses, and Métis or Inuit populations could confront different institutional circumstances altogether.

Alaska provides a further comparison. Volunteers defended the territory through the Alaska Territorial Guard, while Unangax̂ civilians were removed from their villages by American authorities. Alaska Native leaders also pursued civil rights, culminating in the 1945 territorial anti-discrimination law discussed in Chapter 99. That measure resulted from sustained Native activism rather than automatic gratitude for war service, but wartime claims about national unity gave additional context to the campaign. Political developments within Indigenous communities should be understood as work carried out by organizers, petitioners, families, and institutions with agency; they were not simply benefits granted after the military recognized Indigenous usefulness.

The war thus intensified arguments about the terms of membership in North American states. Service could increase access to employment, education, or public visibility, but it could not substitute for governments honoring rights over land, citizenship, and self-government. Indigenous political leaders transformed wartime language into petitions and organizational demands, sometimes using the state's own promises as grounds for criticism. By 1944 one institutional response in the United States was the founding of the National Congress of American Indians. Its development becomes clearer when understood as both a wartime event and a continuation of earlier Indigenous struggles over federal policy.

Division 5 · Section 101.18

The National Congress of American Indians

The National Congress of American Indians was established in Denver in November 1944, while the war was still underway. According to NCAI's history and Smithsonian archival records, close to eighty delegates from roughly fifty tribes and associations gathered for the constitutional convention. Their meeting mattered because federal policy affected different Indigenous nations through common institutions while those nations retained distinct interests and governments. Coordinating advocacy did not require them to surrender their individual identities. Delegates sought a durable organization capable of presenting arguments concerning treaty rights, political authority, public understanding, and the welfare of communities whose concerns were often treated separately by federal officials.

The founding was not the beginning of intertribal political organization. Earlier Indian rights associations, tribal delegations, reform movements, and Native intellectuals had challenged dispossession and paternalistic administration. Chapter 92 traced some of these earlier voices, and Chapters 95 and 97 analyzed the limits of reform. The new organization drew from that history while responding to circumstances of the 1940s. Wartime mobilization heightened the visibility of Indigenous service, but uncertainty about future federal policy and persistent discrimination also encouraged collective advocacy. It would be inaccurate to claim that military service alone caused the founding or that the convention spoke for every Indigenous nation in the United States.

The NCAI founding constitution survives in archival records and contemporary organizational publication. Its preamble commits the delegates to protecting rights under treaties, promoting the common welfare, preserving cultural values, and advancing public understanding. Those commitments make clear that the organization was not intended merely as a veterans' association or ceremonial patriotic society. Its purposes concerned collective political relationships and the future powers of tribal communities. The wording also reveals the strategic position of leaders who spoke in legal and civic terms familiar to federal institutions while insisting on distinct rights that ordinary citizenship did not fully express.

Division 5 · Section 101.19

Casualties, Demobilization, and Unequal Recognition

The end of the Second World War in 1945 brought demobilization, homecomings, and bereavement to Indigenous families across North America. Some service members returned with injuries or illnesses, and others did not return at all. The consequences extended through kinship networks and communities that had helped support soldiers during their absence. Official casualty lists identify service members and dates but rarely record the full experience of the households affected. Ceremonies of remembrance, family correspondence, and later testimony are needed to understand how wartime loss was incorporated into Indigenous community life. The chapter should acknowledge sacrifice without assuming that every family experienced service as a source of honor unaccompanied by anger or grief.

Demobilization required soldiers to find work, restore household relationships, and adjust from military routines to civilian institutions. Skills acquired in transport, mechanics, administration, medicine, or other military trades could create options, but labor markets were changing as defense contracts contracted. Some veterans returned to reservations, reserves, or northern settlements; others sought employment in the towns and cities where wartime work had expanded. Decisions were shaped by family needs, available housing, health, and continuing relationships to Indigenous communities. A veteran's choice to live in a city did not necessarily mark permanent severance from tribal citizenship or political obligations.

Veterans' benefits presented a central institutional issue. The United States offered programs intended to support education, employment, housing, and reintegration, and Canada maintained systems for returning service members. Access depended on information, applications, eligibility criteria, local administration, and the capacity of institutions to assist. Veterans living far from offices or confronting discriminatory local officials could experience benefits differently from those living in cities with well-established administrative services. The existence of a statute did not by itself establish equal treatment. On the other hand, historians should avoid claiming that every Indigenous veteran was excluded; documentation supports varied outcomes requiring analysis by jurisdiction and circumstance.

Indigenous veterans and civilian workers had gained experience, mobility, skills, networks, and claims to public respect, while many institutions restricting political authority remained in place. Some improvements were immediate; others required decades of organizing and litigation. The fact that veterans served states claiming to defend freedom created a compelling basis for further demands, but no single campaign or law resolved the contradictions. Division 5 · Section 101.20

Indigenous North America Transformed: Synthesis

By 1945 Indigenous North America had participated in the Second World War through overlapping military, economic, political, and civilian experiences. American Indian soldiers served in European and Pacific operations; First Nations, Métis, and Inuit members entered Canadian forces; Indigenous women contributed in uniform and through wage work and family labor; Alaska Native volunteers helped defend distant coasts; and Unangax̂ communities endured occupation, forced removal, confinement, and the loss of homes. Greenland and northern Canada acquired new strategic importance as air routes and weather stations connected the Arctic to a global war. The chapter's principal conclusion is not that every community followed the same path, but that wartime institutions reached Indigenous homelands through multiple forms of power.

Military participation often expanded an individual's opportunities while leaving collective rights unsettled. Training, service, and public honors could produce skills or recognition; they could also expose people to stereotyping and discriminatory institutions. The difference between integration in a military unit and equality in the civilian state was especially important for veterans. American citizenship and Canadian legal status operated differently, and in neither country did uniformed service automatically resolve treaty claims or the limitations of federal Indian policy. Indigenous soldiers were members of particular nations, families, and communities as well as members of Allied forces. Those affiliations were neither uniform nor mutually exclusive.

Wartime economic change also produced mixed results. Defense plants and supply contracts offered employment and encouraged migration, especially toward industrial cities in the United States. Agricultural and resource communities adjusted production and labor when adults departed. Indigenous women performed indispensable paid and unpaid work. Some migrants formed intertribal relationships that later contributed to urban political and social life, but formal postwar relocation programs were still in the future. A sound causal explanation therefore distinguishes labor demand, household choice, administrative pressure, and explicit coercion, rather than describing all movement as voluntary modernization or as government relocation.

Alaska makes the range of outcomes particularly visible. Guard volunteers acted to defend Indigenous homelands against possible attack, while Unangax̂ people from the Aleutians were removed by American authorities claiming to protect them. Attu's inhabitants were taken to Japan by occupying forces, a different form of wartime captivity. These distinct histories show why it is insufficient to speak of 'Native participation' only through military enlistment. The actions of armies and civilian agencies affected who could remain in a village, whether homes survived, and whether families could return. Military victory did not undo those consequences, and the record of Indigenous survival does not erase governmental responsibility.

Political organization gives another measure of transformation. The NCAI founding in Denver in 1944 expressed a coordinated Indigenous demand to defend treaty rights, cultural values, and political institutions. That development drew on earlier movements and did not arise automatically from the service of soldiers. Alaska Native rights organizations and Canadian First Nations advocates continued their own work within distinct legal settings. Such movements demonstrate that Indigenous people were political actors who evaluated Allied claims about democracy against their own experiences and negotiated with states from positions shaped by older forms of sovereignty and community authority.

This chapter closes in 1945 without declaring that the war completed a transition from dispossession to equality. The following chapter isolates the specialized histories of Indigenous code talkers, whose military communications deserve their own detailed evidence-based account. Chapter 103 follows veterans, migration, institutional change, and expanding political expectations into the postwar period. Those later developments were possible in part because wartime experience reshaped networks and arguments, but they were not inevitable. Indigenous North America emerged from the war with new experience and renewed claims, alongside unresolved struggles over land, citizenship, public services, and the power to govern Indigenous lives.

Chapter Summary

Indigenous North America entered the war with varied national, legal and economic institutions; military service did not erase these differences.

Canada entered the war in 1939 and the United States in 1941; Indigenous responses reflected homeland defense, economic opportunity, family traditions and distinct political commitments.

Conscription posed especially important questions about citizenship, treaty relationships and the limits of state authority, with different rules in the United States and Canada.

In European and Pacific campaigns Indigenous personnel performed combat and support roles; official decorations recognize individual acts but cannot substitute for the history of entire units.

Indigenous women served in military branches, aviation support and civilian industries while assuming substantial family and community responsibilities.

Alaska Native Territorial Guard volunteers defended remote communities using local expertise, even as wartime institutions expanded territorial authority.

Japanese forces took Attuans to Japan, while U.S. authorities forced 881 Unangax̂ from nine other communities into camps with dangerous conditions and lasting consequences.

Greenland and northern Canada became strategic routes and sites of military construction under colonial administrations; local consequences varied by region.

Defense industries and wartime labor demand reshaped Indigenous employment and migration, but new wages and mobility did not automatically create equality or sever community membership.

In 1944 NCAI’s founding extended older Indigenous political advocacy; 1945 brought return, loss and expanded expectations while leaving central questions of sovereignty and rights unresolved.

Chapter Reflection

The Second World War connected Indigenous North American history to events across oceans while reaching directly into Indigenous homelands. Its significance lies as much in transformed relationships between communities and governments as in the number of people who wore uniforms.

Military service could represent individual choice, collective obligation, skilled work, and participation in a global struggle. Indigenous people were not made less Indigenous by their participation; neither were they placed automatically on equal terms with the governments they served.

The experiences of Unangax̂ civilians show that governments invoking protection could inflict injury through decisions about removal, confinement and the right to return. Recognition of Indigenous endurance must never obscure the administrative choices that created suffering.

By 1945 wartime labor, military participation and political coordination had widened possibilities and expectations, but the central issues of land, sovereignty, citizenship and discrimination remained contested.
