Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1953, Inuit families from the east coast of Hudson Bay boarded a Canadian government ship and were carried north to islands they had never seen. They were people with deep knowledge of their own environment, yet they were about to be placed in a landscape where that knowledge could not be assumed to apply.

At Inukjuak, then commonly called Port Harrison, in northern Quebec, a number of Inuit families boarded the government supply vessel C.D. Howe. Over the following weeks the ship carried them, together with several families taken on at Pond Inlet on Baffin Island, to the High Arctic islands of Ellesmere and Cornwallis. The destinations were Craig Harbour, later replaced by the settlement of Grise Fiord, and Resolute Bay. The distance was well over a thousand miles and the change in latitude was great: the families were moving from the subarctic edge of Hudson Bay into one of the coldest and darkest inhabited regions on Earth. Months of continuous winter darkness, different game, different ice, and an almost complete absence of familiar materials such as driftwood awaited them. Federal officials had presented the move as an opportunity. Many of the people who made the journey later remembered the promises that had been made, and the long delay before those promises were honored, very differently.

The relocation raises the central question of this chapter. How could an Indigenous people who possessed extensive environmental knowledge, working economies, and functioning social institutions become the subject of decisions made by distant governments about where they should live, how their children should be educated, what names they should carry, and even whether their dogs should be kept? The question has no simple answer, and it cannot be answered by looking at a single government or a single year. Between roughly 1900 and 1980, Inuit homelands across Alaska, northern Canada, and Greenland were transformed from regions in which Inuit communities governed their daily lives largely through local kin-based authority into regions that were increasingly administered, mapped, serviced, policed, and sometimes militarized by outside states.

The chapter therefore studies a transformation rather than an event. It follows the arrival of trading posts, missions, police detachments, and schools; the effects of commercial whaling, the Arctic fox trade, and Greenland's fisheries; the Cold War construction of radar lines and air bases; the evacuation of thousands of Inuit patients to southern hospitals during the tuberculosis crisis; the pressure and persuasion that led families into permanent settlements; and the changes in housing, labor, transportation, and family life that settlement brought. Because these processes unfolded under three different state systems, the chapter works constantly by comparison. The Canadian Arctic, Alaska, and Greenland did not experience the same policies at the same times, and Inuit responses varied accordingly.

Inuit are the central historical actors of this narrative. Hunters, trappers, midwives, catechists, interpreters, carvers and printmakers, schoolchildren, parents, elected councilors, and organizers repeatedly adapted outside institutions to their own purposes, refused them, or reshaped them. Inuit families chose wage work, schooling, and settlement for reasons of their own, even when the range of available choices had been narrowed by policy. This chapter does not treat the century as a passage from "traditional" life to "modernity." It also does not treat it only as cultural destruction. It treats it as a period in which unprecedented administrative authority was extended into Inuit homelands, and in which Inuit built new political institutions out of that experience.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied, and each kind of evidence carries its own limits. Government reports, police patrol records, trading-post journals, missionary periodicals, medical files, and legislation document the intentions and procedures of institutions. Photographs and films were frequently produced by outsiders for outside audiences. Inuit oral testimony, collected by commissions, community organizations, and scholars, records experiences that official archives often omit, but it is retrospective and must be read as such. Later public inquiries, including the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples and the Qikiqtani Truth Commission, assembled documents and testimony and reached findings; their conclusions are themselves historical evidence, and students should distinguish them from the records produced at the time of the events.

The chapter's boundaries are carefully drawn. The earlier histories of Inuit, Yupik, and Unangax̂ peoples belong to Chapter 35, and American rule in Alaska to 1945 is treated in Chapter 99. Military service and wartime participation are the subject of Chapter 101, and the Alaska land settlement is treated in Chapter 115. The modern land-claims agreements of Canadian Inuit and the creation of Nunavut belong to Chapter 116. Chapter 100 explains the historical transformations that made modern Inuit political mobilization both necessary and possible; Chapter 116 explains how that mobilization produced new agreements and institutions.

The chapter ends in the late 1970s, when Iñupiaq, Canadian Inuit, and Kalaallit leaders met at Barrow, Alaska, in 1977 to found the Inuit Circumpolar Conference, and when Greenland gained Home Rule in 1979. These were not the first acts of Inuit political life, but they marked the moment when Inuit appeared as organized political actors across the circumpolar world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the Inuit homelands of Alaska, the Canadian Arctic, and Greenland around 1900, and explain why Inuit history cannot be confined within modern national borders.
  2. Explain how Inuit hunting economies, kinship systems, and community authority worked before widespread settlement centralization, recognizing regional variation.
  3. Compare Canadian, Danish, and American systems of Arctic administration and explain how their differences shaped Inuit experience.
  4. Analyze how commercial whaling, the fur trade, credit, and Greenland's sealing and fishing economies changed Inuit household economies without simply replacing subsistence.
  5. Assess the role of missions, schools, and medical institutions, and explain how Inuit communities selectively engaged with literacy, Christianity, and formal education.
  6. Evaluate the effects of Cold War militarization on Inuit lands, mobility, and employment, using the Thule relocation of 1953 as a comparative case.
  7. Reconstruct the High Arctic relocations of 1953–1955, distinguishing official explanations, contemporary records, Inuit testimony, and later inquiry findings.
  8. Distinguish among coerced relocations, pressured movements, and voluntary but constrained moves, using the Ahiarmiut and other cases.
  9. Interpret the tuberculosis crisis, medical evacuation, residential schooling, and sled-dog policies as interlocking transformations affecting families and knowledge transmission.
  10. Examine everyday life in permanent settlements, including housing, wage labor, women's responsibilities, and the relationship between cash and subsistence.
  11. Trace the emergence of Inuit political organizations, artistic institutions, and Greenlandic Home Rule, and explain their roots in earlier experience.
  12. Evaluate the Inuit Circumpolar movement as a cross-border political development, and explain how Inuit agency operated throughout the century.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did three different colonial systems, Canadian, American, and Danish, draw Inuit societies into administered territories, and why did their timing and policies differ?
  2. In what ways did commercial economies expand Inuit opportunities, and in what ways did credit, price fluctuation, and dependence on imported goods limit Inuit autonomy?
  3. Why did governments increasingly favor permanent settlements, and how did Inuit families experience and respond to that preference?
  4. How should historians weigh official justifications for relocations against Inuit testimony and later inquiry findings?
  5. Why should the High Arctic relocations, the Ahiarmiut moves, and the Thule removal be compared rather than treated as identical events?
  6. What were the benefits and the harms of the tuberculosis campaign, and how can both be acknowledged without minimizing either?
  7. How did schooling, language policy, and the loss of dog teams affect the transmission of knowledge between generations?
  8. How did Inuit artists, organizers, and elected representatives use institutions created or encouraged by outside governments for their own purposes?
  9. What roles did evidence from oral testimony, administrative records, and later commissions play in reconstructing this history, and what are the limits of each?
  10. Why did Inuit political mobilization develop at local, regional, national, and international levels at the same time, and why does it not appear as a sudden break with the past?

Historical Context

Inuit societies of the twentieth century descended from the Thule tradition, whose migrations eastward from the Bering Strait region across the Arctic over the preceding several centuries are examined in Chapter 35. By the nineteenth century, Inuit and closely related peoples lived across a vast arc of coastline and tundra: Iñupiat in northern and northwestern Alaska, Inuvialuit in the Mackenzie Delta and western Arctic coast, Inuinnaqtut and related groups in the central Arctic, Inuit of Baffin Island, Hudson Bay, Nunavik and Labrador, and Kalaallit and Inughuit in Greenland. These peoples were linked by related languages, by common technologies suited to sea-ice and tundra environments, and by long-established networks of travel, marriage, and trade. They were not linked by a single political authority, and communities differed in economy, dialect, religion, and local custom.

Outside contact before 1900 was already substantial but extremely uneven. Danish and Norwegian colonization of western Greenland began with the mission and trading settlement established by Hans Egede in 1721, and a Danish royal trading monopoly was in place from the 1770s. Moravian missionaries had established stations on the Labrador coast beginning in 1771. In the western Arctic, American steam whalers entered the Bering Strait from 1848 and by the 1880s were operating from shore stations near Point Barrow, while commercial whalers wintered at Herschel Island in the Canadian western Arctic in the 1890s. The United States purchased Alaska from Russia in 1867, and Canada acquired the Arctic islands through a transfer from Britain in 1880 after having acquired the Hudson's Bay Company's territories in 1870. In the central and eastern Canadian Arctic, Scottish and American whalers, the Hudson's Bay Company, and a small number of missionaries and police established intermittent presence. Much of the Canadian central and high Arctic remained effectively outside sustained administration at the beginning of the twentieth century.

Epidemic disease had already devastated many communities. Introduced infections struck Iñupiat and Inuvialuit communities during the whaling era, and later epidemics, including the 1918 influenza pandemic, caused terrible losses on the Labrador coast and elsewhere. Disease, together with the depletion of whales, walrus, and other game in some regions, changed the demographic and economic landscape. These disasters did not destroy Inuit institutions, but they shaped how families approached outside traders, missionaries, and medical services.

Two interpretive cautions guide this chapter. The first is that Inuit societies were not static before 1900. They had adopted rifles, metal tools, cloth, tea, and new boats; some had converted to Christianity; many traded with outside merchants and worked for whalers. The second is that the political geography of the twentieth century, including the later territory of Nunavut and the concept of Inuit Nunangat, should not be projected backward. The chapter uses such modern names as reference points, but it narrates the early twentieth century in terms of the administrative jurisdictions, regions, and communities that actually existed.

Division I · Section 100.1

Inuit North America at the Beginning of the Twentieth Century

Around 1900 the Inuit world stretched in an arc of roughly four thousand miles from the Bering Sea coasts of Alaska, across the Arctic coast of Canada and its islands, to the western and northwestern shores of Greenland and, in smaller numbers, its east coast. Precise population figures for the period are uncertain, since few governments conducted systematic counts and Inuit mobility complicated those that did. The Inuit population of the Canadian Arctic numbered in the low thousands, the Iñupiat of Alaska numbered a comparable order of magnitude, and Greenland, whose administration kept more regular records, had a population on the order of twelve thousand people around 1901, the great majority Kalaallit living on the west coast. Epidemics, migration, and counting practices make any single figure for these regions an approximation, and this chapter uses numbers sparingly for that reason.

Inuit were linked by closely related languages and dialects that form a continuum across the Arctic. Iñupiaq was spoken in northern and northwestern Alaska. In the Canadian western Arctic the Inuvialuit spoke Inuvialuktun. Further east the dialects commonly grouped as Inuktitut were spoken across much of what is now Nunavut and Nunavik, while Inuinnaqtun was spoken in the western part of what is now Nunavut. In Labrador the dialect known as Inuttitut was spoken. In Greenland, Kalaallisut became the standard written language of the west coast, while the Inughuit of the northwest spoke Inuktun and the people of the east coast spoke Tunumiit oraasiat. These forms were not mutually intelligible in every case, but speakers in adjacent regions could often communicate. Neighboring peoples were distinct. Yupik peoples in southwestern Alaska and on St. Lawrence Island, and the Unangax̂ of the Aleutian chain, spoke languages that are related to but different from the Inuit languages, and their histories, treated in Chapters 35 and 99, should not be folded into Inuit history.

The Arctic environment was not uniform. The Alaskan North Slope and the Chukchi coast supported bowhead whaling, walrus and seal hunting, and caribou hunting inland. The Mackenzie Delta offered whales, fish, and waterfowl and was bordered by forest. The central Arctic combined sea-ice hunting with caribou and fish. Inland Inuit of the Keewatin barrenlands, sometimes called Caribou Inuit, relied principally on caribou and freshwater fish. Hudson Bay, Baffin Island, and Labrador offered seals, walrus, whales, char, and caribou in varying proportions. Greenland's west coast, warmed by currents that kept much of the sea open, supported seal hunting and, later, fishing, while the far north of Greenland was among the harshest and most ice-bound hunting environments in the world.

Inuit travel and kinship networks crossed what outsiders would later treat as boundaries. Marriage, trade, and hunting connected communities along the Alaskan and Canadian western coast. Across Smith Sound, Inuit from the Baffin region had migrated to northwestern Greenland in the nineteenth century, an episode associated with the leader Qitdlarssuaq, which renewed contact between the Inughuit and Baffin Island and Ellesmere Island communities. Nunavik and Labrador communities were connected by the coast. Such networks mean that the history of Inuit in the twentieth century cannot be understood entirely within the frameworks of Canada, the United States, or Denmark, even though those states determined the legal and administrative conditions under which Inuit lived. A principal theme of this chapter is the way those states divided and administered a world that Inuit had understood as continuous.

Historical Vocabulary
Inuit, Eskimo, Iñupiat, Kalaallit, and Inuit Nunangat

Inuit means "the people" and is the plural of Inuk. It is the self-designation used across the Canadian Arctic and is the basis of Inuit organizational names. In Alaska, Inuit of the north and northwest use Iñupiat (singular Iñupiaq), and in Greenland people of Inuit descent are commonly called Kalaallit (singular Kalaaleq), although the term has more specific meanings in different contexts.

Eskimo was applied by outsiders to Inuit, Yupik, and related peoples. Official records of the period, including Canadian government departments and the identification-disc system, used it routinely. It is now widely regarded as an exonym with contested origins and is used in this chapter only when quoting or describing the terminology of historical institutions.

Inuit Nunangat ("the place where Inuit live") is a modern term used by Inuit organizations for the Inuit homeland in Canada, comprising the Inuvialuit Settlement Region, Nunavut, Nunavik, and Nunatsiavut. It is a twentieth-century political and conceptual construct and is used here as a reference point, not as a description of administrative geography in 1900.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat the terms as interchangeable. A person in Alaska may be Iñupiaq, Yup'ik, or Unangax̂, and each is a distinct people.

Division I · Section 100.2

Hunting Economies, Seasonal Mobility, Kinship, and Community Authority

Before the widespread centralization of population into permanent settlements, most Inuit lived in small, mobile communities whose movement was organized around the seasonal availability of animals. A camp might consist of several related households, ranging in size from a few dozen people to somewhat larger gatherings at favored sites. Families moved between winter camps on the sea ice, spring and summer camps on the coast or inland, and autumn sites for caribou or fishing, following routes and schedules shaped by generations of experience. The details varied by region and by year. Where marine mammals were abundant, communities might remain near the coast through much of the year; where caribou were central, as among inland Inuit of the Keewatin barrenlands, families followed herds across the interior and relied on fishing at lakes.

The ringed seal was the most important animal for many coastal communities. Hunters waited at breathing holes in the ice (aglu), hunted at floe edges, and in open water used kayaks (qajaq) or larger skin boats (umiaq). Bearded seals, walrus, beluga, narwhal, and in some regions bowhead whales were taken. Caribou provided meat and, especially, skins for winter clothing. Fish such as Arctic char were caught at weirs and through the ice. Dogs (qimmiit) pulled sleds (qamutiik) and assisted in hunting, and in many regions their number and quality determined how far and how reliably a family could travel. Dwellings varied: snow houses were used on the sea ice in the central and eastern Canadian Arctic during winter, while in Alaska, Greenland, Labrador, and elsewhere, semi-subterranean houses of sod, stone, and whalebone or driftwood were common. These regional differences matter. The snow house is a widely recognized symbol of Inuit life but was not the universal dwelling.

Women's labor was central to the economy. Women prepared and sewed skin clothing, whose design and quality were essential for survival in extreme cold, scraped and processed hides, maintained the stone lamp (qulliq) that provided heat and light in many regions, managed the distribution of food within households, and in many communities served as midwives. Men's hunting depended on women's work, and the division of labor was a collaboration. Children learned skills by participation. Elders were custodians of knowledge about weather, ice, animals, genealogy, and the proper conduct of hunting and sharing, and their authority was based on experience rather than office.

Distribution of food was a core obligation. Hunters shared catches according to kinship, partnership, and custom, so that success was spread through the camp and the failure of any one household did not mean starvation. Kinship (ilagiit, extended family) was the primary organizing structure, and it extended through naming relationships: a child could be named for a deceased relative and, in many communities, was treated as embodying that relationship, addressed by the kin terms appropriate to the namesake. Customary adoption transferred children among households and was a common means of balancing household needs. Names were often not tied to gender, and a person might be known by several names. These practices are important for understanding why later administrative systems of registration, which required a single fixed name and a patrilineal surname, produced so many difficulties.

Leadership was situational. A skilled and respected hunter, often older, might lead a camp, and the term isumataq, usually glossed as "one who thinks" or one who provides leadership, is documented in some regions. Authority was exercised through persuasion and the weight of experience and kin relationships, and forms of authority and enforcement varied by community; they did not require the administrative institutions familiar to colonial officials. Disputes were handled by community pressure, mediation, and in some cases ostracism or, rarely, killing. Spiritual specialists (angakkuit, singular angakkuq) mediated relationships with animals and the unseen world, and many communities observed taboos and ritual obligations governing hunting. Many of these practices changed as Christianity spread, a process examined in section 100.8.

Historians know about early twentieth-century Inuit society from several kinds of sources, and each has limits. Ethnographic work by outsiders, including Knud Rasmussen's reports from the Fifth Thule Expedition of 1921–1924, the work of Diamond Jenness, and later anthropological studies, recorded much valuable information, but was produced by observers with their own questions and frameworks. Inuit oral histories, gathered by community projects, schools, and commissions, record knowledge in Inuit terms and from Inuit perspectives, including the concept now known as Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, a modern term for Inuit knowledge and ways of knowing. Students should treat that term as a late-twentieth-century articulation of older knowledge systems, not as a name used in 1900. Combined with archaeological evidence and the records of traders and police, such sources allow a reasonably full reconstruction of the economy while leaving many regional details uncertain.

Finally, students should not think of this economy as simply "traditional" and therefore opposed to the commercial world. Inuit had by 1900 incorporated rifles, steel traps, cloth, tea, flour, and manufactured boats and tools into hunting life, and families routinely combined subsistence activities with trade. The question for the twentieth century was not whether Inuit would engage with outside economies but on what terms, with what degree of control, and at what cost to mobility and autonomy.

Division I · Section 100.3

Canadian Administration, Trading Posts, Missions, and Northern Authority

In the early twentieth century, the Canadian state's presence in the Arctic was thin. Canada claimed the Arctic islands and mainland coast, but those claims were exercised through a small number of institutions rather than through settled government. The principal outside institutions were trading posts, missions, and the police. Each was a point of contact, and each carried particular and sometimes conflicting interests.

Trading posts were the most important point of daily contact. The Hudson's Bay Company, which dominated the trade across most of the Canadian Arctic, expanded its network of posts in the early twentieth century, and Revillon Frères, a French firm, competed with it in parts of the eastern Arctic until the mid-1930s. Independent traders and, in Labrador, Moravian trading operations also existed, with the Hudson's Bay Company purchasing the Moravian trade in the 1920s. The posts supplied Inuit with rifles, ammunition, traps, flour, tea, tobacco, cloth, and other goods in exchange for furs and other products. Their location influenced where Inuit families chose to spend part of the year, a development examined in section 100.6.

Missions were the second institution. Anglican missionaries, among them Edmund Peck in the Baffin region, translated scripture into Inuktitut and promoted the syllabic writing system adapted from systems used for Cree and other languages. Roman Catholic Oblate missionaries established stations at places such as Chesterfield Inlet in the early twentieth century. In Labrador the Moravian Church, present since 1771, ran stations with schools and trading operations. Missions are examined in section 100.8, but at this point it is important to recognize them as institutions that were also instruments of Canadian presence, since missionaries often acted as interpreters, medical providers, and in practice administrators.

The third institution was the police. The Royal North-West Mounted Police, renamed the Royal Canadian Mounted Police in 1920, established posts at places such as Herschel Island in the western Arctic in the early 1900s and, in the 1920s, at Pond Inlet, Craig Harbour, and Dundas Harbour in the eastern Arctic. These posts served several purposes at once: law enforcement, the collection of information, the administration of relief, the enforcement of game regulations, and the assertion of Canadian sovereignty against rival claims. The government's annual Eastern Arctic Patrol, which began in 1922, carried officials, medical staff, and supplies. Legal encounters illustrated the confrontation between Canadian law and Inuit custom. In 1923 the trial of Nuqallaq at Pond Inlet, who had killed the trader Robert Janes, was held in the Arctic itself, partly to demonstrate Canadian jurisdiction. Cases of this kind show the police acting as both investigators and representatives of an expanding legal order.

A question of jurisdiction long left Inuit in an ambiguous legal position. Federal authorities and the province of Quebec disputed responsibility for Inuit living in northern Quebec, and in 1939 the Supreme Court of Canada decided, in a reference commonly known as Re Eskimos, that Inuit were "Indians" within the meaning of the federal government's constitutional power over "Indians and lands reserved for the Indians." The decision established federal jurisdiction. Yet Inuit were not placed under the Indian Act, from which they were excluded, a distinction explained further in Chapter 85. The result was that Inuit were under federal responsibility but without the legal framework governing First Nations, and policy toward them evolved through administrative practice and departmental programs rather than a single statute.

The experience varied by region. In Labrador, which was part of Newfoundland, a separate Dominion until it joined Canada in 1949, Inuit communities had a long relationship with the Moravian Church, and the 1918 influenza epidemic was catastrophic at stations such as Okak and Hebron. In the western Arctic, Inuvialuit had lived through the whaling era, severe epidemics, and a transformation toward trapping, with the Mackenzie Delta region organized around posts and, later, the town of Aklavik. In the central Arctic, contact came later and often through trading posts and police patrols, and in the High Arctic and Keewatin interior some families encountered outsiders only occasionally until mid-century. Administrative jurisdictions also differed: the Northwest Territories administration, the Quebec government's claims over northern Quebec, and Newfoundland's Labrador each treated Inuit differently. Understanding the century requires holding these distinctions in view and recognizing that "Canadian administration" was not a single policy.

For Inuit, the new institutions were not simply impositions. Families used trading posts, took advantage of medical help when it was available, learned syllabics, sometimes worked as guides and interpreters for police and missionaries, and made use of the new authority of constables and missionaries in disputes. At the same time the presence of outside authorities began to limit Inuit options, for example when game laws restricted hunting of certain species or when relief and police supervision were tied to particular places. The interplay of selective use and growing constraint is characteristic of the period.

Division I · Section 100.4

Danish Greenland, American Alaska, and Contrasting Colonial Systems

Greenland and Alaska offer two contrasting models of colonial administration. Both can be compared with the Canadian Arctic, and the comparison helps explain why Inuit political responses took different forms in each place.

Greenland was a Danish colony with a distinctive structure. Danish rule in west Greenland dated from Hans Egede's mission of 1721, and from the 1770s the Royal Greenland Trading Department held a monopoly over trade. The Danish government pursued a policy of protecting Greenlandic society from outside influence and kept the colony closed to most outsiders, justifying the arrangement as a protection of the Greenlandic hunting economy. The monopoly controlled prices and the supply of goods and regulated the market in seal products. For Greenlanders, the system provided a measure of stability but also limited economic choice and restricted contact with the wider world. The colony's population was overwhelmingly Greenlandic and Christian, as the Lutheran Church of Denmark and, earlier, Moravian missionaries had established churches, schools, and a seminary at Nuuk (then Godthåb) that trained Greenlandic teacher-catechists beginning in the nineteenth century. Greenlandic literacy was extensive, and a Greenlandic-language periodical, Atuagagdliutit, began publication in the 1860s. These institutions meant that Greenlanders had, by 1900, a significant educated class and a tradition of publishing in their own language.

Danish administration also included local participation. In the late nineteenth century, local boards of elected Greenlanders, known as councils of guardians (forstanderskaber), were created to manage certain local matters, particularly the distribution of funds associated with the seal catch. In 1911 two provincial councils (landsråd) were established, for the north and south, with advisory functions. Later legislation in 1925 reorganized local government with municipal councils. The councils were consultative bodies within a Danish administration, and their powers were limited. They nevertheless gave Greenlanders experience in public deliberation and a basis for later demands for greater authority. Their significance in the twentieth century is that they existed at all: no comparable institutions existed for most Canadian Inuit at the time.

International status mattered. Greenland's sovereignty was disputed by Norway in the 1930s, and in 1933 the Permanent Court of International Justice ruled in the Eastern Greenland case in favor of Denmark, confirming Danish sovereignty over all of Greenland. The ruling is an example of how decisions about Inuit homelands were made among states, with Inuit as the unrepresented subjects. In this respect Greenland resembled the Canadian Arctic, where sovereignty disputes with Norway over the Sverdrup Islands, resolved in Canada's favor when Norway recognized Canadian sovereignty in 1930, and later disputes with the United States, helped to motivate the establishment of police posts.

Alaska represented a different system. After the 1867 purchase, American authority in the territory expanded slowly. Compared with Greenland, Alaska was open: whalers, traders, miners, missionaries, and canneries entered with few barriers, and Alaska Natives, including the Iñupiat, were not governed through a trading monopoly. Schooling was provided initially by the federal Bureau of Education, with mission schools of several denominations, including Presbyterian, Episcopal, Quaker, and Catholic, operating alongside federal schools in Iñupiat communities. Reindeer herding, introduced from Siberia in the 1890s with the aim of providing a livelihood and food source, became an economic experiment in northwestern Alaska, though its results were mixed. Alaska Natives' legal status in this period was complicated, involving questions of citizenship and land that are treated in Chapter 99. For present purposes the essential contrast is that Alaska's Iñupiat dealt with a more commercially open, more weakly regulated, and more American-settler-influenced environment, while Greenland's Kalaallit lived within a more closed and state-managed colonial system.

These differences shaped later developments. Greenlanders' relatively strong institutional base, literacy, and councils meant that when Danish policy changed after the Second World War, Greenlandic political leadership could emerge from existing institutions. The Alaskan Iñupiat, whose communities were more exposed to commercial and military development, organized on the basis of regional corporations and local governments. Canadian Inuit, whose relationship with the state developed later and through administrative rather than representative channels, built their political organizations in the 1960s and 1970s. The result was three different political paths, which in the 1970s began to converge in the Inuit Circumpolar movement.

Division II · Section 100.5

Commercial Whaling, Coastal Economies, and the Alaskan Arctic

The Iñupiat of Alaska's northern coast lived through the rise and collapse of industrial whaling within a few decades. American steam whalers entered the Bering Strait in 1848 and, after the 1870s, moved increasingly into the Arctic Ocean to hunt bowhead whales, prized for oil and baleen. By the 1880s shore-based stations were operating near Point Barrow and elsewhere, taking advantage of the spring whale migration along the lead of open water, and ships wintered at Herschel Island in the Canadian western Arctic in the 1890s. The commercial whaling industry reached communities such as Barrow (now Utqiaġvik), Point Hope, and Wainwright and brought outside wages, goods, and diseases at the same time.

Iñupiat engaged with the whaling economy in several ways. They worked for stations and ships, supplied meat, skins, and provisions to crews, and traded for rifles, ammunition, flour, tea, tobacco, cloth, and manufactured goods. Some men served as crew, guides, or hunters for the whalers. Iñupiat adopted selected whaling technologies, including shoulder guns and explosive bomb lances, and used them in their own hunts. Yet commercial whaling also had serious costs. Epidemics struck communities exposed to crews from many places. Commercial whalers and the intensive hunting of bowheads, walrus, and other game reduced the animals on which Iñupiat depended, and in some areas the 1880s and 1890s saw famine. Alcohol and violence accompanied some whaling stations. The effects on communities were uneven, with some places benefiting from trade and others suffering depopulation.

The industry's decline was swift. In the early twentieth century, falling demand and prices for whalebone, driven by the introduction of substitute materials, and the substitution of petroleum for whale oil, ended the profitability of the Arctic whaling fleet. By about 1910 most commercial whaling had ended. Shore stations closed, but the commercial infrastructure and the habit of buying imported goods remained. Many Iñupiat had come to rely on rifles, ammunition, flour, and other store goods, and the end of the whaling economy left them seeking new sources of cash, principally through fur trapping, reindeer herding, and later through seasonal and wage work. This is a first example of a pattern central to the century: external commercial booms created new needs and dependencies, and their collapse left communities adapting without recovering their earlier conditions.

Through all of this, Iñupiat whaling itself continued. Whale hunting remained a central economic, social, and ritual institution in coastal communities, and its organization is a clear example of Indigenous institutions persisting through commercial change. Whaling was organized around the umialik, the captain who owned or managed an umiaq, organized a crew, and was responsible for the hunt. A successful captain acquired prestige and obligations, and the whale was distributed according to well-established rules, with shares for crew members and for community members. In many Iñupiat communities, the spring whaling season ended with a festival, now widely known as Nalukataq, in which the community shared food and celebrated the successful hunts. Captains' wives played important roles in preparing for and hosting the hunt. Whaling thus linked economy, kinship, ceremony, and leadership.

Iñupiat whaling also had a political future. In 1977, the International Whaling Commission imposed a ban on the bowhead hunt based on scientific concerns about the population's size, which would have halted subsistence whaling in Alaska. Iñupiat whaling captains and communities protested and, with the support of the North Slope government, founded the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission in 1977 to represent whaling communities, and the dispute helped to catalyze the Inuit Circumpolar Conference examined in section 100.20. Thus a commercial era that had ended decades earlier left a legacy: whaling had become a symbol of Iñupiat cultural identity, and its defense an occasion for modern political organization.

The sources for this period include the logbooks and correspondence of whalers, the memoirs of traders such as Charles Brower, who established a whaling and trading operation at Barrow, and the recorded memories of Iñupiat elders. Whaler and trader sources describe Iñupiat as suppliers and employees and are often silent on Iñupiat motives and institutions. Oral testimony, by contrast, describes the same period from within the community, emphasizing the persistence of captains' leadership and sharing. Reading these sources together is necessary to avoid two errors: the notion that Iñupiat were swept passively into a commercial economy, and the notion that they stood apart from it.

Division II · Section 100.6

The Fur Trade, Trading Posts, Credit, and Market Dependence

In the Canadian Arctic and Labrador, the principal commercial development of the early twentieth century was the expansion of the trade in white (Arctic) fox fur. Fox pelts had been valuable for a long time, but in the 1920s high fur prices, the extension of trading posts, and the availability of steel traps, rifles, and ammunition drew many Inuit households into regular trapping. The Hudson's Bay Company's network of posts, supplied by the company's annual ships, among them the Nascopie, became the key institution, supplemented by competing traders and, in the western Arctic, a trapping economy that also included muskrat. Posts were located along the Hudson Bay coast, in Baffin Island, in Labrador, and in the Mackenzie Delta, and, as the network grew, many Inuit households arranged their seasonal movements to visit a post for trade.

The trade system operated through credit. A trapper could obtain supplies, such as ammunition, traps, tea, flour, tobacco, cloth, and equipment, in advance, and repay them with furs at the end of the season. Furs were valued in the company's accounting units, and goods were priced in the same units, so that exchange involved a negotiation among prices set at the post, in company headquarters, and in distant fur markets. When fox prices fell, as they did sharply at the end of the 1920s and during the Depression, trappers' purchasing power declined, debt accumulated, and the supplies on which hunters had come to depend became harder to obtain. A bad season could mean hunger. The effects fell hardest where families had reduced their seal and caribou hunting in order to trap.

Historians have debated the extent of dependency in these relationships. In one view, the credit system created a lasting subordination to the company, binding families to posts and to fluctuations in distant markets. In another, Inuit used the trade selectively, adjusted their trapping to their needs, took advantage of competition among traders, and retained control over their hunting and their movement. The evidence supports elements of both. Competition between the Hudson's Bay Company and rivals gave Inuit some bargaining power where it existed, and where the company had no competitor, its power was greater. Company managers varied in their practices, some of them acting as informal relief officials and others pressing trappers to maximize fur production. And the commercial economy did not replace the subsistence one in a simple sequence. Households hunted seals and caribou for food and skins and trapped foxes for trade in the same year, and the surplus from commercial work often supported the equipment, such as rifles, boats, and ammunition, that made subsistence hunting more effective.

The geography of posts mattered. Trading posts attracted camps, and over time some posts became the nucleus of small communities, with families spending more of the year near them, particularly in winter when supplies and, increasingly, relief and police services were available. This was an early form of the centralization that later governments would accelerate. Yet the shift was gradual and varied. Many families continued to live mostly on the land through the 1940s, visiting posts seasonally, and settlement did not become typical until the 1950s and 1960s. Where posts did draw people in, they also altered gender and age relationships, for example by increasing demand for women's labor in preparing skins, and by creating new roles, such as interpreter, post servant, and trader's assistant, that gave some Inuit new forms of status.

The Thule trading station in northwestern Greenland, founded in 1910 by Knud Rasmussen and Peter Freuchen and supplying the Inughuit, represents a variation: a private trading institution that operated alongside, and later under, Danish authority, and that gave the Inughuit access to goods while also tying them to the station's trading terms. In Labrador, the trade combined with Moravian stations. In Alaska, the post-whaling economy turned to trapping, reindeer herding, and wage work, as noted above. Across these regions the common feature was incorporation into a trade system whose terms were set by markets outside the Arctic.

Division II · Section 100.7

Greenland's Commercial Transformation: Sealing, Fishing, and State Development

In Greenland the economic changes of the twentieth century took place within the framework of a state monopoly. For most of the twentieth century's first half, the Royal Greenland Trading Department purchased seal products, whale products, and other goods from Greenlandic hunters and supplied imported goods, with prices set by Danish authorities. Greenland's economy depended principally on seal hunting, supplemented by fishing, and the colonial administration was funded in part by the trade's proceeds. A significant outside industry was the cryolite mine at Ivittuut (Ivigtut), in southwestern Greenland, which supplied a mineral used in aluminum production and was operated by a Danish company; it provided employment and revenue, though it involved relatively few Greenlanders as a proportion of the population.

From the 1920s a major change began in the coastal waters of southwestern Greenland. Warming conditions in the North Atlantic, whose causes and exact timing are matters of scientific study, brought cod northward along the west coast, and fisheries for cod expanded greatly in the following decades. The Danish administration encouraged this development, building fish-processing facilities and promoting fishing, and many Greenlanders moved from seal hunting to fishing or combined the two. The change affected settlement patterns. Communities in the southwest grew, while some small hunting settlements in the north and elsewhere declined, and the population became more concentrated near fishing grounds and processing stations. At the same time seal hunting remained fundamental to food supply and to cultural identity, and for many families, hunting and fishing coexisted as parts of a mixed economy.

The Second World War disrupted Greenland's colonial arrangements. After Germany occupied Denmark in April 1940, Greenland was cut off from the Danish government. The two provincial councils and the Danish colonial governors assumed responsibility for administration, and in 1941 the Danish minister in Washington, acting on his own authority, signed an agreement allowing the United States to establish military bases and a defensive presence in Greenland. American and Canadian supplies replaced Danish ones, and the cryolite mine, important to Allied aluminum production, was protected. For Greenlanders, the war years meant a period of relative self-management, greater contact with the outside world, and the realization that Greenland could govern itself in practice. These experiences contributed to postwar demands that Denmark change the colonial system, a process examined in section 100.19.

After 1945, Danish policy turned to modernization. Danish commissions in the late 1940s examined Greenlandic conditions, and the result was a plan, often referred to as G-50, that proposed investment in health, education, housing, fishing, and infrastructure. In 1950 the trade monopoly was formally ended and other commercial activity was gradually permitted, though state trading institutions continued to play a major role. In 1960 a further commission produced what is often called the G-60 plan, which concentrated investment in a smaller number of larger towns, with the aim of making services cheaper and the fishing industry more productive. The effects included the closure or decline of small settlements and the relocation of residents to larger towns, together with large public-works projects, such as the apartment block known as Blok P in Nuuk, completed in the mid-1960s. A particularly controversial case was the closure of the Qullissat coal mining town on Disko Island in 1972, which compelled the town's residents to resettle elsewhere.

Historians should be careful not to present postwar modernization in Greenland as either an unqualified improvement or an unqualified decline. It brought improvements in health, housing, schooling, and employment, and it responded to demands for better living standards. It also involved a heavy reliance on Danish planners and Danish-language training, rapid change in settlement and employment, and, in the view of many Greenlanders, insufficient consultation. Danish and Greenlandic officials, and Greenlandic political leaders, disagreed over the pace and aims of modernization, and the debates helped to form the Greenlandic political movements that emerged in the 1970s. Greenlandic experience therefore illustrates a general feature of the century: that state-led development created opportunities and pressures simultaneously, and that Indigenous communities responded as political actors, not simply as beneficiaries or victims.

Evidence for this period includes Danish government reports and commission documents, Greenlandic newspapers and periodicals, the records of local councils, and the oral and written testimony of Greenlanders who lived through the changes. Danish sources emphasize policy intentions, and Greenlandic sources emphasize local effects. Comparing them is essential.

Division II · Section 100.8

Christian Missions, Early Schools, Medicine, and Cultural Negotiation

Missionary activity was among the most pervasive of the outside institutions affecting Inuit communities, and its effects were more complex than the simple replacement of one religion by another. Different churches worked in different places, and each had its own approach to language, education, and Inuit participation.

In Labrador, the Moravian Church had established mission stations from 1771, including Nain, Hopedale, and Hebron, and its work was characterized by the use of Inuttitut, the creation of schools, and a distinctive church life that included music and literacy. The Moravians' effect was profound: by the early twentieth century, most Labrador Inuit were Moravian Christians and literate in their own language. In the eastern and central Canadian Arctic, Anglican missionaries, including Edmund Peck, who worked in the Baffin region from the 1890s, translated scripture and hymns and taught syllabic writing, which spread widely. Roman Catholic Oblate missionaries established stations in the Keewatin and Hudson Bay regions and in the western Arctic, so that in some communities Anglican and Catholic missions competed. The competition affected communities in practical ways, because adherence to one church or another could shape alliances, access to schooling, and relations with traders. In Greenland, the Lutheran church of the Danish state, along with earlier Moravian work, established a church system staffed largely by Greenlandic catechists, and in Alaska, Presbyterian, Episcopal, Quaker, Catholic, and other missions operated among the Iñupiat.

Syllabics deserve particular attention. The writing system, adapted for Inuktitut by missionaries in the late nineteenth century, was learned by many Inuit, often from other Inuit rather than from missionaries directly, and it spread through the eastern Arctic even in places where missionaries were rare. Literacy enabled Inuit to read scripture, correspond, and record information in their own language, and some Inuit became catechists and lay leaders. It would be a mistake, however, to regard literacy only as a mission product. Inuit adopted it for their own ends, and its spread shows Inuit initiative in making use of an outside technology.

Inuit engagement with Christianity was selective and varied. Many Inuit embraced it, sometimes after a period of resistance or ambivalence, and the church provided new forms of community life and, for some, new forms of leadership. Practices associated with the angakkuit and with older ritual obligations declined in many places, although some elements of earlier belief and practice persisted, adapted, or were reinterpreted within Christian frameworks. In certain communities conversion occurred within a few decades, typically in the 1920s and 1930s in parts of the central and eastern Arctic, and in others it was earlier. Missionaries also criticized and sought to change aspects of Inuit practice, including naming customs, marriage, adoption, and shamanism, and Inuit responded with accommodation, selective adoption, or quiet persistence. It is important not to assume that conversion meant complete assimilation of missionary values. How Christianity was understood and practiced depended on the community.

Missions also provided early schools and medical services. Mission schools taught literacy, scripture, and some arithmetic; in Greenland, schools were more developed and Greenlandic-language instruction was standard, and Greenlandic teachers were trained at the Nuuk seminary. In the Canadian Arctic, mission schools were small and often seasonal, and the government began to take over a larger role in education only after the Second World War. Medical services were limited. The Anglican mission established a hospital at Pangnirtung in 1930, and Catholic missions operated small medical facilities, but many communities had no resident medical staff, and the Eastern Arctic Patrol's physicians visited only once a year. Missions' medical work was welcomed by many Inuit, even as epidemics, including influenza and later tuberculosis, continued to cause great harm. This limited medical capacity set the stage for the postwar tuberculosis crisis examined in section 100.13.

The evidence for missionary history includes mission periodicals and reports, which tend to emphasize conversion and progress, church registers, translations, and Inuit oral accounts and memoirs, which often describe the same events differently. The institutions' own records, for example, may describe the abandonment of shamanic practice as the result of conversion, while Inuit accounts may describe a longer process of negotiation. As elsewhere in this chapter, historians must read missionary sources critically.

By the 1930s, therefore, the Arctic was not an untouched region awaiting state intervention. It was a region in which trade, missions, the police, and limited medical and educational services had already become part of Inuit lives, but in which Inuit retained control over much of their movement, subsistence, and community organization. The postwar expansion of state authority, examined in the next division, differed from what came before in scale, directness, and ambition.

Division III · Section 100.9

The Militarization of the Arctic and the Expansion of State Authority

The Second World War and the Cold War transformed the Arctic from a remote periphery into a strategic frontier. Military planners in the United States, Canada, and Denmark came to regard the Arctic as the shortest route between North America and the Soviet Union and as a place for airfields, weather stations, and radar. The resulting construction projects brought roads, runways, buildings, and thousands of outside workers into regions where permanent infrastructure had been minimal, and they led governments to take a new interest in the people living there.

During the Second World War the United States and Canada built airfields and weather stations along northern air routes. In Greenland, American forces established bases under the 1941 agreement. In the eastern Canadian Arctic, the United States constructed air bases at places such as Fort Chimo (now Kuujjuaq) and Frobisher Bay (now Iqaluit), which, with Goose Bay in Labrador, formed part of the air ferry route to Europe. After the war, Canada and the United States established joint Arctic weather stations beginning in 1947, including at Resolute on Cornwallis Island, and the Resolute site became an important airfield. In Alaska, wartime and postwar installations included the Naval Arctic Research Laboratory at Barrow, built on a site close to the Naval Petroleum Reserve designated in the 1920s, and the Alaskan Air Command's network of stations; the experience of Alaska Natives in the war, including service in the Alaska Territorial Guard, is treated in Chapter 101.

The largest postwar construction was the Distant Early Warning (DEW) Line, a chain of radar stations across the northern coast of Alaska and Canada to Greenland and Iceland, built in the mid-1950s as part of continental air defense, together with the Mid-Canada Line and other installations. The DEW Line employed many construction and maintenance workers, some of them Inuit, and it created new airstrips, roads, and supply routes. For some Inuit, DEW Line work provided cash income and new skills, and it brought communities into closer contact with southern Canada and the United States. For others it meant disruption of hunting areas, introduction of alcohol and imported goods, and long-term environmental consequences from abandoned and contaminated sites, many of which required later cleanup. The effects varied by location, depending on the size of the installation and the nearness of communities.

Military development also had direct consequences for Indigenous land. The best-documented case is Greenland's Thule region. In 1951, under a defense agreement between the United States and Denmark made within the framework of NATO, the United States was allowed to establish defense areas in Greenland, and construction of a large air base at Thule, in the northwest, began soon afterward. The base lay near the settlement of Uummannaq (Dundas), whose residents, the Inughuit, had long hunted in the area and had been connected to the Thule trading station. In 1953, as the base expanded, the Danish authorities ordered the community to relocate, and roughly twenty-seven families, numbering over a hundred people, were moved some 100 kilometers north to the area of Qaanaaq. The residents were given little time and had limited choice, and they were moved to a region where hunting conditions differed. Danish authorities built houses and a new settlement at Qaanaaq, and the move was presented as necessary and, in Danish accounts, accompanied by assistance, but the Inughuit lost access to hunting grounds and to a place of long habitation.

The Thule removal continued to be disputed for decades. Inughuit groups pressed claims for return and compensation. In the 1990s Danish authorities investigated the history of the base, including the circumstances of the relocation and the 1968 crash of a U.S. B-52 aircraft carrying nuclear weapons near the base, an accident whose cleanup involved many workers and became the subject of later health concerns. A Danish court ruled in 1999 that the state owed compensation to the Inughuit, and in 2003 the Danish Supreme Court decided that the Inughuit had no right to return to their former lands. The case is important for comparison with Canadian relocations because it shows the effect of great-power military planning on Indigenous communities, because a national government (Denmark) acted under a treaty with another state (the United States), and because Inughuit later became litigants and political actors. Students should note differences from the High Arctic relocations: the Thule removal was caused by the construction of a foreign military installation, and the High Arctic relocations by Canadian government policy, though sovereignty and strategic concerns were present in both.

In Alaska, Cold War development also affected the Iñupiat. A notable example was Project Chariot, a proposal in the late 1950s by the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission to use nuclear explosions to excavate a harbor at Cape Thompson, near Point Hope, as part of an experimental program for peaceful uses of nuclear explosives. The people of Point Hope, joined by scientists and others, opposed the plan, citing risks to the animals and people of the area, and the project was abandoned in the early 1960s. The episode is often seen as an early example of Alaska Native political resistance to outside development, and it influenced later organizing around land, a subject that belongs to Chapter 115.

Taken together, these developments expanded state authority in several ways. They created transport and communication links that made administration easier. They increased governments' interest in knowing who lived in the Arctic and in establishing services there. And they created a context in which the presence of Indigenous communities near strategic installations appeared to officials as a matter of policy. They did not, by themselves, cause the centralization of Inuit population into permanent settlements, but they helped create the conditions for it, as the next section shows.

Division III · Section 100.10

Canadian Federal Administration and the Movement Toward Permanent Settlements

In the 1940s and 1950s the Canadian government greatly expanded its presence in the Arctic. Several developments converged. The war had shown Canadian officials the strategic importance of the north and the weakness of the existing administration. The postwar welfare state introduced new programs, including family allowances, old-age pensions, and health insurance measures, which were extended to Inuit. Public criticism, including reports of hunger and illness in Inuit communities, pressed the government to act. And a growing belief among officials that Inuit would have to adapt to a changing economy shaped plans for education, housing, and employment. In 1953 the government created the Department of Northern Affairs and National Resources, which gave northern administration a central institutional home, and northern service officers were posted to the Arctic to coordinate services.

Family allowances, introduced in Canada in 1944 with payments beginning in 1945, were an important mechanism. In the Arctic, payments were often made in kind, through credits at trading posts for specified foods and goods, rather than in cash, and officials sometimes tied payments to children's school attendance and health. Inuit families welcomed the added resources, which were particularly valuable in years when hunting was poor, but the program also increased the incentives to remain near posts, where credits could be used and where registration and supervision were easier. Officials viewed family allowances as a social measure and also as a way to influence behavior. Old-age pensions and other benefits had similar effects.

Administration required identification. From the early 1940s, the federal government assigned Inuit numbers, and the individual received a disc, made of fiber or metal, stamped with an "E" number for the eastern Arctic or a "W" number for the western, and later registered on a list. The numbers were used for family allowance payments, medical records, and other administrative purposes. The system treated each person as having a single fixed identity, recorded by a name written by an official, often a missionary, trader, or police officer. This did not fit Inuit naming practices, in which a person might have several names, names might be shared among relatives or carry kinship relationships, and names did not necessarily indicate gender or lineage. Officials struggled to record names consistently, and the resulting records contained inconsistencies and errors, and in some cases separated members of the same family under different surnames. The discs also treated people in ways that many Inuit later found demeaning, as the numbers took the place of names in some official processes. The system continued in various forms into the early 1970s.

Government also began to build infrastructure in the form of schools, nursing stations, housing, and administrative offices. These were increasingly located in a limited number of settlements, which officials considered more efficient than providing services to dispersed camps. Some policymakers also believed that the Arctic's game resources could not support the growing Inuit population, that the fur trade's decline had left many Inuit in poverty, and that wage employment and settlement offered a better future. Others, particularly in the police and among missionaries, warned of dependency. The balance among these views changed through the 1950s and 1960s, but the direction of policy was toward permanent communities, a shift that is the subject of an important debate examined in section 100.16.

The voting franchise illustrates the uneven character of expanding citizenship. The federal vote was extended to Inuit in 1950, although practical opportunities to vote came later and unevenly because polling places were few and information limited. The Northwest Territories Council, based first in Ottawa and later in Yellowknife, gained elected members from the Arctic in the 1950s and 1960s, and in 1965 Abraham Okpik was appointed as one of the first Inuit members. In Labrador, which joined Canada only in 1949, and in northern Quebec, provincial authorities played larger roles. The provincial government of Quebec expanded its presence in Nunavik in the late 1950s and 1960s, which created a distinct pattern of administration, and led eventually to the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975, a subject treated in the chapters on modern land claims.

Division III · Section 100.11

The High Arctic Relocations of 1953–1955

The relocation of Inuit families to the High Arctic in 1953 and 1955 is among the most thoroughly documented episodes in twentieth-century Inuit history, examined through contemporary administrative documents, a federal inquiry, and relocated families' testimony. In summer 1953, ten families comprising 54 people—seven families from Inukjuak (Port Harrison), northern Quebec, and three from Pond Inlet on Baffin Island—were transported by the C.D. Howe toward Craig Harbour on Ellesmere Island and Resolute Bay on Cornwallis Island. Three Inukjuak families and one Pond Inlet family, 22 people, went to Resolute; four Inukjuak families and two Pond Inlet families, 32 people, went to Craig Harbour. Six additional families, four from Inukjuak and two from Pond Inlet, joined in 1955, making 16 families and approximately 92 people in the two settlements by then. These are the figures reconstructed by the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples in its 1994 report (Introduction, p. 7), with a stated uncertainty of one or two people; they exclude Inuit special constables and their families already at Craig Harbour. The sequence was therefore two movements in different years, not a single 1953 transfer. Craig Harbour was subsequently succeeded by Grise Fiord as a residential settlement.

Government officials gave several explanations at the time and afterward. They said that Inukjuak and the surrounding region were overpopulated in relation to game, that families were dependent on relief and family allowances, and that the High Arctic offered better hunting, including more game, so that the families could return to a self-sufficient hunting life. Officials also expected that the High Arctic's population of game, including muskoxen and Peary caribou, walrus, and seals, would support the families. The families were told, according to later testimony and the commission's findings, that they would be able to return to Inukjuak after a period, often described as one or two years, if they wished. Some families agreed to go, and the question of how far their agreement was informed and voluntary is central to later debate.

Later commissions and Inuit testimony stress an additional purpose. The Canadian government in the early 1950s was concerned about its sovereignty in the High Arctic, and the presence of Canadian citizens in the region, it was believed, would strengthen Canada's claim. The Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples concluded that the establishment of Inuit settlements was in part motivated by sovereignty concerns, a conclusion that rested on documentary evidence from the period. Inuit leaders and others have used the phrase "human flagpoles" to describe the effect of this motive. The phrase is a later characterization, not an official description, and students should treat it as an interpretation of the policy's effect and a statement of how the relocation has been remembered.

The conditions the families found were severe. The High Arctic lies far north of Inukjuak, and winter darkness lasts for months. The sea ice, the animals, and the terrain differed from those the Inukjuak families knew. Wood for building and fuel was scarce, and the families arrived late in the season with limited equipment. They lived at first in improvised shelters, and the first winters were hard. The Pond Inlet families, who had more experience of the High Arctic environment, helped the others, and communities developed from the shared experience, but the groups spoke different dialects and had different customs. Families in Resolute Bay and Grise Fiord were separated by distance, and in some cases family members were divided between locations. The families also had to adapt to a different hunting economy, which in the first years brought hunger and hardship. Inuit testimony describes cold, hunger, isolation, and homesickness, and also the resourcefulness with which the families survived and eventually built communities.

The promise to return was a major grievance. Testimony and the commission's findings indicate that many families were unable to return for years, and that the government did not provide transport back as promised. Some families eventually returned to Inukjuak in the following decades, while others stayed in the High Arctic, and the communities of Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay continue today. The consequences included separation from relatives, loss of access to traditional hunting grounds, and long-lasting grief, matters that relocated families and their descendants have described in testimony.

The relocation was investigated several times. A government-commissioned report in 1990 by the Hickling Corporation concluded, in the view of many readers, that the relocation had been carried out for the purpose of improving Inuit welfare and that families had gone voluntarily, although it acknowledged shortcomings in how the move was carried out. Inuit and the Makivik Corporation, the organization representing Inuit of Nunavik, disagreed, and the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples undertook its own inquiry, publishing in 1994 a report on the relocation. The commission reviewed documents and heard testimony and concluded that the government had failed in its obligations, that the families had not given fully informed consent, that the promises of return had not been kept, and that the relocation had been motivated in part by the desire to assert sovereignty. In 1996 the federal government and the relocated families reached an agreement under which a fund of ten million Canadian dollars was established, and in 2010 the Minister of Indian Affairs and Northern Development delivered a formal apology in the Inuit communities involved. The sequence of investigations itself is a historical process: successive inquiries reached different conclusions, in part because they used different standards of proof and different kinds of evidence.

Among those relocated as a child in 1953 was John Amagoalik, who later became a leading Inuit political figure and one of the principal advocates for the creation of Nunavut. His life, like those of other relocated people, shows how an experience of administrative displacement could feed into later political leadership. Other relocated people, including Larry Audlaluk, have written and spoken publicly about the events. Their accounts are retrospective, made decades after the events, and must be read alongside the records of the time.

Historical Vocabulary
Relocation, Resettlement, and Centralization

Relocation in this chapter refers to the movement of a community or group of families to a new place under the direction or with the support of a government. The term is used for cases ranging from the High Arctic relocations to the Thule removal. Resettlement is often used in official language for the establishment of people in a new community and carries a connotation of assistance, while centralization refers to the concentration of population in fewer, larger communities, whether by government policy or by the cumulative decisions of families.

Why it matters: The choice of word affects interpretation. "Resettlement" can obscure coercion, while "forced removal" may overstate it where families made decisions under constraints. This chapter uses the most precise term supported by evidence for each case.

Potential misunderstanding: Not all movement of Inuit families to settlements was relocation by government order. Many moves were the product of combinations of pressure, need, and choice.

Division III · Section 100.12

The Ahiarmiut Relocations and the Wider History of Settlement Displacement

The High Arctic relocations should not be allowed to stand for all relocations of Inuit in the mid-twentieth century. Other movements occurred for different reasons and under different circumstances. The history of the Ahiarmiut, an inland Inuit group of the Keewatin barrenlands in what is now Nunavut, illustrates this difference clearly.

The Ahiarmiut lived inland from Hudson Bay around Ennadai Lake and depended principally on migrating caribou, supplemented by freshwater fish. They were not coastal marine hunters, and the environments to which they were moved were not necessarily suited to their skills or subsistence patterns. In 1949 a federal weather and radio station was established at Ennadai Lake; its employees and supplies altered local relationships with outside authorities. Officials interpreted requests for food and assistance as signs of dependency, but accounts from Ahiarmiut families also record how the station and fluctuating caribou availability changed an already complex hunting economy. The subsequent relocations were decisions of government officials rather than a continuous journey voluntarily planned by the community.

The sequence can be reconstructed more precisely from Canada's Ahiarmiut Relocation Claim Settlement chronology and testimony. In 1950, without adequate consultation or supplies, officials moved Ahiarmiut families from Ennadai Lake to an unfamiliar island in Nueltin Lake; finding too little shelter and game, the families made a difficult return journey on foot. In 1957, government-organized movements took families to North Henik and Oftedal Lakes, where insufficient caribou and support contributed to starvation and the deaths of seven community members. In 1958, after survivors walked for days toward the Padlei trading post, authorities flew them to Arviat (then Eskimo Point). Further movements between 1959 and 1960 took Ahiarmiut from Arviat toward Rankin Inlet and Whale Cove; most had eventually settled in Arviat by 1969. The federal government's 2019 apology recognized multiple failed and forced relocations between 1950 and 1960. These dates outline a series of administrative interventions, not an itinerary that every Ahiarmiut household experienced identically.

The Ahiarmiut case differs from the High Arctic relocations in several ways. The Ahiarmiut were inland caribou hunters, while the Inukjuak families were coastal people moved to another coastal region. The Ahiarmiut moved gradually in a series of steps, each presented as a response to a particular problem, whereas the High Arctic program involved two organized shipborne transfers, in 1953 and 1955, that created new settlements. And while sovereignty was a major element of the High Arctic relocations, the Ahiarmiut moves were connected more closely to welfare administration, the location of services, and the weather station. What the cases share is the exercise of government authority over where Inuit families lived, with consequences for subsistence, family relationships, and connection to the land.

The late 1950s also saw hunger among other inland groups in the Keewatin barrenlands, with famine reported in several areas. Historians and Inuit testimony point to a combination of causes, including fluctuations in caribou herds, dependence on trade goods, disruptions of the fur trade, and the inadequacy of relief. In response, governments moved some families to the coast and provided relief and, in some cases, wage employment. The nickel mine at Rankin Inlet, which operated from 1957 to 1962, offered work to Inuit and drew families from the interior, and its closure in 1962 left many people without employment and required further relocation. The Rankin Inlet case illustrates the interaction of wage labor, administrative planning, and Inuit decision-making.

Other relocations took place in different circumstances. In Labrador, the closure of the Moravian stations at Nutak and Hebron in the 1950s, carried out with the involvement of the Newfoundland provincial government, led to the resettlement of their residents in other communities. The Labrador Inuit have since pressed claims about the decision, and the Moravian Church has issued apologies for its role. In Greenland, as noted, the G-60 plan and the closure of Qullissat led to resettlement. In Alaska, there were comparatively few directed relocations of Iñupiat communities, though economic change and consolidation of schools affected settlement. Differences among these cases make it essential to ask, for each relocation: who made the decision, with what stated purpose, with what degree of consent, with what promises, and with what consequences?

A useful classification, with the caution that real cases overlap, distinguishes three types. Some relocations were coerced or directed, with families having little real choice, as in the Thule removal. Some were organized by government with the agreement of families who nonetheless acted under pressure or on the basis of incomplete information, as in the High Arctic. And others involved movements to settlements by families responding to a mixture of policy incentives, economic need, schooling for children, medical care, and the decline of subsistence conditions, with decisions made by families themselves, albeit within narrowed options. The last category is the most numerous and perhaps the least dramatic, but it transformed the Arctic more than any single relocation.

Cause and Effect

Why Relocations Occurred in the Canadian Arctic, 1950–1960

Long-term causes

The expansion of the fur trade and credit economy, concerns about Arctic sovereignty, the growth of the welfare state, and a belief among officials that existing Inuit economies could not support Inuit populations.

Short-term causes

The collapse of fox prices, periodic hunger in some regions, the extension of family allowances and relief, the establishment of weather stations and administrative posts, and the Cold War strategic environment.

Immediate context

The creation of a central northern department in 1953 and the arrival of northern service officers who could organize movements by ship and aircraft.

Event or transformation

The High Arctic relocations, the Ahiarmiut moves, and the broader movement of families to settlements.

Immediate effects

Hardship, family separation, and the establishment of new communities; heightened dependence on government assistance in some cases.

Long-term consequences

Public inquiries, apologies and compensation, Inuit political mobilization, and enduring public memory of relocation as a symbol of colonial administration.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the relocations. Welfare concerns, sovereignty, economics, and administrative convenience combined in different proportions in different cases, and historians continue to debate how much weight each deserves.

Division IV · Section 100.13

Tuberculosis, Medical Evacuation, and the Separation of Families

Tuberculosis was among the most serious health crises of twentieth-century Inuit history. Tuberculosis was present in Inuit communities from the early part of the century, and it spread in conditions that included crowded winter housing, malnutrition in years of poor hunting, and contact with outside populations. By the 1940s and 1950s, rates of infection and disease among Inuit were many times higher than in the general Canadian population, and the disease was a leading cause of illness and death. Similar problems affected Alaska Natives and, to a lesser extent, Greenlanders, and in all three regions public health authorities launched campaigns against the disease.

In Canada, the federal Department of National Health and Welfare assumed responsibility for Inuit health in the mid-1940s. The response relied on surveys, treatment, and isolation. From the late 1940s, medical teams traveled on the Eastern Arctic Patrol and, increasingly, on a government ship, the C.D. Howe, equipped with X-ray facilities, to examine Inuit communities in the summer, and those found to have tuberculosis, or suspected of having it, were often taken south for treatment. Patients were sent to sanatoria and hospitals in southern Canada, among them institutions in Hamilton, Ontario, in Edmonton, Alberta, and elsewhere in Quebec, Manitoba, and other provinces. The scale was large. At the height of the evacuations, a considerable proportion of the Inuit population was at some time hospitalized in the south, and in some communities large numbers of people, including parents of young children, were removed in a single season. Because accurate figures vary among sources, this chapter does not provide a single total.

The medical case for evacuation was serious. Tuberculosis was infectious and often fatal, effective drug treatment became available beginning in the late 1940s, and the Arctic had few hospital facilities. Treatment in the south saved many lives, and by the 1960s, as treatment improved and public health measures took effect, tuberculosis rates declined substantially. Many Inuit who recovered in southern hospitals credited the treatment. This is part of the historical record and must not be discounted.

The harms were also serious and have been described in testimony, in the findings of the Qikiqtani Truth Commission and other inquiries, and in the accounts of survivors and families. People were removed from their communities, sometimes with little notice, and sent far away for months or years. Patients and families often received limited information about where they were going, how long they would remain, and how they were doing. Communication between patients and families was limited by distance, language, and the absence of interpreters, and letters could be delayed or lost. Many patients found themselves in an unfamiliar environment, speaking a language not understood by staff, eating unfamiliar food, and enduring prolonged confinement. Children were sometimes sent south alone or separated from parents, and some were adopted or placed in institutions. Some patients died in southern hospitals, and families were often not told, or were told later, and burial locations were sometimes unrecorded or unmarked, leaving families unable to learn what happened to relatives. Patients who returned after long absences sometimes found that the community had changed, that they had lost proficiency in Inuktitut, or that their family relationships had been disrupted. The experience also affected the knowledge available to communities, since elders, hunters, and parents were absent for long periods.

Efforts to address these harms came decades later. Inuit organizations and the federal government have in recent years supported research to identify patients who died in southern institutions and to locate their burial places and inform families, a project known as Nanilavut ("let's find them"), launched in 2017. In 2019 the Prime Minister of Canada, in a ceremony in Iqaluit, offered an apology to Inuit for the federal government's handling of tuberculosis, acknowledging the suffering caused by the treatment and by the separations. These recent developments are part of broader reckonings with institutional history, some of which are treated in Chapter 127.

Historians must distinguish between the medical campaign's benefits and the circumstances in which it was carried out. The central issue is not whether treatment was needed but how it was administered: how decisions were made, how families were informed, whether Inuit were consulted or accompanied, and whether the methods respected family bonds. Comparisons with other regions are instructive. Greenland's tuberculosis campaign, conducted largely within Greenland and with more Greenlandic medical staff, caused less separation over great distances. Alaska's campaign involved sanatoria in the territory and in the Pacific Northwest. Differences in geography and administration produced differences in how families experienced treatment.

Medical files document institutional decisions and outcomes, and testimony describes how those decisions felt and what they meant to families. Reading them together allows historians to weigh the benefits of treatment against the costs of separation.

Division IV · Section 100.14

Residential Schools, Hostels, Language, and Educational Transformation

Formal schooling for Inuit children expanded dramatically after the Second World War. Before then, schooling had been provided by missions, with limited and seasonal instruction in the Canadian Arctic, more developed church-run schooling in Greenland, and a combination of federal and mission schools in Alaska. After 1945 governments undertook major programs of school construction and enrollment, regarding education as essential to preparing Inuit for wage employment and for participation in the wider society.

In the Canadian Arctic, the federal government established day schools in settlements beginning in the 1950s, and in the 1950s and 1960s, as it expanded, students from outlying camps were housed in hostels or residences. In the western Arctic, church-run residential schools in places such as Inuvik, including Stringer Hall and Grollier Hall, housed Inuit children, along with Indigenous students from other nations, under arrangements in which the churches operated residences and the federal government funded and supervised the system. In the eastern Arctic, federal hostels and schools were established at places such as Iqaluit and Chesterfield Inlet, and in the 1960s, older students were sent to larger centers, including the Churchill Vocational Centre in Manitoba. The details and responsible parties varied. Some institutions were church-run residential schools of the kind treated in Chapters 87 and 88, others were federally administered hostels and day schools, and the Inuit experience of them was shaped by those differences. Inuit were, for a time, excluded from the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement of 2006, which provided redress to former students of recognized residential schools, and Inuit former students later pursued separate claims, an issue connected to the reckoning treated in Chapter 127.

The effects on children and families were profound. Children, some as young as six, were separated from parents, in some cases for most of the year, at an age at which they would traditionally have been learning skills and knowledge from family members. Instruction was in English or French, and Inuit languages were not supported in most schools, and in some, were discouraged or punished. The curriculum was drawn from southern Canada and often had little connection to Arctic life. Many former students recall homesickness, loss of language, harsh discipline, and in some institutions physical and sexual abuse. Some recall also positive experiences of learning, friendship, and opportunity. The long-term consequences included weakened intergenerational transmission of language and skills, strained parent-child relationships, and the need for later healing.

Government policy also tied schooling to family support. As noted, family allowance payments were in some years linked to school attendance, which gave parents financial reasons to send children to school or to move to settlements with schools. Parents faced a difficult choice. Many wanted their children to be educated, seeing schooling as a means to opportunity in a changing economy, and some Inuit parents actively sought schools. But they did not want education to cost them their children's language, knowledge, or presence. Inuit parents and students also contested educational conditions, by asking for local schools and instruction in Inuktitut and by speaking out, and in some places by resisting, in ways that later became part of broader demands for Inuit-controlled education. The creation in 1975 of the Kativik School Board in Nunavik, under the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement, and later developments in Nunavut and elsewhere, are examples of Inuit gaining more control over schooling.

In Greenland, schooling developed along different lines. Greenlandic-language instruction was long established, with trained Greenlandic teachers, so that by the early twentieth century literacy was widespread. After the Second World War, Danish modernization policy expanded schooling and increased the use of Danish as a language of instruction and advancement, with many Danish teachers brought to Greenland, and a number of Greenlandic children were sent to Denmark for education. A notable episode, in 1951, involved a group of twenty-two Greenlandic children who were sent to Denmark for schooling and to be raised as Danish-speaking Greenlanders; many did not return to their families and some lost contact with their language. The Danish government apologized to the survivors in 2020. Greenlanders in the 1960s and 1970s increasingly criticized language policy, and the 1979 Home Rule Act designated Greenlandic as the principal language of Greenland, with Danish taught thoroughly. In Alaska, the federal Bureau of Indian Affairs and the territorial and later state governments operated schools, including boarding schools such as Mt. Edgecumbe in Sitka, and many Iñupiat students were sent away for high school until litigation in the 1970s, known from the lead plaintiff as the Molly Hootch case, resulted in an agreement that required local high schools in villages. The case affected Yup'ik and other Alaska Native communities as well as Iñupiat.

Education changed knowledge transmission. Where children had learned hunting, sewing, and the handling of dogs by observation and participation, they now spent much of their childhood in classrooms and residences, and some who returned to camps or settlements no longer had the skills or the language fluency of their elders. Yet it would be wrong to treat knowledge transmission as simply broken. Parents, grandparents, and communities continued to teach, many students retained or recovered their languages, and later movements for Inuit-language education built on that persistence, as examined in Chapter 123. Education also created a generation of literate, bilingual Inuit who became interpreters, teachers, politicians, and organizers, and their experiences, including those of the schools, informed the Inuit political agenda of the 1960s and 1970s.

Sled Dogs, Transportation, and the Disruption of Mobility

The qimmiq (plural qimmiit), the Inuit sled dog, was fundamental to Inuit life across much of the Arctic. Dog teams pulled sleds over the sea ice and tundra, carrying families, supplies, and the products of the hunt between camps. Dogs assisted hunters by locating seal breathing holes and polar bears, and they were a measure of a family's capacity and independence. A hunter with a strong team could travel far to find game, visit relatives, and trade at posts. The care of dogs was part of household labor, and dogs required food, which meant that a family's hunting had to support both people and animals. The importance of dog teams varied by region. In Greenland, particularly the north and east, in the Canadian Arctic, and on the Alaskan North Slope, they were central, while in some places, boats and other forms of transport mattered more.

In the mid-twentieth century, dog teams in many communities were severely reduced. Disease was one factor: epidemics of canine distemper and other illnesses periodically struck dog populations, and in some years large numbers died. Government regulation was another. Authorities, particularly in the Northwest Territories and in northern Quebec, adopted dog-control ordinances that required dogs to be tied or confined within settlements, and police officers enforced these rules. As families moved to settlements, free-running or poorly controlled dogs came into conflict with residents, especially children, and officials cited public-safety and disease concerns. Dog bites and the spread of rabies were recognized dangers. At the same time, keeping a team in a settlement was difficult, as dogs needed food and space, and families often had to take their dogs out onto the land for winter hunting.

The killing of dogs by police is a major theme of Inuit testimony. The Qikiqtani Truth Commission, established by the Qikiqtani Inuit Association to investigate government actions in the Baffin region from about 1950 to 1975, documented many accounts from the 1950s and 1960s of the RCMP shooting dogs, in some cases in large numbers, in communities including Pangnirtung, Pond Inlet, Qikiqtarjuaq, Cape Dorset (Kinngait), and Iqaluit. Inuit witnesses described the killing as devastating, since it removed their means of travel, hunting, and independence, and in some cases forced families to remain in settlements or to rely on government assistance. Similar accounts have been collected in Nunavik and elsewhere. A federal review of the RCMP's role, undertaken in the 2000s, found no policy of mass dog killing, and the Qikiqtani Truth Commission reported that it did not find a single directive ordering a general slaughter, but that the killing of dogs was widespread, that it often occurred in connection with settlement and dog-control measures, and that its effects on Inuit were severe. Students should note the distinctions. The evidence supports the conclusion that dogs were killed in many communities and that the consequences were grave, while the explanations differ: disease control, public safety, enforcement of local ordinances, and, in Inuit interpretation, an element of pressure to settle. Not all incidents were identical, and the existing evidence does not show one uniform national policy.

The introduction of the snowmobile transformed travel in the same decades. Motorized toboggans were adopted widely in the Arctic in the 1960s, in Alaska as "snow machines" and in Canada as "ski-doos," and offered speed and convenience. Many Inuit hunters adopted them willingly, valuing the ability to cover distances quickly, and snowmobiles were useful for hunters working from settlements. But snowmobiles required fuel, parts, and cash, which tied hunters more closely to the wage and credit economy, and they did not substitute for the dog team's ability to find a route, to warn of danger, or to run on fuel obtained by hunting. Breakdowns far from settlements could be dangerous. The shift from dogs to machines thus changed the relationship between hunting and the cash economy, and between travel and the settlement as a supply base.

The loss of dog teams therefore had several overlapping effects. It reduced independence in travel and hunting, increased dependence on cash and on supplies from the settlement, and shifted the knowledge of dog handling and sled travel away from younger generations in some places. In some regions it contributed to the decline of long-distance travel and visiting. The loss also had symbolic and emotional significance, and elders' testimony often links it to a larger sense of loss of control over their own lives. Later efforts to revive Inuit dog teams, and to preserve the qimmiq as a distinct breed, which Canada recognized as a heritage breed in the early 2000s, reflect the dog's cultural importance.

Division IV · Section 100.16

Permanent Villages, Wage Employment, Housing, and Changing Family Life

By the 1960s, large numbers of Inuit in the Canadian Arctic lived in permanent communities for most of the year. In Greenland, urbanization and concentration in larger towns proceeded under the Danish modernization programs, and in Alaska, Iñupiat communities such as Barrow, Kotzebue, and Point Hope had long been fixed settlements to which schools, stores, and jobs were added. The experience of settlement can be understood through its material and social aspects.

Housing was a first change. Many Inuit had lived in snow houses, sod houses, tents, and small wooden dwellings built with scavenged materials. Government housing programs, beginning in the 1950s, introduced prefabricated houses, which were small and often inadequate for large families, were poorly insulated, and were difficult to heat, and in the mid-1960s, rental housing programs provided more units. The "matchbox" houses of the 1950s and 1960s are widely remembered in Inuit testimony for their small size. Houses brought electricity, heating by oil, and, later, running water in some communities, but also required rent, fuel, and maintenance and led to overcrowding in many places. In Greenland, the construction of apartment blocks in towns such as Nuuk brought modern housing but also a sharp contrast with earlier settlement forms. In Alaska, federal and state housing programs began later and produced similar patterns of rapid change.

Employment and income also changed. Wage work in settlements, in construction, in government services, at military installations, in mines such as the Rankin Inlet nickel mine, and in cooperatives and stores supplemented or replaced trapping. Many Inuit took wage jobs willingly, some with success, but wage employment was limited, often seasonal, and frequently filled by outsiders in the higher-skilled positions. Social assistance and family allowances provided additional income. The result was a mixed economy in which cash and subsistence were combined. Households used wages to buy rifles, snowmobiles, fuel, and store food, and hunting provided food and cultural continuity. The relationship between the two was often supportive, and also often a source of tension, because wage work took time from hunting and hunting required cash for equipment.

Food also changed. Imported store food, including flour, sugar, canned goods, and processed foods, became a larger share of diets, and its cost was high in the Arctic. Country food (meat and fish obtained by hunting and fishing) remained culturally and nutritionally important and was often shared across households. Changes in diet had health consequences, which later public health research has examined, and the sharing of country food continued to express kinship and community obligation even in settlements.

Family life and gender roles changed. Women's labor was reorganized. Sewing and skin preparation continued, but women also took wage work, as clerks, cleaners, nurses' aides, and in cooperatives, and, particularly in the arts, as artists whose work brought income to households. Women in settlements managed household budgets, children's schooling, and relations with government and church institutions, and in many cases they became community leaders. Men's roles changed as wage work, snowmobile travel, and hunting from settlements replaced long seasonal camps, and some men found themselves in jobs with lower status than hunting had provided. Children's lives shifted toward schooling, and the relationship between children and elders changed as children spent less time on the land. Elders, whose authority had rested on knowledge of the land and its resources, found their roles redefined. Social problems, including alcohol abuse, family violence, and suicide, were recognized in many communities in the 1960s and 1970s, with causes understood by Inuit and researchers to include rapid social change, loss of autonomy, and trauma from separations. Historians should not describe these problems as the inevitable result of "modernization," but as consequences of specific historical conditions.

Yet settlement was not purely a loss. Many families chose to live in settlements for reasons of access to clinics, schools, jobs, and social life, and for the security of heated housing and regular food, particularly after hunger and illness. Communities developed new institutions, including churches, cooperatives, hamlet councils, and community organizations, and Inuit adapted the settlements to their own purposes, with seasonal hunting trips, camps near communities, and the maintenance of kinship networks. The anthropological and Inuit literature on this period shows both change and continuity: new forms of sociability, new tensions, and persistent practices of sharing and kinship.

Historical Debate

Were Permanent Settlements Primarily a Means of Delivering Services or an Instrument of Colonial Control?

The question: Historians and Inuit commentators disagree about how to characterize the policy of settlement centralization in the postwar Arctic.

Interpretation A — A means of service delivery: Some scholars and former officials argue that governments sought to provide health care, schooling, housing, and social support efficiently to a scattered population, that conditions in many camps involved hunger and disease, and that many families themselves chose settlements to obtain these services.
Interpretation B — An instrument of colonial control: Others, including many Inuit witnesses and the Qikiqtani Truth Commission, emphasize that settlement made Inuit easier to administer, supervise, and educate, tied family support to place and school attendance, accompanied the loss of dogs and mobility, and reflected assumptions that Inuit life on the land had no future.
Interpretation C — Overlapping and varied purposes: Many historians conclude that both explanations apply: individual officials had different motives, policy responded to real needs and to the aims of control and sovereignty, and outcomes varied from place to place.

Evidence: Administrative records, program regulations, economic data on fur prices and game, Inuit testimony, and later commission findings. These sources do not always agree, and they describe different regions and years.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns motive, the degree of coercion, and the weight to give Inuit choice relative to government pressure, rather than the basic facts of settlement.

Why it matters: How the policy is characterized affects how responsibility is assigned, how communities understand their history, and how present policy on housing, services, and consultation is framed.

Division V · Section 100.17

Inuit Political Representation and the Emergence of Modern Organizations

Inuit political organization in the 1960s and 1970s grew out of the experiences described in the preceding sections: relocation, medical evacuation, schooling, registration, and the loss of control over local decisions. It also grew out of new opportunities. A generation of Inuit had learned English or French, attended school or worked for the government, and gained skills as interpreters, administrators, and community leaders. Some had been educated in southern institutions and met other Indigenous people, and increasingly they questioned the paternalism of northern administration.

The earliest institutions of political participation were local and consultative. Settlement councils and hamlet councils, advisory bodies created by governments, gave Inuit a voice in local matters, though with limited authority. Cooperatives, formed in the late 1950s and 1960s in places such as Cape Dorset, Povungnituk, and other communities, gave Inuit experience in running businesses and making collective decisions, and they became bases for later political organizing. In the Northwest Territories, the territorial government expanded Inuit representation, and in 1965 Abraham Okpik was appointed to the Territorial Council, one of the first Inuit to sit in a Canadian legislative body. In Nunavik, the Quebec government's expansion in the 1960s prompted Inuit cooperatives and local leaders to articulate their own concerns, and in Labrador, Inuit organized in the early 1970s, forming the Labrador Inuit Association in 1973.

Abraham Okpik's most noted contribution was Project Surname. Okpik was appointed in 1969 to lead an effort to replace the identification number and disc system with surnames. He and his assistants traveled through Arctic communities, consulting with families about the names they wished to use, and recording them. Inuit chose among family names, nicknames, the names of fathers, and Christian names, and the process, carried out principally between 1968 and 1971, resulted in the registration of surnames for Inuit in the Northwest Territories and neighboring regions. The project was a reform and not a solution to every problem: surnames also fit Inuit naming practices imperfectly, and the project was administered by government. Yet it was a notable instance of an Inuit leader reforming a state system, and many Inuit regarded the end of the numbers as the return of personal names, though the numbering system's history remained a painful memory.

Inuit also began to organize nationally. In 1971, Inuit leaders founded Inuit Tapirisat of Canada, meaning "Inuit will be united" in Inuktitut, as an organization to represent Inuit interests in dealings with the federal government. Its founders included Inuit who had been educated and employed in southern Canada and others from northern communities. The organization pressed for Inuit participation in decisions affecting the north, for improved services, for recognition of Inuit rights, and, within a few years, for land claims. It was renamed Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami in 2001. The founding of the organization was a turning point in the representation of Inuit as a distinct people in Canadian politics, and it provided an institutional base for subsequent negotiations.

Political mobilization was linked to the wider context. In the late 1960s, Indigenous leaders across Canada reacted strongly against the federal government's 1969 White Paper on Indian policy, which proposed ending the separate legal status of First Nations, and Inuit took part in the broader discussion of Indigenous rights. In 1973, the Supreme Court's decision in the Calder case, concerning Nisga'a title in British Columbia, led the federal government to adopt a policy of negotiating comprehensive land claims. Large resource-development proposals in the north, including pipeline and hydroelectric projects, raised issues of land and consultation. Inuit organizations argued that they needed formal recognition of their rights in land and resources, and by the mid-1970s they were developing land-claims proposals, including a 1976 proposal to establish a territory called Nunavut. These developments, and the negotiations they led to, belong to Chapter 116 and to Chapter 117, which treat the modern agreements; the present chapter is concerned with their origins in earlier experience.

In Alaska, a parallel process occurred. Iñupiat leaders created regional organizations, among them the Arctic Slope Native Association in the 1960s, to protect land and rights, especially after the discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 raised the stakes. This movement, together with the broader Alaska Native land claims movement, led to the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, treated in Chapter 115. In 1972 Iñupiat created the North Slope Borough, a regional municipal government that gave the Iñupiat of the North Slope a means of taxation and local control, and Eben Hopson was its first mayor. In Greenland, political parties formed in the mid-1970s, as discussed in section 100.19. The simultaneous development of Inuit organizations in all three regions in the early 1970s is itself an important fact, and it prepared the ground for the circumpolar movement.

Inuit organizations' strategies drew on earlier practices. They used the language of rights and nationhood, while grounding their claims in long occupancy and knowledge of the land, and they combined political pressure with research, such as the Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project, which documented traditional land use through interviews and maps in the 1970s, a project that used Inuit knowledge as evidence in political negotiations. The project showed how knowledge held by hunters and elders could be converted into a form that governments would recognize.

Inuit Art, Language, Media, and Community-Controlled Cultural Institutions

The postwar decades also saw the emergence of Inuit art as a significant cultural and economic institution, and of efforts to protect Inuit languages and to build media in those languages. These developments were not independent of the wider political changes; they were another way in which Inuit created institutions through which to speak for themselves.

Inuit had long made carvings, tools, clothing, and decorative objects, and their material culture had been collected by whalers, traders, and explorers. In the late 1940s, a new commercial market for Inuit sculpture began to develop in southern Canada, encouraged by the federal government and by individuals such as the artist and civil servant James Houston, who promoted carving in northern Quebec and Baffin Island and helped to market it in the south. Carvings in stone, bone, and ivory, depicting animals, hunters, and spiritual figures, found buyers, and for many families the sale of carvings became a significant source of cash. Government agencies and organizations such as the Canadian Handicrafts Guild and the Hudson's Bay Company assisted in marketing.

Graphic arts followed. Beginning in the 1950s, printmaking and drawing programs were introduced in Cape Dorset (Kinngait), where Houston and others arranged for artists to be trained in print techniques, and the West Baffin Eskimo Co-operative, founded in 1959, became the institutional base for the community's art production. The first official annual collection of Kinngait prints was catalogued in 1959, and the annual releases that followed made the community's work internationally known. Kenojuak Ashevak, whose image The Enchanted Owl appeared in the 1960 print collection, became among the most recognized Inuit artists, and other artists including Pitseolak Ashoona, whose drawings and memoir recorded her life and her community's history, and Kananginak Pootoogook, contributed to the community's reputation. Printmaking spread to other communities, including Puvirnituq (Povungnituk) in Nunavik, Baker Lake, Holman (Ulukhaktok), and Pangnirtung, each developing its own styles and institutions.

The history of Inuit art illustrates the interaction of Inuit agency and outside influence. Inuit artists made creative choices about subject, style, and technique, drew on knowledge of animals, stories, and daily life, and developed distinctive individual and community styles. Women artists, among them Kenojuak, Pitseolak, and many others, were prominent in printmaking and drawing, and in textile arts such as the work in Baker Lake, which gave women new forms of income and status. At the same time, art production was shaped by government sponsorship, by the preferences of southern buyers, by non-Inuit advisers who influenced technique and marketing, and by the role of intermediaries and cooperatives in setting prices and selecting work. Scholars debate the extent to which these influences shaped content and the extent to which Inuit artists adapted the market to their own purposes. Many Inuit art cooperatives, which Inuit managed and owned, became important community institutions, and by the 1970s Inuit artists increasingly exercised more control over production and sale. The topic is treated again from the standpoint of cultural resurgence in Chapter 126.

Language and media were additional fields of cultural organization. Syllabics and Roman orthography were used in publications, and Inuit-language periodicals such as Inuktitut, published by the federal government and later by Inuit Tapirisat, circulated in northern communities. Radio broadcasting in Inuktitut, which began on a limited scale through the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation's northern service, expanded in the 1960s and 1970s, and satellite communications, available in the Arctic from the early 1970s, made new forms of broadcasting possible. In the late 1970s Inuit organizations carried out experiments in Inuktitut television, which led to the founding of the Inuit Broadcasting Corporation in 1981. In Greenland, radio and the long-established Greenlandic press gave Greenlanders a public sphere in their own language, and in Alaska, Iñupiat communities had local radio and publications, including the Tundra Times, a statewide Alaska Native newspaper founded in 1962 with significant Iñupiat involvement, which became an important forum for political discussion. Inuit writers, storytellers, and filmmakers began in these years to produce works for Inuit audiences, a development examined further in Chapter 126.

Historical Debate

Did Twentieth-Century Arctic Modernization Expand Inuit Opportunities While Undermining Indigenous Autonomy?

The question: Schooling, health care, housing, employment, and political representation expanded in the postwar Arctic. Did these developments enlarge Inuit options, weaken Inuit control over their own institutions, or both?

Interpretation A — Expanded opportunity: Scholars and many Inuit emphasize that the new institutions improved health, extended literacy, created jobs, and provided platforms for political leadership, and that Inuit chose to use them for their own purposes.
Interpretation B — Undermined autonomy: Others stress that the same institutions displaced community-controlled arrangements, were designed and administered by outsiders, diminished mobility and language, and increased dependence on governments and cash.
Interpretation C — Simultaneous and uneven effects: A third approach holds that the effects were simultaneous: the same institution, such as the school or the cooperative, could both diminish and expand Inuit capacity, depending on who controlled it. This interpretation points to the later gain of Inuit control over schools, art cooperatives, broadcasting, and governments as evidence that institutions could be reshaped.

Evidence: Health and education statistics, employment data, testimony, the records of cooperatives and organizations, and the later history of Inuit-controlled institutions.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns how to weigh benefits against losses, whether autonomy is best measured by individual choice or collective control, and whether the changes were avoidable.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians describe the era, either as one of modernization, one of colonial imposition, or one of negotiated transformation, and bears on contemporary discussions of self-government.

Greenlandic Political Reform and the Establishment of Home Rule in 1979

Greenland's twentieth-century political development culminated in the creation of Home Rule in 1979. It is a distinct trajectory, since Greenland was an established colony with councils and an educated elite, and its relation to Denmark was determined by negotiation within a single national framework rather than by federal or territorial administration.

The Second World War, as noted, left Greenland temporarily self-managing, and in the late 1940s the Danish government appointed commissions on Greenland's future. In 1950 the two provincial councils were merged into a single Greenland Provincial Council, the Landsråd. Greenlandic representatives took part in the consultations that led to the constitutional change of 1953. A revised Danish constitution, adopted that year, ended Greenland's status as a colony and made it an integral part of the Danish state, with representation in the Danish parliament, the Folketing, through two seats. Many Danes regarded this as a progressive act of equality, and a number of Greenlandic politicians supported it. The change was, however, determined largely by Danish authorities, and a number of Greenlanders later criticized the speed and manner of incorporation. In practice, incorporation meant that Greenland came under Danish legislation and administration and that the policies of the 1950s and 1960s, including modernization and the expansion of Danish-language education, were pursued as policy within the Danish state.

In the 1960s and 1970s, dissatisfaction grew. Greenlanders criticized the pace and direction of modernization, the dominance of Danish administrators and workers, the use of Danish as a language of advancement, the displacement of Greenlandic labor by skilled Danish workers, and the lack of Greenlandic control over decisions. A younger generation, many educated in Denmark, played a leading role in articulating these concerns. Greenlandic students in Denmark organized and drew on the global movements of the time, including decolonization and Indigenous rights movements. An important event was the 1972 referendum on whether Denmark should join the European Economic Community. Danes voted in favor, and Greenland, as part of Denmark, became part of the Community, even though a substantial majority of voters in Greenland had voted against. The result, which highlighted Greenlanders' lack of control, strengthened the movement for greater autonomy.

Political parties and increasingly assertive constitutional demands emerged in this environment. Siumut ("forward") developed as a movement before becoming a party in 1977; Inuit Ataqatigiit also took shape in the second half of the 1970s, articulating a stronger demand for Greenlandic control and Indigenous political recognition. The Home Rule process developed in two stages that must not be confused. An internal Greenlandic Home Rule Committee established in 1973 formulated proposals and submitted its report to Danish authorities in February 1975. That work led to a joint Greenlandic-Danish Home Rule Commission formed in 1975, whose recommendations were published in 1978. Denmark then adopted the Greenland Home Rule Act in November 1978. A referendum in Greenland on 17 January 1979 approved the arrangement by a clear majority, and Home Rule entered into force on 1 May 1979. Elections to the new Greenlandic legislature took place in April; Jonathan Motzfeldt became head of the Home Rule government. This chronology demonstrates that Greenlandic initiatives informed the negotiations rather than simply receiving a reform handed down by Copenhagen.

Home Rule transferred significant responsibilities from Denmark to Greenlandic institutions, including local government, taxation, education, cultural affairs, social welfare, health, and, in time, fisheries and other areas, while Denmark retained responsibility for foreign affairs, defense, the constitution, and the legal system. Greenlandic was recognized as the principal language, with Danish to be taught thoroughly. Questions about control of subsurface resources were addressed through a joint arrangement and remained a matter for later negotiation. Home Rule was a major step toward the political recognition of Kalaallit and their institutions, but it was not independence. Greenland remained within the Danish realm, depended on Danish financial support, and did not control foreign or defense policy, matters that continued to be significant given the American military presence at Thule. Later changes included Greenland's withdrawal from the European Community in 1985 following a 1982 referendum, and the introduction of a broader self-government arrangement in 2009, which lies beyond this chapter's scope but is related to the themes treated in Chapter 148.

Historical Vocabulary
Home Rule

Definition: Home Rule refers to an arrangement in which a territory within a state exercises authority over specified domestic matters through its own elected institutions, while the state retains other powers.

Historical context: In Greenland, the Home Rule Act of 1978, which took effect in 1979, defined a division of responsibilities between Greenlandic institutions and the Danish state. It followed Greenland's incorporation into Denmark in 1953 and grew out of political movements of the 1970s.

Why it matters: Home Rule was a milestone in the political recognition of Kalaallit and a model discussed by other Indigenous peoples, but it was granted by act of the Danish parliament and was not itself independence.

Potential misunderstanding: Home Rule differs from the later 2009 self-government arrangement and from sovereignty as a state. In North American usage, similar words may refer to quite different arrangements.

The Inuit Circumpolar Movement and the Transformation of Arctic Politics

By the mid-1970s, Inuit organizations in Alaska, Canada, and Greenland were pursuing similar goals: recognition of rights in land and resources, protection of the environment and hunting, control over education and language, and political voice. Their leaders knew one another, since relatives and kin crossed borders, leaders had met at political events, and Inuit travel and communication had increased. The step from parallel organization to a joint movement was taken at Barrow, Alaska.

The initiative came from Eben Hopson Sr., an Iñupiaq leader and the first mayor of the North Slope Borough. His experiences in the Alaska Native land-claims movement, borough government, struggles over oil development, and the defense of subsistence hunting informed his conviction that Inuit across the Arctic should coordinate responses to governments and international bodies. Inuit leaders met in preparatory discussions in 1976; Hopson then convened delegates from Alaska, Canada, and Greenland at Barrow (now Utqiaġvik), 13–18 June 1977, for the first Inuit Circumpolar Conference. The conference established a continuing organization, and Hopson served as its founding chair from 1977 to 1980. International regulation of bowhead whaling was already a concern, but the International Whaling Commission's June 20–24, 1977 decision to halt the Alaskan bowhead subsistence hunt followed the June 13–18 inaugural conference; it did not prompt its organization. The later dispute sharpened the newly established movement's sense that distant decisions about marine mammals could threaten Inuit food security and political autonomy; it must not be described as the immediate cause of the earlier meeting.

The conference debated shared concerns. Delegates discussed the protection of land, ocean, and wildlife, including whales and other marine mammals; the dangers of resource development, including oil and gas exploration and mining, and their effects on the environment; Inuit rights to participate in decisions about the Arctic; the preservation of Inuit languages and cultures; and the need to coordinate their approaches to national governments and international bodies. Participants also stressed the unity of Inuit as one people divided by state borders. The conference produced resolutions and the foundation of a continuing organization, which held further general assemblies, including a notable one in Nuuk, Greenland, in 1980, and which later became the Inuit Circumpolar Council, a body with status in international forums. Hopson died in 1980, but the organization continued to grow.

The significance of the circumpolar movement lies in several features. It demonstrated that Inuit political identity was not limited by the borders of Canada, the United States, and Denmark, and that Inuit could act as a people, not only as minorities within different states. It used the experiences of the preceding decades, including relocation, medical separation, schooling, and loss of control over land and animals, as a basis for shared demands. It connected local concerns, such as whaling, to international institutions, and it prepared Inuit for participation in later international forums on environmental protection, Arctic governance, and Indigenous rights, subjects treated in Chapter 143 and in Chapter 138. And it showed that Inuit political resurgence developed through local, regional, national, and international initiatives at once.

Synthesis: Inuit in the Twentieth Century

Looking back across the century, three conclusions stand out. First, the Arctic did not undergo a single uniform transition. Whaling and trading changed Alaska and the western Arctic before 1920, Greenland was transformed by fisheries and then state planning, and the central and eastern Canadian Arctic experienced its most dramatic administrative change after 1945. Second, the principal institutions, trading posts, missions, police, schools, clinics, and settlements, were not simply imposed. Inuit used them, adapted them, and sometimes refused them, but they did so within conditions in which governments held far greater power, and in which policy could sharply narrow the options. Third, the experiences of relocation, separation, and loss of autonomy, together with the education, skills, and organizations that Inuit built, created the conditions for political mobilization.

The circumpolar movement of the 1970s left much undone. In Canada, the Inuit land claims negotiations were still in progress, and the territory of Nunavut would not be created until 1999. In Alaska, the settlement framework of 1971 left unresolved questions about the relationship between Native corporations, tribal governments, and subsistence. In Greenland, Home Rule was a first step toward greater self-government. These developments are treated in Chapters 115 and 116. What the century had established was the existence of Inuit as increasingly organized political actors, aware of their shared history, with institutions, leaders, and a program, positioned to negotiate the land, government, and sovereignty arrangements that would define the next stage of their history.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Inuit Authority and Institutions, 1900–1979

What Changed

  • Settlement shifted from mobile seasonal camps toward permanent communities, a change that was gradual and uneven and was most rapid in the Canadian Arctic after 1945
  • The economy shifted from hunting with limited trade to a mixed economy of wages, transfers, trapping, art, and subsistence
  • Governments extended registration, schooling, medical care, policing, and housing into the Arctic, and in some cases moved families
  • Leadership shifted from camp-based authority to elected councils, cooperatives, and regional, national, and international organizations
  • Transportation shifted from dog teams to snowmobiles and aircraft

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship networks, naming relationships, and obligations of sharing continued to organize community life
  • Hunting and the consumption of country food remained culturally and economically significant
  • Inuit languages continued to be spoken, although under pressure, and were the basis of later language movements
  • Inuit continued to assert their place in and responsibility for their homelands

When and for whom: Change was gradual in Alaska and Greenland and more abrupt in parts of the Canadian Arctic. Children, patients, relocated families, and inland caribou hunters experienced change most directly, while some elders and families who remained on the land longer experienced greater continuity.

Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that Inuit society was transformed without being replaced. Treating the century as a simple loss of tradition or a simple modernization overlooks the persistence of Inuit institutions and the creation of new ones.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Inuit societies in Alaska, the Canadian Arctic, and Greenland experienced the twentieth century as a transformation from largely locally governed, mobile communities toward increasingly administered settlements. The central argument is that the period was neither a straightforward passage from tradition to modernity nor merely a story of cultural destruction. It was a period of expanding state authority, commercial incorporation, and social change, in which Inuit adapted, resisted, and built new institutions.

At the beginning of the century, Inuit lived in a connected homeland stretching from Alaska to Greenland, speaking related languages, organized in kin-based communities, and sustained by marine and inland hunting economies shaped by detailed environmental knowledge. Contact with outsiders was already extensive but uneven. Whalers, traders, missionaries, and police had established themselves in different regions at different times, and the three states involved, Canada, the United States, and Denmark, administered their Arctic territories under different systems: a thinly stretched federal presence in Canada, an open and commercially active territory in Alaska, and a Danish colony with a state trading monopoly in Greenland.

Commercial changes created new opportunities and new dependencies. Commercial whaling transformed Iñupiat coastal communities and then collapsed, leaving imported goods and cash needs while Iñupiat whaling continued as a community institution. The Arctic fox trade drew Canadian Inuit into a credit economy whose terms were set by distant markets, but trapping and subsistence hunting continued together. In Greenland, sealing and a growing fishery shaped an economy within a Danish monopoly, followed by postwar modernization. Missions brought literacy, new religious life, and early schools and medicine, and Inuit engaged with them selectively.

The Second World War and the Cold War expanded state authority. Airfields, weather stations, and the DEW Line brought infrastructure and employment, and in Greenland the construction of Thule Air Base led to the 1953 removal of the Inughuit community at Uummannaq. In Canada, new welfare programs, family allowances, identification discs, and a central northern department drew Inuit into a system of administration that favored permanent settlements.

Relocations were among the most dramatic expressions of this authority, but they were not all alike. The High Arctic relocations of 1953–1955 moved families from northern Quebec and Baffin Island to Ellesmere and Cornwallis islands, with official explanations of welfare and game, promises of return, and, as later inquiries concluded, sovereignty concerns, and brought severe hardship. The Ahiarmiut experienced repeated moves in the interior. Other relocations were coerced, pressured, or the result of families' own choices under constrained conditions. Evidence for these events includes contemporary records, Inuit testimony, and later commission reports, which must be distinguished from one another.

Health and education reshaped family life. The tuberculosis campaign saved lives and also separated thousands from their families, with deaths away from home and long silences. Federal day schools, hostels, and church-run residential schools expanded access to education while removing children from families and undermining Inuit languages, and in Greenland and Alaska similar tensions arose. The decline of dog teams, through disease, regulation, and the killing of dogs in many communities, together with the adoption of snowmobiles, altered mobility and independence, though evidence shows differing causes and not one uniform policy.

Everyday life in permanent settlements combined new housing, wage employment, imported foods, and schools with continuing hunting, sharing, and kinship. Women's responsibilities, childhood, and the position of elders changed, and communities developed new institutions. Settlement offered services and security to many families while diminishing autonomy, and historians debate whether it is best understood as service delivery, colonial control, or both.

Inuit also built new forms of cultural and political organization. Cooperatives, art-producing communities such as Kinngait, Inuit-language media, and local councils gave Inuit control over new institutions. Abraham Okpik's Project Surname reformed the identification system, and Inuit Tapirisat of Canada, founded in 1971, brought Inuit together as a national political body. In Alaska, Iñupiat established regional organizations and the North Slope Borough. In Greenland, a series of political reforms led from incorporation in 1953 to Home Rule in 1979.

The first Inuit Circumpolar Conference at Barrow in 1977, convened by Eben Hopson Sr. in response to threats to whaling and to the pressures of resource development, brought Inuit from three states into a shared organization. It demonstrated that Inuit political identity extended beyond national borders and that political resurgence had developed through local, regional, national, and international initiatives over many years.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized that Inuit were historical actors throughout. They adopted rifles, literacy, wage work, and snowmobiles when these served their purposes, resisted or reshaped policies that harmed them, and used the experiences of the period as the basis for demands for recognition and self-government. These demands were taken up in the land-claims negotiations and the creation of new governments treated in later chapters, beginning with Chapters 115 and 116.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a people with deep knowledge of their environment and functioning institutions encountered governments with far greater administrative reach, and how that encounter transformed daily life without ending Inuit political existence. The relocations, medical evacuations, schools, and settlement policies were not simply events that happened to Inuit; they were processes in which families made decisions under changing conditions, sometimes with real choices and sometimes with very few.

The chapter also reveals how historians know what they know. Government records, commercial ledgers, commission reports, and oral testimony each illuminate part of the past and obscure another part. The High Arctic relocations in particular show how successive inquiries, drawing on different evidence and standards, reached different conclusions, and how historical understanding itself changes as Inuit voices are admitted into the record.

Within the larger textbook, the chapter connects the reorganization of Indigenous political institutions in Part XVII with the later chapters on land, law, and nationhood. It explains why Inuit political organization emerged when it did and why it took shape locally, regionally, nationally, and across borders. The land-claims agreements and new governments treated in Chapters 115 and 116, and the international cooperation treated in Chapter 143, build directly on the organizations and experiences examined here.

Finally, the chapter illustrates a theme that runs through the whole textbook: continuity within change. Kinship, language, sharing, and attachment to land persisted through the century, and Inuit created new institutions, from cooperatives to circumpolar councils, to defend them. Understanding that history, with its losses and its achievements, is necessary for understanding the Inuit nations and governments that exist today.
