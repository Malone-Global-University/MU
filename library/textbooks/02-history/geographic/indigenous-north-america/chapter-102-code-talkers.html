Code Talkers

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Photographs and operational reports from the Pacific War show Navajo Marines crouched beside field radios, passing the orders of the battalions around them in a language that most of their enemies, and most of their own officers, could not understand. The scene is real, but it is easily misread. The voices on those radios were not simply speaking Diné bizaad; they were operating a trained military communications system.

Between 1943 and 1945, Navajo code talkers served in Marine Corps signal units across the Pacific. They transmitted fire-support requests, reports of enemy positions, orders for troop movements, and requests for supplies over radio and field-telephone networks that the Japanese military could monitor. What made their work valuable was not the Navajo language alone. It was the combination of fluent bilingual speakers, a specially devised vocabulary and spelling alphabet, memorization, rigorous training, transmission discipline, and the integration of these men into Marine units that depended on fast, accurate communication. This chapter treats code talking as that combination, rather than as a story of a language that was simply "unbreakable."

The Navajo program, the largest and best documented of the Second World War, was neither the first nor the only Indigenous military communications program. In October 1918, Choctaw soldiers of the U.S. Army's 36th Division used their language on the telephone lines of the Western Front in France. In the Second World War, Comanche soldiers supported the 4th Infantry Division in Normandy, Meskwaki soldiers served with the 34th Infantry Division in North Africa and the Mediterranean, Hopi servicemen used their language in the Pacific, and speakers of other languages, including Cherokee, Oneida, Chippewa, Muscogee, Kiowa, and Lakota or Dakota, were employed in varying ways. These programs differed in size, procedure, and surviving documentation. Some relied on relatively direct translation; others built specialized vocabulary. This chapter keeps those differences visible.

The historical paradox at the center of the chapter is stark. For decades before the war, federal policy had worked to displace Indigenous languages through English-only instruction, boarding schools, and assimilationist pressure examined in Chapter 87. Many code talkers had themselves been schooled under those conditions. Yet the armed forces of the same government came to regard these languages as a communications asset. Understanding that contradiction requires attention to what the military actually wanted, what Indigenous soldiers actually did, and how Indigenous communities understood both the languages and the service.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines the communications problem, the Choctaw precedent, the history of language suppression, and the Army and Marine Corps decisions that led to World War II programs. The second explains in technical detail how the Diné program was proposed, staffed, designed, and trained. The third follows Navajo code talkers through the Pacific campaigns from Guadalcanal to Okinawa. The fourth turns to Comanche, Meskwaki, Hopi, and other Native communicators. The fifth traces the long history of secrecy, declassification, congressional recognition, and the debate over what it means to honor a language.

The evidence is uneven and must be handled with care. Original Marine Corps and Army documents survive, including recruitment correspondence, code vocabularies, and some operational reports held in federal archives. Veterans' interviews, tribal records, museum collections, and memoirs supplement them. But the wartime record is incomplete, the Navajo program was classified for more than two decades, and decades of commemoration, journalism, and film have layered patriotic legend over documentary history. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what contemporary sources establish, what participants later recalled, and what remains uncertain.

The chapter also asks whose history this is. Code talking was an accomplishment of individual Indigenous servicemen, but it rested on languages that belonged to living communities with their own governments, ceremonies, and histories of survival. The men were not only soldiers; they were citizens of nations such as the Navajo Nation, the Comanche Nation, and the Meskwaki Nation, whose political institutions are discussed across this volume, including Chapter 97. Their story is therefore a story about Indigenous intellectual contribution as well as military performance.

Chapter 101 examines Indigenous participation in the Second World War as a whole, including enlistment, home-front mobilization, and the general course of service. This chapter assumes that background and concentrates on one distinctive form of service. It ends with the questions that follow into Chapter 103: how the nation that had relied on these languages treated their speakers and communities after the guns fell silent.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Indigenous-language communication appealed to military planners and why an unfamiliar language does not by itself constitute a secure code.
  2. Trace the origins of code talking among Choctaw soldiers in 1918 and assess the surviving documentary evidence.
  3. Analyze the contradiction between federal language-suppression policy and wartime military reliance on Indigenous languages.
  4. Describe the Army and Marine Corps decisions that produced Second World War code-talker programs.
  5. Explain how Philip Johnston's proposal, Marine Corps approval, and the work of the first Navajo recruits each contributed to the Diné program.
  6. Distinguish direct use of the Navajo language from coded substitutions, the spelling alphabet, and later revisions.
  7. Evaluate operational reports, including the Iwo Jima communications assessment, as historical evidence with both value and limits.
  8. Compare the Navajo, Comanche, Meskwaki, Hopi, and other Indigenous programs in scale, method, and documentation.
  9. Assess claims that the code was "unbreakable" or transmitted "without error," identifying who made them and what they can establish.
  10. Explain the secrecy imposed on the Navajo program and the 1968 declassification, and distinguish this from the history of other programs.
  11. Trace congressional recognition from 2000 through 2013 and evaluate the difference between honoring individuals and honoring Indigenous communities.
  12. Interpret code-talker history as part of the survival and revitalization of Indigenous languages and the exercise of Indigenous intellectual sovereignty.

Historical Context

Two histories converge in this chapter: the history of military communications and the history of United States language policy toward Indigenous peoples. Each needs a brief introduction.

By the First World War, mass armies had outgrown the communications methods of earlier wars. Commanders relied on couriers, signal flags, telegraph, field telephones, and, increasingly, wireless radio. Each carried a risk. Wire could be tapped or cut by shellfire; radio could be intercepted by anyone with a receiver. Encrypting messages with codebooks or cipher machines protected content, but encryption was slow, required equipment and trained clerks, and could break down under combat conditions. The problem was not merely secrecy. Commanders needed messages to be quick, accurate, and understood by tired people in the field. Any system that improved security at the expense of speed could cost lives.

At the same time, the United States had pursued, since the nineteenth century, policies intended to transform Indigenous societies. Federal Indian boarding schools, many of them operated with federal funds, discouraged or prohibited the use of Indigenous languages and favored English-only instruction; an 1887 order by the Commissioner of Indian Affairs required that instruction in schools receiving federal money be conducted in English. These policies are covered in Chapter 87 and Chapter 88. Their effects varied. In some communities language transmission to children weakened sharply; in others, including the Navajo Nation, large numbers of children continued to grow up speaking their language at home even after school experiences that discouraged it. Indigenous families, elders, and communities maintained their languages through daily life, ceremony, and family relationships.

Indigenous men also had a long and complicated history of military service for the United States, which Chapter 94 examines for the First World War. Many served despite uncertain citizenship; the Indian Citizenship Act was not passed until 1924, and some states continued to restrict Native voting afterward, as discussed in Chapter 93. Native service therefore cannot be reduced to simple patriotism or to coercion. People joined for many reasons, including warrior traditions, family and community expectations, economic opportunity, loyalty to their homelands, and obligations as citizens of Indigenous nations.

Finally, the chapter depends on a distinction in vocabulary. Historians and veterans use "code talker" for Indigenous-language communicators, but the term covers different practices: the direct translation of messages into a Native language, the use of Native words with special agreed meanings, and layered systems with substitution alphabets. Treating all programs as identical would obscure the evidence.

Origins, Language, and Military Communications

The communications problem, the Choctaw precedent, the history of language suppression, and the road to World War II programs.

Section 102.1

Indigenous Languages, Military Communication, and the Problem of Secrecy

A commander in the early twentieth century faced a problem that no amount of courage could solve: how to tell hundreds or thousands of dispersed people what to do, quickly enough to matter, without telling the enemy as well. The telegraph and telephone had made it possible to coordinate armies across great distances, and portable wireless radio extended this to units on the move. Each technology, however, created a new vulnerability. Telephone wires could be tapped or cut. Radio signals traveled through the open air and could be received by anyone with the right equipment and frequency. A message sent in plain language might reveal where an artillery barrage would fall or when an attack would begin.

Militaries responded by encrypting messages. Codebooks assigned words or phrases to groups of letters or numbers, and ciphers transformed the letters themselves according to a keyed system. These methods could be strong, but they were also slow and laborious. A message had to be written out, encoded by hand or machine, transmitted, received, and decoded, often by personnel working under fire and in poor light. Codebooks could be captured. A system that depended on many pages of tables invited error and delay. During fast-moving operations, as in amphibious landings or armored advances, commanders sometimes sent urgent orders in the clear because there was no time to encrypt them. Enemy intelligence services understood this and listened for it.

Against this background, the idea of using a language unfamiliar to the enemy had an obvious appeal. If a message could be spoken directly into a radio by a fluent speaker, understood immediately by a fluent listener, and passed along without elaborate encryption, then speed and security might both improve. Military planners reasoned that few enemy personnel would speak an Indigenous North American language, that such languages had no widely available dictionaries or textbooks, and that their grammar and sound systems differed so greatly from European and Asian languages that even a skilled linguist would struggle to follow them in real time.

Yet this reasoning must be carefully qualified, because it is the foundation of a popular misconception. An unfamiliar language is not automatically a secure code. A determined adversary with access to recordings, native speakers, or captured speakers could in principle learn to understand it, and a language used in plain form transmits its content directly to anyone who does understand it. Security depended on several additional conditions: the rarity of speakers among the enemy, the adoption of agreed substitute terms for military concepts that the language did not name, the use of specialized alphabets for names and unfamiliar words, procedures for passing messages, and the discipline to follow those procedures every time. The most successful programs paired linguistic expertise with deliberate design and training.

Equally important, the people who carried out this work needed to be bilingual in a particular sense. A code talker had to understand English military terminology and tactical situations well enough to render an order accurately, and to speak the Indigenous language fluently enough to transmit it without hesitation. Many who volunteered had grown up speaking an Indigenous language at home and learned English in school or through contact with non-Native neighbors. Fluency in both was an acquired and valuable skill, though neither the Army nor the Marine Corps always recognized the intellectual labor involved.

The military therefore did not simply discover a ready-made secret language. It created, over several decades and in several different ways, communications systems that drew on Indigenous linguistic knowledge and fitted it into military procedure. The remainder of this chapter follows that process, beginning with the first documented instance.

Historical Vocabulary
Code Talker

Definition. A military communicator who transmitted messages orally in an Indigenous North American language, usually using either direct translation or agreed substitute terms for military concepts. The term is retrospective and is now applied to Indigenous veterans of both world wars, though it was not used uniformly during the wars themselves.

Historical context. The earliest documented use occurred in 1918. During the Second World War, "code talker" is most closely associated with the Navajo Marines, but Army programs and other Marine and Navy personnel also used Indigenous languages, and recognition legislation later adopted the term broadly.

Why it matters. The label can suggest that the language itself functioned as a code. In fact, programs ranged from near-direct translation to elaborate coded vocabulary, and each depended on trained personnel and procedures.

Potential misunderstanding. "Code talker" is not a synonym for "interpreter," nor for "cryptographer." Some code talkers invented vocabulary; others used existing terms; all were also soldiers, typically assigned to ordinary units and exposed to the same hazards.

Section 102.2

The Choctaw Telephone Squad and the First World War, 1918

The earliest well-documented American Indigenous-language communications program emerged in France in the final weeks of the First World War. Choctaw soldiers served in several companies of the 36th Division, which had been organized from National Guard units of Texas and Oklahoma. Oklahoma was then home to the Choctaw Nation and other Indigenous nations, and Choctaw men were among the regiments that sailed to Europe in 1918 as part of the American Expeditionary Forces. The division fought in the Meuse-Argonne region during the October 1918 offensives, one of the largest American operations of the war.

The circumstances were difficult. German forces had learned a great deal about Allied communications. American field telephone lines were sometimes tapped, and some messages were intercepted, which in turn allowed German forces to anticipate movements and in some cases to use that knowledge in countermeasures. American officers were also alarmed by apparent German awareness of planned operations. According to accounts preserved in military and later commemorative sources, an American officer noticed that Choctaw soldiers in his command were speaking to one another in their own language and recognized the possibility of using it to pass messages over the telephone. Accounts differ in the details of who first proposed the idea and how it was first tried.

The well-known outline, nonetheless, is documented. Choctaw speakers were assigned to field telephone duty in companies of the 142nd Infantry. They translated military messages into Choctaw and transmitted them to fellow Choctaw speakers at other positions, who rendered them back into English for the officers who needed them. As in later programs, vocabulary posed a problem. Choctaw had no existing terms for many modern military ideas such as artillery, machine guns, or battalion designations, and the soldiers improvised descriptive expressions. The January 23, 1919, Army memorandum itself records descriptive substitutions, including a machine gun rendered as “little gun shoot fast” and battalions distinguished by one, two, or three “grains of corn.” These are contemporary reported examples rather than proof of a complete surviving codebook; the wording and context can be checked against the original memorandum.

The military evidence consists principally of a report written in January 1919 by the commanding officer of the 142nd Infantry, which described the use of Choctaw communications and its effect. The report was written after the Armistice, as part of an official account of the regiment's experience, and it assessed the practice favorably. The source is valuable because it is contemporary with the events. It is also limited. It is an officer's retrospective summary rather than a transmission log, and it does not provide a complete roster or the full technical procedures.

Estimates of how many Choctaw soldiers were involved differ across sources. Many accounts name fourteen men; others cite different numbers depending on how participants are defined and which period of service is counted. The difference reflects incomplete records and the later gathering of names by tribal and commemorative efforts, not necessarily contradictory evidence. This chapter therefore avoids a single exact figure and treats the number as approximate.

Joseph Oklahombi is frequently mentioned in popular accounts of the Choctaw soldiers. A Choctaw soldier in the 36th Division, he received high decorations for combat action in the Meuse-Argonne campaign, and his service is deservedly celebrated. His fame rests chiefly on that combat record. Students should be careful not to merge distinct stories: a person may be a celebrated Choctaw soldier without being documented as a member of any particular telephone squad. Verified service records, unit histories, and tribal documentation are the proper basis for assigning roles.

The Choctaw program was small and short-lived, and it did not lead to a permanent system. It nonetheless set a precedent, for it demonstrated that an Indigenous language could solve a real problem of battlefield communication. Chapter 94 treats the broader story of Indigenous participation in the First World War; here the Choctaw experience matters as the first link in a chain that continued through the Second World War.

Primary Source Window

Report on the Use of Choctaw Communications, January 23, 1919

Creator: Commanding officer, 142nd Infantry, 36th Division · Date: January 23, 1919 · Type: Official military memorandum, preserved in federal archives and reproduced in educational collections

Context: Written weeks after the Armistice, the report summarizes the regiment's wartime experience with Choctaw soldiers who passed messages by telephone during the October 1918 operations.

Paraphrase of the source: The report describes how Choctaw speakers were placed at different points on the telephone network, how messages were conveyed in Choctaw, and how the officer judged that the practice improved the security of communications at a time when German interception was a concern. The wording of the original should be consulted directly in the archival copy.

Analysis: The source confirms that the Choctaw program existed, that it was used in combat, and that the Army regarded it as a successful experiment. It also shows how officers framed Indigenous-language communication: as a practical tool, defined by its military utility.

Limitations: It was written by an officer after the fact, and it describes outcomes in general terms. It does not supply a codebook, a complete list of participants, or independent evidence from German sources about whether any message was successfully intercepted.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the Army command. Choctaw soldiers' own accounts, tribal memory, and the views of Choctaw families appear in other sources.

Section 102.3

Indigenous Language Suppression and the Paradox of Wartime Recruitment

The wartime use of Indigenous languages cannot be understood without recalling how the federal government had treated those languages in peacetime. This chapter does not retell the history of boarding schools, which belongs to Chapter 87 and Chapter 88, but it must recall the key features that bear on code talking.

From the late nineteenth century, federal Indian policy treated the replacement of Indigenous languages with English as a means of assimilation. Officials argued that English instruction would prepare Native people for citizenship and wage work and would weaken tribal ties. In 1887, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs directed that instruction in schools receiving federal funds be conducted in English. Boarding schools, both federal and mission-run, often punished students for speaking their languages. Veterans' recollections, memoirs, and oral histories describe such punishments, and they are consistent with the broader documentary history of the institutions, although experiences differed from school to school and from child to child.

The effects on language transmission were uneven. In some communities, a generation of children returned home with reduced fluency, and the use of the language declined. In others, family life, ceremony, and community institutions sustained it. The Navajo language is a notable example of a language that remained widely spoken by children and adults through the first half of the twentieth century, in part because of the size and geographic dispersion of the Navajo Nation, the strength of kinship and ceremonial life, and the fact that many families lived at a distance from schools. This helps explain why recruiters later found a relatively large pool of fluent Navajo speakers. It does not mean the language was unaffected by assimilation pressure.

When war came, the contradiction became visible. The institutions that had discouraged Native children from speaking their languages were part of the same federal system whose armed forces now sought fluent speakers. Historians describe this as a paradox, but the term must be used carefully. The government did not hold one coherent attitude toward Indigenous languages. The Bureau of Indian Affairs, mission schools, the Marine Corps, and the Army operated under different priorities. A signal officer seeking a secure communications method was not implementing education policy, and may not have known or cared about the history of English-only instruction. The contradiction lay in the larger relationship between a state that sought to displace these languages and the same state's later practical reliance on them.

Indigenous people did not experience this as a simple irony. Families and communities preserved their languages privately and publicly. Soldiers often maintained fluency despite schooling, and some used their languages with each other in camp and on leave. Many Indigenous veterans later described their languages as a source of pride and identity, and some saw their wartime use as a vindication of what their elders had insisted on. At the same time, others recalled the humiliation of having been punished for the same speech that the military later valued. Both responses appear in the sources, and neither should be taken as universal.

It is also important not to portray Indigenous languages as having acquired significance only when the military found them useful. These languages carried law, history, kinship, ceremonial knowledge, and ways of understanding the world long before any general took notice of them. Their value to the armed forces was a narrow, instrumental slice of a much larger body of knowledge. The wartime programs drew on a resource that Indigenous communities had sustained against considerable pressure, and the credit for that sustenance belongs to those communities.

The contradiction also had a generational dimension. In many families the language transmitted at home included geographical knowledge, relationships, humor, ceremony, and the terms of everyday labor. School authorities often treated that fluency as an obstacle to English-language education, while military recruiters later assessed it as an uncommon skill. Code talkers did not acquire a new language for military service; they carried into uniform knowledge sustained by relatives and communities despite institutional obstacles. Consequently, military accounts that celebrate the officers who adopted the idea but omit the language communities leave out a central condition of its success. The historical question is not only what the Marine Corps did with Diné bizaad, but why fluent Diné speakers were available to do it at all.

Multiple Perspectives

What Did Military Use of Indigenous Languages Mean?

The viewpoints below are analytical syntheses of patterns documented by historians and in veterans' accounts, not quotations from individuals.

Military planners: Indigenous languages were a practical resource. Their value lay in speed and security under combat conditions, and the questions that mattered were recruitment, training, and procedure.
Indigenous soldiers: Service could express family tradition, nationhood, and a sense of obligation to the United States and to their homelands. Using the language in the military could also be a source of pride, even for men who remembered being punished for speaking it at school.
Indigenous families and communities: Elders and relatives saw the languages as inheritances that had survived pressure to abandon them. Some regarded military use with pride; others worried about what it meant to place sacred or community knowledge in the service of a state that had harmed them.
Federal education and Indian affairs officials: Their policies had historically favored English, and few thought of Indigenous language competence as a national asset. The military's interest did not immediately change those policies.
Section 102.4

From World War I Experiments to World War II Communications Programs

The experience of 1918 did not produce a standing program. After the war, the Choctaw precedent was remembered in some Army circles but was not systematically incorporated into doctrine. By the late 1930s, however, the strategic situation had changed. War in Europe and Asia was spreading, American forces were expanding, and officers were thinking about radio security. Some reportedly recalled the earlier experiment, and others became concerned about the intelligence capabilities of potential adversaries.

The Army was the first to act in the Second World War period. Beginning around 1940 and 1941, Army signal personnel considered and began to train soldiers who spoke Indigenous languages. Comanche recruits entered training for communications duty; Meskwaki soldiers served in a National Guard division that later deployed to North Africa; and Army records and tribal histories document interest in speakers of Chippewa, Oneida, and other languages. The exact chronology varies by program and by source, and some of these efforts were small, informal, or unevenly recorded.

The Marine Corps took a somewhat different path. Its operational problem was amphibious warfare, in which units landed on contested beaches, advanced rapidly, and relied heavily on radio communications for fire support and coordination. Early Pacific operations demonstrated the danger of insecure transmissions, and Marine officers were receptive to proposals that promised to improve speed and security. This receptivity, as the next division explains, produced the Navajo program of 1942.

These differences should be kept in view. The Army programs typically involved smaller groups attached to infantry divisions and signal companies. The Marine Corps program became a large specialized communications effort with a continuing recruitment pipeline, a dedicated training process, and an evolving vocabulary. The programs also differed in method. Some relied on relatively direct translation. Others, notably the Navajo program, added a layer of agreed substitute terms and a spelling alphabet. The distinction among direct translation, substituted vocabulary, and layered systems is important enough to treat as a theme of the whole chapter.

Radio itself explains much of the shift. In the First World War, telephone wire carried the Choctaw messages; in the Second, handheld and portable radios made voice communication possible across moving fronts, and a great deal of important traffic passed over the air. Radio increased both the possibilities and the dangers of interception. A system that allowed a person to speak a message once into a microphone and be understood immediately by a fluent listener fitted this setting well.

The Army's revival of interest had its own chronology and administrative setting. Its units confronted the problem of intercepted tactical radio traffic while training for large-scale warfare, and some commanders experimented with pairs or teams of Indigenous-language speakers. These developments preceded the creation of the Marine Corps Navajo program and involved different officers, branches, and command structures. The Army's Comanche, Meskwaki, Chippewa, and Oneida arrangements should not be treated as subordinate components of a single Navajo program. Their smaller scale could reflect the size and missions of particular formations, the number of speakers available for a given unit, and command decisions about whether a formal vocabulary was necessary. Comparison therefore requires asking how each program was authorized, who provided instruction, what messages it handled, and which records survive.

Cause and Effect

Why Code-Talker Programs Emerged

Long-term causes

The expansion of radio and telephone communication, the vulnerability of encrypted systems to delay and error, and the persistence of Indigenous languages and bilingual speakers despite assimilation policy.

Short-term causes

The outbreak of the Second World War, concerns about enemy interception, the mobility of amphibious and armored operations, and the example of the 1918 Choctaw experiment.

Immediate trigger

Officers' recognition that a trained group of fluent speakers could transmit secure orders faster than conventional encryption allowed.

Immediate effects

Army and Marine programs recruited Native speakers, developed vocabulary and procedures, and deployed code talkers to multiple theaters.

Long-term consequences

Code talkers became central to later memory of Indigenous service, and their history became a vehicle for broader debates about language, recognition, and sovereignty.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the programs. They required Indigenous bilingualism, institutional support from particular officers, operational pressure, and the training that turned individual fluency into a military system.

The Diné Code Talker Program: Recruitment, Design, and Training

How Diné Marines created, learned, and operated a specialized military communications system.

Section 102.5

Philip Johnston, Marine Corps Recruitment, and the 1942 Proposal

The Marine Corps program began with a proposal from an outsider who happened to know the language. Philip Johnston, a Los Angeles engineer and First World War veteran, had grown up as the child of a missionary family on and near the Navajo Reservation. As a child he learned Navajo, and he later became known as someone who could speak it as a non-Native person, a rare skill. After the United States entered the Second World War in December 1941, Johnston read about the difficulty of battlefield communications and conceived the idea of using Navajo as a basis for military communications.

In February 1942 he approached officers at the Marine Corps' Amphibious Corps headquarters in San Diego. A demonstration took place at Camp Elliott, where Johnston presented his concept to Major General Clayton B. Vogel and other officers, including signal officer Major James E. Jones. According to accounts drawn from Marine records and later reminiscence, Navajo speakers in the demonstration transmitted messages that the officers could not understand and that were then rendered back into English. The officers were persuaded that the idea merited a trial, and Vogel recommended recruiting Navajos for a pilot program.

Several points deserve emphasis. First, Johnston's contribution was the proposal and its advocacy. He recognized the potential of the language and brought it to the attention of the people with authority to act. He did not design the working code. That work was done by the Navajo recruits themselves, as the next section describes. Later narratives that make Johnston the sole inventor of the program overstate his role and underestimate the role of Diné Marines.

Second, the Marine officers' receptivity was not accidental. They were responsible for a force about to undertake amphibious operations in the Pacific, and they had practical reasons to worry about the security and reliability of radio communication. They also considered whether the Navajo language was likely to be understood by Japanese or German forces. Some accounts claim that German scholars had studied Indigenous American languages before the war, which raised concern about security. This claim appears in the secondary literature and should be treated as part of the reported background rather than as a verified fact.

Third, the authorization was cautious. The Marine Corps approved a limited initial recruitment, commonly given as thirty men, to test whether Navajo speakers could develop and use a workable code. The pilot was an experiment, and its success was not assured.

Fourth, recruitment drew on the Navajo Nation's population, which numbered in the tens of thousands, with a large proportion fluent in the language. Recruiters sought young men who were bilingual and met Marine physical and educational standards. Recruitment depended on volunteers, and Diné recruits made individual decisions based on their own circumstances. Questions about the quality of English proficiency, the physical examinations, and the recruits' ages and schooling affected who was accepted.

The contemporary recruitment correspondence reveals that the proposal had to survive institutional skepticism. The ability to speak a little-known language offered no guarantee that messages could be carried reliably between units or that soldiers fluent in Navajo would also understand specialized English commands. Officers raised questions about bilingual proficiency, the time required to train operators, and the number of men needed to provide communications during sustained operations. The Marine Corps did not accept every element of Johnston's reasoning without qualification. The pilot recruitment allowed officers to test the concept under practical conditions; the successful result depended on the recruits' work after enlistment. Students examining the correspondence should notice how the documents talk about Navajo men as prospective military personnel and how much less they reveal about the language makers' intellectual decisions.

Section 102.6

The Original Twenty-Nine: Marine Training and the Creation of the Code

In May 1942, twenty-nine Navajo men entered Marine Corps training. They are commonly called the original twenty-nine, and they became the founding group of the Navajo code talkers. Most were young; many had attended boarding schools, and they came from different parts of the Navajo Nation. They first went through basic training at the Marine Corps Recruit Depot in San Diego, learning the standard skills of a Marine recruit, including drill, rifle handling, discipline, and physical conditioning. They then moved to communications training at Camp Elliott.

Their assignment was unusual. The Marine Corps had approved Johnston's idea but had not specified how the code would work. Under the supervision of Marine communications officers, the recruits were responsible for devising a vocabulary that could carry military information in Navajo. Accounts of this process, from veterans and from Marine records, indicate that they worked as a group, discussing terms for military equipment, units, and operations, and choosing words that were both memorable and distinct. The result was a working code vocabulary and a spelling alphabet, tested in exercises over the summer of 1942.

This collaborative design deserves emphasis because it corrects a common impression that the Marines simply issued Navajo-language messages. The recruits made choices that required knowledge of the language and understanding of military needs. They had to decide how to refer to submarines, tanks, battalions, and airplanes in ways that would be natural to Navajo speakers and unrecognizable to outsiders. They had to settle on words that would not be easily confused with one another when transmitted through noisy radios. These decisions reflected linguistic creativity and judgment.

Individual recruits left testimony about their experiences. Chester Nez, a member of the original group, later published a memoir recounting his schooling, enlistment, training, and combat service; he died in 2014, and he is widely reported as the last surviving member of the original twenty-nine. John Brown Jr. and Carl Gorman, both members of the original twenty-nine, also provided recollections. Gorman became well known after the war as an artist and teacher. Memoirs and oral histories provide valuable detail about daily life, but they were produced decades later, and details of dates, places, and group membership can differ. Careful historians cross-check them against service records and training rosters.

The initial group also included men who were sent to instruct subsequent classes. The Marine Corps found the first tests encouraging and expanded recruitment, so the original twenty-nine were followed by hundreds more. Over the course of the war, the program is commonly said to have included roughly four hundred Navajo code talkers, with some variation in published figures depending on how members are counted.

The training also had a social dimension. The recruits were living in a military world with unfamiliar customs, and many were far from home for the first time. They faced the ordinary difficulties of boot camp and some of the prejudices of the period. Their experience as Native Marines varied, but the shared project of building and learning the code created a sense of common purpose.

A decisive distinction is between the men who entered the initial program and the much larger number who subsequently trained as code talkers. The early recruits took part in developing terms and an alphabet that later classes would have to memorize; instructors therefore needed a stable and transmissible system. Once a term had been agreed, it could not simply be replaced by an individual's preferred wording in the middle of an operation, because a receiver serving in another unit might use a different interpretation. That constraint transformed creative bilingual translation into a shared professional discipline. The expansion of the program depended on recruits teaching those conventions, officers arranging training, and signal sections finding ways to incorporate the resulting operators. The founding group’s service can be credited without treating every subsequent improvement as the work of those twenty-nine men alone.

Section 102.7

How the Navajo Code Worked: Substitution, Alphabet, and Military Terminology

The Navajo program is widely misunderstood because it is often described as if Navajo were simply spoken instead of English. The documented system was more complex. It involved at least three elements: ordinary Navajo used for general content, a vocabulary of specially chosen terms for military concepts, and a spelling alphabet for words and names without designated equivalents.

Navajo is an Athabaskan language, as Chapter 1 explained, with a complex grammar and a sound system that includes tones and sounds unfamiliar to speakers of English or Japanese. In the 1940s it had no widely used written standard, and few non-Navajo people spoke it. These features helped the program, but they were not enough. Navajo had no existing words for many of the things a Marine commander needed to discuss, such as submarines, bombers, or regiments. The code solved this problem by assigning Navajo terms to English military concepts. Birds stood for aircraft, fish for ships, and other animal or descriptive words for equipment and units. A word meaning "iron fish" represented a submarine, a documented example. Such terms made sense to Navajo speakers, who could learn and remember them, and were opaque to outsiders even if they understood some Navajo.

The initial vocabulary is documented by the National Archives as consisting of 211 terms, which was later expanded to 411. The expansion occurred as operations revealed the need for additional terms, and a revised dictionary was issued as the war went on. The vocabulary included terms for units, aircraft, ships, and other equipment, and for months and some places and military situations.

For words that did not have a designated equivalent, such as personal names, unfamiliar place names, or technical terms, the code used a spelling alphabet. Each letter of the English alphabet was assigned a Navajo word whose English translation began with that letter. For example, a word meaning "ant" stood for A, a word meaning "bear" for B, "cat" for C, and "deer" for D. To spell a word, the code talker would say the Navajo word for each letter in turn. The decoder would translate each back to its English meaning and take the first letter.

A significant refinement involved the problem of repetition. Cryptanalysts exploit the fact that certain letters in English appear more frequently than others. If the same Navajo word always stood for E, repeated transmissions might reveal patterns. To reduce this risk, the code added alternative Navajo words for the most frequently used letters, so that the same letter could be rendered in several ways. The program also revised the vocabulary during the war.

The role of memorization is central to this system. Code talkers memorized the vocabulary and alphabet and were trained not to write them down in the field. A system based on memory could be used orally at speed and did not depend on carrying a codebook that could be captured. This was a major operational advantage over many conventional codes. It also placed considerable demands on the training and the people. Code talkers had to master hundreds of terms and perform under stress.

The code's architecture also helps explain why the military cared about repeated letters and standard spellings. A straightforward substitution alphabet can leak statistical patterns: the same plaintext letter appears with a characteristic frequency, and the same repeated plaintext word can produce recognizable sequences. The Navajo program reduced that vulnerability by assigning multiple possible code words to frequently used English letters. A communicator therefore had to choose the appropriate term, pronounce it clearly, and rely on the receiver's exact knowledge of the corresponding English initial. This design was more elaborate than simply translating an ordinary sentence and made constant practice essential. It was not a modern mathematical cipher, but neither was it a casual conversation intended to conceal an order from listeners who happened not to know Navajo.

Historical Vocabulary
Code, Cipher, and Substitution

Definition. In cryptography, a code replaces words or phrases with other words or symbols according to an agreed list; a cipher transforms letters or characters according to a systematic rule. Substitution is the general process of replacing one element with another.

Historical context. The Navajo system combined features. It used agreed substitute terms for military concepts, which resembles a code, and a spelling alphabet in which Navajo words stood for English letters, which resembles a substitution cipher, all conveyed through a natural language.

Why it matters. Calling the Navajo program "just a language" overlooks the substitution layers that gave it much of its security. Calling it "a cipher" overlooks that most messages were conveyed in spoken Navajo with a specialized vocabulary.

Potential misunderstanding. The term "code" is used loosely in popular accounts. Strictly, different programs used different mixes of translation and substitution.

Section 102.8

Training, Transmission Protocols, Code Security, and Operational Readiness

Code talkers trained to perform a specific function within the Marine Corps communications system. Training emphasized the encoding and decoding of messages, accurate transmission over radio and telephone networks, and the discipline of radio procedure. A message begun in English by a commander would be rendered into the code by a code talker, transmitted to a counterpart, and translated back for the recipient. Each step could introduce errors, and the training included repeated drills to achieve speed and accuracy.

Marine units used a range of communication equipment. Portable radios, such as the TBX and the later SCR-300 and SCR-536 sets, were employed in the field; their range, battery life, and susceptibility to interference varied. Field telephones and wire lines were used where possible, since wire could not be intercepted by radio. Code talkers were trained on this equipment and, in the field, worked in pairs or teams at different locations in a network. The security and speed of such networks depended on routine: proper call signs, orderly message handling, and care to avoid giving away information through patterns of traffic.

The advantages of a memorized code were considerable. Conventional encryption required written tables, machines, or clerks, all of which slowed the process and which could be lost. Because code talkers carried the code in memory, they could transmit and receive messages in less time, with fewer materials. Marine officers valued this speed, especially during amphibious operations in which units were dispersed and fast communication affected the coordination of fire and movement.

The system also had vulnerabilities. Messages could be intercepted, and repeated patterns could in principle give cryptanalysts footholds. Voice characteristics could identify transmissions as Navajo even if the content was unintelligible, which could attract attention. Code talkers could be captured, killed, or wounded, and equipment failed. The use of alternate terms, revised vocabularies, and standard operating procedures addressed some of these risks. A frequently cited story concerns Joe Kieyoomia, a Navajo soldier in the Army who was captured by the Japanese in the Philippines and was reportedly questioned about Navajo-language messages; he was not a code talker and, according to accounts, could not make sense of the code. This anecdote illustrates both the interest of Japanese intelligence in the problem and the barrier the code posed, but it remains an anecdote rather than a systematic evaluation.

Students should therefore understand the program as the product of several elements working together: linguistic knowledge, designed substitution, memory, training, and communications discipline. If any element weakened, so did the whole. A code talker without training could not transmit accurately; a trained signal unit without a secure vocabulary could not maintain secrecy; and a vocabulary that was not revised might become predictable. The code's reputation for effectiveness depended on these elements as much as on the language.

Operational readiness also depended on things that a dictionary could not guarantee. The receiving station needed an effective signal, an operator available at the right time, and a command post prepared to act on the decoded message. A radio might be blocked by terrain, weakened by exhausted batteries, damaged by salt water, or unusable when a unit had moved beyond its range. A field telephone offered a different set of problems: wires took time to install and could be broken by movement or fire. Code talkers therefore worked inside broader signal sections rather than in isolation. They needed to recognize call signs, maintain schedules, repeat uncertain messages when procedure required it, and avoid introducing ambiguity. An accurate code word was only useful if the message reached the correct military recipient while the information was still relevant.

Historical Debate

Were Indigenous Languages Alone Responsible for Code-Talker Success?

The question: How much of the code talkers' effectiveness should be attributed to the Navajo language itself, and how much to substitution systems, training, radio procedure, and the military context?

Interpretation A — Language as the decisive factor: Some popular accounts emphasize the difficulty of the Navajo language for outsiders and present it as the reason the code was never broken.
Interpretation B — A system, not a language: Many historians emphasize that the language was necessary but not sufficient. The substitution vocabulary, spelling alphabet, memorization, training, and discipline all contributed to security and effectiveness.

Evidence: The Navajo code dictionary, training records, officers' assessments, and veterans' testimony indicate a layered system. Evidence about Japanese attempts to decipher messages is more limited and often indirect.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ on how much weight to place on the language versus the training, and on how reliable claims of non-interception are. The disagreement concerns interpretation and the limits of the evidence.

Why it matters: If the language alone were responsible, any Indigenous language could be a code. If it was a system, then the intellectual labor of the code talkers and the organization of the program deserve emphasis.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Communications Assignment, from Briefing to Delivery

The following reconstruction is based on documented procedures and veterans' accounts in general terms. It does not describe a particular person or transmission.

Briefing and drafting. A commander or staff officer determined that a message needed to be sent, such as a request for artillery fire, a report on enemy positions, or an order to a neighboring unit. The message was written or stated in English and passed to the code talker assigned to that unit's radio.

Encoding. The code talker translated the message into Navajo, using memorized substitute terms for military concepts and the spelling alphabet for names and unfamiliar words. Speed mattered, and so did accuracy.

Transmission. The message was spoken into a field radio or telephone to another code talker, using proper procedure. In combat, conditions could be loud, the equipment could fail, and the operator might be under fire, carrying heavy gear, or working in a foxhole, on a beach, or in a shelter.

Reception and decoding. The receiving code talker listened, translated back into English, and wrote down the message for the recipient. Errors in listening or terminology could change meaning, so repetition or confirmation was sometimes necessary.

Delivery. The decoded message was handed or relayed to the commander, who acted on it. Fatigue, interrupted sleep, hunger, heat, and fear affected all these steps, and the code talker was also expected to function as a Marine in the unit.

What the evidence shows. The task was technically specialized but inseparable from the dangers faced by other Marines.

Division III · Sections 102.9–102.12

Code Talkers in the Pacific War, 1942–1945

How the code functioned in actual combat, and why rapid, reliable communication mattered in amphibious warfare.

Section 102.9

Guadalcanal and the First Operational Deployment

The first major American offensive in the Pacific began on August 7, 1942, when the 1st Marine Division landed on Guadalcanal and neighboring islands in the Solomon Islands. The campaign lasted into early 1943 and combined sea, air, and land fighting. Guadalcanal was also the setting for the first operational use of Navajo code talkers. The first deployments are generally dated to the autumn of 1942, after the initial landings, and they involved Navajo Marines who had completed training in California and been assigned to units in the Solomons. Published accounts differ on exact dates and numbers, reflecting the incompleteness of early records.

The communications environment was exacting. Units were spread across jungle terrain and ridges with limited visibility. Radio sets were affected by humidity, heat, and rough handling. Japanese forces could monitor American radio transmissions, and Marine commanders were aware that insecure messages might reveal intentions or provide targeting information. Messages concerned artillery adjustment, patrol reports, supply requests, and the movement of units. In such circumstances, an oral system that could be used quickly without a codebook offered clear benefits, even though it was new and untested.

Navajo Marines were integrated into the communications networks of Marine units, typically attached to regimental or battalion signal sections. They worked in pairs or small teams, transmitting and receiving messages and sometimes moving with command groups. The reaction of commanders was mixed at first. Some were skeptical of a new system that they had not seen used, and the code talkers had to demonstrate competence. Officers' later assessments, preserved in Marine Corps correspondence and reports, were generally favorable and encouraged the expansion of the program.

Care is needed about what can be claimed. It is accurate to say that Navajo code talkers took part in the Guadalcanal campaign and contributed to Marine communications. It is not accurate to say that they determined the outcome of the campaign. Guadalcanal was decided by naval and air battles, logistics, intelligence, ground combat, disease, and the endurance of the forces on both sides. The code talkers' contribution was real, but it operated within a much larger effort.

The deployment also marked the beginning of the learning process in the field. Lessons from early use led to adjustments in vocabulary, procedure, and training. For example, units discovered terms that were needed but missing, and gaps were reported back for incorporation into later versions of the code. This feedback between operational experience and the evolving code was part of the program's growth and helps explain the expansion of the vocabulary over time.

For the code talkers, the Solomons introduced the physical hardships of Pacific service: tropical illness, poor food, heavy rain, and the constant danger of ambush and artillery or air attack. Many later recounted that they were treated by comrades as Marines first. The program's reputation grew within the corps as officers noticed its performance.

The timing of the first Pacific deployment deserves care. The Guadalcanal landings began in August 1942, before the newly recruited Navajo Marines could have been present at every opening action. The campaign continued for months, however, allowing later arrivals to take part while the American position remained contested. Describing an individual as having served “at Guadalcanal” does not by itself specify a landing date, unit assignment, or particular engagement. For that reason, retrospective biographies should be matched wherever possible to unit rosters and the chronology of Marines arriving on the island. The distinction does not diminish the service of early code talkers; it prevents their contributions from being folded into scenes in which they cannot be independently placed.

The command network was more complicated than a single conversation between two Navajo Marines. A headquarters might need information from a forward observer, send instructions to a subordinate unit, or report its situation to a higher formation. An Indigenous-language message could move through such a network alongside ordinary encoded traffic, maps, dispatch runners, and liaison officers. The value of code talkers was greatest when the message could be passed rapidly without broadcasting its tactical content in an intelligible form to an enemy listener. But the message still had to be checked, addressed, and integrated into military decisions. This explains why accounts from infantry units, signal operators, and commanders may emphasize different parts of the same communications event.

Section 102.10

Expanding Operations: Bougainville, Tarawa, Saipan, Guam, and Peleliu

As the Marine Corps carried the war across the Pacific, the Navajo program expanded. The campaigns that followed Guadalcanal included Bougainville in the Solomon Islands (beginning November 1943), Tarawa in the Gilbert Islands (November 1943), and later operations in the Marshall Islands, Saipan (June 1944), Guam (July 1944), and Peleliu (September 1944). Each of these operations was an amphibious assault against defended positions, and each placed communications under severe strain.

Amphibious operations created particular demands. Landing craft carried troops across water, often under fire, to beaches where units mixed and lost their formations. Communications between ships, landing craft, beach parties, and forward units had to be established quickly. Radio sets were often soaked or damaged during landings, operators were killed or wounded, and the situation changed minute by minute. Messages included requests for naval gunfire and air support, reports of casualties and enemy positions, instructions for reinforcements, and calls for supplies. In these conditions, the ability to pass information quickly and securely mattered.

Different battles made different demands. At Tarawa, the narrowness of the landing, the reef, and heavy casualties produced communications breakdowns, and accounts of code talker contributions there are less detailed than for some later campaigns. At Saipan and Guam, the larger land areas and longer operations required coordination among several divisions and attached units, with artillery coordination and movement of supplies. At Peleliu, the fighting on difficult coral ridges and in caves produced prolonged, close combat in which communications between small units were essential.

The evidence for individual campaigns varies. The National Archives preserves operational reports for some operations, including Guam, Peleliu, Okinawa, and Iwo Jima, that mention Navajo communications. Other operations are less fully documented, and some assertions about particular battles appear mainly in later commemorative accounts. The wartime record has limits: reports were written for military purposes, were often concerned with broad operations rather than individual communications, and were affected by the conditions under which they were produced. Historians therefore distinguish between participation that is directly documented, claims that are plausible but less directly supported, and statements that cannot be verified.

The program meanwhile grew in numbers, with new classes of Navajo recruits trained and sent to divisions. The code itself was refined. As the war continued, additional vocabulary entries and alternative spelling terms were adopted, a process designed both to meet operational needs and to reduce repeated patterns. Training evolved, and code talkers who had served in combat sometimes returned to instruct new recruits, bringing field experience into the classroom.

The changing geography of the island war created new operational problems. A landing on a narrow coral reef involved open approaches and vulnerable beach exits; fighting in jungle hills placed signal lines behind ridges and vegetation; fighting among fortified caves demanded repeated adjustments of artillery and supply routes. At Tarawa, for example, the initial struggle to move men and equipment across the reef made every communications link fragile. In the Marianas, commanders had to coordinate land forces with ships and aircraft across a much larger battle space. Code talkers participated within systems engineered to meet these conditions, but the evidence for their exact contributions is uneven. The historian's task is to connect a documented assignment to its particular campaign rather than attribute every successful order to the Navajo network.

The geographical spread of the program also increased the demands of standardization. A code talker trained in California might be sent to a Marine division whose other Navajo operators had been taught by a different instructor. If each group improvised terms independently, a code that worked between friends could become unreliable between units. Expanded dictionaries, repeated drills, and instruction in code procedures responded to the problem. A commander's confidence depended on being able to assign operators who had not previously served together and still expect them to understand the same messages. These institutional requirements help explain how the program grew from a small experiment to a resource used in major campaigns without becoming merely an informal language practice.

Section 102.11

Iwo Jima, February–March 1945: Code Talkers Under Combat Conditions

The Battle of Iwo Jima began on February 19, 1945, when three Marine divisions, the 3rd, 4th, and 5th, were committed to the capture of a small volcanic island that the Japanese had heavily fortified with tunnels, caves, and interconnected defensive positions. The fighting lasted into late March and caused very heavy casualties on both sides. Navajo code talkers served in the operation, attached to Marine communications units, and the battle has become the best-known setting of the program.

The initial landings placed unusual demands on communications. Marines came ashore on black volcanic sand under intense fire. Units were pinned down, leaders were wounded, and radio networks had to be established under fire while the assault proceeded. The 5th Marine Division's signal personnel used Navajo code talkers extensively in the first days. According to accounts based on Marine records, code talkers operated continuously, passing orders and information among regiments and between the beach and command posts.

The best-known assessment comes from Major Howard Connor, the 5th Marine Division's signal officer. He reported that six Navajo radio networks operated during the first two days and that they transmitted more than 800 messages without error. This account is widely cited. It is also an officer's assessment rather than an audit of every transmission, and it should be read as a documented military judgment, not as independent proof that all communications in the campaign were flawless. It does show that a senior communications officer at the time regarded the system as exceptionally reliable under heavy pressure.

The operational significance lay in speed and teamwork. Code talkers relayed requests for fire support and reports of enemy locations quickly, and their messages were understood by trained counterparts. They worked alongside radio operators who used other methods, wiremen who strung telephone lines under fire, messengers who carried written orders, and staff officers who decided what to send. The performance of any single method depended on the others.

Students should resist the temptation to attribute victory at Iwo Jima to code talkers or to any single communications system. The battle was determined by the overwhelming Marine and naval force committed, by the courage and endurance of the Marines, and by the tactical decisions of both commanders, in a struggle that cost thousands of lives on both sides. Code talkers contributed to the Marines' ability to coordinate, and that contribution was valued by their commanders, but the broader causes of the outcome were complex.

The fighting on Iwo Jima illustrates the distinction between a communications achievement and a single decisive cause of victory. A message could report enemy fire, request artillery adjustment, identify the position of a unit, or transmit an urgent instruction when the beaches and the island interior were under sustained attack. Each such message participated in a chain of decisions involving observers, commanders, gun crews, and infantry. The Navajo system contributed by permitting some of that traffic to move rapidly and with better protection against enemy understanding. It did not remove delays caused by shelling, damaged equipment, lost contact, or mistaken locations. Serious operational history must place signal work amid those physical and organizational limits.

Provenance matters especially for the familiar figure of more than eight hundred messages. Many institutional histories attribute the figure and an assessment of exceptional reliability to Major Howard Connor, signal officer of the 5th Marine Division. Such an attribution is meaningful evidence of how military participants later characterized the code, but it is not the same thing as an identified log containing eight hundred individually checked entries. National Archives research describes separate surviving wartime Marine Corps reports that mention Navajo use in several campaigns, including Iwo Jima. Students should compare the actual documents with later summaries, paying attention to the dates, authors, commands, and specific assertions. Distinguishing these forms of evidence is compatible with recognizing the importance of the code talkers’ performance.

Section 102.12

Okinawa, Final Operations, and the Human Experience of Code Talking

The final major campaign of the Pacific War, the Battle of Okinawa, began on April 1, 1945, and continued into June. American forces, including Marine divisions and Army units, fought a prolonged campaign against a determined Japanese defense on an island close to the Japanese home islands. Navajo code talkers served on Okinawa, and the National Archives preserves operational reports that mention them. As on earlier islands, they were assigned to signal units and worked in the same conditions as other Marines.

The war's end in August 1945, after the atomic bombings and the Soviet declaration of war, brought the operational phase of the program to a close. Occupation duty followed for some, and demobilization began. Navajo code talkers returned home over the next months.

The human experience of the work extended well beyond its technical side. Code talkers were exposed to enemy fire, often at the front, and they carried radios and other gear in addition to their weapons. Some were wounded, and some were killed. Fatigue, loss of sleep, illness, and the strain of constant responsibility were common. Because a radio could draw fire, an operator might be at special risk. Comradeship within units was important, and many code talkers later spoke of the bonds formed with other Marines.

The responsibilities of a code talker were also broader than the code. They were Marines assigned to signal units and were expected to perform other duties when necessary. Some also served as scouts or in other roles. Interview and memoir evidence suggests that the experience of communicating under fire was inseparable from the dangers faced by all Marines in the same units.

Veterans' memories show different emotional responses. Some described pride in their work, others emphasized the horror of combat, and some were reluctant to talk about the war at all. Many later recounted that they had little sense, at the time, that their work would become famous; they understood that it was classified and that they were to say little about it. These recollections should be read with care: they were recorded decades later, and memory, social context, and the demands of commemoration shape what is told. Still, they provide important evidence on how the work felt to the participants.

A further question concerns how Navajo Marines were treated by others in the corps. The sources include accounts of respect and comradeship, but also of stereotyping and mistaken identity. Some code talkers were reportedly mistaken for Japanese by other American personnel, and some accounts describe escorts for their protection. A popular film later depicted code talkers as accompanied by bodyguards with orders to kill them if captured; the documentary support for such an order is generally regarded as lacking, and the story should be treated as dramatization.

On Okinawa, the scale of the invasion magnified the organizational burden of communications. Marine units operated alongside large Army formations and within an extensive naval and air support structure. Messages had to cross command levels as divisions advanced through difficult terrain, and the long duration of the campaign meant that fatigue and equipment maintenance became persistent problems. Reliable communication depended not only on the code's concealment but on signal plans, assigned frequencies, functioning sets, disciplined transmission, and personnel capable of interpreting messages. A code talker could perform his task correctly and still be unable to complete a transmission when a radio failed or a command post was displaced. The ordinary failures and recoveries of military communications are part of the history, even when individual code-talker logs do not survive.

The personal meaning of such service is harder to recover from unit documents. A Marine officer's report may list that a signal section operated effectively without recording the fear, exhaustion, homesickness, or satisfaction experienced by the men using it. Later veteran testimony can illuminate those experiences, particularly the relationship between Diné identity and Marine service. Yet interviews conducted after public recognition may be shaped by years of storytelling and by the expectations of audiences seeking stories of extraordinary courage. Neither source type should displace the other. Read together, operational records and Indigenous reminiscences allow a fuller account of men who were simultaneously communications specialists, combatants, relatives, and members of a living language community.

Section 102.13

The Comanche Code Talkers and the Normandy Invasion

The Comanche program offers an important contrast to the Navajo program. It was smaller, developed within the Army rather than the Marine Corps, and focused on the European theater. Seventeen Comanche men were recruited and trained for Army communications duty and assigned to the 4th Infantry Division, in the division's signal organization. The Comanche Nation had in earlier centuries been a major power of the southern Plains, as Chapter 50 explains, and its language remained in use in southwestern Oklahoma communities.

The Comanche communications system relied on specialized vocabulary. As with Choctaw and Navajo, the language lacked terms for many modern military ideas, and the soldiers created descriptive expressions. Accounts report, for example, that a word referring to a turtle was used for a tank and that a term for a sewing machine was used for a machine gun, and that Adolf Hitler was referred to by an expression that translates roughly as "crazy white man." These examples come from Army and tribal accounts and illustrate the improvisational character of the vocabulary. The Comanche code was less elaborate than the Navajo code, with a smaller vocabulary, and it did not generally use the same kind of alphabet system.

The Comanche code talkers participated in the Normandy invasion that began on June 6, 1944, when the 4th Infantry Division landed at Utah Beach. A distinction should be made between the seventeen men who were recruited and trained and the smaller number who landed in Normandy. Sources differ on exact counts, with commonly cited figures indicating that fourteen served in Europe and that a smaller group was among those who landed on D-Day. These discrepancies reflect incomplete records and differing definitions of participation.

Charles Chibitty is the best-known Comanche code talker. He served in the European campaign and later became a prominent voice for the recognition of code talkers. On November 30, 1999, the Army’s military intelligence community honored him with the Knowlton Award, and he was widely recognized as one of the last survivors before his death in 2005. His interviews, along with those of other Comanche veterans, provide valuable testimony about the program, including the challenge of inventing words and the experience of working in a combat zone.

After Normandy, Comanche code talkers served in subsequent campaigns in France and across northwestern Europe. Their work, like that of other code talkers, was embedded in the broader operations of the 4th Infantry Division. As in the Pacific, it would be inaccurate to say they determined the outcome of battles, but they contributed to communications in a major campaign.

Comanche military vocabulary provides unusually concrete evidence of Indigenous linguistic ingenuity. Charles Chibitty later explained terms that connected new machines to familiar images: a tank could be called a turtle, a machine gun compared to a sewing machine, and Hitler described through a phrase translated as “crazy white man.” These were not proof that the Comanche language lacked sophisticated concepts; they were practical analogies devised for unfamiliar twentieth-century military referents. They worked only when sender and receiver shared the agreed meaning. Tribal language expertise therefore shaped both the code and the training. The Comanche examples also show why comparing a short substitution phrase with a full Navajo code dictionary requires care: both are codes, but the scale and operating procedures were different.

Utah Beach placed the 4th Infantry Division in a massive combined operation with precisely timed naval, air, and ground components. Code talkers assigned to divisional communications could help pass tactical messages as units moved inland, but the surviving accounts should not be stretched to place every trained Comanche recruit on the landing craft of June 6. Distinguishing the original group of seventeen from the men who reached Europe, and from those specifically documented at Normandy, is a matter of historical responsibility. Chibitty's later testimony gives the program a human voice, while unit records establish the military setting. Later honors—including his 1999 recognition—demonstrate how long formal acknowledgement lagged behind service.

Section 102.14

Meskwaki Code Talkers in North Africa and the Mediterranean

The Meskwaki (Fox) people have lived in central Iowa since the nineteenth century, where they purchased land that became the Meskwaki Settlement near Tama, an arrangement that gave the community an unusual degree of control over its territory. The Meskwaki language belongs to the Algonquian family. During the Second World War, Meskwaki soldiers served in the Army, and some were used as communicators in a National Guard division that deployed to the Mediterranean theater.

The Meskwaki code talkers are associated with the 34th Infantry Division, which participated in the North African campaign beginning in late 1942 and subsequently in operations in Italy. Historical and commemorative sources identify eight Meskwaki code talkers. Their task was similar in principle to that of other Army code talkers: to transmit military messages in their language, using agreed vocabulary where necessary, in order to reduce the risk of interception.

Reconstructing the Meskwaki program poses difficulties. Documentation is limited, and individual assignments are not always clear from the surviving records. Some information comes from tribal sources, family accounts, and later commemoration rather than from detailed wartime reports. Historians therefore have to be careful about specific claims, such as which men performed which tasks at which times. The number eight itself comes from compiled lists and has been repeated in subsequent literature, and it is best understood as the commonly cited number of identified Meskwaki code talkers rather than an exhaustive official total.

Compared with the Navajo and Comanche programs, the Meskwaki program was small and operated in a smaller formal framework. Unlike the Navajo program, it did not develop a large printed dictionary and extended training program, and unlike the Comanche program, it is more thinly documented in the sources available to historians. Nevertheless, its inclusion is important to a complete history. It shows that Indigenous nations in the Midwest, as well as the Plains and Southwest, contributed to Army communications and that the experience of code talkers in the Mediterranean was part of the larger story.

The Meskwaki case begins with a distinctive homeland history. In the nineteenth century, members of the Meskwaki community purchased land near Tama, Iowa, establishing the settlement that remained a center of community life even as federal and state institutions exerted influence. This setting mattered because young men serving with an Army formation did not arrive as anonymous speakers of an exotic tongue. They belonged to a nation with its own political relationships, historical memory, and educational experiences. Language ability was transmitted within that community, not manufactured by Army training. When military accounts omit the settlement's institutions, they risk treating the soldiers' communicative skill as a curious personal trait rather than a collective cultural resource.

The 34th Infantry Division's operations offer a different battlefield geography from the Marine Corps island campaigns. The American force entered combat in North Africa, then participated in the Italian campaign, where rugged terrain, river crossings, and prolonged engagements made communication difficult. Meskwaki personnel associated with the division worked in this Army environment rather than in the Marine Corps amphibious system. Descriptions of the small program should distinguish what records establish about the unit's service from what is specifically established about code use in a particular battle. A soldier's presence in the 34th Division does not demonstrate that he transmitted an Indigenous-language message at every major engagement in which the division fought.

There is also an archival reason for the smaller public profile of the Meskwaki program. Unlike the Navajo Marines, who created a standardized and much-discussed vocabulary, Meskwaki communicators do not appear in surviving materials with an equally extensive technical record. Later tribal recognition and family testimony therefore carry considerable weight. These sources can recover individual names, experiences, and the significance of service within Meskwaki history, but they must be distinguished from a contemporary radio log or Army directive. The scarcity of detailed records is not evidence that a program was unimportant; it is a limit on how confidently historians can reconstruct its day-to-day procedures.

Section 102.15

Hopi Code Talkers and Indigenous Communications in the Pacific

The Hopi people live on the mesas of northern Arizona, surrounded by the Navajo Nation, and maintain villages with long histories, as Chapter 30 describes for the Pueblo world. Hopi is a Uto-Aztecan language. During the Second World War, Hopi servicemen used their language in military communications in the Pacific theater.

The Hopi program is less fully documented than the Navajo program. Sources differ on how many Hopi men were recruited, how many received training in code-related duties, where they were assigned, and how many have been formally recognized. The U.S. Army documents ten recognized Hopi code talkers: eight associated with the 81st Infantry Division and two with Army Air Forces service. Some museum or commemorative accounts mention eleven; that figure should not be treated as an independently confirmed roster without further documentation. Where recruitment, training, assignment, and recognition are distinct stages, each can produce a different count, and it would be misleading to combine them into a single unsupported number.

Hopi code talkers used their language to protect military information in Pacific operations, and their work involved the same elements of vocabulary, procedure, and transmission discipline that characterized other programs. The Hopi language, like Choctaw and Comanche, lacked existing terms for many military concepts, and speakers developed expressions to cover them. The precise vocabulary and operational use are not as well documented in widely available sources as the Navajo dictionary.

Hopi veterans were later recognized by their own tribal government and by federal institutions. The Hopi Tribe honored its code talkers, and they were among the Indigenous nations whose service was acknowledged in the later congressional recognition discussed in section 102.19. The experiences of Hopi veterans, like those of other code talkers, include both the memories of wartime service and the experiences of a long postwar silence before recognition.

The Hopi case also illustrates the importance of cultural context. Hopi language carries religious and ceremonial knowledge, and community concern for protecting that knowledge shapes how language is shared. The use of Hopi in military contexts therefore raised questions that were meaningful to the community, though sources on community debates are limited and must be handled with caution.

The best-supported numerical account comes from U.S. Army reporting about ten Hopi code talkers. Eight served with the 81st Infantry Division, while two additional men were associated with Army Air Forces service. Contemporary or later accounts that cite eleven may apply a different standard of identification, but without an explicit roster they should not override those ten documented cases. The distinction also prevents an error of geography and command: a man assigned to the 81st Infantry Division and one serving in the air forces did not necessarily perform identical communications functions or serve in the same place. Recognition programs can be valuable evidence, but commemorative totals must still be examined for the categories used.

The 81st Infantry Division's Pacific service unfolded within a joint military system in which divisional operations were connected to shipping, aviation, and higher headquarters. The Hopi-language contribution should be explained in that operational setting rather than presented as a duplicate of the Marine Corps Navajo program. Available Army accounts establish the association of eight Hopi men with the division, but they do not provide the same detailed codebook and transmission history available for the Navajo program. A responsible narrative therefore discusses the capabilities of bilingual communicators and the division's needs while reserving judgment on precisely which term was spoken in a specific radio exchange. Students can learn from this difference in documentation as well as from the service itself.

The history of recognition also shows how community knowledge can correct a public record built around one more famous program. Hopi families and tribal institutions preserved accounts of service when general histories of code talking focused on the Navajo Marines. Tribal recognition, followed by attention from the Army and congressional medals, brought additional participants into the wider narrative. Such recognition is most useful historically when it identifies service branches, units, and the basis for counting each person. It is also important culturally: Hopi speakers did not need military approval to make their language significant, even though the wartime program later became a major public symbol of its endurance.

Section 102.16

Other Native Code Talkers and the Diversity of Wartime Programs

Besides the Navajo, Comanche, Meskwaki, and Hopi programs, soldiers speaking many other Indigenous languages served in military communications. The documented list includes speakers of Cherokee, Choctaw (again in the Second World War), Oneida, Chippewa or Ojibwe, Muscogee (Creek), Kiowa, Pawnee, and Lakota or Dakota, among others. Smithsonian and National Security Agency materials identify numerous participating nations and note that practices varied by theater and unit.

It would be a mistake to assume that these programs followed the Navajo model. Some units used relatively direct translation of messages into a Native language, with little additional coding. Others developed specific vocabulary substitutions. The scale of formal training varied considerably, from structured programs with instruction to informal arrangements in which Native soldiers in a unit were asked to transmit messages in their language. Some programs were small, involving a handful of men; others involved larger groups. The theaters of operation also differed: some served in the Pacific, some in Europe, and some in North Africa or Italy.

The documentation for these programs is uneven. In some cases, unit records mention Native-language communication; in others, the only evidence is the recollection of veterans or tribal histories compiled later. Identifying participants is a persistent challenge. Rosters may be incomplete, men served under different assignments, and wartime records did not always note language use. Historians therefore distinguish between documented wartime service and later recognition, and acknowledge that surviving records may remain incomplete. Some people recognized as code talkers have solid documentary support, while for others the evidence rests on testimony.

The diversity of the programs matters for several reasons. It corrects the impression that code talking was a single, uniform practice, and it prevents the Navajo case from standing in for all. It shows that Indigenous nations across the continent, from the Great Lakes to the Plains and the Southeast, contributed. And it highlights the range of Indigenous linguistic knowledge that was drawn upon, with each language carrying its own history and community.

The use of many Indigenous languages should be studied as a network of local programs rather than a single federal project with one standard operating manual. Unit officers might discover bilingual personnel through enlistment records, personal recommendation, or training exercises. They could then organize exchanges between speakers who already knew one another, or assign them to work with signal personnel who understood the unit's military requirements. Whether the resulting practice was documented in written orders depended on military priorities and the size of the unit. This helps explain why the historical record includes comprehensive materials for one program and only scattered references for another. The category “code talker” is analytically useful, but it can obscure different kinds of labor and different degrees of formality.

Naming an Indigenous nation in a commemorative list is not equivalent to identifying all the veterans who used its language or proving that the practice was standardized. Some references describe communications at a particular maneuver or engagement; others relate to formal training for repeated operational use. The difference matters when interpreting numbers. A recognized veteran count may reflect a program's surviving administrative roster, the results of later tribal research, or eligibility rules under federal recognition legislation. It need not equal the total number of speakers who ever helped a military unit communicate. Rather than forcing an exact continental total, the chapter should show students how definitions and sources affect the histories that can be written.

Comparative study also clarifies what was shared across programs. Choctaw, Comanche, Meskwaki, Hopi, Diné, and other speakers brought bilingual competence and relationships with communities that sustained their languages. Most had undergone ordinary military training and faced ordinary risks of war, even when their communications duties were unusual. Yet the languages differed in structure, numbers of speakers, writing traditions, and relationships to federal schooling. The Army and Marine Corps differed in command practices and operational demands. Those differences explain why examples of one language's code words cannot simply be transferred to another program. The common historical achievement was the adaptation of Indigenous linguistic knowledge to specific wartime needs, not a single universal algorithm.

Secrecy, Recognition, Historical Memory, and Language Preservation

The history of code talking beyond the battlefield: classification, declassification, commemoration, and the future of Indigenous languages.

Section 102.17

Demobilization, Secrecy, and the Unrecognized Return Home

When the war ended, Indigenous code talkers returned to their families and communities, in many cases to reservations and rural homes with limited economic opportunity. The broader story of Indigenous veterans' postwar experience belongs to Chapter 103; this section concerns the particular consequences of code-talker secrecy.

The Navajo program was classified. The Marine Corps considered the code a military secret that might be useful in future conflicts, and veterans were instructed not to discuss it. The restriction remained in effect for more than two decades after the war. As a result, Navajo code talkers returned home unable to describe publicly what they had done in the service. Some told their families little or nothing; others shared limited details. The obligation to silence was a heavy one for men who had endured combat and who might otherwise have been celebrated.

The secrecy affected recognition in several ways. Public accounts of the Pacific War seldom mentioned the Navajo program, and veterans did not receive the kind of attention that might have come to them if their work had been known. Families often did not know the details of what their relatives had done. The Navajo Nation and other communities could not fully honor the contribution. And the code talkers themselves could not use their experience in civilian life, for example in describing their qualifications to employers or in telling their own stories.

The secrecy restrictions affecting other programs were different. The Smithsonian notes that Comanche veterans were not subject to the same formal prohibition on discussing their service. Programs differed in their classification, and their veterans' experiences with disclosure therefore differed too. This is a good example of why the Navajo case cannot stand for all. The reasons for the different treatment likely involved the size and nature of the programs and the military's view of their continuing value, though the sources do not fully explain every difference.

Veterans also faced the same postwar conditions as other Native servicemen. Many returned to communities with poverty and limited services, and some faced discrimination in the states where they lived. State barriers to Native voting in Arizona and New Mexico, for example, persisted until the late 1940s, a contradiction that Chapter 93 discusses in the context of citizenship. For code talkers the irony was particularly sharp, since their language had been used to help win the war, yet their civil rights and public recognition lagged.

The classified status of the Navajo program could create an unusual silence within families. A veteran might be able to describe general Marine service, the location of a campaign, or companions without being able to explain the exact wartime function that made his assignment distinctive. A spouse or child could know that a relative had served while learning little about the specialized radio work until declassification. That silence shaped the sources historians later found: family photographs might survive, while public wartime accounts scarcely mentioned the code. The passage from secrecy to recognition was therefore not simply the publication of a technical dictionary. It involved veterans deciding when and how to narrate a previously restricted aspect of their lives.

Secrecy did not operate in the same way for all code talkers, and that distinction explains uneven historical memory. Comanche veterans could discuss their language-based service under circumstances different from those facing Navajo Marines, yet a lack of official secrecy did not automatically bring national attention. A smaller veterans' community, weaker archival visibility, and public concentration on dramatic Pacific-war stories could keep the Comanche or Meskwaki experience relatively obscure. The practical result is that historians must trace recognition along at least two separate lines: the removal of formal restrictions on discussion and the social process through which journalists, museums, schools, and legislators came to listen. Confusing these would oversimplify why the stories became publicly known when they did.

Section 102.18

Declassification in 1968 and the Recovery of Code Talker History

In 1968, the Navajo code was declassified. The decision allowed veterans to speak about their work, and it permitted historians and the public to learn about the program from official records. Declassification did not produce immediate fame. Public attention grew gradually over subsequent decades, as veterans' organizations, tribal governments, journalists, museums, and archivists worked to document and publicize the story.

Veterans played the central role. Navajo code talkers began to speak publicly, to gather for reunions, and to form associations; the Navajo Code Talkers Association was established in 1971. They gave interviews, participated in parades and ceremonies, and in time wrote memoirs. Families also contributed by preserving photographs, letters, and stories. Tribal governments and organizations, including the Navajo Nation, supported commemoration. Museums and archives collected and preserved materials, and federal archives made records available. Chapter 107 describes the growth of national Indigenous organizations that formed part of the setting for such efforts.

Reconstructing the history required comparing veterans' testimony with surviving records. The two kinds of evidence did not always agree on details, and historians had to reconcile them. Official records document organization, training, and some operations; veterans provide the human experience and detail that records omit. At the same time, memory is fallible, and decades of retelling could blend or exaggerate. The most reliable accounts rest on both sources.

Popular culture complicated the picture. Books, documentaries, and films brought the story to wide audiences but often reshaped it. Cinematic dramatizations, such as the 2002 film Windtalkers, invented characters and plots, including the idea of bodyguards with orders to kill code talkers if captured. Historians generally treat such elements as fiction. The challenge for educators is to use popular interest as an entry point while distinguishing dramatization from documented history.

Recognition also developed unevenly. The Navajo program, with its larger size, better documentation, and earlier declassification, received attention first and most extensively. Comanche, Meskwaki, Hopi, Choctaw, and other programs were discussed less widely for many years, and in some cases the participants' names and service were recovered only through the work of tribal historians and families. The imbalance is itself a historical fact that the textbook has tried to correct by treating the programs together.

The establishment of the Navajo Code Talkers Association in 1971 marked an important change in the organization of memory. Veterans could meet not only as former members of military units but as a group whose special work connected them to Diné language and community history. Reunions, interviews, commemorations, and educational presentations made it possible to assemble testimony that had been dispersed among families. These efforts did not automatically solve the archival problem. A recollection recorded decades after combat still required dates, unit context, and comparison with military papers where available. The value of the association lay partly in making such evidence visible and in giving veterans and their relatives greater control over how the history was publicly represented.

The growing fame of the program also introduced a new risk: a single dramatic story could come to stand in for a complex military practice. Accounts describing a code that was never broken sometimes leave out the work of training, repetition, code revision, radio maintenance, and command integration. Films may create fictional incidents because their narrative aims are different from those of scholarship. The task of historical writing is not to replace praise with cynicism but to preserve the distinction between testimony, documented military assessment, and entertainment. Indeed, a more technical and careful account often makes the achievement more intelligible: the men designed and maintained an effective communications system under the operational constraints of war, which was a difficult professional accomplishment in its own right.

Section 102.19

Congressional Recognition, Medals, and the Politics of Historical Memory

Federal recognition of code talkers came in stages. On July 28, 1982, President Ronald Reagan issued Proclamation 4954 designating August 14, 1982, National Navaho Code Talkers Day, a statement of public honor that focused on the Navajo program. Over the following years, state and local governments, military organizations, and museums added commemorations.

The next major step came in 2000, when Congress passed legislation authorizing medals for Navajo code talkers of the Second World War. In July 2001, a ceremony at the Capitol honored the original twenty-nine Navajo recruits with the Congressional Gold Medal; five original members were then living, of whom four—John Brown Jr., Chester Nez, Allen Dale June, and Lloyd Oliver—attended in person; Joe Palmer was represented by his son, along with other families, and silver medals were provided for other Navajo code talkers. The ceremony expressed national gratitude and made the program publicly visible in a new way.

The Code Talkers Recognition Act of 2008 extended recognition beyond the Navajo. The law authorized the awarding of Congressional Gold Medals to tribes whose members had served as code talkers in the First or Second World War, with silver duplicates for individual code talkers or their families. The act reflected the work of Indigenous communities and members of Congress to correct the imbalance between recognition of Navajo code talkers and recognition of others. On November 20, 2013, Congress held a ceremony honoring code talkers from numerous Indigenous nations; the U.S. Mint reported that thirty-three tribes had been identified as eligible for medals at that time.

The medals raise questions about what recognition means. Awarding medals to individual veterans honors their service, but the recipients of the 2008 act's gold medals were tribes, in recognition of the nations whose languages and members were involved. The distinction is meaningful. Recognizing an individual veteran acknowledges courage and service; recognizing an Indigenous nation acknowledges the community whose language and knowledge made the programs possible. The 2008 act's structure thus incorporated an acknowledgment of Indigenous collective contribution.

Recognition also came late. Most code talkers had died by the time of the major ceremonies, and the honors were often received by families. Recognition in itself did not address the broader conditions of Native communities or the continuing struggles over language and sovereignty. The politics of memory also involved the uses of code-talker history in national narratives: it could be invoked to celebrate American unity, to illustrate Native patriotism, or, as many Indigenous scholars and community members have emphasized, to prompt reflection on the history of language suppression and the obligations of the United States to Indigenous nations.

Federal honors also reveal the difference between a law's stated purpose and the practical form of an award. The 2000 legislation distinguished the original Navajo code creators from later Navajo veterans who carried the system into combat. The 2001 ceremony honored a founding group that had already been reduced by death to five living members, four of whom were present. The 2008 statute adopted a broader framework, authorizing gold medals for tribal nations whose members had served as code talkers and silver duplicates for eligible individuals. Students should read the statutory definitions, not just ceremony speeches, because they determine who could be recognized, how communities were represented, and what documents families had to provide.

Medals and proclamations changed the public landscape but could not repair every loss caused by the years of obscurity. Families of deceased code talkers frequently became the recipients of posthumous honors, and tribal representatives carried the history of men who were no longer alive to testify. The 2013 recognition ceremony offered an opportunity to discuss nations that had rarely appeared in mainstream accounts. At the same time, celebratory language sometimes minimized the contradictions of federal language policy or treated Native languages only as instruments of American victory. A fuller civic reckoning recognizes both military achievement and the independent cultural and political value of the language communities whose knowledge made it possible.

Section 102.20

Indigenous Languages, Sovereignty, and the Historical Legacy of the Code Talkers

The history of code talking joins three themes that run through this volume. The first is military innovation: Indigenous soldiers, working with Army and Marine Corps officers, turned linguistic knowledge into a communications capability, and in the Navajo case built a sophisticated system of vocabulary and procedure. The second is the persistence of Indigenous languages despite federal policies designed to replace them. The third is the long, uneven process by which the United States recognized this contribution.

The central historical argument of the chapter can now be stated plainly. Native American code talkers transformed Indigenous linguistic knowledge into a valuable military communications capability at the same time that federal assimilation policies had attempted to suppress many of those languages. The paradox is real, but it should be understood historically. The languages had not become valuable because of the war; they had always been valuable to the communities that spoke them. The war created a context in which an outside institution perceived a narrow instrumental use.

The legacy of the code talkers is therefore bound up with the future of Indigenous languages. Language preservation matters for cultural transmission, political identity, and the functioning of community institutions such as ceremony, law, and education. In 1990, Congress enacted the Native American Languages Act, which declared a federal policy of supporting Indigenous language preservation, and later legislation and tribal programs have supported immersion schools and other efforts, topics that Chapter 123 and Chapter 124 examine. The Navajo Nation, like many others, has established language programs and institutions to sustain its language among younger generations.

A distinction is vital between honoring a language for its wartime utility and supporting its continued use. Commemoration can celebrate the past without securing the future. Many Indigenous languages, including some used in code-talker programs, are endangered, with decreasing numbers of fluent speakers. The code talkers' legacy is honored most fully when it is connected to the work of language revitalization by Indigenous communities themselves, including the control of educational and cultural institutions, a sovereignty-based concern explored later in the volume.

The chapter also connects to the larger wartime transformations of Chapter 101, which examined Indigenous military service, home-front mobilization, and wartime migration, and it leads into Chapter 103, which turns to postwar Indigenous America. Code talkers returned to a nation that had used their languages and that now faced questions about termination, relocation, and self-determination, issues the next chapters pursue. The code talkers' story suggests that Indigenous peoples' knowledge and political status were more important to the nation's history than official policy had acknowledged.

The chapter's technical history therefore has a political conclusion. A government can honor a code talker while still failing to support the schools, families, and tribal institutions that maintain the language from which his skill came. Military recognition is important evidence of service, but language survival depends on whether new generations can learn and use the language in homes, classrooms, ceremonies, local media, and public affairs. The work of dictionaries and community teachers should not be mistaken for a military afterlife of the code: it serves purposes that predated the wars and will continue long after the medals are put away. Historians who explain the source of the code talkers’ knowledge place the communities, not only the military organizations, at the center of the narrative.

The most productive comparison is between two kinds of transmission. In battle, trained operators sent tactical information to someone who had to understand it immediately and act. Across generations, Indigenous families and communities passed on languages that carried relationships, places, moral obligations, and historical memory. Military secrecy rewarded the first form of transmission only when the content remained hidden from the enemy. Cultural continuity depends on the second form reaching new speakers. The contrast clarifies why the story is not simply one of a successful secret code later revealed. It is also a story of sustained Indigenous intellectual work, the partial recognition of that work by outside institutions, and the continuing authority of Indigenous nations to determine what language preservation means.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Language Policy Before 1918 to the Present

What Changed

  • Before 1918, federal policy generally discouraged Indigenous languages in schools; in wartime, the military used them for communications; after 1968, public recognition of the programs grew
  • Secrecy surrounding the Navajo program ended with declassification, and formal federal recognition developed between 2000 and 2013
  • Federal policy shifted, by 1990, to a declared goal of supporting language preservation

What Stayed the Same

  • Indigenous communities continued to transmit their languages through family and ceremony
  • Indigenous soldiers continued to serve and to understand service in their own terms
  • Questions about the power to define the value of Indigenous languages continued to be contested

Pattern. Change was gradual, uneven, and contested, and was most visible in federal policy and public memory rather than in a sudden transformation of all institutions. Indigenous communities experienced greater continuity in their relationship to their languages than federal institutions did in their attitudes toward them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Indigenous military communications programs of the First and Second World Wars, with particular attention to the Diné (Navajo) code talkers. Its central argument is that Native American code talkers transformed Indigenous linguistic knowledge into a valuable military communications capability at the same time that federal assimilation policies had attempted to suppress many of those languages.

Section 102.1 explained why twentieth-century militaries sought secure, rapid communication and why Indigenous languages seemed attractive. It emphasized that an unfamiliar language is not automatically a code: security depended on bilingual personnel, agreed vocabulary, procedure, and discipline.

The Choctaw program of 1918 was the first well-documented example. Choctaw soldiers of the 36th Division used their language on telephone lines in France, and a January 1919 report by the commanding officer of the 142nd Infantry recorded the practice. Sources differ on the number of men involved, and individual fame, as in the case of Joseph Oklahombi, should be distinguished from documented role.

Federal language policy formed the backdrop. English-only schooling and boarding school practices discouraged Indigenous languages, yet many communities preserved them. The wartime use of those languages created a paradox, but one that must be understood as the result of different federal institutions pursuing different priorities, and one that Indigenous families and soldiers interpreted in varied ways.

In the Second World War, the Army and the Marine Corps developed programs along different lines. Army efforts involved Comanche, Meskwaki, and other speakers in smaller groups, while the Marine Corps built the large Navajo program. Philip Johnston's 1942 proposal led to the recruitment of the original twenty-nine Navajo Marines, but the working code was developed by those recruits.

The Navajo system combined ordinary Navajo, specialized vocabulary for military concepts, and a spelling alphabet. The documented vocabulary grew from 211 to 411 terms, and alternative terms for frequently used letters reduced repetition. Memorization, training, and radio discipline were essential. The code's effectiveness was the product of linguistic knowledge combined with military communications discipline.

In the Pacific War, Navajo code talkers served from Guadalcanal through Okinawa. The best-known assessment, Major Howard Connor's report on Iwo Jima, described six networks transmitting over 800 messages without error in the first forty-eight hours. The chapter presented this as a documented officer's judgment rather than independent proof of flawlessness, and it cautioned against crediting code talkers alone with battlefield outcomes.

Other Indigenous nations made distinct contributions. Seventeen Comanche men were trained, and a smaller number landed in Normandy; eight Meskwaki code talkers are commonly identified in connection with the 34th Infantry Division; Hopi servicemen used their language in the Pacific; and speakers of Cherokee, Oneida, Chippewa, Muscogee, Kiowa, Pawnee, Lakota, Dakota, and other languages served in varying ways. The programs differed in method, scale, and documentation.

After the war, secrecy prevented Navajo code talkers from speaking publicly until the code was declassified in 1968. Recognition then developed gradually: a 1982 proclamation, the 2000–2001 honors for the original twenty-nine, the Code Talkers Recognition Act of 2008, and the November 2013 ceremony for other nations. The recognition of tribes, as well as individuals, acknowledged the communities whose languages made the programs possible.

The chapter closed by connecting code-talker history to Indigenous language preservation and sovereignty. Honoring a language for its wartime utility is different from supporting its continued life, and the code talkers' legacy is fully honored only when linked to the work of Indigenous communities to sustain and revitalize their languages. These themes lead from Chapter 101's treatment of wartime service toward the postwar questions of Chapter 103.

Chapter Reflection

The history of code talkers matters because it shows how Indigenous knowledge entered the machinery of modern war without ceasing to belong to Indigenous communities. The men who transmitted messages in Choctaw, Navajo, Comanche, Meskwaki, Hopi, and other languages were soldiers, but they were also citizens of nations whose languages carried histories far older than any military system. Their work was a form of intellectual contribution, requiring creativity, discipline, and memory.

The chapter also reveals how institutions remember. The same federal system that discouraged Indigenous languages relied on them in war, kept the Navajo program secret for decades, and then honored it with ceremonies and medals. Each stage reflected different priorities and different relationships of power. Looking closely at these stages helps students see that recognition is itself a historical process, shaped by who has the authority to speak and to be heard.

The history connects wartime service to the long struggle over language and sovereignty. Chapter 101 explained the broader participation of Indigenous people in the Second World War, and Chapter 103 turns to the postwar world of veterans, demographic change, and new political expectations. Between them, code-talker history shows that Indigenous nations remained distinct political and cultural communities throughout the war years, with their own languages and understandings of service.

Finally, the code talkers' legacy remains open. Many Indigenous languages are endangered, and their survival depends on the decisions and resources of communities and governments today. Remembering the code talkers is most meaningful when it supports the continuing life of the languages they spoke, not only their past usefulness. That is the larger lesson this chapter offers for the textbook's continuing story of Indigenous nations.
