Mexico, Texas, and the Indigenous Borderlands

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Between 1821 and 1845 three kinds of sovereignty overlapped across the lands between the Arkansas River and the Rio Grande: the claims of a new Mexican republic, the claims of Anglo-American settlers who first lived under Mexican law and then created a republic of their own, and the continuing authority of Indigenous nations that governed, traded, fought, and negotiated across the same ground. Indigenous nations exercised extensive authority outside many Mexican and Texan settlement enclaves, although the location and extent of effective control varied across the region.

A map of Mexico published in London in 1834 by the cartographer John Arrowsmith, represented by a photostat in the Texas State Archives map catalog, shows provinces, roads, presidios, and Indigenous settlements in neat lettering. Maps of this kind communicated confidence. They divided the continent into jurisdictions and implied that a province named on paper was a province governed in fact. Across much of the territory they depicted, that confidence was misplaced. Comanche, Apache, Kiowa, Wichita-affiliated, Caddo, Tonkawa, and other Indigenous nations determined where travelers could safely go, which towns could keep their herds, which trade routes remained open, and which settlements could expand. A map showing political claims does not show who could enforce them.

On March 19, 1840, a delegation of Comanche leaders, accompanied by women and children, came to San Antonio to discuss peace with officials of the Republic of Texas, which had declared independence from Mexico in 1836 and which claimed, on paper, a territory reaching to the Rio Grande. The council that followed ended in the killing of a number of Comanche leaders inside the council house and a street fight in which more Comanche and several Texans died. Within five months, a Comanche expedition rode hundreds of miles to the Gulf coast and plundered the port of Linnville. Both events occurred on ground that no government could claim to control, and both are best understood as episodes in a contest among political systems rather than as a single "frontier" story told from one side.

This chapter continues Part XII of Volume VI, moving the textbook's attention from the Florida wars of Chapter 67 to a different geopolitical environment. The question it asks is how Indigenous nations exercised military, economic, and diplomatic power across the Mexican and Texan borderlands between Mexican independence and the annexation of Texas by the United States, and how their actions shaped the region that the United States inherited. The answer developed here emphasizes four points. Indigenous nations held substantial political and military authority. Mexican independence transformed, but did not eliminate, the frontier relationships inherited from Spain. Texas colonization introduced a new and rapidly expanding competitor for land. And the political geography of 1845 was the product of Indigenous decisions as much as of the decisions of capitals.

The chapter treats the region as an interconnected system, not as a series of national frontiers. Comanche bands traded in New Mexico, hunted on the southern Plains, and raided deep into Chihuahua, Durango, and Coahuila. Apache communities negotiated with Mexican presidios, traded with Pueblo and Hispano neighbors, and sometimes fought the same Mexican states that other Apache groups supplied. Cherokee, Shawnee, Delaware, Kickapoo, and other migrants displaced from the United States built new settlements in East Texas and sought recognition from whichever government would grant it. Texas colonists, Mexican officials, and Indigenous leaders each interpreted the same agreements differently. Modern boundaries between the United States and Mexico, or between Texas and Oklahoma, are of limited use for describing such a world.

The evidence for this history is uneven and the chapter treats it carefully. Mexican provincial reports, presidio records, Texas government papers, treaty texts, military reports, settler memoirs, newspapers, and travelers' accounts survive in quantity, but nearly all were written by non-Indigenous observers with particular interests. Comanche, Apache, Cherokee, and other Indigenous perspectives survive more thinly, in the words of leaders recorded by translators, in later oral histories, in ledger and pictographic art, and in the archaeological and ecological record. Historians must read these sources against one another and must say plainly where the Indigenous voice is mediated or missing. Period terms such as "savage," "hostile," and "barbarian" appear throughout the documents; this chapter identifies them as the language of their authors and does not adopt them.

The chapter also keeps to its place in the book's division of labor. The origins of Comanche power belong to Chapter 50, Apache migrations to Chapter 51, and the removal of southeastern nations to Chapter 66. The United States–Mexico War of 1846–1848 and the territorial transfers that followed belong to Chapter 69. Chapter 68 therefore ends at the threshold of that war, with annexation, unresolved boundaries, and the continuing authority of Indigenous nations over much of the region.

  4. Identify the independent interests of Kiowa, Wichita-affiliated, Caddo, Tonkawa, Lipan, and migrant Indigenous nations in the borderlands.

Historical Context

The borderlands of 1821 were the product of three centuries of Spanish colonial policy and more than a century of Indigenous political change. By the late eighteenth century, New Spain's northern frontier consisted of a thin and uneven line of presidios (garrison posts), missions, ranches, mining towns, and villages stretching from California and Sonora through Chihuahua, New Mexico, Coahuila, and Texas. The Bourbon reforms of the 1770s reorganized this line, and the Reglamento of 1772 sought to place presidios at regular intervals. In practice the frontier remained a patchwork of settlements separated by large areas in which Indigenous nations held the initiative. Chapter 42 examined this world in detail.

The central change of the eighteenth century had been the rise of equestrian Indigenous power. Chapters 49 and 50 traced how horses, trade, and warfare allowed Comanche communities to dominate the southern Plains, to displace Apache and other rivals, and to build a commercial network extending from New Mexico and Texas to the Mississippi Valley. Spanish officials in New Mexico and Texas, unable to defeat the Comanche, negotiated with them. The agreements of the mid-1780s, including the New Mexico peace of 1786, introduced a diplomatic system that recognized Comanche leaders as political counterparts, exchanged gifts, and regulated trade. Spain also adopted a related "peace establishment" policy toward some Apache communities, settling them near presidios and providing rations. Chapter 51 and Chapter 55 discuss these developments from Apache and continental perspectives.

The northern frontier also faced pressure from the east. The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 brought the United States to the borders of Spanish Texas, and filibustering expeditions, including the Gutiérrez–Magee expedition of 1812–1813, attempted to challenge Spanish rule. The Adams–Onís Treaty, signed in 1819 and ratified in 1821, established a boundary between Spanish and United States claims along the Sabine, Red, and Arkansas rivers. That agreement was negotiated between two states; the Comanche, Wichita, Caddo, and other nations whose homelands lay on or beyond those rivers were not consulted. Spanish Texas itself remained thinly settled, with a Tejano population of only a few thousand.

Spanish rule ended in 1821 after the Mexican independence movement united insurgents and former royalists around the Plan of Iguala. The new state inherited a northern frontier in fiscal and military difficulty, a mining economy damaged by a decade of war, and a diplomatic system that depended on regular payments to Indigenous partners. How that inheritance held, strained, and broke is the subject of the first half of this chapter. Students should remember that Indigenous nations did not enter the nineteenth century as passive neighbors of Spain and Mexico. They had built the commercial and diplomatic systems that Spanish and Mexican officials were trying to preserve.

Section 68.1

The Indigenous Borderlands after Mexican Independence

The independent Mexican state that emerged in 1821 inherited territorial claims across the northern provinces of New Spain; it became a federal republic after the overthrow of the First Mexican Empire. After the abdication of the short-lived empire of Agustín de Iturbide in 1823, the Constitution of 1824 created a federal republic of states and territories. In the north, the principal units were the state of Chihuahua; the state of Sonora and Sinaloa (known for a time as the Estado de Occidente, then divided in 1830); the state of Coahuila y Texas; the state of Durango; and the federal territory of Nuevo México, with Nuevo León and Tamaulipas to the east. These were jurisdictions on paper. Their capitals, garrisons, and tax collectors reached only a fraction of the territory the maps assigned to them.

The distinction that governs this chapter is between claimed and effective authority. A government exercises effective authority when it can collect revenue, enforce its laws, move troops, protect its settlers, and regulate commerce. By that standard, Mexico governed towns and their immediate surroundings, the roads between them when those roads were guarded, and mining districts that could pay for protection. It did not govern the Llano Estacado (Staked Plains), the Edwards Plateau, the upper Red and Brazos drainages, the Apache mountain ranges of the Sierra Madre and the Gila country, or the buffalo ranges that supplied the Comanche economy. Those regions were governed, in the sense of being organized by recognized leaders, enforceable customs, and political relationships, by Indigenous nations.

The same distinction applies to Texas after 1836. The Republic of Texas claimed a boundary at the Rio Grande from its source to its mouth, a claim that included Santa Fe and most of the Indigenous lands examined in this chapter. In reality the Republic's settlements lay along the lower Colorado, Brazos, and Trinity rivers, in the Nacogdoches district of East Texas, and around San Antonio. Beyond that line lay Comanchería. Mexico, for its part, never recognized Texan independence in this period. The overlapping claims of two states, neither of which could enforce them, left Indigenous nations free to negotiate with either or both.

Modern borders provide a poor framework for this period. The Rio Grande boundary between the United States and Mexico was not fixed until 1848 and the present Texas–Oklahoma boundary on the Red River was a line between competing claims in 1821. Comanche bands traveled and traded across all of these boundaries. The Apache homelands of the Gila, the Mimbres, the Sierra Madre, and the Big Bend region spanned what are now two countries and four states. Cherokee, Shawnee, and Delaware migrants crossed the Sabine from Louisiana and Arkansas Territory without regard for the line drawn by the Adams–Onís Treaty. Historians of the region have therefore adopted the term "borderlands" to describe a zone of interaction rather than a line of separation.

  • Rivers and corridors: the Rio Grande, Pecos, Red, Arkansas, Brazos, and Colorado rivers, and the Santa Fe Trail, which opened after 1821 and joined Missouri to New Mexico.

Section 68.2

Comanchería: Indigenous Power across the Southern Plains

Comanche power in the early nineteenth century rested on a combination of mobility, ecology, commerce, and military organization that Chapter 50 traced to its origins. Comanche people, who call themselves Numunuu, the People, organized themselves into a number of autonomous bands. Historians commonly identify the Penateka (often translated "Honey Eaters" or "Wasps"), who ranged across the southern portion of the Plains toward central Texas; the Nokoni, associated with the middle Plains and Texas; the Kotsoteka ("Buffalo Eaters"); the Yamparika ("Root Eaters"), who lived further north toward the Arkansas River; and later the Quahadi (Kwahadi), whose prominence on the Llano Estacado grew in the middle of the century. The names, memberships, and ranges of these groups shifted, and sources of the 1820s–1840s do not always agree on which band is meant. Each band had its own leaders, and cooperation among them depended on kinship, shared interests, and persuasion.

The Comanche economy was built on the horse and the bison. Horses provided mobility for hunting, warfare, and trade; they also functioned as wealth, as a medium of exchange, and as a form of social capital that allowed men to marry, to give gifts, and to gain followers. The southern Plains offered large herds of bison, and Comanche women processed hides, meat, and tallow for consumption and trade. The pasture and water required by very large horse herds also created ecological pressures, including competition for sheltered winter river bottoms, that scholars argue affected Comanche decisions in this period. Population estimates for early nineteenth-century Comanche communities range widely, from perhaps ten thousand to well over twenty thousand, and epidemics in the first half of the century reduced those numbers.

Commerce linked the Comanche to several systems of exchange. In New Mexico, Comanche traders attended fairs at Pecos, Taos, and other communities, exchanging bison products and horses for agricultural goods, metal tools, and textiles. Hispano traders known as comancheros and bison hunters known as ciboleros carried this commerce out onto the Plains. In the east, Comanche, Kiowa, and Wichita-affiliated traders dealt with American traders on the Red and Arkansas rivers. In 1834 a United States dragoon expedition from Fort Gibson attempted to contact the Comanche and the Wichita, and a treaty was made with representatives of several nations at Camp Holmes in 1835. These agreements are important chiefly as evidence that the United States, Mexico, and Texas all dealt with the Comanche as a political counterpart.

The Comanche also conducted raids. Raiding was a regular feature of the Plains economy in which young men gained prestige and wealth, communities acquired livestock and captives, and leaders demonstrated their capacity to protect their followers. It was not the only mode of Comanche external relations, and a Comanche band that raided one settlement could trade with another and negotiate with a third. The decision to raid, trade, or negotiate with a specific community depended on a leader's circumstances and his reading of the local balance of power. Later sections show how these decisions changed in response to Mexican weakness, Texan aggression, and shifting ecological conditions.

Historians use the term "Comanche Empire" to describe the scale and reach of this system, a usage popularized by Pekka Hämäläinen's 2008 study. The term emphasizes that Comanche power was not marginal to the regional order but central to it, and that Comanche leaders shaped the policies of Spanish, Mexican, Texan, and American governments. It does not mean that the Comanche had a centralized state, a permanent capital, a bureaucracy, or fixed borders, and the concept remains debated (see the Historical Debate in Section 68.10). Chapter 68 adopts the more cautious word "Comanchería" for the homeland and zone of influence, and discusses the debate explicitly.

Historical Vocabulary
Comanchería

Definition. A term used by Spanish and Mexican officials and later by historians for the homeland of the Comanche on the southern Plains and for the wider region within which Comanche political and commercial influence operated.

Historical context. Spanish documents used the word to describe the lands where Comanche bands lived and hunted, usually without precise boundaries. Modern scholars use it to denote a region whose edges shifted with the seasons, with alliances, and with the movement of bison and horses.

Why it matters. The term acknowledges that large parts of what governments claimed were Indigenous homelands in which Comanche leaders held effective authority.

Potential misunderstanding. Comanchería was not a nation-state with fixed borders and a central government. Comanche bands were autonomous, and the limits of the region varied from year to year.

Section 68.3

Apache Nations and the Northern Mexican Frontier

The term "Apache" is a Spanish rendering that covers a family of related Athabaskan-speaking peoples who call themselves by a variety of names, including Ndé and Indé. As Chapter 51 explained, these peoples migrated into the Southwest and the southern Plains in earlier centuries. By the 1820s they lived in a series of distinct communities spread across a very large territory. Treating them as a single political body, or assuming that the actions of one group represented all, is among the most common errors in the older literature and is rejected here.

The principal groups relevant to this chapter are the following. The Chiricahua, living in the mountains and valleys of southwestern New Mexico, southeastern Arizona, and northern Sonora and Chihuahua, comprised several local groups, which anthropologists commonly call Chihenne (Warm Springs or Mimbres), Chokonen, Nednhi, and Bedonkohe. The Western Apache lived in the mountains of central and eastern Arizona. The Mescalero lived between the Rio Grande and the Pecos, in the mountains of southern New Mexico and western Texas. The Jicarilla lived in the mountains and plains of northern New Mexico and southern Colorado. The Lipan, historically a Plains-oriented group, lived in the southern Plains and the Texas hill country and the lands along the Rio Grande. The Kiowa Apache (Plains Apache), discussed in Section 68.4, lived in association with the Kiowa. These designations are conventions; Apache communities themselves organized around local groups and bands under leaders whose authority rested on reputation and kinship.

Apache economies were varied. Many Apache communities hunted and gathered, harvesting agave, mesquite, and other plants and hunting deer and bison where available. Some, including parts of the Jicarilla, Mescalero, and some Chiricahua communities, practiced some farming in suitable locations. Apache communities also acquired livestock, including horses, mules, and cattle, through trade, through raiding, and through arrangements with presidios. Kinship structured political life: extended families, led by respected men and women, formed local groups, and these groups cooperated in some circumstances, including defense, ceremonies, and raiding. Leadership was situational, and no Apache individual spoke for all Apache communities.

Relations with Spanish and Mexican settlements varied accordingly. In the late eighteenth century, Spanish officials in Chihuahua and Sonora established peace establishments (establecimientos de paz), settling Apache communities near presidios such as Janos, Fronteras, and San Buenaventura, and issuing rations of food, tobacco, clothing, and sometimes liquor. Some Chiricahua and Mescalero leaders accepted these arrangements, which gave them access to supplies and security. The arrangements also allowed Spanish officials to monitor Apache movements and to use some Apache auxiliaries as scouts. Other Apache groups, notably some Western Apache and the Nednhi of the Sierra Madre, stood outside the system, while others entered and left it according to circumstances. Mexican independence therefore inherited a set of pacts, not a single policy.

The legacy of these arrangements shaped the following decades. A peace establishment could last as long as rations arrived and Apache communities saw an advantage in staying; when supplies faltered, Apache communities could move away, resume other ways of obtaining goods, or join groups that had never accepted the arrangement. The next sections show that this was precisely what began to happen in the late 1820s and 1830s.

The use of the collective name Apache can also hide a practical problem that confronted Mexican officials: the people they met at a presidio could not necessarily guarantee the behavior of relatives or neighboring groups who had not attended the council. A treaty negotiated with one local leader might reduce violence along one road while leaving another valley exposed to raids. Conversely, the violation of a promise by one garrison did not necessarily cause every Apache community to abandon diplomacy. This uneven political geography is important for evaluating reports that announced an "Apache peace" or an "Apache uprising." Such language often described the limited experience of a single official or district. Historians must identify the parties, place, and duration of an agreement before interpreting its significance.

Apache political autonomy also shaped the economic choices available to households. A family might seek supplies from a peace establishment, move seasonally through familiar gathering and hunting territory, exchange animals with nearby settlements, or join an expedition organized by relatives. Those choices depended on access to land and water, the safety of travel routes, the location of kin, and the reliability of government commitments. Women and elders as well as prominent male negotiators bore the consequences when a settlement was attacked or a ration agreement failed. Treating negotiations only as decisions by military leaders would therefore miss the social system on which Apache diplomacy depended. The later history of conflict cannot be explained simply as a contest between governments and undifferentiated raiders.

Section 68.4

Kiowa, Wichita, Caddo, Tonkawa, Lipan, and Other Borderlands Nations

The borderlands were not a contest between two or three great powers. They were a mosaic of nations whose interests overlapped but did not coincide, and whose decisions affected the balance of the region. The nations discussed here were neither interchangeable nor merely subordinate to larger neighbors.

The Kiowa, a Plains people, had migrated from the northern Plains and by the early nineteenth century dominated the country along the upper Arkansas and Red rivers. After a period of conflict in the eighteenth century, the Kiowa and the Comanche made peace in the first decade of the nineteenth century, and the alliance became one of the structural features of the southern Plains. The Kiowa Apache, a small Athabaskan-speaking community, lived with the Kiowa and participated in their councils and ceremonies. Kiowa and Comanche communities often traveled, raided, and traded together, though each retained its own leaders and made its own decisions. In 1840, near Bent's Fort on the Arkansas, the Kiowa and Comanche made peace with the Cheyenne and Arapaho in an agreement sometimes called the Great Peace of 1840, which ended a long conflict and altered the balance of Plains diplomacy.

The Wichita-affiliated peoples, including the Waco, Tawakoni, Kichai, and Taovaya, were semi-sedentary farmers and traders who lived in villages along the Red, Brazos, and Trinity rivers and their tributaries. Their towns, with grass houses, agricultural fields, and a regional trading system, had been crucial nodes of exchange between the Plains and the Mississippi Valley since the eighteenth century. The Wichita-affiliated communities often allied with the Comanche, who were important customers and partners. Their population, however, had been reduced by disease and warfare, and by the 1830s they were under pressure from both Texan settlement and other Indigenous migrants.

The Caddo, comprising the Hasinai in East Texas and the Kadohadacho in the Red River country, were agricultural peoples with a long history of political organization, trade, and diplomacy. Their confederacies had dealt with the Spanish, French, and Americans for more than a century. After the Louisiana Purchase and the Adams–Onís Treaty, Caddo communities lost land to American settlement; during the 1830s many Caddo remained in East Texas and became parties to Texan treaties. The Tonkawa, a hunting and gathering people of central Texas, were weakened by disease and by conflicts with larger neighbors, and by the late 1830s and 1840s they allied with the Texans against the Comanche. The Lipan Apache, once a major power on the Plains, had been pushed southward and by the 1830s were a small, vulnerable community along the lower Rio Grande and the Texas hill country. Lipan leaders sought alliances with the Texans against the Comanche and the Mexicans, and some Lipan served as scouts and auxiliaries.

Eastern Indigenous migrants added still another layer. The Alabama and Coushatta, southeastern peoples, had settled in East Texas in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and lived near Spanish and Mexican jurisdictions. Delaware, Shawnee, Kickapoo, and Cherokee communities, many displaced by American expansion, established settlements in East Texas and along the Brazos and Trinity rivers (Section 68.11). Each of these nations had its own diplomatic strategy. Some, such as the Tonkawa and the Lipan, sought Texan allies against the Comanche. Others, including the Wichita-affiliated peoples, tried to balance commerce with the Comanche and relations with Texas. The Caddo, Alabama, Coushatta, and migrant nations tried to retain land and avoid being drawn into the larger wars. Historians reading the surviving documents must therefore ask which nation, which community, and which leader is speaking in each record.

Differences in subsistence systems affected the risks that each nation faced. Wichita-affiliated and Caddo villages depended on cultivated fields, reliable access to rivers, and the ability to maintain regional trade. Their homes and stores of food could be targeted by settlers or rival war parties, and the loss of a village site threatened both harvests and political relationships. More mobile Plains hunting communities faced different vulnerabilities, including the loss of horses, bison access, and routes to trading partners. Neither way of life guaranteed security. Political decisions that appear inconsistent in surviving reports—making peace with Texans in one year, seeking another alliance in the next—often responded to these distinct material conditions rather than to a lack of coherent policy.

The political relationships among smaller nations were also consequential in their own right. Caddo, Tonkawa, Wichita-affiliated, and migrant communities supplied information, intermediaries, trading contacts, and sometimes fighters to neighboring powers. Officials in Texas and Mexico frequently wrote about them as potential allies or obstacles, but such labels reveal the officials' objectives more clearly than the nations' own. A Tonkawa leader negotiating with Texas, for example, was not thereby committing all Indigenous peoples of central Texas to a common cause. Nor did commerce with Comanche parties erase disagreements over land, captives, or access to hunting grounds. A continent-centered history must reconstruct these overlapping relationships before describing any resulting coalition as unified.

Division II — Mexican Independence and the Transformation of the Frontier
Section 68.5

Mexico's Independence in 1821 and the Reorganization of Northern Government

Mexican independence was achieved through a negotiated settlement, not a clean revolution. The Plan of Iguala, announced in February 1821 by Agustín de Iturbide, united insurgents and royalist officers around three guarantees: independence, the primacy of the Catholic Church, and the union of Americans and Europeans. The Treaty of Córdoba in August 1821, signed by Agustín de Iturbide and the Spanish official Juan O'Donojú, acknowledged Mexican independence on the scene; Spain's government rejected its validity and did not formally recognize Mexican independence until 1836. The Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City on September 27, 1821. The northern provinces joined the new state with little fighting. For the garrisons and settlements of the north, the change meant new flags and new oaths but, initially, few new resources.

The political arrangements that followed were unstable. Iturbide proclaimed himself emperor in 1822 and was overthrown in 1823. The Constitution of 1824 established a federal republic in which states held substantial authority, and for the next decade Mexico's political life was dominated by contests between federalists, who favored state autonomy, and centralists, who favored a stronger national government. These contests, which culminated in the centralist constitutions of 1835 and 1836, had direct consequences in the north, because they changed who paid the garrisons, who appointed local officials, and how much autonomy Texas, Coahuila, Chihuahua, and Nuevo México enjoyed. Section 68.12 returns to the centralist turn as a cause of the Texas Revolution.

The northern frontier also lacked fiscal resources. The wars of independence had damaged mining and agriculture, and the new government inherited debts and a weak tax system. The presidial companies, which had been supported by the Spanish crown, now depended on state and national budgets that were often in arrears. Soldiers were paid irregularly, equipment decayed, and many companies were under strength. A presidial reglamento issued in 1826 attempted to reorganize frontier defense, but the money to carry it out was often unavailable. The result was a frontier in which official claims and actual capacity diverged more widely than they had under Spain.

Indigenous nations evaluated these changes in practical terms. For Comanche, Apache, and other leaders, what mattered was whether Mexican officials would continue to supply gifts, trade goods, and rations, whether they would keep their commitments, and whether they could threaten effective retaliation. Their assessments differed from place to place. In New Mexico, where Hispano and Pueblo communities were numerous and trade with the Comanche was well established, the transition was relatively smooth. In Chihuahua and Sonora, where peace establishments depended on regular payments, the effects of fiscal weakness were more pronounced. In Texas, where a small Tejano population and a thin military presence faced both Comanche and migrant Indigenous communities, officials turned to immigration as a solution to their security problem (Section 68.7). Indigenous leaders negotiated independently with regional authorities and often did not distinguish between a change of government in Mexico City and a change in the local balance of power.

Section 68.6

Presidios, Peace Establishments, and the Breakdown of Frontier Diplomacy

Spanish frontier policy in the late eighteenth century had combined military force with diplomacy. The presidio system provided garrisons; the gift system provided annual presents to Indigenous leaders as a way of cementing alliances; the ration system fed Apache communities settled near presidios; and trade regulations channeled commerce through authorized posts. Spanish officials understood these arrangements as a means of keeping the peace at a lower cost than continuous war. Indigenous leaders understood them as relationships in which gifts expressed respect and obligation, and in which failure to deliver them could signal a breach.

Independence strained each element of this system. The cost of rations and presents fell on state and national treasuries that were unable to meet them. Officers and soldiers who went unpaid were reluctant to risk their lives or to expend scarce resources on diplomacy. The turnover of governors and commandants in a period of political instability meant that agreements made by one officer were not always honored by his successor. The expulsion of Spanish-born residents from Mexico in the late 1820s, and the threat of Spanish reconquest culminating in the failed Barradas expedition of 1829, further diverted resources and attention. None of these changes automatically produced war, but together they eroded the material basis of the peace.

The deterioration was not uniform. In Chihuahua and Sonora, peace establishments such as those at Janos and Fronteras came under strain in the late 1820s and early 1830s, as rations were reduced or delayed and Apache communities abandoned the posts or resumed raiding. In New Mexico, trade and diplomacy with the Comanche and other groups continued, with periodic disruptions, through the 1820s and 1830s. In Texas, Mexican officials negotiated with Comanche representatives at San Antonio during the 1820s and sought agreements that would protect the small settlements of the province, but their capacity to fulfill commitments was limited. Historians have therefore cautioned against describing a single "breakdown" of frontier diplomacy and have emphasized regional chronologies.

Explanations for the deterioration require attention to Indigenous decisions as well as Mexican policies. Apache leaders who left peace establishments did so for varied reasons: unreliable supplies, conflicts with settlers, the killing of relatives by Mexican militias, internal rivalries, and the attraction of other economic opportunities. Comanche leaders, in turn, assessed the opportunities created by Mexican weakness and the demand for livestock in American markets. They did not simply exploit weakness; they also negotiated, and some bands maintained peaceful relations with Mexican communities while others raided them. Mexican policy also contributed. Local officials sometimes seized Apache people, broke commitments, or tolerated violence by settlers, and several Mexican states adopted measures that offered payments for Apache scalps (Section 68.9). The renewed conflict of the 1830s was therefore the product of several interacting decisions.

The financial details of a peace arrangement were therefore political facts, not incidental expenditures. A promise to deliver livestock, grain, blankets, or manufactured goods at an appointed time could reassure a community that the agreement remained in force; a missed payment might be interpreted as a withdrawal of obligations or as evidence that local officials had lost authority. At the same time, families residing near a presidio did not surrender all independent economic choices. They might trade outside the post, join relatives elsewhere, or seek alternative supplies when official distributions failed. The resulting mobility frustrated officials who expected a peace settlement to function as permanent confinement. Understanding the disagreement requires reconstructing what each side believed the arrangement obligated the other to do.

The surviving records themselves can exaggerate a narrative of sudden collapse. A commandant writing to request reinforcements had reasons to emphasize threats, while a governor defending his administration might claim that negotiations were proceeding successfully. Indigenous leaders' statements survive mostly through the same officials' reports, often in Spanish translation. Historians therefore compare expense accounts, lists of distributions, correspondence from neighboring presidios, reports of local violence, and the movements of particular communities before concluding that a peace establishment had failed. This evidentiary problem connects Mexican independence to the larger issue of who could make commitments credible in a frontier shared by governments and Indigenous nations.

Historical Vocabulary
Presidio and Peace Establishment (establecimiento de paz)

Definition. A presidio was a Spanish and later Mexican garrison post, usually combining a fortified compound with a settlement of soldiers' families. A peace establishment was an arrangement under which Apache communities settled near a presidio and received rations in exchange for peace and sometimes military cooperation.

Historical context. The peace establishments were introduced in the late eighteenth century and relied on the regular delivery of food, clothing, tobacco, and other goods. They were also sites of surveillance and conflict, and Apache participation was voluntary and reversible.

Why it matters. The establishments show that Spanish and Mexican officials negotiated with Apache communities and depended on them, and that the failure of the supply system had political consequences.

Potential misunderstanding. The establishments were not reservations in the later sense. Apache residents could leave, and many did.

Section 68.7

Mexican Colonization Policy and the Settlement of Texas

Spain had begun to open Texas to American settlers just before independence. Moses Austin, a Missouri entrepreneur, received permission in 1820–1821 to settle families in Texas; he died in June 1821, and his son Stephen F. Austin carried the project forward. The Mexican government confirmed Austin's grant in 1823, and on August 18, 1824 it passed a national colonization law that left the details of land distribution to the states. On March 24, 1825, the legislature of Coahuila y Texas adopted its own colonization law, which authorized empresarios, contractors who recruited and settled families in return for land premiums, to bring settlers into designated areas of Texas. Immigrants were required to be Catholic, or to profess to be, and to swear allegiance to Mexico.

Austin's first colony, on the lower Brazos and Colorado rivers, received the authorized three hundred families, often called the Old Three Hundred, and other empresarios, including Green DeWitt, Martín De León, and Haden Edwards, recruited settlers elsewhere. Land was cheap relative to the United States, and Mexican officials hoped that immigrants would develop the economy and provide a buffer against Indigenous raiders and American expansion. Migration from the United States grew rapidly. By 1836, estimates suggest about thirty thousand Anglo-American settlers, approximately 3,500 Tejanos, and roughly five thousand enslaved Black people, alongside Indigenous populations inadequately represented in settler counts. These groups were not necessarily counted as mutually exclusive in every contemporary report, and all totals are approximate.

Slavery was a central contradiction. Many American immigrants, particularly from the southern states, brought or purchased enslaved laborers. Mexican law moved gradually against slavery: the national government prohibited the slave trade in 1824, the Coahuila y Texas constitution of 1827 prohibited the further introduction of slaves, and the Mexican president Vicente Guerrero decreed the abolition of slavery in September 1829, though Texas was subsequently exempted and the exemption was contested. Colonists responded by classifying many enslaved people as contract servants, and enforcement was weak. The question of slavery shaped the motivations of colonists, the attitude of Mexican officials, and later the politics of the Texas Revolution. It also shaped relations with Indigenous nations, since colonists and Indigenous leaders disagreed about land, captives, and labor.

The Mexican government was ambivalent about the success of its own policy. The Law of April 6, 1830 attempted to restrict further immigration from the United States and to promote Mexican settlement, but it was widely resented and, in 1834, partly repealed. Mier y Terán's reports (Source Window I) had recommended stronger control, and the garrisons established under his plans, at places such as Anahuac and Velasco, produced friction with colonists. The colonization system had been intended to strengthen Mexican control, but in the course of a decade it had created a large population with weak ties to the Mexican state.

Colonization took place within territories where Indigenous authority remained substantial. Austin and other empresarios negotiated with Karankawa, Tonkawa, and other nations who lived along the coast and rivers, and the Karankawa, a coastal people, were reduced by violence and disease and displaced from their lands during the 1820s and 1830s. Further inland, settlements were exposed to Comanche and other raiding. Migration from the United States was therefore not a matter of settling empty land. It required negotiation, compromise, or violence with Indigenous nations who regarded the same ground as their own, and the land titles issued by Mexican authorities were titles to land that Indigenous communities used and defended.

Historical Vocabulary
Empresario

Definition. A contractor who, under Mexican colonization law, agreed to recruit and settle a specified number of families in a designated area in return for land and other premiums.

Historical context. The empresario system built on Spanish practice and was authorized by the colonization laws of 1824 and 1825. Empresarios such as Stephen F. Austin acted as intermediaries between settlers and Mexican officials, and sometimes also with Indigenous nations.

Why it matters. The system explains how a Mexican government seeking security and economic growth allowed a large, culturally distinct settler population to take root in Indigenous homelands.

Section 68.8

Horses, Bison, Livestock, Trade, and the Borderlands Economy

The borderlands economy was an interlocking system of exchange in which Indigenous nations were producers, consumers, and traders. Understanding it requires distinguishing several modes of acquisition that contemporaries and later writers often confused: consensual trade, diplomatic gift exchange, tribute and protection payments, raiding, and the exchange of captives. Each followed different rules and had different political meanings.

Trade was the broadest and, over much of the period, the most continuous of these. Comanche, Kiowa, Wichita-affiliated, and other nations exchanged bison hides and robes, dried meat, tallow, horses, and mules for corn, flour, metal goods, cloth, knives, and sometimes firearms and ammunition. In New Mexico, Pueblo, Hispano, and Indigenous traders met at Taos, Pecos, and other points. From 1821 the Santa Fe Trail from Missouri brought American merchants and manufactured goods into New Mexico, and in 1833 Bent's Fort on the upper Arkansas opened as a trading post that served Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, and Comanche customers. On the Red River, American and Indigenous traders operated posts that linked the Plains to the Mississippi Valley. In East Texas, Caddo and other peoples traded with American merchants and, after 1836, with Texans.

Horses and mules were the central commodities. The Plains horse economy depended on both natural increase and replenishment from Mexican ranches, and Indigenous traders and raiders obtained very large numbers of animals from northern Mexico and Texas. These animals supplied Comanche and Kiowa communities and also entered American markets: horses and mules taken in the borderlands were sold or traded to American merchants on the Red and Arkansas rivers and in Missouri. In this way Mexican livestock losses were connected to American commercial demand, a linkage emphasized by historians of the region. Cattle, sheep, and goats were also taken, and meat provisioning for Plains communities and trade partners depended on these sources.

Distinguishing raiding from trade is essential. Raiding involved the seizure of livestock, goods, and captives, and was sometimes accompanied by killing. Trade involved reciprocal exchange under agreed terms. Tribute and protection arrangements, in which Mexican communities or officials paid goods to Indigenous leaders in return for security, occupied an intermediate category and are documented in New Mexico and elsewhere. The exchange of captives, discussed in Section 68.18, was a related but distinct activity. A single Indigenous community could engage in more than one of these activities, and Mexican communities likewise traded with some Indigenous groups while suffering raids from others. The borderlands cannot be reduced to warfare alone.

Ecology shaped the economy. Bison herds on the southern Plains were subject to drought, to competition for winter range, and to the demand for robes and hides, and by the 1840s some scholars argue that pressure on bison and pasture was increasing. Drought and epidemic disease, including a smallpox outbreak around 1839–1840 reported in several Plains communities, affected Indigenous populations unevenly. These environmental pressures did not determine political decisions, but they altered the incentives of leaders who relied on horses and bison. Chapter 81 returns to the later destruction of the bison economy.

The economic power of Indigenous nations had political consequences. Comanche access to large horse herds and trade networks gave them resources that Mexican frontier communities lacked. Mexican governments, depending on mining and agriculture, could not match this mobility. Indigenous economic power thus translated into political leverage, allowing Comanche leaders to negotiate with Mexico, Texas, and the United States on terms that governments could not simply dictate.

These commercial chains depended on labor that official diplomatic and military reports rarely described. Within Comanche communities, households cared for large horse herds while women and other family members prepared food, processed hides, and maintained the material resources that made extended travel possible. At trading places, Indigenous intermediaries interpreted prices, kinship expectations, and obligations that outsiders could easily misread. Goods acquired through a raid might pass through multiple exchanges before reaching an American market, so the final purchaser did not necessarily know the circumstances in which they had been obtained. Likewise, a Mexican merchant buying an animal from a peaceful trading partner could still participate in a wider economy affected by raiding. These connections complicate any neat division between a legitimate commercial frontier and a violent one. Commerce and conflict drew on many of the same roads, animals, families, and intermediaries, though that overlap never erased the difference between consensual exchange and coercion.

Division III — Expanding Conflict and the Political Crisis of the 1830s
Section 68.9

Apache–Mexican Conflict in Chihuahua and Sonora

By the early 1830s the peace arrangements in Chihuahua and Sonora were failing. Documents from the presidios at Janos and Fronteras record complaints about reduced rations, delayed supplies, and the departure of Apache communities from the posts. Some Chiricahua and other Apache groups left and resumed raiding, and others, including communities that had remained at peace, found themselves caught between the raiders and the Mexican militias that retaliated. The dating of the breakdown varies by place, and historians generally place its beginning in the late 1820s and early 1830s, with escalation in the middle and late 1830s.

The Mexican response was shaped by state and local authorities, not by central policy. Chihuahua and Sonora, whose territories were exposed and whose treasuries were thin, relied heavily on militias and on volunteer forces recruited locally. Militia expeditions sometimes attacked Apache camps indiscriminately, and their targets could include communities that had been at peace. The attacks created grievances, and Apache communities responded with raids, with revenge expeditions, and with attacks on ranches, mining camps, and travelers. In Sonora, authorities adopted regulations during the 1830s that organized volunteer campaigns against Apache groups. In Sonora, lawmakers authorized rewards for Apache scalps by the mid-1830s; Chihuahua also adopted bounty policies during the subsequent escalation. The chronology, eligibility rules, and amounts differed among jurisdictions, so their decrees should not be conflated.

The scalp bounty policy illustrates how official incentives could expand violence. Payments were offered to anyone who produced scalps, and the system could not distinguish reliably between the scalps of Apache people who had raided and those who had not, or between Apache scalps and those of other Indigenous or Mexican people. Documented episodes show that bounty hunters attacked peaceful communities. A particularly consequential episode occurred on April 22, 1837, in the Animas Mountains region northwest of Janos, along a trading route toward the Santa Rita del Cobre mines. A party led by the American trader John J. Johnson, having offered provisions to an Apache camp, opened fire with a concealed swivel gun. Juan José Compá and other Apache leaders were among those killed. A detailed scholarly reconstruction records nineteen deaths and approximately twenty wounded; other accounts give different figures and locations. The killing intensified Apache hostility and the subsequent prominence of Chihenne leaders such as Mangas Coloradas.

Apache responses were not uniform. Some Apache groups sought peace and tried to renew agreements with Mexican officials; others pursued revenge, and still others sought new trading partners. Western Apache and Chiricahua communities moved between the Sierra Madre, the Gila country, and the settlements, taking advantage of mountainous terrain. The Mexican states differed in policy and in outcomes, and there was no single "Apache war." Distinguishing among Chihenne, Chokonen, Nednhi, Mescalero, and other groups, and between Chihuahua and Sonora, is essential for accurate analysis.

Civilian populations bore much of the cost. Ranchers, miners, and villagers lived under constant threat, and many abandoned outlying settlements and withdrew toward larger towns. Mining operations in the Santa Rita region and elsewhere were disrupted, and commerce along roads such as the one between Chihuahua City and Santa Fe became hazardous. Apache communities suffered as well, from militia attacks, from the killing of women and children, from the loss of access to rations and markets, and from the disruption of ordinary life. The scale of these losses is difficult to quantify, because Mexican records emphasize raids and Apache records survive mostly in later oral history. Historians describe a spiral in which retaliation, economic disruption, and official incentives reinforced one another and in which decisions at the local level frequently drove escalation.

Section 68.10

Comanche Raiding and the Crisis of Northern Mexico

Comanche campaigns into northern Mexico, which had occurred on a smaller scale before independence, expanded during the 1830s and 1840s. Comanche, Kiowa, and Kiowa Apache parties traveled south along established routes across the Rio Grande, sometimes covering very long distances, and struck ranches, haciendas, and villages in Chihuahua, Coahuila, Durango, and Nuevo León, with raids sometimes reaching Tamaulipas, Zacatecas, and San Luis Potosí. Mexican reports describe the destruction of herds, the abandonment of ranches, and the deaths and capture of many residents. Historians generally agree that the 1840s saw the most intense phase, though exact figures for losses are uncertain and often derived from official complaints.

Several conditions explain the expansion. Mexican defensive weakness, produced by unpaid garrisons, political turmoil, and the diversion of forces to civil conflicts including federalist rebellions in the northern states, made raiding less risky. The Texas Revolution and the continuing hostility between Mexico and Texas drew Mexican attention eastward. Comanche leaders also had incentives to obtain livestock for their own herds and for trade, as markets for horses and mules existed on the Red and Arkansas rivers and elsewhere. Captives, discussed in Section 68.18, were also sought for ransom, labor, and adoption. Alliances among Comanche, Kiowa, and other groups made large expeditions feasible. And the growth of Texas settlement and conflict with Texans reduced Comanche access to the Texas frontier, which may have increased the attractiveness of the Mexican north.

These raids must be distinguished from commerce and diplomacy. Comanche parties that traded in New Mexico or negotiated with Mexican officials on one occasion could raid communities elsewhere, and the same band could pursue different policies toward different Mexican communities. Some Mexican communities paid tribute or negotiated truces, and Mexican officials in several states sought peace with Comanche leaders. Treating every Comanche appearance as a raid, or every Comanche agreement as lasting peace, misrepresents the evidence. Comanche leaders conducted foreign policy toward multiple governments simultaneously, and their decisions reflected assessments of advantage, risk, and obligation.

The consequences for frontier communities were devastating. Documentation from Mexican state archives and contemporary accounts describes deaths, captures, destruction of property, depopulation of rural districts, and economic disruption. Families were separated; ransoming or recovering captives was costly and uncertain. Mexican states struggled to organize defense, and some settlements withdrew or relocated. The region's political instability deepened as local communities lost confidence in state and national authorities. Recognizing these consequences does not require attributing violence to an innate Comanche disposition. Comanche leaders acted within a political and economic system that rewarded raiding, in a context shaped by Mexican weakness, American markets, and Texan expansion, and they also engaged in trade, diplomacy, and alliance. Explanations that depict Indigenous peoples as motivated solely by violence ignore the evidence of Comanche political agency, while accounts that omit the suffering of Mexican communities ignore the evidence of harm.

Historical Debate

Does "Empire" Describe Comanche Power?

The question: Historians have used "empire" to describe the Comanche political and commercial system, most prominently in Pekka Hämäläinen's 2008 book The Comanche Empire. Does the term illuminate how Comanche power worked, or does it import assumptions about centralization that the evidence does not support?

Interpretation A — "Empire" accurately captures the scale and structure of Comanche power: Supporters argue that the Comanche system extended across a vast region, subordinated or coerced neighboring peoples, controlled trade routes, extracted resources from Spanish, Mexican, and other communities, and set terms for Spanish, Mexican, Texan, and American governments. On this view, "empire" is an appropriate analytical term for a political economy built on horses, bison, trade, and raiding, and it corrects the older picture of the Comanche as marginal raiders.
Interpretation B — "Empire" misleads: Critics argue that the Comanche lacked a central government, a bureaucracy, a standing army, or territorial administration, and that bands acted autonomously, sometimes at odds with one another. They worry that "empire" implies a unified state and may obscure variation among bands and the importance of kinship and consensus, and they note that Comanche power was often reactive and dependent on neighbors.
Interpretation C — The term is useful if defined carefully: Others suggest that "empire" can describe hegemony and regional influence without implying a centralized state, and that the critical point is to specify what the term means and what it does not.

Evidence: The debate draws on Spanish, Mexican, and American records of diplomacy and trade, descriptions of Comanche leadership, evidence of band autonomy, and records of how Comanche leaders obtained tribute, trade concessions, and captives.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and interpretation more than the basic facts. Scholars largely agree on the reach, economic importance, and political influence of Comanche power; they differ on whether the word "empire" clarifies or distorts it.

Why it matters: The choice of term affects whether Comanche history is read as a story of marginal frontier peoples or as that of a regional power whose decisions shaped the continent.

Section 68.11

Cherokee, Shawnee, Kickapoo, and Other Migrants in Mexican Texas

While Comanche and Apache nations contested the Mexican north, another set of Indigenous communities settled in East Texas. Pressure from American settlement, treaties that ceded land, and removal policy east of the Mississippi pushed groups of Cherokee, Shawnee, Delaware, Kickapoo, Choctaw, and others westward. Chapter 66 traced the removal policy and its consequences; this section is concerned with the migrants who reached Texas in the 1810s and 1820s, before and apart from the formal removal of the 1830s, and with the new political communities they built.

The Cherokee in Texas were a group of Western Cherokee who had moved from the Arkansas country and settled in East Texas around 1819–1820, in the area north of Nacogdoches between the Angelina and Neches rivers. Their leader Duwali, known to Anglo-Americans as Chief Bowl or Bowles, was a prominent figure who, according to most accounts, was in his later years when he was killed in 1839. Richard Fields was another important Cherokee leader of the same period. Chapter 64 discussed the Cherokee Republic and Cherokee political reform east of the Mississippi; the Texas Cherokee were a distinct community with its own leaders. They farmed, raised livestock, traded, and maintained relations with Caddo, Shawnee, Delaware, Kickapoo, and other neighbors, while seeking from Spanish and then Mexican authorities a formal grant of the land they occupied.

The Mexican government did not issue the title. Cherokee petitions were received sympathetically by some officials and rejected or delayed by others, and no definitive land grant was completed. The question of title became entangled with the Fredonian Rebellion of 1826–1827, in which the empresario Haden Edwards and his supporters proclaimed an independent republic at Nacogdoches after a dispute with Mexican authorities. Richard Fields and an associate negotiated with Edwards' faction an arrangement that would have recognized Cherokee and allied claims, but Mexican officials worked to retain Cherokee loyalty, and Duwali and other Cherokee leaders withdrew support from the rebellion. Fields and his associate were killed by members of their own community. The episode shows how Indigenous leaders weighed competing offers from competing authorities, and how internal divisions affected choices.

Other migrants settled in East Texas and along the Brazos and Trinity rivers. Shawnee communities lived near the Sabine and Red rivers. Kickapoo groups established villages in East Texas and moved between Texas, the Indian Territory, and later northern Mexico. Delaware communities settled along the Brazos and Trinity, and their members served as guides and interpreters. The Alabama and Coushatta, discussed in Section 68.4, were longer-term residents. These groups had distinct histories, leaders, and interests, and their relationships with each other and with Caddo and other neighbors were complex.

Mexican authorities, American colonists, and Indigenous migrants understood territorial rights differently. Mexican officials treated land as a matter of state grants and empresario contracts, in which Indigenous communities held no formal title unless granted. American colonists, accustomed to survey and patent systems, regarded unpatented land as available for settlement. The Cherokee and their neighbors, who had built houses and fields and who had agreed to relationships with Mexican officials, considered occupation and use as the basis of a claim and expected recognition. These conflicting understandings meant that Indigenous communities in East Texas lived in legal uncertainty, a vulnerability that increased as Anglo-American settlement spread. The migrants of East Texas illustrate how U.S. dispossession created new Indigenous political communities in Texas, whose own security depended on recognition by whichever government held power.

Section 68.12

The Texas Revolution, 1835–1836, and Indigenous Political Strategies

The Texas Revolution grew from disputes over Mexican politics, taxation, immigration, slavery, and the status of Texas within the Mexican federation. In 1834–1835 the government of Antonio López de Santa Anna moved from a federalist to a centralist position, dissolving state legislatures and replacing the federal constitution with centralist laws in 1835–1836. Many Texans, including some Tejanos, objected to the loss of state autonomy, and fighting began at Gonzales in October 1835. In November 1835 a Consultation of Texans established a provisional government, and on March 2, 1836 a convention at Washington-on-the-Brazos declared independence. The Alamo fell on March 6, Goliad followed in late March, and Texan forces under Sam Houston defeated Santa Anna at San Jacinto on April 21, 1836. The military narrative is not the subject here; it matters because it created a new claimant to sovereignty in the borderlands and shaped the choices of Indigenous nations.

Those choices were made under uncertainty. Federalist and centralist Mexican factions, Texan insurgents, and Indigenous governments each had interests that did not coincide. Mexican officials, and in some accounts Mexican agents, sought the support or neutrality of Indigenous nations, and rumors of Indigenous alliance with Mexico circulated among Texans. The Texan side, aware of the vulnerability of its eastern settlements, sought to ensure that the Cherokee and their neighbors would not join the Mexican side. Indigenous leaders weighed the promises of both and the likelihood that either government could keep them. For most Indigenous nations, the revolution was one episode in a longer process, and neutrality was the most common strategy.

The most important diplomatic result was the treaty negotiated on February 23, 1836 between the Texas provisional government and the Cherokee and associated groups. The Consultation had in November 1835 resolved to send commissioners to treat with the Cherokee and others, and the commissioners were Sam Houston and John Forbes. The treaty, signed at Bowles' village, set out a territory for the Cherokee and the associated bands, described in relation to the old San Antonio road, the Sabine, the Angelina, and the Neches, and promised that these lands would be held by the signatories. The associated groups included Delaware, Shawnee, Kickapoo, Caddo, Alabama, Coushatta, and other communities, according to the document.

The treaty was not ratified: the Texas Senate tabled it on December 29, 1836, and declared it null and void on December 16, 1837. Houston continued to defend its authority, but under Lamar the Republic denied that it secured Cherokee title to the land. The grounds offered at the time included doubts about the authority of the commissioners and the provisional government, the claim that Cherokee occupation was illegal under Mexican law, the desire of settlers and speculators for the land, and suspicion of Cherokee ties to Mexico. The rejection left Indigenous communities that had been promised recognition without legal protection, and it laid the groundwork for the events of 1838–1839 (Section 68.14). The treaty itself, preserved in the Texas State Library and Archives, is a central source for understanding Indigenous strategy during the revolution.

Division IV — The Republic of Texas and Indigenous Resistance
Section 68.13

Sam Houston, Comanche Diplomacy, and the Limits of Texas Authority

The Republic of Texas established de facto independence after San Jacinto and the Treaties of Velasco of May 1836, though Mexico did not recognize it and continued to regard Texas as a rebellious province. A constitution was adopted in 1836, Houston was elected the Republic's first regularly elected president in September, and he took office in October 1836. The constitution barred a president from immediately succeeding himself, so Houston served until December 1838. In December 1836 the Texas Congress claimed a boundary running along the Rio Grande to its source, a claim that included Santa Fe and large Indigenous territories. The Republic lacked the money, population, and army to enforce it.

Houston brought to Indigenous diplomacy a personal history that distinguished him from many Texan leaders. He had lived among the Cherokee in the early 1829–1832 period and was familiar with Indigenous councils and the conventions of treaty-making. He regarded war with Indigenous nations as expensive and unwinnable for a young republic with a small treasury, and his administration emphasized negotiation, trade, and restraint. His government made overtures to Comanche, Caddo, Wichita-affiliated, and other nations, and sought to establish trading relationships and peaceful boundaries. These efforts produced limited agreements and little durable change, in part because the government could not control its own citizens and in part because of divisions within Indigenous communities.

Houston's constraints were considerable. Settlers and land speculators sought land on the frontier and resisted any recognition of Indigenous territorial claims. The Texas Congress was often hostile to Indigenous treaties, as the rejection of the 1836 Cherokee treaty showed. Public opinion in settlements that had suffered attacks, such as the Fort Parker raid of May 1836, in which Comanche and allied warriors killed several settlers and took captives, demanded retaliation. Houston's own political opponents, including supporters of Lamar, criticized his policy as weak. The Republic's military strength was limited to a small regular force and to volunteers and ranger companies raised for specific campaigns.

The objectives of Comanche leaders differed from those of Houston. Comanche leaders sought trade at reasonable terms, respect for their hunting grounds, and security for their bands. They regarded the return of captives as a matter to be negotiated, often through ransom, as in earlier relations with Spanish and Mexican officials. They did not accept the Republic's claim to the lands they used, and they did not regard themselves as subjects of Texas. Houston, for his part, sought peace along the settlement frontier and hoped to keep Indigenous nations apart from Mexico, but he also assumed that the Republic would continue to expand. These aims could coincide for a time, but they could not be reconciled over the long term. Without an agreed boundary that both sides could enforce, even a well-intentioned peace policy was vulnerable.

The possibilities for diplomacy varied sharply among Indigenous communities. Groups with towns near the settled east could negotiate about recognizable village territories and agricultural improvements, but those same settlements were easily reached by incoming surveyors and land speculators. Mobile Comanche groups negotiated over trade, safe travel, captive exchange, and access to much larger hunting regions. A Texas government that understood a treaty as permission for settlement beyond a designated line might confront leaders who understood it as an undertaking to keep trespassers away from their range. Houston's personal experience with Cherokee political life did not resolve those different conceptions of territorial authority. Nor did his preferences bind the legislature or the next president.

The Republic also possessed conflicting instruments of policy. Its officers and Indian agents could invite delegations to councils while other officials authorized land surveys, volunteer expeditions, or local defensive operations. Settlers who had suffered livestock losses or attacks often considered negotiation a reward for raiders; traders and officials responsible for the treasury could regard an expensive military campaign as a greater danger. Indigenous leaders observed these disagreements and evaluated whether any promise issued in the capital would be honored by men living on the frontier. This gap between diplomatic commitment and enforcement would become particularly visible in the fate of the Cherokee treaty and in the changing policy of the Lamar administration.

This history complicates a simple contrast between a peaceful Houston and a warlike Lamar. Houston's administrations did seek negotiated settlements, but Houston also presided over an expanding settler republic that claimed land occupied by Indigenous nations. His peace policy was not equivalent to broad recognition of Indigenous independence, and it could not eliminate the material incentives pushing settlers west. Lamar's approach made those pressures more openly coercive, yet the underlying dispute over land and authority persisted across both presidencies. The crucial measure of success is therefore not the number of councils called or declarations issued, but whether agreements protected communities and permitted them to make decisions about their own territories.

Section 68.14

Mirabeau B. Lamar and the Expulsion of the Texas Cherokee, 1838–1839

Mirabeau B. Lamar, inaugurated in December 1838, took a markedly different approach. In his message to the Texas Congress he advocated a vigorous military policy toward Indigenous nations, and his administration rejected the idea that the Cherokee and associated peoples held legal title to East Texas land. Lamar's priorities included the expansion of the Republic, the opening of Indigenous lands to settlement, and an expensive program of military and territorial ambition, including the unsuccessful Santa Fe Expedition of 1841 that sought to extend Texan claims to New Mexico.

The conflict with the Cherokee developed through several linked episodes. In 1838 the Córdova Rebellion, led by Vicente Córdova, a Tejano from Nacogdoches who sought to reassert Mexican authority, attracted a small number of Indigenous and Tejano supporters and was put down by Texan forces. In the autumn of 1838 Anglo settlers in East Texas were killed in what became known as the Killough massacre, which Texans attributed to Indigenous attackers. In May 1839 Texan troops killed Manuel Flores, a Mexican agent, and obtained papers that allegedly described a Mexican plan to organize Indigenous nations against Texas. Texan authorities treated these documents as proof that the Cherokee were conspirators. The documents, and the interpretation placed upon them, are central to the controversy.

A careful reading distinguishes documented activity from accusation. Mexican officials did attempt to enlist Indigenous support against Texas, and the Flores papers reflect that effort. Some Cherokee and other individuals may have engaged with Mexican agents, and Duwali and other leaders had a long history of seeking recognition from Mexico. But the evidence that the Cherokee leadership as a body had committed to a Mexican alliance or participated in the attacks of 1838 is weak, and Cherokee leaders consistently stated that they sought peace. The Texas government also had an interest in the Cherokee land, and many settlers and officials had pressed for removal long before the Flores documents surfaced. Historians have therefore emphasized that accusations of collaboration served as a justification for a policy that had other motives.

In July 1839 a Texas commission negotiated with the Cherokee, offering compensation for improvements if they agreed to leave, and Duwali declined. Texan forces under officers including Thomas Rusk, Kelsey Douglass, and Edward Burleson then advanced. The Battle of the Neches, fought on July 15–16, 1839 in what is now northeast Texas, ended with the defeat of the Cherokee and their allies. Duwali, who was wounded, was killed by Texan soldiers on July 16. Estimates of Cherokee and allied dead vary, with accounts suggesting dozens killed, and Texan losses were far lighter. Texan troops then destroyed villages and crops across the region, and the Cherokee, Delaware, Shawnee, Kickapoo, and other communities scattered, some moving north to the Indian Territory and some to Mexico.

The expulsion transformed East Texas. It opened the land to settlement, ended the experiment in Indigenous self-government that had developed there, and signaled to other nations that the Republic would not honor agreements that conflicted with settler ambitions. It also placed a number of displaced communities in precarious positions, forcing them to seek refuge elsewhere. The episode belongs to the larger pattern traced in Chapters 65 and 66, in which Indigenous nations that had already been displaced found no secure place beyond the reach of expanding settler states.

Section 68.15

The Council House Fight of 1840: Diplomacy, Captivity, and Violence

In January 1840 Penateka Comanche leaders met with Texan officials and indicated that they wanted peace. The Texas government replied that it would negotiate in San Antonio and that the Comanche must bring in captives. On March 19, 1840, a Comanche delegation arrived, led by Muguara (Muk-wah-ruh) and including other leaders along with women and children—approximately sixty-five people according to one widely used reconstruction, though sources differ. The Texan commissioners were Colonels Hugh McLeod and William G. Cooke, with Lieutenant Colonel William S. Fisher commanding troops stationed in the city. San Antonio's Council House stood on Main Plaza.

The delegation brought only a few captives, including several Mexican children according to one account. One was Matilda Lockhart, a teenager taken in 1838, who was reported by Texan witnesses to have been severely mistreated and to have described other captives still held. The Comanche leaders explained that other captives were with other bands and could be brought in for ransom. Texan officials had decided in advance, according to most accounts, that the Comanche leaders present would be held as hostages until all captives were returned. The commissioners informed Muguara of this decision during the council, and soldiers entered the room. Muguara, according to accounts, responded that the Comanche would not accept the arrangement and that the captives held by other bands might be killed if the delegates were detained.

Violence erupted in the council room and spread into the plaza and surrounding streets. According to most accounts, the twelve Comanche leaders in the council room were killed, and in the streets and neighboring buildings a total of roughly thirty-five Comanche people, including some women and children, died. Approximately thirty others, chiefly women and children, were taken prisoner according to a frequently cited reconstruction. Texan casualties are given as about seven killed, with others wounded, though accounts differ. Participants disagreed, then and since, about who fired first, about whether the Comanche had been warned, and about whether the Texas government had a plan for the capture of the leaders. Texan officials presented the event as a response to Comanche treachery and bad faith. Comanche accounts, preserved indirectly, treat it as a betrayal of a diplomatic meeting.

Diplomatic expectations explain the importance of the episode. From the Comanche perspective, a delegation that came with women and children and under a declaration of peace was observing protocols that Spanish and Mexican officials had long respected. Negotiating the return of captives through ransom and exchange was customary. The Texan decision to detain delegates as hostages violated those protocols and treated the delegation as an enemy force. From the Texan perspective, the Comanche had failed to bring all captives as demanded and had previously attacked settlements, and the government sought to compel compliance. The two sides were operating under different conceptions of diplomacy, and neither had the means or the will to bridge the gap.

The consequences were severe. Comanche leaders who had favored negotiation lost credibility; those who favored war gained. Captives held by Comanche communities were in greater danger, and some were reportedly killed, though evidence about specific cases is uneven. The Council House Fight remained a landmark in Comanche memory and in Texan narrative, though the two memories differed. It is best understood as a turning point in Comanche–Texan relations, and as a case where the failure of diplomatic communication, combined with settler pressure, produced escalation.

Section 68.16

The Great Comanche Raid of 1840 and the Texas Frontier War

In the summer of 1840 a Comanche force, associated with the Penateka leader Potsanaquahip (Buffalo Hump), moved southeastward from the Hill Country toward the Texas coast. The expedition, which included Comanche warriors from several bands and, according to some accounts, allied participants, may have numbered from several hundred to more than a thousand people, estimates varying widely. It passed through the settlements and on August 6 struck Victoria, where it took horses and killed residents, and on August 8 it attacked Linnville, a port on Lavaca Bay. Many of Linnville's residents took to boats, and the raiders plundered warehouses, taking goods that had been unloaded or stored.

The connection to the Council House Fight is generally accepted by historians, though the exact motivations are debated. Comanche leaders had reasons for revenge, and the expedition appears to have been planned over a period after March. It also had economic objectives: the Texas coast offered goods and horses on a large scale. And it served a political purpose, demonstrating that the Republic could not protect its settlements. Whether the raiders intended to reach the coast from the beginning or adapted as the expedition progressed is uncertain, and historians draw on Texan accounts, which dominate the record, and limited Comanche testimony.

The raiders, laden with plunder and driving large herds, retreated northwest, and Texan forces mobilized. At Plum Creek, near present Lockhart, on August 12, 1840, a force of Texan volunteers and rangers under officers including Felix Huston, Edward Burleson, and Ben McCulloch, together with Tonkawa auxiliaries, attacked the Comanche column. The fighting was confused and covered a large area; the Comanche lost many horses and some of the plunder, and a number of warriors were killed. Texan accounts claimed large Comanche casualties, while later historians suggest the numbers may have been lower and that the battle was less decisive than some narratives claim. Texan casualties were light. Captives taken at Linnville were in some cases killed during the retreat, though other captives survived.

Texan retaliation continued. In October 1840 a force under Colonel John H. Moore attacked a Comanche camp on the upper Colorado River and reported heavy casualties, including women and children, though numbers vary by source. The Republic also expanded its ranger forces and continued to press into Comanche country. These actions did not end Comanche raiding. Comanche war parties continued to operate in Texas and Mexico throughout the early 1840s, and Texan settlement did not expand rapidly beyond its existing frontier during this period. The raid and its aftermath show the limits of Texan power: the Republic could mobilize volunteers for a short campaign but could not secure its frontier or compel Comanche submission.

The significance of the raid is therefore twofold. It demonstrated the organizational capacity of Comanche leaders to coordinate a long-distance expedition and to strike at the commercial heart of a new republic. And it prompted Texan responses that hardened both sides. Later narratives, in Texan memory and in popular histories, have sometimes exaggerated the battle of Plum Creek as a decisive Texan victory or portrayed the raid as an inexplicable outburst; both views simplify a complex series of decisions.

Cause and Effect

Why the Borderlands Fell into Escalating Conflict by 1840

Long-term causes

Indigenous equestrian power, the fiscal weakness of Spanish and Mexican frontier administration, the spread of settlement from the United States, and overlapping and unenforceable territorial claims.

Short-term causes

Reduced rations and presents after independence, state-level scalp bounties and militia campaigns, the Texas Revolution, the rejection of the Cherokee treaty, and Lamar's policy of expansion and military action.

Immediate triggers

The Neches campaign of 1839, the dispute over captives in 1840, and the killing of Comanche delegates at San Antonio.

Effects

Displacement of the Texas Cherokee, loss of Comanche confidence in Texan diplomacy, the Great Raid and retaliation, continued Comanche pressure on northern Mexico, and a durable atmosphere of hostility.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced the crisis. Indigenous decisions, Mexican and Texan policies, local actors, markets, ecology, and contingent events interacted, and different participants made choices that could have gone differently. Peace remained possible at several points, including at San Antonio in March 1840.

Section 68.17

New Mexico, Diné, Pueblo, Ute, and the Western Borderlands

The borderlands extended far beyond Texas. In the territory of Nuevo México, with its capital at Santa Fe, a Hispano population of tens of thousands and a number of Pueblo communities lived in a region encircled by Comanche, Apache, Diné (Navajo), and Ute neighbors. Under Spanish rule, New Mexico's Pueblo communities had been recognized as corporate bodies with land grants. Mexican independence extended the language of citizenship to Pueblo people, and Mexican law no longer drew the Spanish colonial distinction between "Indians" and "Spaniards," but Pueblo communities continued to maintain their own governments, ceremonial systems, and land holdings. Chapters 43 and 44 traced their earlier history, including the Pueblo Revolt of 1680.

New Mexico's political history in this period was turbulent. Governors appointed by Mexico City held office for short periods, and the territory's connection to the national economy was weakened by distance. The opening of the Santa Fe Trail in 1821 transformed commerce, bringing American merchants and cheap manufactured goods and integrating New Mexico with the Missouri economy. In 1837 a revolt broke out in northern New Mexico, led by a coalition of Pueblo and Hispano communities, against the centralist government's policies and taxes. The rebels, among whom Taos Pueblo and nearby communities were prominent, killed the governor, Albino Pérez, in August 1837, and were defeated in early 1838 by forces loyal to Manuel Armijo, who became governor and held power, with interruptions, into the 1840s. The uprising shows that Pueblo communities were active political participants in Mexican New Mexico, not passive subjects.

New Mexican relations with the Comanche were comparatively stable through much of the period, resting on the trade and diplomacy established in the late eighteenth century. Comanche bands traded at New Mexican settlements, and the comanchero and ciboleros networks carried goods and bison products across the Plains. The relationship was not free of conflict, and Comanche raids on some communities occurred, but the pattern of exchange and negotiation continued. In 1841 Armijo's forces captured the Texan Santa Fe Expedition, which sought to extend the Republic's claims over New Mexico, and the episode confirmed that Texan claims to the Rio Grande remained rhetorical.

The Diné, or Navajo, lived west of the Rio Grande in a large territory that included the canyons and mesas of northwestern New Mexico and northeastern Arizona. Diné communities herded sheep, farmed, traded, and raided, and their relationships with New Mexican communities included commerce, conflict, and diplomacy. Spanish and Mexican officials negotiated several agreements with Diné leaders, including the prominent headman Narbona, but the agreements were fragile. New Mexican raiding parties also took Diné captives, and Diné raiders took livestock and captives from New Mexican communities, producing a cycle of retaliation. The Ute, living in the mountains and valleys to the north and west, traded at Abiquiu and Taos and sometimes allied with and sometimes fought against New Mexican communities, the Diné, and Comanche bands. The Jicarilla and Mescalero Apache likewise maintained their own relations with New Mexican towns. Chapter 71 treats the later Diné experience of United States conquest and the Long Walk; Chapter 69 treats the consequences of United States occupation of New Mexico.

Compared with Texas, New Mexico shows a different pattern. Indigenous and Hispano communities had lived in close proximity for two centuries, with intermarriage, interdependence, and long-standing institutions of exchange. Diplomatic practice, gift-giving, and captive exchange were established parts of the regional system. New Mexico also lacked the rapid Anglo-American colonization seen in Texas. These differences underline the larger point of this chapter: the Mexican–Indigenous borderlands were a set of regional systems with different structures, not a single frontier.

Pueblo participation in the 1837 uprising also illustrates why official ethnic categories are an unreliable guide to political allegiance. Particular Pueblo communities and Hispano villages could cooperate against unpopular taxation or central-government appointments without sharing identical long-term aims. Their local leaders were accountable to different constituencies, and the presence of Indigenous participants did not transform the revolt into a single pan-Pueblo political movement. After its suppression, communities still had to defend fields, water, local offices, and rights asserted under older land grants. These concerns continued regardless of which governor held Santa Fe. The episode belongs in this chapter because it demonstrates the capacity of Indigenous communities to intervene directly in the politics of Mexican territorial government.

The Diné and Ute cases bring a different kind of sovereignty into view. Officials who negotiated with one Diné headman or one Ute group could not assume that other communities had accepted the same commitments. Trade partnerships could coexist with raiding between particular settlements, and both sides had reasons to seek captives, animals, or restitution. The resulting disputes were not simply the consequences of distance from Mexico City: they reflected regional systems of wealth, family obligation, territorial use, and historical grievance. The same caution applies to Pueblo towns, whose settled agricultural lands and institutions gave them political objectives distinct from those of surrounding mobile communities. New Mexico thus provides an essential comparison with Texas rather than a peripheral afterthought.

Section 68.18

Captivity, Slavery, Kinship, and the Human Experience of Borderlands Warfare

Captivity was a pervasive feature of borderlands life. Comanche, Apache, Diné, Ute, and other Indigenous communities took captives, as did Mexican and Hispano New Mexican raiders and militias, and, increasingly, Texans. Captives included women, children, and men, and their fates varied widely. Historians insist on distinguishing among several institutions that contemporary sources and later popular writing often merge: chattel slavery, captivity, adoption or kinship incorporation, debt servitude, and other forms of dependency.

Chattel slavery involved the treatment of a person as property that could be bought, sold, and inherited, and it characterized the system of African American slavery in Texas as well as certain forms of Indigenous captive trade. Captivity, as it operated on the Plains, was often temporary and variable: captives might be held for ransom, adopted, put to work, traded, or killed. Adoption involved incorporating a captive into a kin group, with the obligations and, sometimes, the rights of kinship. Debt servitude, widespread in Mexico, bound laborers through debts owed to employers. Spanish New Mexico had developed a category of detribalized Indigenous people, genízaros, many of them descendants of captives, who lived in Hispano society in a dependent status. These categories overlapped in practice and were not identical, and individual captives could move from one to another.

Comanche practices illustrate the variety. Captive women and children were often incorporated into Comanche households, where women might marry and children might be raised as Comanche. Captive boys sometimes became herders and, in some cases, warriors. Other captives, particularly adult men and those with value for ransom, were held for exchange. Comanche communities also sold captives to New Mexican traders and Plains neighbors, and ransom was a recognized practice through which Mexican and later Texan families recovered relatives. Some captives, including Cynthia Ann Parker, who was taken as a child in the Fort Parker raid of May 1836, grew up within Comanche communities and had difficulty readjusting to Anglo-American society when they were later recovered. The experiences of captives varied by age, gender, circumstance, and the policies of their captors, and surviving narratives, many of them written for Anglo-American audiences, must be read with caution.

Mexican and Hispano communities likewise held Indigenous captives. Apache, Diné, Ute, and Comanche people were captured in raids and campaigns and placed in households as servants, sometimes baptized and sometimes sold. Mexican law abolished slavery in 1829, but the status of Indigenous servants was often ambiguous, and practices of servitude persisted under other names. Historians such as Andrés Reséndez have argued that Indigenous enslavement in the Southwest and northern Mexico lasted well beyond the legal abolition of slavery and involved very large numbers of people over several centuries; exact numbers are uncertain and debated. Texans, too, took Comanche captives, notably after the Council House Fight, and held women and children in San Antonio.

Women and children bore a particular burden. They were targets of raids, were vulnerable in households, and were valued as labor and as members of kin groups. Women within Comanche households processed hides and meat and managed domestic production, and their labor supported the economy of the trade in robes. Mexican families lost members to raids and spent resources on ransom, and settlement patterns changed as households moved toward larger towns for security. Across the region, people sought to maintain or reconstruct communities through kinship ties, ransom networks, intermarriage, and relocation. Chapter 53 examines captivity and slavery across the continent; this section emphasizes that in the borderlands these institutions shaped the daily lives of families on every side.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Herds, and Mixed Communities

Daily life in the borderlands was organized around the household and the herd. Comanche households moved with the seasons, following bison and grass, and women processed hides, prepared food, and managed camp life while men hunted, raided, and conducted diplomacy. Horses were central to every part of this life, and a family's wealth was measured in animals. Apache communities, depending on location, combined foraging, hunting, some farming, and livestock. Pueblo and Hispano villages in New Mexico were organized around irrigated agriculture, sheep, and seasonal trade. Tejano ranchers and Anglo-American farmers in Texas lived on scattered ranches and plantations, with enslaved labor on many cotton farms along the Brazos and Colorado.

Mixed communities were common. Delaware, Shawnee, and other migrants married into Caddo and other communities. Hispano and Pueblo families intermarried and shared institutions. Captives incorporated into Comanche households married and raised children. Traders, interpreters, and mediators lived between worlds, learning several languages and sometimes belonging to more than one community. The surviving evidence for these ordinary lives is thin and uneven: it consists of travelers' descriptions, missionary and official reports, captive narratives, oral history, and archaeology, and historians must be careful not to treat any single account as representative.

Section 68.19

Houston's Return, the Tehuacana Creek Councils, and the Diplomacy of 1841–1845

Sam Houston returned to the presidency in December 1841, after Lamar's administration had left the Republic deeply in debt and its Indigenous relations in disarray. Houston resumed a policy of negotiation. He appointed agents to deal with Indigenous nations, sought to restore trading relationships, and emphasized councils as a way to reduce conflict. The Republic's resources remained limited, and Houston faced opposition from supporters of Lamar and from settlers who favored military action. Meanwhile, Mexico mounted raids into Texas in 1842, including the brief capture of San Antonio, which diverted attention and resources.

Houston's strategy relied on trading houses and councils. Government-sponsored trading posts, such as those established in 1843 by the Torrey brothers near the Brazos, were intended to give Indigenous nations a reason to keep the peace by supplying goods and receiving furs, hides, and other products. Councils were held at several locations, including Bird's Fort in 1843, where a treaty was concluded in September with several nations, among them Caddo, Wichita-affiliated, Delaware, and other groups, though Comanche leaders were not parties. Subsequent meetings were held at Tehuacana Creek, where delegations from Caddo, Wichita-affiliated peoples, Delaware, Comanche, and other nations met Texas commissioners during 1843 and 1844. These meetings were occasions of ceremony and speeches, with gifts exchanged and promises made.

The October 1844 council at Tehuacana Creek was the most significant. Texas commissioners negotiated with Comanche leaders, including Penateka leaders such as Buffalo Hump, along with representatives of other nations, and an agreement was signed on October 9, 1844. Its text committed the parties to peace, arrangements for authorized trade, the handling of captives, and protection against unauthorized crossings into Indigenous hunting grounds. Crucially, proposed articles specifying a fixed territorial boundary were struck during the October 1844 council before the treaty was signed. The Texas Senate subsequently ratified the signed agreement on January 24, 1845, and President Anson Jones signed it on February 5, without the deleted fixed-boundary articles. The agreement reflected the willingness of some Comanche leaders to negotiate, but it should not be mistaken for Texan recognition of a permanent Comanche territorial boundary.

What it did not settle is equally important. The boundary was the central unresolved issue. Texas negotiators considered lines delimiting Indigenous hunting grounds, but the final ratified text did not preserve a fixed line. Comanche leaders had no reason to assume that Texan proposals could unilaterally define their lands, and they had little reason to believe that the Texas government could restrain its settlers. Not all Comanche bands were represented, and leaders who signed could not bind others. Texan enforcement capacity was limited, and settlers continued to move westward. Ranger companies, such as those of Jack Hays, continued to fight Comanche parties, and in 1844 engagements such as Walker's Creek showed both sides adapting to new weapons. As a result, the peace of 1844 was fragile, and it was soon overtaken by annexation.

Primary Source Window IV

The Tehuacana Creek Treaty of October 9, 1844

Creators: Commissioners representing the Republic of Texas and leaders of the Comanche and other Indigenous nations · Date: October 9, 1844 · Type: Treaty text with associated council records and correspondence

Context: The treaty concluded a series of councils at Tehuacana Creek during Houston's second presidency. An accessible transcription of the October 9, 1844 treaty is maintained by the Lipan Apache Tribe of Texas. The published Texas Indian Papers, 1844–1845 records the proposed boundary articles being struck during council negotiations; it should be read alongside the October 1844 council proceedings, which record the removal of proposed fixed-boundary articles before the treaty was signed, and the subsequent 1845 ratification.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: The treaty records commitments of peace, authorized trade, limits on incursions into Indigenous hunting grounds, and provisions concerning captives. Proposals for a definite frontier boundary did not become part of the final ratified agreement. The names of Indigenous participants as recorded in the text reflect the transcription practices of the commissioners.

Analysis: The treaty shows several Indigenous nations negotiating with the Republic on matters of trade and territory, and it illustrates how Indigenous leaders used diplomatic procedures to seek recognition and security.

Limitations: The text reflects Texan drafting and translation. It does not establish a fixed boundary, demonstrate effective enforcement, or reveal how each Indigenous party understood territorial access. The agreement's effects were limited by the Republic's weak enforcement capacity and by annexation.

Perspective: The source represents the formal terms agreed by negotiators. It does not represent Comanche dissenters or settlers who opposed the treaty, nor Mexican perspectives.

Section 68.20

Texas Annexation, Indigenous Sovereignty, and the Borderlands on the Eve of War

The annexation of Texas by the United States came through a joint resolution of Congress in March 1845, acceptance by a Texas convention and the Texas Congress in the summer of 1845, and the formal admission of Texas to the Union in December 1845. Mexico, which had never recognized Texan independence, broke diplomatic relations with the United States in 1845. The boundary was in dispute: Texas claimed the Rio Grande, while Mexico, if it conceded any Texan territory, pointed to the Nueces River. Texas retained control of its public lands as a condition of annexation, a provision that complicated later federal Indian policy in the state.

Annexation altered the claims and responsibilities of expanding states but did not extinguish Indigenous sovereignty or create effective control over the borderlands. The Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, and other nations remained in possession of much of the territory the United States and Texas now claimed. The United States, which had acquired Texas' claims and its obligations, began to deal with Indigenous nations through its own diplomacy; in 1846 it negotiated a treaty with Comanche and other nations, which, like earlier agreements, did not settle questions of boundaries and sovereignty. The United States, like Mexico and the Republic of Texas before it, claimed more than it could govern.

Indigenous power was also entangled with the causes of the approaching war. Historians such as Brian DeLay have argued that Comanche and other raiding in northern Mexico weakened Mexican states, deepened regional disaffection from Mexico City, and contributed to a perception among Americans that Mexico could not govern or defend its northern provinces. On this view, Indigenous warfare was not a background condition but a factor shaping American expansionist attitudes and Mexican responses. The argument is debated, and other causes, including slavery, American territorial ambition, and Mexican political instability, are also emphasized, but the interpretation underlines that Indigenous actions had continental consequences.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Political Power Before and After Independence and Annexation

What Changed

  • The governments claiming the region changed from Spain to Mexico, to the Republic of Texas, and then to the United States
  • The Spanish gift and ration system weakened, and some peace arrangements failed
  • Anglo-American settlement grew rapidly in Texas, and migrant Indigenous communities in East Texas were displaced
  • Comanche and Apache conflicts with northern Mexican states intensified, and new weapons and markets altered raiding

What Stayed the Same

  • Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, and other nations retained control of large territories and the capacity to shape regional politics
  • Indigenous leaders continued to negotiate with several governments at once
  • Trade, captivity, and kinship networks continued to connect communities across borders
  • Governments continued to claim more territory than they could administer

Pattern. Change was uneven and contested: it was fastest in East Texas and the Mexican north, and slower on the Plains. Settler and migrant communities experienced the sharpest disruption, while Plains nations experienced greater continuity of power. Change was abrupt for the Texas Cherokee and gradual for most Plains communities.

The borderlands of 1845 were therefore not empty ground awaiting incorporation. They were a contested space in which Indigenous nations, a weakened Mexican republic, and an expanding United States held overlapping claims, and where the outcome of the coming war would depend in part on Indigenous decisions. Chapter Summary

Chapter 68 has argued that the borderlands between the Arkansas River and the Rio Grande were not empty territory waiting to be absorbed by the United States. Between Mexican independence in 1821 and the annexation of Texas in 1845, Comanche, Apache, Kiowa, Wichita-affiliated, Caddo, Tonkawa, Lipan, Pueblo, Diné, Ute, and migrant nations exercised significant political, economic, and military authority. Governments claimed far more than they governed.

The chapter began with the distinction between claimed and effective authority and with the political geography inherited by Mexico. Comanchería, a zone of Comanche homeland and influence rather than a fixed state, rested on horses, bison, trade, diplomacy, and raiding. Apache peoples were a family of distinct communities, including Chiricahua, Mescalero, Western Apache, Jicarilla, and Lipan, whose relations with Mexican authorities varied. Other nations pursued their own interests, and none was simply subordinate.

Mexican independence changed the frontier's government but not its fundamental conditions. Fiscal weakness, political instability, and the limited reach of Mexico City strained the presidios, gifts, rations, and peace establishments inherited from Spain. The deterioration was regionally uneven and resulted from Indigenous decisions as well as Mexican policies, including local militia actions and state scalp bounties.

Mexican colonization policy brought a large Anglo-American population into Texas through the empresario system. The policy was meant to strengthen Mexican control but created a settler society with weak ties to Mexico, entangled with slavery, and located within Indigenous homelands. Mier y Terán's reports show that Mexican officials recognized the gap between claim and capacity.

The borderlands economy joined Indigenous, Mexican, Texan, and American markets through trade in horses, mules, hides, and manufactured goods, alongside tribute, raiding, and the exchange of captives. Historians distinguish these activities and recognize that Indigenous economic power gave Indigenous leaders political leverage. The 1830s and 1840s brought escalating Apache and Comanche conflict with northern Mexican states, with devastating effects on civilians of all communities.

In Texas, Cherokee and other migrants built communities and sought recognition. The February 1836 treaty promised land but was rejected, and under Lamar the Texas Cherokee were expelled after the Battle of the Neches in 1839, a campaign justified by accusations that were only partly supported by evidence. Houston's diplomacy and Lamar's military policy offered contrasting approaches, and both were limited by the Republic's weakness.

The Council House Fight of March 1840 and the Great Comanche Raid of August 1840 illustrate how diplomacy failed when participants held different understandings of protocol, captives, and boundaries. Plum Creek and subsequent campaigns did not end Comanche power. New Mexico and the western borderlands showed other patterns of Pueblo, Diné, Ute, and Apache relations, and captivity, slavery, and kinship shaped everyday life across the region.

Houston's second administration revived diplomacy, culminating in the Tehuacana Creek treaty of October 1844; proposed fixed-boundary articles had been deleted before the treaty was signed, leaving territorial limits unresolved. Annexation in December 1845 transferred Texan claims to the United States without extinguishing Indigenous sovereignty. The chapter concludes that Indigenous actions shaped the political geography the United States inherited and formed part of the context for the war treated in Chapter 69.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 68 matters because it corrects a map. Older narratives treated Mexican Texas and the Republic as stages in the westward advance of the United States, with Indigenous nations as obstacles. The evidence examined here shows a region governed by several overlapping systems, among which Indigenous nations were often the most effective.

The chapter also reveals processes that run through the textbook: governments claiming more than they could govern, treaties understood differently by their parties, migrants displaced from one homeland creating new communities in another, and economic systems linking Indigenous nations to distant markets. These patterns appeared earlier in the Covenant Chain and the diplomacy of the Great Lakes and recur in later treaty and reservation chapters.

What followed was not inevitable. Peace agreements were reached and broken, and different decisions by Mexican, Texan, and Indigenous leaders might have produced other outcomes. Yet the conflicts of these years shaped the world inherited by the United States, and Chapter 69 examines how war and territorial transfer changed Indigenous geopolitics without ending Indigenous sovereignty.

Finally, the chapter shows why careful reading of sources matters. Records of captivity, violence, and diplomacy were written by participants with interests, and historians must read them critically while keeping in view the suffering of all communities and the political agency of Indigenous nations.
