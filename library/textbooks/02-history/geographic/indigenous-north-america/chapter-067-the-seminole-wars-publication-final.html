The Seminole Wars

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On the last Monday of 1835, two attacks occurred within hours of each other in the Florida interior. Near Fort King, a federal Indian agent was killed outside the army post he had helped to direct. Some distance to the south, a column of United States soldiers marching between Fort Brooke and Fort King was destroyed. The coincidence was not an accident, and it marked the point at which a long political struggle over removal became open war.

The date was December 28, 1835. The agent was Wiley Thompson, who had spent the previous two years pressing Seminole leaders to accept the terms of a removal treaty that many of them regarded as invalid. The soldiers were a command of roughly one hundred men under Major Francis Dade, sent northward from Tampa Bay to reinforce the garrison at Fort King. Seminole fighters, assembled in secret over preceding weeks, struck both targets on the same day. The attack on Dade's column left only a handful of survivors. The killing of Thompson removed the official most directly associated with the removal process. Together the two actions announced that a substantial number of Seminole communities would not leave Florida without being compelled, and that they were prepared to resist compulsion with organized force.

Even this opening episode shows why the Seminole Wars are difficult to narrate responsibly. Nineteenth-century American writers called the destruction of Dade's column a "massacre," a word that placed the event outside the category of legitimate warfare and helped justify the vengeance that followed. Seminole participants and many later historians have preferred to describe it as a military engagement in which a trained force marching through contested country was ambushed by an opponent who had chosen the ground. The surviving accounts come from a few wounded soldiers, from army inquiries, and from later interviews with Seminole participants recorded by American officers, each shaped by purpose and by the circumstances of recording. This chapter therefore treats the event, as it treats the entire conflict, as something to be reconstructed from evidence of unequal quality rather than copied from the vocabulary of any one side.

The conflict that began in this way belongs to a much longer story. The people called Seminoles by outsiders had formed in Florida over the eighteenth century from several sources: Muscogee-speaking migrants from the Creek country to the north, Miccosukee-speaking communities, remnants of older Indigenous peoples of the peninsula, and allies who had left slavery in the southern colonies and states. Florida was Spanish territory for most of that period. It was a borderland where Spain, Britain, and then the United States competed, and where Indigenous towns exercised a degree of independence that no neighboring government could eliminate. The conflicts that historians now call the Seminole Wars arose from the collision between that independence and the expansion of a slaveholding republic that wanted Florida's land, wanted an end to a refuge for people escaping slavery, and eventually wanted to remove the Seminoles altogether.

Conventional United States history divides the story into three wars: the First Seminole War of 1817 to 1818, the Second of 1835 to 1842, and the Third of 1855 to 1858. The Seminole Tribe of Florida and many Seminole historians emphasize instead a substantially continuous struggle against encroachment and removal that did not end with any treaty or declaration. Both ways of dividing the past are legitimate and each reveals something. The three-war framework follows the rhythm of American military campaigns and congressional appropriations. The longer framework follows the persistence of Seminole communities and the refusal of many of them to accept any settlement that required leaving their homeland. This chapter uses the conventional numbering for clarity, explains it, and returns to the question in a Historical Debate near the end.

A second theme runs alongside the first. Black Seminole communities, descended from people who had escaped slavery in the Carolinas and Georgia or who had been born in Florida, were not marginal participants in these wars. They were neighbors, allies, interpreters, farmers, soldiers, and political actors whose fate was bound to the outcome. American slaveholders feared them, American commanders sometimes described the conflict in terms that made them central, and Black Seminole leaders such as Abraham and John Horse negotiated, fought, and sought safety on their own account. Any history that reduces the conflict to Indigenous resistance on one side and American armies on the other will misunderstand why the war began, why it lasted, and why it was so difficult to end.

The chapter also asks why a vastly more powerful state failed to accomplish its stated objective. The Second Seminole War lasted roughly seven years, cost the lives of approximately fifteen hundred American soldiers according to commonly cited estimates, and consumed sums that contemporaries found staggering, yet it ended without the removal of every Seminole from Florida. Part of the explanation lies in geography, since the wetlands, hammocks, rivers, and prairies of the peninsula punished large conventional forces. Part lies in Seminole strategy, which relied on dispersal, mobility, and the use of ecological knowledge. Part lies in the political contradictions of the American war effort, including quarrels among commanders, shifting policies, and reliance on short-term volunteers. And part lies in the willingness of Seminole families to endure extraordinary hardship rather than accept deportation.

None of this should obscure the cost. Seminole towns were burned, fields destroyed, families captured, and thousands of people deported across the Gulf to Indian Territory, a process whose later stages belong to the fuller narrative of Chapter 66. Resistance did not prevent grave losses. It did, however, prevent the elimination of the Seminole presence in Florida, and that outcome shaped the later history of the Seminole Tribe of Florida, the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida, and the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma. The chapter builds on the origins of federal removal policy traced in Chapter 65 and the removal experiences of the Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw examined in Chapter 66, and it prepares the way for the wider continental conflicts of Chapters 68 through 72.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Seminole communities formed in Florida during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and why they should not be described as a single centralized polity.
  2. Analyze the role of slavery, fugitive communities, and slaveholder interests in the origins of the First and Second Seminole Wars.
  3. Trace the sequence of events from the Creek War and the destruction of the fortified settlement at Prospect Bluff through Andrew Jackson's invasion of Spanish Florida and the transfer of Florida to the United States.
  4. Evaluate the treaties of Moultrie Creek, Payne's Landing, and Fort Gibson, distinguishing the federal claim of consent from disputes over the authority of signatories and the positions of Seminole communities.
  5. Distinguish the roles of Micanopy, Osceola, Jumper, Alligator, Abiaka, Coacoochee, and other Seminole leaders, and explain why no single figure can stand for Seminole resistance as a whole.
  6. Explain how Florida's wetlands, rivers, hammocks, and prairies functioned as strategic resources, and why geography alone does not account for the outcome of the war.
  7. Assess the military and diplomatic contributions of Black Seminole leaders, including Abraham and John Horse, and the ways their interests converged with and differed from those of other Seminole groups.
  8. Interpret contested events, including the destruction of Dade's column, the Battle of Lake Okeechobee, and the seizure of Osceola and Coacoochee under a flag of truce, by comparing the kinds of evidence available.
  9. Analyze the transition to a war of attrition and its consequences for Seminole civilians, subsistence systems, and families.
  10. Compare the official endings of the wars in 1818, 1842, and 1858 with Seminole understandings of continuing resistance.
  11. Evaluate the historiographical debate over whether the Seminole Wars were three separate conflicts or phases of one prolonged struggle.
  12. Connect the military outcomes of the wars to the survival of Seminole communities in Florida and to the later tribal governments descended from them.

Historical Context

The Florida peninsula in 1800 was home to people whose ancestors had arrived at different times and from different directions. The older Indigenous populations of Florida, including the Timucua and Apalachee, had been devastated during the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries by epidemic disease, Spanish mission disruption, and raids by Carolina-allied forces, developments discussed in Chapter 41. Into the depopulated country came Muscogee-speaking people from the Creek towns to the north, who moved south beginning in the early eighteenth century in search of hunting grounds, trade, and autonomy from the political pressures of the Creek heartland. They were joined by Miccosukee-speaking communities, by survivors of earlier Florida peoples, and by people who had left slavery in the English colonies. Outsiders eventually applied the name Seminole to the diverse communities that resulted, a term whose history is explored in a Vocabulary box in Section 67.1.

Spain held Florida for most of this period, apart from the British interval from 1763 to 1783, and its authority outside a few posts was thin. Spanish officials valued Florida as a buffer against the English colonies and later the United States, and they treated Seminole towns as political partners whose goodwill mattered. Trading firms operating from Spanish Florida supplied manufactured goods in exchange for deerskins, and Seminole communities raised corn, beans, squash, and other crops, gathered coontie and wild foods, hunted, and kept substantial herds of cattle and horses. Settlements were dispersed, often organized around towns with their own leaders, and the political ties among them were real but not centralized.

The United States, meanwhile, was expanding southward. Georgia and the Mississippi Territory wanted land, and slaveholders in both wanted to prevent Florida from serving as a refuge. The War of 1812 and the Creek War of 1813 and 1814, treated in Chapter 63 and Chapter 28, drove thousands of Muscogee refugees into Florida, increased tensions along the border, and gave American commanders a pretext for crossing it. The result was a sequence of interventions in which territorial ambition, security claims, and the defense of slavery became inseparable. Understanding the Seminole Wars requires holding all three of these motives in view, along with the political choices of Seminole leaders who were not simply reacting to American pressure but making their own calculations.

A word about scope is needed. This chapter does not retell the ideological and legislative origins of removal policy, which belong to Chapter 65, nor the removal experiences of the five southeastern nations, which belong to Chapter 66. It refers to removal treaties and deportations when they explain Seminole resistance and American strategy. Its own responsibility is the political and military history of the Seminole Wars: the formation of the communities that fought, the diplomacy and campaigns that structured the conflict, the role of Black Seminoles, the nature of the American effort, and the survival of Seminole Florida.

Division I — Seminole Florida and the Origins of War · Section 67.1

Seminole Peoples, Florida Homelands, and the Borderlands World

The Seminoles were not a single people who migrated intact into Florida, and they did not come into existence when American armies arrived. Their communities formed over the eighteenth century through the movement and combination of several populations. The largest group consisted of Muscogee-speaking people from the Creek towns of present-day Georgia and Alabama who moved into northern and central Florida beginning in the early eighteenth century. Some were drawn by hunting territory and trade, others by the desire to live at a distance from the political pressures of the Creek heartland, and others by the opportunities that Spanish Florida's unsettled interior offered. Among them were people from the Oconee and other Lower Creek towns. A second group spoke Mikasuki, a language distinct from Muscogee though related to it within the Muskogean family, and the communities that spoke it lived mainly in the northern peninsula around Lake Miccosukee and later farther south. To these were added surviving descendants of older Florida peoples and, increasingly, people who had escaped slavery in the English colonies and the later southern states.

The result was a cluster of towns rather than a state. Seminole political life was organized primarily at the level of the town, and towns were linked through clan relationships, intermarriage, shared ceremonial life such as the annual Green Corn ceremony, and councils at which leaders negotiated matters of common concern. Authority was exercised by town leaders, councils, and individuals respected for particular abilities, and the standing of a leader depended on persuasion and performance rather than command. Some figures held greater prestige than others. The leaders of the Alachua communities in north-central Florida, associated with Cowkeeper (Ahaya) in the mid-eighteenth century and with his successors, acquired particular prominence because of the size of their settlements and their herds. But prominence did not amount to sovereignty over all Seminoles, and Spanish, British, and American officials who sought a single chief with whom to deal repeatedly misread this political structure. Students should be especially wary of narratives that present the Seminoles as a unified tribe under one ruler, because that assumption shapes how treaties, signatures, and military leadership are interpreted throughout this chapter.

The Alachua country, centered on a great savanna known in the later period as Payne's Prairie, offers the clearest early window into Seminole life. When the naturalist William Bartram traveled through northern Florida in the 1770s, he described prosperous towns, extensive planting fields, orchards, and large herds of cattle and horses, and he recorded a political world in which leaders negotiated with British and Spanish officials from a position of considerable independence. Bartram's account, published in the following decade, is a valuable but outsider's description, written by a visitor with particular interests, and it must be read alongside Spanish administrative records, archaeological evidence from town sites, and Seminole historical memory preserved in tribal sources. These sources together show an economy that combined agriculture, cattle raising, hunting, fishing, and the gathering of wild plants such as coontie, whose starchy root provided a staple food. They also show a commercial orientation toward the deerskin trade and later the cattle trade, supplied by trading houses operating under Spanish authority.

Florida as Borderland

Florida was a borderland in the full sense of the word: a space in which several political powers claimed authority and none could impose it consistently. Spain, which had first colonized the peninsula in the sixteenth century, retained nominal sovereignty over East Florida and, for much of the period, West Florida, apart from the years 1763 to 1783 when Britain held both. Spanish officials depended on Indigenous alliances to defend the colony against the English colonies and later the United States. They traded with Seminole towns, distributed gifts, and sought to maintain the goodwill of leaders whose military capacity exceeded that of the small Spanish garrisons. British commercial and military influence persisted after 1783 through trade networks and, during the War of 1812, through direct military outreach, a point of obvious concern to American officials. The United States, for its part, treated Florida as both a security problem and a prize. American leaders from Thomas Jefferson onward discussed its acquisition, and expansionist settlers and officials in Georgia pressed for action.

The Seminoles stood at the center of this contest, and they acted within it rather than merely suffering it. They allied with Spanish and, on occasion, British authorities when doing so served their interests. They bargained over trade and land, and some leaders participated in short-lived political ventures, such as the efforts of the adventurer William Augustus Bowles in the late 1790s to build a Muskogee state with British and Indigenous support. Seminole leaders understood the stakes of American expansion from experience. By the early nineteenth century they had seen American forces invade Alachua during the Patriot War of 1812 and had watched the destruction of towns, the capture of cattle, and the killing of leaders. This knowledge conditioned their later diplomacy. The historical Seminole Wars were not the first confrontation between Seminole communities and the United States. They were the continuation, on a much larger scale, of a conflict already under way.

This chapter's account must therefore resist a common narrative in which Seminole history begins with the arrival of American troops. Seminole communities had histories, political traditions, and strategies of their own long before 1817. They also had diversity. Mikasuki and Muscogee speakers did not always share the same interests, and the Black communities allied with them occupied a position different from either. These differences would matter during the wars, in decisions about whether to fight, negotiate, or emigrate, and in the eventual division of the Seminole people into the several communities that exist today.

Historical Vocabulary
Seminole

Definition: A name applied to the diverse Indigenous communities of Florida that emerged during the eighteenth century from Muscogee-speaking and Mikasuki-speaking migrants, surviving older Florida peoples, and others, and that later developed into the present Seminole and Miccosukee peoples.

Historical context: The word is generally traced to the Spanish term cimarrón, meaning wild or runaway, which entered Muscogee as a term for people living apart from the Creek towns. Scholars discuss the details of the derivation, and the original connotations may have varied, but there is broad agreement that the word reflects the Creek and Spanish perception of these communities as autonomous. The term was adopted in English-language usage during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and eventually became a political identity claimed by the communities themselves.

Why it matters: The name reminds students that Seminole identity is the product of a historical process, not a primordial category, and that it was shaped by autonomy, mobility, and the incorporation of different peoples. That history makes it more, not less, authentic as a Native identity: peoples in every region of North America have changed in composition and name over time.

Potential misunderstanding: Because the word is connected to runaways, some popular writing has described the Seminoles as a group of Creek "outcasts" or "renegades," language that echoes the vocabulary of their opponents. It is also misleading to treat Seminoles as a single polity. The communities that bore the name spoke at least two languages, lived in separate towns, and held different political positions on the questions of the era.

Division I · Section 67.2

Black Seminole Communities, Maroon Settlements, and the Struggle over Slavery

The history of Black communities in Florida is older than the Seminoles themselves. From the late seventeenth century, Spanish authorities in St. Augustine offered sanctuary to people escaping slavery in the English colonies, a policy that combined strategic calculation, religious aims, and rivalry with Britain. A royal decree of 1693 granted liberty to fugitives who accepted Catholic baptism and swore allegiance to the Spanish crown, and although Spanish practice was inconsistent and slavery itself remained legal in Spanish Florida, the policy drew people southward. In 1738 Spanish officials established a free Black settlement at Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, north of St. Augustine, which was settled by refugees and organized with its own militia. Fort Mose was an important precedent. It demonstrated that Black communities in Florida could be organized as free settlements with legal standing and military roles, and it linked the later history of Black Seminoles to an older tradition of freedom-seeking in the Spanish borderland.

After the cession of Florida to Britain in 1763, many residents of Fort Mose left for Cuba with the departing Spanish, but the movement of people across the border did not stop. During the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, enslaved people in Georgia and the Carolinas continued to flee southward, some reaching Seminole towns, some settling in independent communities, and some joining the Indigenous towns as allies or dependents. Many came from the rice-growing coastal regions, where they had developed distinctive languages and agricultural expertise, and they brought skills that were valuable in Florida: knowledge of rice and corn cultivation, experience with livestock, familiarity with English-speaking officials and traders, and in many cases fluency in several languages. The Seminoles called Black people by a Muscogee term often rendered estelusti. The communities that formed in the early nineteenth century near Alachua, along the Suwannee River, and later in central and southern Florida became known to outsiders as the "Seminole Negroes" or "Indian Negroes," and in modern scholarship as Black Seminoles.

The relationship between these communities and the Seminole towns was neither uniform nor easy to summarize. Some Black Seminoles lived in autonomous villages of their own, growing crops, raising livestock, and bearing arms, and they owed their Seminole neighbors a portion of their harvest or other recognition of an alliance. Contemporary American observers described such people as "slaves" of Seminole leaders, and the label has persisted in some literature, but historians have emphasized that the relationship differed from chattel slavery as practiced in the American South. Black Seminoles in these arrangements lived apart from their Seminole patrons, controlled their own labor, kept most of what they produced, were not subject to sale in the same manner, and often exercised significant influence. Others, however, were held by Seminole owners in conditions that were more constraining, and some had been acquired through purchase or capture. Still others were fully incorporated into Seminole families through marriage or adoption and eventually counted as members of Seminole clans. A responsible account must therefore distinguish among maroons living in independent settlements, people in tributary or allied relationships, people held more directly as property, and people absorbed into kinship networks, and it should recognize that individual status could change over a lifetime.

Why Slaveholders Feared These Communities

American slaveholders understood the existence of these communities as a direct threat. Florida was a short distance from the cotton and rice plantations of Georgia, and Black Seminole settlements demonstrated that escape was possible and that fugitives could live in relative security, form families, and bear arms. Georgia planters repeatedly demanded that the federal government secure Florida or help them recover people who had escaped. Slave catchers raided across the border, and Creek leaders aligned with the United States sometimes joined them, seeking captives for sale. Black Seminole communities, aware of these dangers, trained for defense, built fortified positions, and relied on alliances with Seminole towns for protection. Their interests thus converged with those of the Seminole leaders in a practical sense: the preservation of Florida as a space beyond American slaveholding authority protected Black freedom and Seminole autonomy alike.

That convergence was not complete. Seminole leaders sometimes treated Black allies as bargaining assets in dealings with American officials, and the possibility that individual leaders might trade away Black communities in exchange for peace was a constant source of anxiety. Black Seminole leaders such as Abraham, who served as interpreter for the principal Alachua leader Micanopy, and John Horse, who rose to prominence during the Second War, therefore pursued their own political strategies. They negotiated with American officers, argued for guarantees of freedom, and in some cases acted independently of Seminole decisions. The evidence for these dynamics comes from American military correspondence, the records of agents and officers, Spanish archival materials, and the oral traditions of Black Seminole descendants, and each type of source reflects particular perspectives. American documents often describe Black Seminoles as appendages of Seminole leaders, while oral and later historical accounts emphasize their autonomy. Historians read the sources against one another to reconstruct a relationship that was, in practice, a negotiated alliance with inequalities, mutual dependence, and shared danger.

This section establishes the foundations for everything that follows. The First Seminole War was triggered in part by American efforts to destroy communities of people who had escaped slavery. The Second involved repeated attempts to separate Black Seminoles from their Indigenous allies and to return them to slavery or deport them. The Black Seminoles' fate, in other words, was central to the conflicts and not a subsidiary theme. The chapter's treatment of slavery is also consistent with the framework of Chapter 53, which examines Indigenous slavery, captivity, and adoption: Seminole society, like many Indigenous societies of the Southeast, contained a range of statuses and relationships that do not map neatly onto categories drawn from the American plantation South.

Historical Vocabulary
Maroon and Black Seminole

Definition: A maroon is a person who escaped slavery and lived in an independent or semi-independent community, and a maroon community is a settlement established by such people. Black Seminole is a modern scholarly term for people of African descent who lived in alliance or association with Seminole communities in Florida, and for their descendants.

Historical context: The word maroon derives from the Spanish cimarrón and was used across the Atlantic world for communities of people who had escaped slavery, from Jamaica to Brazil to Florida. In the early nineteenth century contemporaries used a variety of terms for Black Seminoles, including "Seminole Negroes" and "Indian Negroes," which reflected American assumptions and which many historians use only in quotation or with explanation. The communities themselves used names derived from their own languages and kinship networks.

Why it matters: The term places Black Seminole history in the wider Atlantic history of resistance to slavery and shows that the Seminole Wars were part of a larger struggle over freedom in the Americas. At the same time, the term Black Seminole signals a specific relationship with Indigenous communities that does not apply to every maroon society.

Potential misunderstanding: The label does not mean that all Black people in Seminole country were free, nor that all were enslaved. Nor should the nineteenth-century racial categories embedded in the contemporary terms be projected backward as though they described stable identities. Many individuals had mixed ancestry, spoke several languages, and moved among communities, and the boundaries among "Black," "Seminole," and "Creek" were negotiated socially rather than fixed by law.

Division I · Section 67.3

American Expansion, the Creek War, and the Road to the First Seminole War

American pressure on Florida intensified during the first decade of the nineteenth century. In 1810 and 1812, the United States asserted claims to portions of West Florida, and in 1812 a group of Georgia adventurers, supported by federal agents and by militia, attempted to seize East Florida in what is known as the Patriot War. The effort failed to bring about annexation, partly because the federal government disavowed it, but American and Georgia forces entered Alachua in 1812 and 1813, destroying towns, killing the Alachua leader King Payne, and seizing cattle and horses. The Seminoles of Alachua withdrew to new locations to the west and south, including the Withlacoochee region, where the leader Micanopy established himself. These campaigns were an early precedent for later conflict, and they demonstrated to Seminole leaders that American forces were willing to cross the border to attack.

The Creek War of 1813 and 1814, examined in the context of the War of 1812 in Chapter 63, transformed the situation. The conflict began as a civil war within the Muscogee Confederacy between the factions known as Red Sticks, who opposed American expansion and the acculturative policies of the Creek leadership allied with the federal agent Benjamin Hawkins, and Creek leaders who sought accommodation. American forces intervened, and in March 1814 General Andrew Jackson's army and its Cherokee and Lower Creek allies defeated the Red Sticks at Horseshoe Bend. In August 1814 the Treaty of Fort Jackson imposed a massive land cession on the Creek Nation as a whole, including on the Lower Creeks who had fought alongside Jackson, an amount commonly estimated at more than twenty million acres. Thousands of Red Stick survivors fled south into Florida, where some joined Seminole towns, others established their own communities, and some sought British support. The arrival of these refugees increased the population and military capacity of the Indigenous communities in Florida, but it also intensified American suspicion that Florida was a base for hostile forces.

Among the refugees were leaders such as Peter McQueen and the prophet Josiah Francis (Hillis Hadjo), whose names appear repeatedly in the documentary record of the period. The Red Stick refugees brought experience of war, a rhetoric of resistance, and a deep hostility to the Americans who had destroyed their towns. Their relationship to the older Seminole communities was complicated, for the refugees were not simply absorbed. They occupied separate towns, pursued their own diplomacy, and in some cases came into tension with older residents. The conventional label "Seminole" thus obscures a landscape of overlapping communities that were adapting to a new situation.

The British Presence and Prospect Bluff

During the War of 1812, British forces entered the Gulf Coast and sought to recruit Indigenous and Black allies against the United States. Royal Marines under Edward Nicolls established a post on the Apalachicola River at Prospect Bluff in 1814, recruited Seminoles, Red Sticks, and people who had escaped slavery, and promised freedom to those who joined the British. When the British withdrew in 1815, they left behind the fortifications, together with substantial quantities of arms and ammunition. The site was occupied by hundreds of Black people, including those who had served with the British and others who had fled from plantations in Georgia and Spanish Florida, together with a smaller number of Choctaw and Seminole allies. The fort and the settlements nearby, which American writers called the Negro Fort, became a community of farmers and soldiers with a command structure that included a Black officer known as Garçon and a Choctaw leader.

The fort's existence was intolerable to Georgia slaveholders and to American commanders. It stood on Spanish territory but controlled access to the Apalachicola River, along which the United States sent supplies to its posts. It was a refuge for people whom their owners regarded as property, and its armed garrison was a visible challenge to the security of slavery. In 1816 General Andrew Jackson, who commanded the southern division of the army, authorized an operation to destroy it. An army force under Lieutenant Colonel Duncan Clinch, accompanied by Lower Creek allies under William McIntosh, and a naval detachment of gunboats advanced up the river. On July 27, 1816, after an exchange of fire, a heated shot from one of the gunboats reached the fort's powder magazine. The resulting explosion killed most of the people inside. Estimates of the number present and the number killed vary, but accounts commonly describe several hundred people in and around the fort and a death toll in the range of two hundred and fifty or more, with only a small number of survivors. Garçon and the Choctaw chief were reportedly executed afterward.

The destruction of the fort was a turning point. It showed that the United States was prepared to violate Spanish territory to destroy a community of people who had escaped slavery, and it demonstrated the military potential of such communities. It also taught Black Seminole and Seminole communities that settlements in exposed locations were vulnerable, and many moved farther from the border. The event remained a powerful memory in Black Seminole history, and it helps explain the wariness with which Black communities would later approach American offers of peace.

Fowltown and the Outbreak of War

In the aftermath, border violence escalated. Georgia settlers and militia raided across the Florida line, Indigenous raiders retaliated, and American officials, notably General Edmund P. Gaines at Fort Scott in southern Georgia, pressed for authority to act. The immediate trigger came over the status of land east of the Flint River. The Seminoles and their Creek neighbors in the border town of Fowltown, led by the Mikasuki-speaking leader Neamathla, refused to accept that the Treaty of Fort Jackson had ceded their lands, and Neamathla warned American troops not to cross the river. On November 21, 1817, after further exchanges, Gaines sent a force that attacked Fowltown and drove out its inhabitants. Nine days later, on November 30, Seminole fighters attacked an American supply boat on the Apalachicola commanded by Lieutenant Richard W. Scott, killing most of the roughly fifty people aboard, who included soldiers and some women and children accompanying them. American officials treated the attack as proof of Indigenous hostility, and the Secretary of War, John C. Calhoun, ordered General Jackson to take command of operations against the Seminoles.

The sequence illustrates how contemporary American accounts and later Seminole interpretations diverge. American officials described the war as a response to Seminole attacks and a defense of the frontier. Seminole accounts, as preserved in tribal historical materials and later scholarship, emphasize the attack on Fowltown, the invasion of Indigenous lands, and the earlier destruction of Prospect Bluff. A balanced reading recognizes that violence occurred on both sides, but it also recognizes that American commanders sought a confrontation, that the territorial claims at stake were contested, and that the conflict cannot be explained by frontier security alone. Territorial conquest, slavery, and the fate of Indigenous alliances were intertwined, and Georgia slaveholders and expansionist commanders stood to benefit from a conflict that extended American authority into Florida.

The First Seminole War, 1817–1818, and Andrew Jackson's Invasion

Andrew Jackson reached Fort Scott in March 1818 with a force that included Tennessee volunteers, Georgia militia, regular troops, and allied Lower Creek warriors under William McIntosh, a combined army of about three thousand men by most accounts. The Lower Creek allies are an important reminder that the First Seminole War was also a conflict among Indigenous peoples: Creek leaders aligned with the United States fought against Seminoles and Red Stick refugees, driven by their own interests, including slave catching, rivalry with Florida communities, and their relationship to the American government. Jackson's army moved down the Apalachicola River and then east toward the Mikasuki towns around Lake Miccosukee, burning settlements and destroying crops as it advanced. The Mikasuki leaders, recognizing the scale of the force, withdrew to the south, and there was little organized resistance. The principal result of the campaign through April 1818 was the destruction of towns and the displacement of their residents rather than a decisive battle.

On April 7, 1818, Jackson's army reached the Spanish post of St. Marks, which the Spanish commander surrendered after minimal resistance. At St. Marks Jackson arrested Alexander Arbuthnot, a Scottish trader who had worked among the Seminoles, represented their grievances to British officials, and served as an advocate and agent. The army then moved east to the Suwannee River, where it attacked the towns associated with the Seminole leader Boleck (Bowlegs) and with Black Seminole communities, destroying them in the last days of April. At the Suwannee the Americans captured Robert Ambrister, a former British Royal Marine who had been in Florida. Jackson convened a court of army officers to try both men on charges that included inciting the Indigenous population to war against the United States. The court found Arbuthnot guilty and sentenced him to death, and it sentenced Ambrister to death but then reconsidered and imposed a lighter punishment. Jackson overruled the second decision and ordered Ambrister shot. Both men were executed on April 29, 1818. Meanwhile, two Red Stick leaders, including Josiah Francis, had been lured aboard an American vessel flying British colors, taken prisoner, and hanged.

From the Suwannee, Jackson turned west toward Pensacola, the capital of Spanish West Florida. He occupied the town in late May after the Spanish governor withdrew to the fortress at Barrancas, and he installed an American garrison. In a matter of weeks the American commander had entered Spanish territory without clear congressional authorization, seized two Spanish posts and the provincial capital, destroyed numerous Indigenous towns, and executed two British subjects. The legality of these actions was immediately contested. Jackson claimed that he acted within orders and that the Seminoles' presence under Spanish protection justified his entry into Florida. His critics noted that the instructions he received from Calhoun had cautioned against attacking Spanish posts, and that the question of whether Monroe had privately approved a larger campaign remained in dispute, a controversy that would emerge when Jackson later claimed, on the basis of a private letter exchange, that he had presidential approval. Congress debated resolutions of censure in early 1819, and they failed.

Seminole Responses and the Meaning of Dispersal

Seminole and Red Stick responses to the invasion deserve more attention than the narrative of Jackson's march usually gives them. Faced with a much larger army, Seminole leaders avoided decisive engagements that could not be won, abandoned exposed towns, preserved their families and herds as far as possible, and withdrew southward, where the terrain was more difficult for American forces and farther from supply lines. This was a strategic choice, not a collapse. The dispersal imposed severe costs, including the loss of crops and cattle and the disruption of settled life. But it preserved the population and its capacity to resume resistance, and it became a template for later Seminole strategy. Black Seminole communities likewise moved southward, many of them settling in the Tampa Bay region and on the Manatee River in a community later called Angola, though the dispersal also made those settlements vulnerable, and in 1821 raiding parties of Creeks, reportedly operating with the encouragement of American slaveholding interests, attacked and carried off many of their inhabitants. Some survivors are traditionally said to have escaped by sea to the Bahamas, where descendants maintain a distinct Black Seminole heritage on Andros Island.

Diplomacy, the Adams–Onís Treaty, and the Transfer of Florida

The invasion produced an international crisis. Spain protested vigorously, and Britain, whose subjects had been executed, expressed concern but chose not to escalate. Within the Monroe administration, the cabinet debated how to respond, and Secretary of State John Quincy Adams defended Jackson's conduct in correspondence with Spain while also returning the captured posts to Spanish control. Adams's defense rested on the argument that Spain had failed to restrain Indigenous attacks from its territory, and it served a larger diplomatic purpose, for Adams was negotiating with the Spanish minister Luis de Onís over the cession of Florida and the boundary between the United States and Spanish territory to the west.

It would be a mistake to treat the Adams–Onís Treaty, signed on February 22, 1819, as simply a consequence of Jackson's campaign. Spain's weakness was a structural condition. Its American empire was breaking apart in wars of independence, its resources were committed elsewhere, and it could not defend a distant, thinly populated province against a determined neighbor. Negotiations had been under way before Jackson's invasion, and the treaty reflected a wider set of bargains, including the settlement of American claims against Spain and the definition of the western boundary of the Louisiana Purchase. Jackson's campaign undoubtedly demonstrated the vulnerability of Spanish Florida and strengthened the American negotiating position, but it was one factor among several. The Senate ratified the treaty in 1821, after delays caused by Spanish hesitation, and Florida was formally transferred to the United States in July of that year. Jackson became the first territorial governor, a symbolic as well as practical sign of what the cession meant.

For the Seminoles, the treaty changed the political situation fundamentally. Spain, with which Seminole leaders had maintained diplomatic and commercial relations for generations, left the scene. The communities that had not been parties to the treaty found themselves residents of a territory claimed by a state that had recently burned their towns. The First Seminole War ended as American wars often did, without a negotiated peace between the combatants. What followed, as the next division shows, was an effort to confine the Seminoles within a reservation and eventually to remove them from Florida entirely.

U.S. Territorial Florida, the Treaty of Moultrie Creek, and Removal Pressure

After 1821, Florida became a territory of the United States, and the Seminoles became subjects of an administration that regarded them as an obstacle to settlement. Territorial officials, many of them from the southern states, sought to open land for planters and farmers, and they viewed the Seminole towns of north-central Florida, with their fields, cattle, and Black allies, as incompatible with that goal. The federal government's immediate policy was concentration. Rather than negotiating a single agreement with Seminole leaders at a time of their choosing, American officials summoned them to a council in 1823 at Moultrie Creek, near St. Augustine, to arrange a boundary between Indigenous and settler land.

The Treaty of Moultrie Creek, concluded on September 18, 1823, required Seminole leaders to relinquish claims to most of the peninsula and accept a reservation of several million acres in the interior, inland from both coasts. The treaty promised annual payments over twenty years, the distribution of food and supplies for a limited time, livestock, and agricultural implements, and a federal commitment to protect the Seminoles within the reservation. In exchange, Seminole communities agreed to move from their existing settlements, many of them in the fertile north, to a district whose sandy soils and flooded lowlands were poorly suited to the agriculture on which they depended. The council was dominated by American officials and the negotiations were conducted under conditions of military pressure. Some Seminole leaders, notably Neamathla, resisted, and American officials worked to undermine his authority, eventually helping to depose him. The principal Alachua leader Micanopy and other signers accepted the treaty, but acceptance was partly a response to circumstances: the destruction of towns in earlier wars, the pressure of American forces, and the promise of rations.

The reservation proved unworkable. Communities uprooted from fertile land struggled to plant, and several years of drought and flood during the 1820s caused repeated food shortages, forcing Seminole families to seek supplies from the army or to roam beyond the reservation boundary to hunt and forage. Territorial authorities treated such movement as a violation of the treaty. The army established a line of posts, the most important of which was Fort King near present-day Ocala, established in 1824, and Fort Brooke at Tampa Bay, to monitor the Seminoles and enforce boundaries. Settlers pressed for the opening of reservation land, and slaveholders and slave catchers continued to claim Black Seminoles as fugitives, harassing communities and demanding their return. American officials sometimes acknowledged the difficulty of the Seminoles' position, but they did not change the underlying structure. The treaty had been designed to confine Seminole communities, not to secure their prosperity, and by the late 1820s influential voices in Florida and Washington were arguing that removal beyond the Mississippi was the only solution.

Payne's Landing and Fort Gibson

The Indian Removal Act of 1830, whose origins are treated in Chapter 65, gave federal officials a general framework for such arguments, and in 1832 they moved to apply it to the Seminoles. In May of that year, at Payne's Landing on the Ocklawaha River, Indian agent James Gadsden met with a group of Seminole leaders and secured a treaty, signed on May 9, that provided for the Seminoles to relinquish their lands in Florida and move west. The treaty's terms were conditional. It stipulated that a delegation of seven chiefs would travel to the Indian Territory to inspect the land assigned to them, that the Seminoles would move only if the delegation judged the land satisfactory and if the Seminoles already living with the Creeks in the West agreed to receive them, and that removal would take place within three years.

What happened next is among the most contested episodes in the history of the Seminole Wars. In the autumn of 1832 the delegation traveled west, accompanied by American officials and by the Black Seminole interpreter Abraham. After months of discussions at Fort Gibson, in what is now Oklahoma, the seven delegates signed an agreement on March 28, 1833 stating that they had examined the western lands and found them acceptable. When the delegates returned to Florida, many of them denied that they had agreed to anything binding on their people. Some claimed they had signed only to confirm that the land was suitable, not to commit the Seminoles to removal. Others charged that they had been pressured, that the interpretation had been misleading, or that they lacked the authority to speak for the communities of Florida. The Senate ratified the Payne's Landing treaty in April 1834, treating the Fort Gibson agreement as satisfying its conditions.

The dispute centered on authority and consent. Seminole political practice did not authorize a small delegation to commit all communities to a decision of such magnitude, and the conditions of the treaty itself required the agreement of the Seminoles in the West and of the delegates' constituents, neither of which had been clearly secured. The federal government's position was that Seminole representatives had signed a binding agreement and that the Seminoles were bound to leave by 1835. Seminole leaders, including Micanopy, Jumper, and Osceola, challenged this view, and the majority of communities rejected removal. The legal question was therefore bound up with the political one, and each side had a different definition of what constituted consent. This chapter does not recapitulate the broader treaty system of the Southeast, which is treated in Chapters 65 and 66, but the Seminole case is a particularly stark example of treaty-making in which a document was created by a small group under American supervision and then enforced against an entire people.

The Pressure Builds, 1834–1835

In 1834 the federal government appointed Wiley Thompson as Indian agent in Florida and charged him with arranging removal. Thompson convened a council at Fort King in April 1835 to announce that the Seminoles would be required to leave. Most leaders refused. Micanopy and others stated that they had not consented to removal and would not go, and Osceola, according to the traditional account, defied Thompson at the council. Thompson responded with escalating measures. He reportedly confined Osceola for a time, and he curtailed the sale of arms and ammunition to the Seminoles, a step that deprived them of the means of hunting as well as of defense. A group of leaders headed by Charley Emathla agreed to emigrate, and in the autumn of 1835 they began to sell cattle and prepare for departure. In late November Charley Emathla was killed, and contemporaries and later historians have generally attributed the killing to supporters of Osceola, who regarded it as punishment for betrayal of the common cause. The incident revealed a political division within the Seminole communities, and it showed that the resistance leaders were prepared to enforce unity by force. By December the army was reinforcing its posts, settlers were abandoning farms, and both sides were preparing for conflict.

Division II · Section 67.6

Seminole Leadership, Political Strategy, and the Decision to Resist

The decision to resist was not made by one man, and no account of the Second Seminole War should reduce it to the career of a single leader. Seminole political life was organized through towns and councils, and the war was prepared and conducted through the cooperation, and sometimes the disagreement, of several leaders with different bases of authority. Understanding those differences is essential to understanding the war's course.

Micanopy, the leading figure among the Alachua communities, held a hereditary position of exceptional prestige. He was the nephew and successor of earlier Alachua leaders and was recognized by American officials as the principal chief of the Seminoles, a title they were eager to assign because it simplified negotiation. His actual authority was more limited. He could convene councils and speak for his own communities, and his opinion carried weight, but he could not command other towns. Contemporary descriptions suggest a man who was cautious and who at times appeared reluctant to commit to open war, but who nevertheless took part in the decision to resist and was present at the Dade engagement according to later accounts. His position reflects the way hereditary standing and practical politics combined.

Osceola, known in Muscogee as Asi-Yahola, was a younger man of Muscogee descent who had grown up in Florida and had acquired a reputation as a capable fighter and an eloquent opponent of removal. He was not a hereditary chief. His influence rested on his skill as a war leader, his capacity to mobilize followers, and his willingness to confront American officials directly. American newspapers and later writers made him the face of the war, and he became a figure of exceptional fame during his captivity and after his death. The fame is itself a historical phenomenon, because it reflected American needs to personalize the conflict and later romanticized memory. It should not lead students to treat Osceola as the Seminoles' supreme ruler. He worked with and sometimes competed with other leaders, and his role in the early war, though significant, was one among several.

A Council of Leaders

Jumper (Ote Emathla) was a respected senior leader and speaker who served as a principal adviser to Micanopy and who played a major role in the planning of the Dade attack. Alligator (Halpatter), a leader of a Mikasuki-speaking community, became a major war leader and was later a principal source for American accounts of the Dade engagement, which he described in interviews with an army officer after capture. Abiaka, known to Americans as Sam Jones, was an elderly Mikasuki leader and spiritual figure whose reputation for resolution and religious authority made him a pivotal figure in the long resistance and, ultimately, in the survival of Seminole communities in southern Florida. Coacoochee (Wild Cat), a young leader and the son of an Alachua chief, emerged during the war as a capable commander and diplomat. Holatoochee, Tuskegee, Chitto Tustenuggee, and other leaders commanded smaller groups and contributed to the conduct of the war. These men differed in language, region, and constituency, and Seminole political practice allowed them to cooperate without surrendering their independence.

Black Seminole leaders formed a further element in the political structure. Abraham, who served as Micanopy's interpreter and adviser, moved between Seminole councils and American officials. John Horse, who emerged as an important figure in the course of the war, was a skilled fighter and negotiator. Other Black Seminoles served as scouts, interpreters, and military leaders of their own communities. Their influence derived from their knowledge of American institutions, their linguistic skills, and their capacity to mobilize fighters, and their interests in the war's outcome were distinct from those of their Indigenous allies.

Opposition to Removal and Disagreement over Strategy

It is important to distinguish opposition to removal from disagreement over how to resist. Most Seminole leaders opposed removal, though they differed in their assessment of what it would cost to prevent it. Some, such as Charley Emathla, concluded that resistance would fail and that emigration under negotiated terms would preserve their people better than war. Others believed that open resistance was the only course consistent with the survival of their communities. Even among those who favored resistance there were differences over timing, tactics, and the use of diplomacy. Some leaders hoped that the army would be deterred by evidence of Seminole strength, and others expected a prolonged conflict. The decision to strike in December 1835 reflected a developing consensus that the American military buildup left little time, and it was made with considerable planning. Seminole leaders gathered fighters, positioned them near the roads along which American reinforcements would travel, and coordinated attacks at separate locations.

The evidence for this planning comes in large part from Seminole statements recorded by American officers after the war began, notably Alligator's account, and from the behavior of the Seminoles themselves. Such sources must be treated cautiously, since the recorders interpreted what they heard and their questions framed the answers. But they agree on the main point: the decision to go to war was deliberate, collective, and strategic. It was not the impulsive act of a single warrior.

Cause and Effect

Why War Came in 1835

Long-term causes

American territorial expansion into Florida; the slaveholding interest in eliminating a refuge for people escaping slavery; the legacy of the First Seminole War and the destruction of Indigenous towns; the confinement of Seminole communities on a reservation with poor farmland under the Treaty of Moultrie Creek.

Short-term causes

The federal removal policy of the 1830s, the Treaty of Payne's Landing, and the disputed Fort Gibson agreement; the appointment of an agent charged with enforcing removal; restrictions on the sale of arms and ammunition; the division among Seminole leaders over whether to emigrate.

Immediate trigger

The American deadline for removal, the killing of Charley Emathla in November 1835, the reinforcement of American posts, and the Seminole leaders' decision to act before more troops arrived.

Event

The coordinated attacks of December 28, 1835, at Fort King and on Dade's column, followed by the Seminole and Black Seminole attacks on plantations along the St. Johns and Atlantic coast and the outbreak of general war.

Immediate effects

The mobilization of regular troops, volunteers, and militia; the abandonment of settlements across northern Florida; the entry of enslaved people from plantations into the conflict; the opening of a campaign that would last years.

Long-term consequences

The deportation of thousands of Seminoles and Black Seminoles; the survival of Seminole communities in southern Florida; the escalation of U.S. military methods; the Seminole understanding of themselves as unconquered.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the outbreak. Removal policy alone does not account for the willingness of leaders to risk war, since the Seminoles had survived earlier pressures through accommodation. The interaction of territorial pressure, the defense of slavery, the contested treaty, the difficulty of subsistence on the reservation, and the political choices of Seminole leaders produced the decision. Contingency also mattered: the timing of the attacks reflected judgments about American troop movements that might have been made differently.

Division II · Section 67.7

December 1835: Fort King, Dade's Battle, and the Beginning of Open War

In late December 1835, the American garrison at Fort King, near present-day Ocala, was small, and the army's principal concern was to reinforce it. The post stood in the interior along a military road that connected it with Fort Brooke at Tampa Bay, roughly a hundred miles to the south. The commander at Tampa, ordered to send reinforcements, dispatched two companies of soldiers under Major Francis L. Dade, which set out on December 23. The column numbered about one hundred men, including officers, with a single six-pound cannon. It marched through pine flatwoods and across streams, with scouts and flankers apparently limited. Contemporary accounts suggest that Dade and his officers did not anticipate a major attack, though they had received warnings of Seminole hostility.

The Seminoles had been watching. Based on Alligator's later account, recorded by an army officer, a force of Seminole fighters, numbering in the range of one to two hundred according to later estimates, positioned itself in the pine woods and palmettos along the road, near present-day Bushnell, over several days. They had agreed to wait until the column was in the open, a plan that required discipline and patience. On the morning of December 28, as the soldiers marched in column along the road, the Seminoles fired a volley at close range that killed Dade and many of the front ranks. The soldiers returned fire, and the fighting continued for some hours as the survivors built a rough barricade of logs. The Seminoles eventually overwhelmed them. According to the American accounts, only three men escaped alive, and one of them died shortly afterward. The Seminoles took weapons and ammunition and left the scene. Black Seminoles were reportedly among the participants.

At roughly the same time, about thirty-five miles to the north at Fort King, Wiley Thompson was walking near the post in the afternoon with Lieutenant Constantine Smith when a party led by Osceola ambushed and killed them. The same group then attacked the nearby store of the sutler, killing several people there. The coordination between the two operations indicates deliberate planning, and it also shows the use of different leaders for different tasks. Osceola and his followers dealt with the agent who had come to symbolize removal, while the main body of Jumper, Alligator, Micanopy, and others engaged the army column.

Terminology and Evidence

Writing about these events requires care with language. American newspapers and officials referred to Dade's defeat as the "Dade Massacre," a label that has persisted in popular memory and on some monuments. The term was appropriate to a nineteenth-century political purpose, since it cast Seminole warfare as atrocity and obscured the fact that the Seminoles had attacked an armed military force on the march. At the same time, accounts of what happened after the first volley indicate that wounded soldiers were killed, a feature of the engagement that fits the harsh conventions of frontier warfare practiced by all parties and that soldiers' accounts naturally emphasized. Terms such as ambush, battle, or engagement describe a military action without assuming either a moral verdict or an interpretation. This chapter uses the term Dade's battle and treats "massacre" as a historical label whose meaning and use are part of the story.

The evidence for the engagement is limited and uneven. Two or three surviving soldiers gave accounts, which differ in detail, and which were influenced by shock, injury, and the desire of the army to explain a defeat. Investigations by the army and the examination of the site when the army returned in early 1836 provided physical evidence, including the positions of bodies. Alligator's narrative, recorded in the 1840s by Captain John T. Sprague, offers the most detailed account from the Seminole side, but it was given in captivity or after deportation and was mediated by interpretation. Seminole oral tradition and tribal historical accounts provide further perspectives. Historians combine these sources, noting where they agree, and they treat claims about specific numbers, the sequence of events, and the behavior of individuals with appropriate caution.

The Wider Outbreak

The attacks of December 28 opened a wider war. On December 31, a force of army regulars and Florida volunteers under General Duncan Clinch crossed the Withlacoochee River and fought a sharp engagement with Seminole warriors, who held a strong position and inflicted a number of casualties before the Americans withdrew. In the following weeks, Seminole and Black Seminole fighters attacked plantations along the St. Johns River and the Atlantic coast, burning sugar mills and houses and drawing hundreds of enslaved people away from their owners, some of whom joined the Seminoles. These raids terrorized settlers, many of whom abandoned their homes and sought shelter in St. Augustine and other towns. The rapid spread of the conflict from the interior to the coast demonstrated the capacity of the Seminoles for coordinated action and revealed the vulnerability of the plantation economy of northeastern Florida. It also made clear that the war could not be treated as a limited frontier incident.

Division II · Section 67.8

The Campaigns of 1836: Withlacoochee, American Offensives, and Seminole Defense

The United States responded to the outbreak of war with an effort to concentrate overwhelming force in the Florida interior. The army sent reinforcements from the north and from the Gulf Coast. General Edmund P. Gaines, commanding in the southwestern division, assembled a force at New Orleans and landed at Tampa Bay in February 1836, and Major General Winfield Scott, the army's senior field commander, was ordered to take overall command in Florida. The two officers were rivals, and the overlapping authority gave rise to disagreement about strategy and responsibility, a pattern of divided command that would trouble the American campaign repeatedly.

Gaines marched toward Fort King and then turned back toward the Withlacoochee, where he found Seminole warriors in strength. In late February and early March 1836 his force fought several engagements, then built a fortified camp, subsequently named Camp Izard after an officer who died in the fighting. The Seminoles besieged the camp for about a week, keeping the soldiers confined and short of food. The siege was lifted when forces under Clinch arrived, but the episode illustrated the character of the war. The army had entered the interior with large forces and plentiful artillery, but it could not bring the Seminoles to a decisive battle. At one point during the siege, Seminole leaders requested a parley, and the possibility of a negotiated settlement emerged, only to be undermined by the arrival of Clinch's troops, which the Seminoles interpreted as a breach of good faith. The episode contributed to Seminole distrust of American diplomacy.

Scott's Campaign

Scott's plan, executed in March 1836, called for three columns to converge on the Withlacoochee region and trap the Seminoles between them. On paper the plan was sound, but it relied on coordinated movements across difficult country, adequate supplies, and accurate knowledge of the enemy's location, none of which was reliably available. The columns marched through flooded woods and swamps, encountered few Seminoles, and were hampered by shortages of food, horses, and transport. The Seminoles had moved their families and their cattle to refuges deeper in the wetlands and avoided the converging columns. Scott returned with little to show for the effort. Disagreements with Gaines and a controversy over the conduct of the campaign led to a military inquiry, and by the summer Scott had been relieved of command in Florida.

The reasons for the failure of the large concentration were structural as well as personal. American forces depended on supply lines that extended through country without roads. Commanders lacked intelligence about Seminole settlements and movements, and they relied on guides whose reliability varied. Much of the force consisted of short-term volunteers and militia whose enlistments expired before campaigns could be completed, and whose discipline and willingness to endure hardship were uneven. The summer and early fall brought heat, mosquitoes, and disease, and soldiers fell ill in large numbers. In these conditions the army could march into the interior but could not remain, and the Seminoles could reoccupy the ground when it left.

Seminole Strategy

Seminole strategy was neither passive nor accidental. Leaders chose positions where terrain favored defense, such as the crossing of the Withlacoochee and hammocks surrounded by water or dense vegetation, and fought when the odds favored them. They moved families and supplies to places the army could not easily reach, and they used scouts to monitor American movements. They launched attacks on isolated detachments, wagon trains, and plantations, striking where they had local superiority and withdrawing before reinforcements arrived. They avoided pitched battles in which American numbers and artillery would prevail. These choices reflected a sophisticated understanding of their own capacity and of the limits of American logistics.

The conduct of the war also depended on cooperation among Seminole and Black Seminole fighters. The fighters did not form a standing army and did not have a unified command. Different leaders commanded their own followers and coordinated through councils and messengers. This decentralization had costs, since it made sustained offensive operations difficult, but it also made the Seminole force resilient, because the capture or death of any single leader did not destroy the organization of the whole. The American effort in 1836 failed to grasp this feature of the enemy. Commanders sought a decisive victory over a Seminole army that did not exist in the form they imagined, and the failure to understand the nature of Seminole warfare contributed to the repeated disappointment of American expectations.

By the end of 1836 the American command had changed hands again. Richard Keith Call, the governor of Florida Territory and a veteran of Jackson's campaigns, led a force into the interior in the autumn, fought engagements in the Wahoo Swamp area in November, and was criticized for failing to press his advantage, and in December the administration replaced him with Major General Thomas S. Jesup. The war was entering a new phase, characterized by larger forces, more systematic methods, and a growing American recognition that victory would require more than a single great campaign.

Division III — War in the Florida Interior · Section 67.9

Geography as Strategy: Wetlands, Rivers, Forests, and Military Mobility

The Florida peninsula in the 1830s presented an army with problems that its training and equipment had not prepared it to solve. The state is low and flat, and much of its interior was a mosaic of pine flatwoods, hardwood hammocks, prairies, lakes, rivers, and wetlands. Water levels changed dramatically with the seasons. During the summer and early autumn wet season, rains flooded prairies and swamps, rivers overflowed, and roads dissolved into mud. During the winter and spring dry season, water receded, ground firmed, and movement became easier. Heat, humidity, and insects made operations in summer hazardous, and the diseases of the lowland South, notably malaria, dysentery, and yellow fever, killed far more soldiers than combat did. To American officers from the North, the country seemed trackless and hostile. To the Seminoles it was a landscape of known resources, routes, and refuges.

Different regions of the peninsula played different roles in the war. The Withlacoochee River, flowing north through central Florida, and its surrounding country, including a densely forested region known as the Cove of the Withlacoochee, served as a core Seminole area in 1835 and 1836, a place of towns, fields, and defensive positions from which warriors could move toward Fort King, Fort Brooke, and the northern plantations. The chain of lakes and rivers in the central peninsula, including the headwaters of the St. Johns and Kissimmee systems, offered corridors for movement and sites for settlement. Farther south, Lake Okeechobee, a large, shallow lake fringed by marsh and sawgrass, formed a barrier and a refuge. South and west of it lay the Big Cypress Swamp, a wooded wetland of cypress strands, pine islands, and prairies, and to the southeast the Everglades, a broad shallow river of sawgrass and water dotted with tree islands known as hammocks. Each of these environments demanded particular skills, and each provided a measure of protection to those who knew it.

Indigenous Knowledge as a Military Resource

Seminole knowledge of the land was cumulative, practical, and specific. Communities knew where water was deep enough for dugout canoes and where it could be waded, which hammocks stayed dry in the wet season, where game and fish were abundant, where coontie and other wild foods grew, and where fresh water could be found when it was scarce. They knew the seasonal patterns of flooding and the movements of deer, turkey, and fish. They used canoes, trails, and temporary camps to move people and goods through country that appeared impassable to outsiders, and they maintained a dispersed settlement pattern, with small clusters of households, that made them difficult to find and difficult to destroy in a single blow. This knowledge was a form of expertise built over generations and enriched by the experience of Black Seminoles who had brought agricultural and other skills, and it gave the Seminoles the ability to evade, ambush, and outlast opponents who depended on roads and rivers.

Equally important was the Seminole decision to use this knowledge strategically. Terrain is not an automatic advantage. The same landscape also imposed hardships on Seminole families, including hunger, exposure, and disease, and it provided no protection against an opponent who learned to adapt. The Seminoles chose when to fight in difficult ground and when to avoid battle, they selected positions along rivers and at the edges of hammocks where attackers had to cross open or flooded terrain, and they exploited the army's dependence on supply lines. Seen this way, geography functioned as a resource that skilled actors made use of, not as a force that determined the outcome of the war.

The Limits of Conventional Operations

American commanders gradually learned that conventional operations had limits. Heavy wagons could not follow a column across swamps. Horses died from lack of forage and from disease. Artillery was of little use in dense woods. Supply by water depended on shallow rivers that were navigable only part of the year, and food, ammunition, and medical supplies often failed to arrive on schedule. Short-term volunteers, accustomed to campaigns of a few weeks, found the conditions intolerable, and the army's regulars suffered from sickness. The Seminoles, by contrast, could disperse and reassemble, living off the land and the cattle and crops they could protect. The army responded with several adaptations: building chains of forts and roads to extend control, using shallow-draft boats and naval detachments to enter rivers and coastal waters, organizing winter campaigns to take advantage of the dry season, and eventually building canoe flotillas to penetrate the Everglades. These adaptations improved American capacity but did not produce a quick solution, and they required enormous expenditure of men, money, and time.

Division III · Section 67.10

Black Seminole Military Leadership and the Defense of Freedom

The Black Seminole communities introduced in Section 67.2 were fully engaged in the Second Seminole War from its first months, and their role in it was military, diplomatic, and political. The stakes were extraordinarily high. For Black Seminoles who had escaped slavery, or who had been born free in Florida under the protection of Seminole alliances, the American victory that the army sought would mean enslavement or re-enslavement. Americans were open about this. Southern slaveholders pressed the army to return people they claimed as property, and army officers frequently regarded the capture of Black Seminoles as an objective on a par with the defeat of Seminole warriors. Under these conditions Black Seminoles had every reason to resist, and many did so from the beginning.

Their contribution took several forms. Black Seminole fighters served alongside Seminole warriors in the field, and the raids on plantations along the St. Johns and the Atlantic coast in early 1836, in which enslaved people joined the Seminoles, were an important part of the war's first phase. Black Seminoles served as scouts, couriers, and interpreters, using their knowledge of English and of American practices to gather intelligence on army movements, to communicate between Seminole leaders and American officers, and to understand the political and military calculations of their opponents. They helped organize defensive communities, maintained agricultural production, and contributed to the Seminole capacity to sustain a long war. Contemporary American officers recognized their importance, often with alarm. They treated Black Seminole fighters as particularly dangerous because they knew American institutions, because they had the most to lose, and because their example threatened the stability of slavery in the South.

Abraham and John Horse

Two figures stand out in the documentary record. Abraham, who had served as Micanopy's interpreter and adviser for years, was a skilled diplomat who participated in the Payne's Landing negotiations and the western delegation, and who became a key intermediary during the war. He negotiated with American commanders, helped to arrange the surrender and emigration of Seminole and Black Seminole groups at various points, and in 1837 and 1838 worked to secure guarantees for the safety of Black communities. American officers described him as shrewd and influential, and the Seminole leaders he served valued his abilities. His role illustrates the position of Black Seminole leaders in a political system in which they were both allies of the Seminole leadership and the representatives of a distinct community with its own interests.

John Horse, known in Spanish as Juan Caballo, was a younger man of mixed African and Seminole ancestry who rose to prominence as a fighter and negotiator. He served as a war leader of Black Seminole groups, was among those seized under a flag of truce in October 1837, escaped from Fort Marion with Coacoochee and others, and later negotiated the terms on which Black Seminoles would emigrate. His subsequent career in Indian Territory and Mexico, which falls beyond the scope of this chapter, demonstrates the continuing struggle of his community to secure freedom. Other Black Seminole leaders, many of them known only through scattered references in military reports, commanded fighters and took part in councils, and their names are less familiar because the records were kept by their opponents.

Slaveholder Demands and the Politics of Surrender

The central political problem for Black Seminoles in the war was the demand by slaveholders and by some American officials that people of African descent be returned to slavery. When Seminole leaders negotiated with General Jesup in the spring of 1837, they insisted on conditions that would protect their Black allies, and Jesup initially gave assurances that Black Seminoles who surrendered would be allowed to accompany the Seminoles west. These assurances were fragile. Florida slaveholders and slave catchers gathered at the army camps, claimed that particular individuals were their property, and in some cases seized people who had come in to surrender. Seminole and Black Seminole leaders interpreted these seizures as a breach of faith, and the incident contributed to the exodus of Osceola, Sam Jones, Coacoochee, and hundreds of followers from the camp at Tampa Bay in June 1837, which collapsed the negotiated settlement.

This episode illustrates both cooperation and tension within the wider Seminole world. Black Seminoles and their Seminole allies shared an interest in resisting removal and American slaveholding power, and in many cases they acted together. But they did not always share the same assessment of how to proceed. Some Seminole leaders were willing to negotiate in ways that could leave Black allies exposed, and Black Seminoles sometimes pursued separate negotiations to secure their own safety. The relationship was unequal in some respects, since Black Seminoles depended on Seminole protection and many were subject to obligations to Seminole patrons. It was also reciprocal, since the Seminoles depended on Black Seminole fighters, interpreters, and farmers. A historical account must keep both aspects in view and avoid simplifying the relationship into either unproblematic solidarity or subordination.

Division III · Section 67.11

U.S. Escalation and Seminole Resistance, 1836–1837

The autumn and winter of 1836 and 1837 saw an escalation of the American effort and a corresponding adaptation by the Seminoles. After Scott's failure, command passed to Governor Richard Keith Call, who organized a campaign in the fall with a force of volunteers and regulars. In November 1836 Call's troops fought in the Wahoo Swamp, a flooded region of the Withlacoochee country where Seminole and Black Seminole warriors held positions that the army could approach only through water and mud. The engagement ended without a decisive result, and Call, facing supply shortages and the expiration of volunteer enlistments, withdrew. Criticism of his conduct reached Washington, and the administration of President Jackson, impatient with the lack of results, replaced him in December with Major General Thomas S. Jesup, who had been serving in the war on the Creek frontier and who brought a more aggressive approach.

Jesup commanded the largest force the army had yet assembled in Florida, including regular troops, volunteers from several states, marines, naval detachments, and a contingent of Creek auxiliaries recruited from the Muscogee Nation in the West. The use of Creek auxiliaries is a reminder that the war involved Indigenous peoples on both sides, and that Creek leaders who joined the American cause did so for their own reasons, including payment, their rivalry with the Seminoles, and a desire to demonstrate loyalty to the United States. The army also constructed forts and roads on a larger scale than before, a chain of posts that gave it control of strategic points, protected supply lines, and provided bases for expeditions. These forts, scattered across the interior and along the coasts, became a permanent feature of the war. They gave American forces places of refuge, but they also tied down large numbers of troops and were frequently unhealthy places in which disease took a heavy toll.

Combined Operations and Economic Warfare

American operations in 1837 combined military pressure on Seminole fighters with efforts to deprive communities of the means to sustain themselves. Troops destroyed fields, seized cattle, burned towns, and attacked settlements where families lived. These actions had a military purpose, since they were intended to reduce the Seminoles' ability to feed fighters and to compel surrender. They also had human consequences, as families lost the crops and herds on which they depended. The destruction of plantations by the Seminoles and Black Seminoles in turn depleted the economic base of northeastern Florida, where planters had abandoned estates and enslaved people had fled or joined the Seminoles.

The army's composition shaped how the war was fought. Regular army troops were professional soldiers, but they were few and many were dying of disease. State volunteers and territorial militias provided numbers but served short terms, resented hardship, and were often undisciplined. Their conduct toward Indigenous communities and Black captives was frequently harsh. Commanders struggled to coordinate these different forces, and rivalries among officers, as well as tension between regulars and volunteers, complicated operations. Military engineers built roads, bridges, and posts, and the Navy, with its shallow-draft boats and sailors, took part in coastal and riverine operations. This apparatus was enormous by the standards of the early republic, and it made the Second Seminole War the most expensive of the so-called Indian wars of its time.

Negotiation and Collapse in the Spring of 1837

In March 1837, as pressure mounted, Jesup opened negotiations with Seminole leaders. On March 6, at Fort Dade, leaders including Micanopy, Jumper, and others agreed to a cessation of hostilities and to assemble near Tampa Bay for emigration. Many Seminole families and Black Seminole groups moved toward the assembly area during the spring, and for a time it appeared that the war might end by agreement. Jesup believed that he had succeeded, and several hundred people gathered in camps near Fort Brooke awaiting transport.

The agreement unraveled in the late spring. Slaveholders and slave catchers appeared at the camps, claiming ownership of Black Seminoles. Some Seminole leaders, including Osceola, Sam Jones, and Coacoochee, who had been wary of the arrangement from the start, decided that it was a trap. On June 2, 1837, they led several hundred people out of the camp, an episode that shattered Jesup's confidence in the assembly and demonstrated the fragility of agreements that did not address the fears of the communities involved. The American commander was left with a war he had expected to be nearly over. His response, discussed in Section 67.13, shifted toward a method of negotiation that would later arouse widespread condemnation.

The effect of the campaigns of 1836 and 1837 on Seminole communities was mixed. Seminole fighters had avoided defeat in the field, had inflicted casualties on American forces, and had held together their political leadership. But the cost to families was increasing. Fields had been destroyed, towns abandoned, and numbers of people had been captured or had surrendered. The long-term consequences of attrition were becoming clear, even as the capacity for organized resistance remained intact.

Division III · Section 67.12

The Battle of Lake Okeechobee, 1837, and the Limits of Military Victory

The largest pitched battle of the Second Seminole War occurred on Christmas Day, 1837, on the northern shore of Lake Okeechobee. In the autumn Colonel Zachary Taylor, a career officer of the regular army, had been sent with a column of regulars and volunteers to pursue Seminole groups that had withdrawn into the south-central interior. Approximately eight hundred men were present with Taylor at the engagement, although some accounts give larger figures for the wider expedition or initial column; his command included regulars of the 1st, 4th, and 6th Infantry and Missouri volunteers under Colonel Richard Gentry. After several weeks of marching, building posts, and following trails, the column found a large Seminole force positioned near the lake.

Seminole leaders at the engagement included Abiaka (Sam Jones), Alligator (Halpatter Tustenuggee), and Coacoochee, who had escaped from Fort Marion the previous month; Florida State Parks also identifies Holata Micco (Billy Bowlegs) among the leaders present. They had chosen their ground deliberately. Sources differ over the exact composition of the command and the extent of other leaders’ participation. They occupied a hammock, a stand of trees on higher ground, behind a broad expanse of sawgrass and mud about a mile across, through which the Americans would have to advance on foot. According to American accounts, the Seminoles had prepared the position, clearing lines of fire and marking trees, and their numbers have been estimated at between about four hundred and five hundred warriors, although estimates vary. The Seminoles' purpose was likely a defensive stand to protect families and supplies behind them and to inflict losses that might discourage pursuit.

The Fighting

Taylor ordered a frontal attack across the sawgrass. The Missouri volunteers advanced first, and they came under heavy fire from the hammock. Colonel Gentry was mortally wounded, and the volunteers, having lost their commander, broke and fell back. The regulars of the 6th Infantry advanced next and suffered severe losses, including the death of their commanding officer, Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Thompson, and of other officers. After a hard fight the regulars drove the Seminoles from the hammock, and Seminole fighters withdrew from the position, helping their families escape farther toward the Everglades. Taylor’s contemporary report recorded twenty-six American soldiers killed and 112 wounded, while some later accounts include additional deaths from wounds. His report estimated Seminole losses as substantially lower than American losses. Estimates of Seminole casualties are uncertain, since they depend on American counts of bodies found and on later Seminole accounts. In either case, the proportion of American officers and men killed or wounded was high.

Taylor claimed a victory. He held the field, the Seminoles withdrew, and in a report to the War Department he described the engagement as a successful blow against the enemy. The army and the press embraced the claim. Taylor was promoted to brevet brigadier general, was given command in Florida in the spring of 1838, and the reputation he gained contributed to his later political career. The engagement became a part of the American narrative of the war, and a battlefield monument and park now commemorate it.

Competing Interpretations

The meaning of the battle is contested. From the American military perspective, the result was a tactical success: the army attacked a prepared position, overcame it, and forced the Seminoles to retreat. From the Seminole perspective, as reflected in accounts from participants and in tribal historical writing, the engagement showed that Seminole warriors could stand against a larger force, inflict severe casualties, and withdraw in good order with their families and supplies. The fact that the Seminoles chose to fight in a defensive position and to retreat when the position had served its purpose is consistent with a strategy aimed at protecting their communities, not at holding ground at all costs.

The deeper question is whether the battle advanced the American strategic objective, which was to compel the Seminoles to leave Florida. On that measure the result was inconclusive at best. The Seminoles were not destroyed, their leaders remained at large, and their communities continued to resist. The army had suffered heavy losses and had pushed the Seminoles farther into the swamps, where they were harder to find. Taylor's tactical success did not end the war, and it was followed by years of further fighting. Students should note the distinction, which recurs throughout the history of this conflict, between winning a battle and achieving a political and military purpose, and between a commander's claim of victory and the longer-term consequences of the engagement.

Organized Seminole resistance continued after the battle for reasons that the history of the preceding sections makes clear. The Seminoles' strategy did not depend on holding any one position. Their communities were dispersed, their leadership was distributed, and their determination to avoid removal was firm. The battle also coincided with a period of heightened American efforts to bring the war to an end by other means, including the seizure of leaders under a flag of truce, which is the subject of the next division.

Division IV — Captivity, Attrition, and the End of the Second Seminole War · Section 67.13

Diplomacy under Arms: Osceola, Flags of Truce, and Captivity

Negotiation was never absent from the Second Seminole War. Seminole leaders sought talks at moments when they could secure terms or gain time, and American commanders pursued talks when force had failed. Parleys took place at army posts and in the field, usually under a white flag, a signal recognized by both sides as a request to speak without being attacked. The conventions of such meetings were not written into any binding agreement between the combatants, but they rested on shared expectations about safe conduct, and they were essential to any end to the war short of annihilation. The events of the autumn of 1837 placed those expectations under severe strain.

After the collapse of the Tampa Bay assembly in June 1837, General Jesup changed his approach. He concluded that the Seminoles' capacity for resistance depended on a limited number of leaders and that capturing them would hasten the end of the war. In September he arranged the seizure of a group of Seminole leaders who had come to a meeting near St. Augustine under a white flag. In October, Seminole leaders including Osceola and Coacoochee, with a party of followers, approached the American camp near Fort Peyton, south of St. Augustine, to negotiate, again under a flag of truce. On October 21, American troops under General Joseph Hernandez surrounded and seized them. They were taken to St. Augustine and confined in Fort Marion, the old Spanish fortress of the Castillo de San Marcos.

Controversy, Imprisonment, and Escape

The seizure aroused immediate criticism in the American press and in Congress, and it has remained controversial. Critics argued that capturing men who had come to talk under a flag of truce was treacherous, violated the customs of war, and would make future negotiations impossible. Jesup and his defenders replied that the Seminoles had themselves broken agreements, that the leaders captured were not honest negotiators but combatants who had used truces to rest and regroup, and that the necessity of ending a costly war justified the action. Jesup also argued that the Seminoles had been informed that the army would treat further evasion as continued hostility. The controversy shows that nineteenth-century Americans disagreed about the ethics of war, and that contemporaries understood the issue as one of honor and legitimacy as well as expediency.

Conditions in Fort Marion were harsh. On November 29, 1837, Coacoochee, John Horse, and about twenty others escaped through a high, narrow window, having reduced their bodies by fasting and used improvised means to descend the walls. Osceola, who was ill and who did not take part in the escape, was transferred in December to Fort Moultrie in the harbor of Charleston, South Carolina. The artist George Catlin visited and painted him there in January 1838. Osceola died on January 30, 1838, after a serious illness; contemporary and later descriptions refer variously to a throat infection (often termed quinsy) and other contributing conditions, and the exact medical diagnosis should not be asserted as certain. He was buried at Fort Moultrie with military honors. The attending physician removed the head, an act documented in the sources and later widely criticized, and subsequent questions about the disposition of his remains have been part of his posthumous history.

Memory and the Persistence of Leadership

Osceola's capture and death transformed him into a symbol. American writers, painters, and the public turned him into a tragic figure, and for generations popular culture romanticized him, often at the expense of the other leaders and communities who sustained the war. For Seminoles, the memory of Osceola's capture under a flag of truce became a lasting example of American bad faith. It is nonetheless essential to avoid concluding that his loss ended Seminole leadership or resistance. Coacoochee escaped and returned to the struggle. Abiaka, Alligator, Micanopy, and other leaders remained active, though Micanopy's role diminished, and Black Seminole leaders continued to negotiate and fight. The war went on for four more years, driven by leaders whose names are less familiar in American memory.

Historical Vocabulary
Flag of Truce and Parole

Definition: A flag of truce, usually a white cloth, signaled a request to parley and was understood to confer temporary protection on those who bore it. Parole in the military usage of the period meant a promise by a prisoner or negotiator, often in exchange for limited freedom, to abstain from hostile action.

Historical context: These practices were part of the customary law of war in the Atlantic world, observed by European and American armies and adopted in dealings with Indigenous nations, who maintained their own protocols of diplomacy, safe conduct, and the calumet, wampum, or other symbols. Neither side had agreed in writing to a common code, so disputes over whether a flag had been honored depended on shared understandings that could be contested.

Why it matters: The seizure of Seminole leaders under a flag of truce shows how deeply the conduct of diplomacy shaped the war, and why Seminole communities, and Black Seminoles in particular, came to regard American promises with suspicion.

Potential misunderstanding: It would be anachronistic to apply later international conventions, such as those codified in the twentieth century, to these events. The question for historians is how contemporaries judged the act, and the answer is that many Americans condemned it by the standards of their own time.

Multiple Perspectives

The Seizure of Osceola and Coacoochee under a Flag of Truce, 1837

From the perspective of Seminole leaders and their communities: The meeting was an effort to negotiate, and the seizure of those who came to it confirmed that American promises could not be trusted. For families still in the field, the episode reinforced the judgment that safety lay in distance from American posts, and for leaders it made future parleys dangerous.
From the perspective of Black Seminoles: John Horse and other Black Seminole leaders were among those captured and confined, and their escape from Fort Marion reflected the immediate stakes: American custody offered no assurance that they would not be returned to slavery. The episode intensified the insistence on firm guarantees of freedom.
From the perspective of U.S. military officials: Jesup and his supporters regarded the capture as a legitimate measure against enemies who had repeatedly used negotiations to gain time, and as a means of shortening a war that was killing soldiers and consuming public funds. Other officers and many in the press considered it a stain on the army's honor.
From the perspective of settlers in Florida: Many settlers, living amid raids and under constant fear, welcomed any action that promised an end to the war, and they pressed for harsher measures. Their concerns about security and the loss of property were real, though they were also bound up with interests in land and slavery.
From the perspective of noncombatants in Seminole communities: Women, children, and elders, the people least visible in official records, bore the consequences of every breakdown in negotiation. The loss of leaders and the collapse of talks meant continued flight, hunger, and the risk of capture.
Division IV · Section 67.14

Coacoochee, Abiaka, and the Continuing Seminole Resistance, 1838–1841

The capture of Osceola and the army's continued operations in early 1838 produced a surge of surrenders, but not the collapse of the resistance. In January 1838, at the Loxahatchee River near Jupiter Inlet, Jesup's forces fought a sharp action with Seminole warriors in which Jesup himself was wounded. Over the following months, large numbers of Seminole and Black Seminole people came into American posts, and Jesup reported the capture or surrender of several thousand. These figures should be treated with caution, since they come from the army's own accounts and combine groups who surrendered, were captured, or were collected for other reasons, but they indicate that the pressure on communities was heavy. Many of those who surrendered were sent west, a process whose logistics and human cost belong primarily to Chapter 66. Jesup left Florida in the spring of 1838, and Zachary Taylor took command.

Those who remained were the most determined. Abiaka, an aged Mikasuki leader and spiritual figure, became the center of a body of communities committed to avoiding removal, and his reputation among Seminoles rested on his steadiness and his religious authority. Coacoochee, after his escape, resumed leadership of fighting groups in the central peninsula, combining military skill with diplomatic energy. Other leaders, including Chitto Tustenuggee, Tuskegee, Halleck Tustenuggee, and, farther south, the Mikasuki leader Chakaika, commanded dispersed groups. These leaders operated through small bands rather than large formations. Communities moved frequently, planted hidden fields, and sent hunting and raiding parties in different directions. The resulting pattern, a network of small groups dispersed over a large area, made the Seminole force difficult to locate and nearly impossible to destroy.

New American Strategies

The army tried several approaches. Taylor, who commanded from 1838 to 1840, divided the peninsula into districts about twenty miles square, each centered on a post, with the intention of clearing each district and protecting the settled north. He also supported the controversial use of Cuban bloodhounds to track Seminole groups, a measure that drew public criticism and produced little. His successors, Walker Keith Armistead and then William J. Worth, each adjusted the strategy again. Throughout, the army relied on a mix of military pressure and offers of negotiation. These alternated with one another, and Seminole leaders, who could see the pattern, calculated when to talk and when to withdraw.

The most important of these negotiations took place in 1839. In May, Major General Alexander Macomb, the army's commanding general, traveled to Florida and met with Seminole leaders, including Abiaka, and concluded an agreement on May 18. Under its terms, hostilities were to cease and the Seminoles were to be permitted to remain in a reserve in the south of the peninsula, in the country below the Peace River, until further arrangements were made. The agreement was a significant concession. It acknowledged, for the first time during the war, that the Seminoles might remain in Florida, and it reflected both the army's weariness and the Seminoles' success in preserving their communities. It also exposed the lack of consensus within the American government. Florida's settlers, territorial officials, and many in Washington opposed any arrangement that left Seminoles in the peninsula, and the agreement was repudiated or ignored by those who wanted removal.

The truce collapsed in July 1839, when a party of Mikasuki warriors under Chakaika attacked a detachment commanded by Colonel William S. Harney at a trading post on the Caloosahatchee River, killing a number of soldiers, though Harney himself escaped. The attack was a direct reply to the incursions of soldiers into the reserve and perhaps to the failure of promised arrangements. It was followed in August 1840 by an attack on the settlement at Indian Key in the Florida Keys, in which Chakaika's party killed a number of residents, including the botanist Henry Perrine. In December 1840, Harney led a canoe expedition into the Everglades, found Chakaika's camp, and killed him, hanging several captives. These episodes illustrate that the war in this phase involved violence against civilians by both sides, and that the line between combatants and noncombatants was frequently crossed.

Coacoochee's Capture and Departure

Under Colonel Worth, who took command in 1841, the army adopted more aggressive methods, discussed in the next section. In June 1841 Coacoochee was detained and sent in chains to New Orleans, then returned to Florida under Worth’s authority so that he might persuade other groups to come in. Coacoochee, acting under pressure but also making difficult calculations about the future of his people, assisted in these negotiations. In October 1841 he and a party of followers departed Florida for the West, arriving in Arkansas by river the following month. Scholars differ on how to interpret his decisions, whether as coerced collaboration, as an effort to secure the best available terms for his community, or as a combination of the two. What is clear is that the removal of one of the leaders of the resistance, by capture and by a negotiated departure, did not end the resistance. Abiaka and others remained in Florida, and Worth's task of finding them proved difficult. The fuller story of deportation and resettlement of those who went west is part of Chapter 66.

Division IV · Section 67.15

The War of Attrition: Food Destruction, Civilian Vulnerability, and U.S. Military Adaptation

By 1841 the United States had been fighting in Florida for six years without achieving its goal. The new commander, Colonel William J. Worth, who took command in the spring, concluded that earlier campaigns had failed because they had been conducted in the wrong season and against the wrong target. The army had avoided summer operations because of disease and had pursued the Seminoles' warriors rather than the system that supported them. Worth reversed both assumptions. He ordered operations throughout the year, including the summer, when Seminole families were most dependent on their growing crops, and he directed small, mobile columns to locate and destroy fields, villages, and caches of food.

The strategy was a war of attrition directed at the means of subsistence. American detachments struck into the Cove of the Withlacoochee, the Big Cypress, the lands around Lake Okeechobee, and the Everglades, burning cornfields, uprooting plantings of potatoes and pumpkins, and destroying cattle. Naval and army boat expeditions moved into waters that had hitherto been inaccessible, guided in some cases by captured Seminoles or Black Seminoles who were compelled or induced to serve. The aim was to compel surrender by depriving communities of food, to make hidden communities visible by following the paths to their fields, and to capture families whose capture would induce their warriors to come in. The strategy exploited the Seminoles' dependence on agriculture, which had been one of their strengths, by making agriculture itself a target.

Consequences for Families and Communities

The effects on Seminole communities were severe. Women, who in Seminole society played central roles in agriculture and in the transmission of clan identity, saw the fields they cultivated destroyed. Children and elders, the most vulnerable members of any community, suffered from hunger and from the exposure of life in flight, and the sources, though thin, suggest that mortality from privation and disease rose. Families were separated by capture and flight. Ceremonial life, including the Green Corn ceremony that depended on the harvest, was disrupted. Livestock, which had been central to the economy, were lost. Communities that had once been settled and prosperous became bands moving between temporary camps. Many families surrendered because they could no longer feed their children, a fact that American officers sometimes noted with satisfaction.

It is important to distinguish the military effects of this strategy from its human consequences. Militarily, the destruction of crops and the capture of families reduced the number of Seminole fighters and pushed groups to surrender. Humanly, it imposed suffering on civilians, much of it on those who took no part in combat. Contemporary American officers justified the policy as the only means of ending the war, and some critics condemned it as cruel. The same logic had been applied in earlier Indigenous wars and would be applied in later ones, including in the campaigns against the buffalo economy discussed in Chapter 81. The Seminole experience is thus part of a pattern in which the United States targeted the economic foundations of Indigenous societies as a means of conquest.

Adaptation on Both Sides

The Seminoles adapted as well. They dispersed into smaller groups, planted smaller and more concealed gardens in hammocks, shifted to the Everglades and Big Cypress, and relied more heavily on wild foods such as coontie, fish, and game. They avoided fires that would show smoke, and they moved their camps often. The strategy of the army had the effect of selecting for those who could live in the most difficult conditions, and it pushed the remaining Seminoles into the southern part of the peninsula, where they developed the settlement patterns and subsistence systems that would characterize Seminole life for the next century, including the chickee, an open-sided, thatched-roof structure suited to wet country.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fields, Families, Trade, and Ceremony in a Time of War

Before the wars, Seminole households in the interior combined planted fields of corn, beans, squash, and sweet potatoes with the gathering of coontie, hunting, fishing, and the keeping of cattle, hogs, and horses. Women organized much of the agricultural work and, within matrilineal clans, passed on membership and often the right to use fields and dwellings. Men hunted, traded deerskins and, later, cattle and other goods at posts and with merchants, and took part in councils and war. Black Seminole households, living in their own villages, grew crops, kept livestock, and traded, and their agricultural skills strengthened the local economy. Ceremonial life, including the Green Corn ceremony with its fasting, dancing, and renewal of fires, linked households within and across towns and marked the agricultural year.

War altered every part of this life. As towns became targets, households shifted to smaller settlements in hammocks and swamps, where they planted what they could and carried seeds with them. Trade with American merchants largely ended, and the acquisition of lead, powder, and cloth became a problem. Families faced decisions about when to flee, when to hide, and when to surrender, often on the basis of rumor and fragments of news. Children grew up in conditions of mobility and danger. The evidence for these experiences is incomplete: American officers recorded the destruction of fields and the capture of families, but they said little about the daily decisions of women and elders. Historians draw on military reports, travelers' accounts, oral tradition preserved in Seminole and Miccosukee communities, and archaeological work at campsites and hammocks to reconstruct a picture of resilience in the face of deprivation.

Division IV · Section 67.16

The Declared End of the Second Seminole War, 1842

By the summer of 1842, the United States government had decided to bring the war to a close. The army had been fighting for nearly seven years. Congress and the public were tired of the expense, the administration found no prospect of the complete removal of the Seminoles by force, and Florida's own settlers, who wanted peace and open land, were pressing for a settlement. Worth, who had by that time pursued his campaign through the summer and into the fall, concluded that the number of Seminoles remaining in Florida was small and that they could be allowed to remain in a limited district in the southwest, away from the settlements, provided they did not trouble the settlers. He estimated the number of remaining Seminoles at about three hundred, a figure commonly cited though uncertain, since Seminole bands avoided counting and the army's knowledge was imperfect.

On August 14, 1842, Worth declared the war at an end. The declaration was a statement of policy and not a treaty. It did not result from a negotiated peace, and there was no document signed by Seminole leaders. The army reduced its forces, withdrew many of its troops, and designated a temporary area in southwestern Florida where the remaining Seminoles might stay. Meanwhile, the government encouraged white settlement through the Armed Occupation Act of August 1842, which offered land grants to settlers who would occupy and cultivate land in the lower peninsula and bear arms in its defense. The declared end of the war and the opening of the land to settlers thus occurred together.

Costs

The war had been enormously costly. An official U.S. Army history places American military deaths at nearly sixteen hundred, predominantly from disease, and the expenditure at approximately thirty million dollars; other studies give different totals depending on the categories included. These were extraordinary costs for the period. These figures come from army and congressional records and from later scholarship, and they should be treated as estimates. Seminole and Black Seminole losses are far harder to determine. Deaths in combat, from privation and disease, and in the course of deportation are poorly recorded, and the number of people deported is itself variously estimated, with commonly cited figures in the range of three to four thousand over the course of the war. The human loss to Seminole communities, in lives, homes, fields, and cattle, was heavy.

Victory, Defeat, Survival

The declared end did not constitute a comprehensive peace. The United States did not defeat the Seminoles in the sense of compelling their surrender, and it did not remove them all. It also did not establish any recognition of Seminole sovereignty. Instead, the government effectively tolerated a remnant, retaining its claim to Florida and its intention to remove the Seminoles at a later date if circumstances allowed. For the United States, the war could be described as a victory in the sense that the great majority of Seminoles had been removed and that Florida was opened to settlement, but it was a costly and unsatisfying one, and many contemporaries thought it a failure. For the Seminoles who remained, the meaning was different. They had survived. They had remained in their homeland, they retained their leadership and their political independence, and they had not signed any agreement surrendering their claims. This understanding, rooted in the experience of resistance, would become the foundation of the idea that the Seminoles of Florida were unconquered. For Seminoles who had been deported, and for Black Seminoles whose status in the West remained uncertain, the war's outcome carried different meanings again.

Division V — The Third Seminole War and the Survival of Seminole Florida · Section 67.17

Florida after 1842: Armed Settlement, Statehood, and Continued Conflict

The thirteen years between the declared end of the Second Seminole War and the outbreak of the Third are sometimes treated as a period of peace. They were not. They were a period in which the remaining Seminole communities, a few hundred people living in the Big Cypress, the Everglades, and the lands around Lake Okeechobee, tried to preserve their autonomy under mounting pressure from a growing settler population and a state that still intended to remove them. The absence of large campaigns concealed a continuing contest over land, mobility, and the terms on which Seminoles could live in Florida.

The Armed Occupation Act of August 1842 was an early sign of the direction of policy. It offered a quarter section of land, 160 acres, to settlers who would occupy it and cultivate it for five years, and who were able to bear arms, in a district of the peninsula south of an established line. The law was designed to place armed settlers in the territory that the army had cleared, and in effect to create a population that would defend itself against the Seminoles and press for their removal. Settlement was uneven, but the Act brought several thousand claimants and extended the settler frontier southward. In 1845, Florida was admitted to the Union as a slave state, joined in a paired admission with Iowa as a free state, and the new state government, controlled by planters and by those who favored removal, pressed the federal government to eliminate the remaining Seminole presence. Settlers, speculators, and officials regarded the Everglades and the lands around them as potentially valuable. The Swamp Land Act of 1850, which transferred federal title to swamp and overflow lands to the states for reclamation, signaled the long-term intention to drain and develop the wetlands that had sheltered the Seminoles.

Seminole Strategies of Survival

The Seminoles used the years after 1842 to rebuild. The communities that remained, mostly Mikasuki-speaking, settled in small clusters of households in hammocks and on the edges of the Big Cypress and the Everglades. They planted gardens of corn, pumpkins, sweet potatoes, and bananas, kept hogs and some cattle, hunted deer and turkey, fished, gathered coontie, and traded hides, plumes, and other goods at a few posts, including those near the army forts established at the edges of their territory. They renewed ceremonial life, including the Green Corn ceremony, and maintained clan relationships and leadership through councils. Leaders of the period included Abiaka (Sam Jones), whose seniority and spiritual authority remained significant, Chipco, Assinwar, and Billy Bowlegs (Holata Micco), who emerged as the principal spokesman in dealings with American officials. Mobility was essential. Families moved with the seasons and with the changing threat of settlers or soldiers.

The federal government kept up pressure through diplomacy and inducement. Officials offered payments and goods to those who would emigrate, and small groups agreed to go, but most remained. Military posts established in the late 1840s and early 1850s, among them Fort Myers on the Caloosahatchee and Fort Dallas on the Miami River, extended American surveillance around the edges of Seminole territory. Incidents of violence recurred. In 1849, killings of settlers on the Indian River and the Peace River, which American accounts attributed to Seminole raiders, produced panic and demands for removal, and officials responded with ultimatums to the Seminole leaders. The Seminole leaders, anxious to avoid renewed war, handed over several individuals blamed for the attacks. Delegations of Seminole leaders, including Billy Bowlegs, traveled to Washington and to the Indian Territory in the early 1850s, and the Seminoles resisted pressure to move. In these years, too, Black Seminole and Seminole groups in the West, dissatisfied with conditions in Indian Territory, including threats of enslavement, began a migration toward Mexico under leaders including John Horse and Coacoochee, an episode that belongs to the borderland history of Chapter 68.

These developments demonstrate that the Seminoles' situation after 1842 was neither one of peace nor of open war. It was a standoff in which both sides pursued their aims by other means. The state and federal governments wanted the Seminoles gone, the settlers wanted the land, and the Seminoles wanted to remain. The balance of power was unequal, and the Seminoles' capacity to remain depended on the difficulty of the terrain, their own skill at avoiding conflict, and the lack of a clear political will in Washington to resume large-scale war. It was a precarious equilibrium, and it would not last.

Division V · Section 67.18

Billy Bowlegs and the Outbreak of the Third Seminole War, 1855

Holata Micco, known to Americans as Billy Bowlegs, was a nephew or relative of earlier leaders named Bowlegs and had survived the Second War. By the early 1850s he was the best-known Seminole leader in Florida. He had traveled to Washington, had met federal officials, and had developed a prosperous community in the Big Cypress, with fields and gardens, including a plantation of bananas and other crops. Officials who met him described a dignified and capable man, and his leadership was important in keeping peace in the early 1850s. He and other Seminole leaders consistently refused to emigrate, while seeking to avoid provocations that might bring renewed war.

By 1855 the pressure had increased. The federal government, with settlers and the state urging action, sent army survey parties into the territory of the Big Cypress and the adjacent country to map the land and to identify areas for settlement and drainage. The surveys were intended in part to prepare the way for American use of the land, and Seminole leaders understood them in that light. In December 1855, a party under Lieutenant George L. Hartsuff entered Bowlegs's settlement area in the Big Cypress and, according to accounts, damaged plantings of bananas and other crops. The Seminoles viewed such acts as an infringement of their property and an expression of contempt. On December 20, Seminole fighters attacked Hartsuff's party, killing and wounding several soldiers. The army regarded this attack as the beginning of a new war, and the territory soon mobilized.

Understanding the Seminole Objection

The Seminoles' objection to surveys and intrusions must be understood in the context of decades of territorial pressure. For Seminole families who had lived through the destruction of towns, the burning of fields, and the pursuit of the army, the entry of armed parties into their gardens was not a trivial matter. It recalled earlier encroachments, and it threatened the one thing that the Seminoles had secured since 1842, the control of a small area in which they could live and plant in peace. American officials, for their part, treated the survey as a routine government activity and the Seminole response as aggression. The difference in perspective illustrates how a minor incident could trigger a major conflict when the underlying relationship was one of unresolved sovereignty and unequal power.

Some historians have debated how much responsibility lies with Seminole leaders for the attack, and whether Bowlegs himself planned it or was drawn into it by younger warriors. The evidence is limited, and the available accounts come largely from American participants. What is clear is that Bowlegs became the principal Seminole leader in the conflict, and that he led his community in a war that he had tried to avoid. Many Seminole leaders appear to have regarded the confrontation as a defensive reaction, not an unprovoked attack.

1855 Compared with 1835

The military and demographic situation in 1855 differed sharply from that of 1835. In 1835 the Seminoles and their Black allies had numbered in the thousands, with large settled towns, large herds, and a network of alliances. In 1855 there were perhaps a few hundred Seminoles, with estimates ranging up to about four hundred, of whom only a modest number were fighting men. Their settlements were concentrated in the Big Cypress and Everglades region, and they had few Black allies, since many Black Seminoles had been deported, had gone to Mexico, or had been absorbed elsewhere. They lacked the numbers to engage in anything resembling the large operations of 1836. By contrast, the United States had a much larger settler population in Florida, a state government and militia, an army with experience in the swamps and a network of forts, and the ability to commit steamboats, supplies, and money. The Seminoles retained their knowledge of the country and their capacity for evasion and ambush, and these advantages would prove considerable. But the balance had moved against them, and the Third Seminole War would be fought by a small people against a state that wanted a final resolution.

Division V · Section 67.19

The Third Seminole War, 1855–1858: Final Campaigns and Forced Departures

The Third Seminole War was a long, small-scale conflict conducted mostly through patrols, raids, and the destruction of settlements, with few set-piece battles. The state of Florida called out volunteers, and the army, supported by naval detachments and by shallow-draft boats, established posts at the edges of the Big Cypress and the Everglades and sent patrols into the country. Command in the department passed through several officers, and the effort was often hampered by divided authority between federal and state forces, by disease, and by the difficulty of finding an enemy that avoided combat. Seminole fighters struck at isolated settlements, supply parties, and patrols, usually in small groups, and then withdrew into the swamps. Raids along the Peace River, the Indian River, and the lower Gulf coast alarmed settlers and kept the state in a posture of defense.

The army's methods paralleled those of the later Second War. Patrols located and destroyed Seminole camps, burned gardens, and seized stores of food and tools. Canoe expeditions entered the Everglades and the lake country, and captured Seminoles, often women and children, were held at posts or sent west. These operations steadily reduced the Seminoles' resources, and they also placed small family groups under severe strain. American officials combined military pressure with persuasion. They worked to bring delegations of Seminoles from the Indian Territory to Florida to urge their kin to emigrate, and they offered financial inducements to leaders and warriors, with payments reported to vary by status and to reach substantial sums for the principal leaders. These offers, combined with the destruction of resources and the difficulty of sustaining a long war, gradually wore down the willingness of some groups to remain.

Billy Bowlegs and the Decisions of Seminole Families

Billy Bowlegs led his followers through the campaigns of 1856 and 1857, but by 1857 and early 1858 he was in contact with American officers, and in the spring of 1858 he agreed to emigrate with a part of his community. In May 1858 Bowlegs departed Florida with a group recorded by the Florida state archives as 163 other people; other historical accounts and passenger lists use different totals or count subsets of that contingent. The group traveled from the Gulf coast through New Orleans toward Indian Territory. The government declared the war ended that month. Bowlegs lived among the Seminoles in the West afterward. Accounts of his subsequent death and purported Union military service are contested and sometimes conflate different Seminole leaders who bore the name Billy Bowlegs; such identification should not be presented as settled here.

His decision should not be read as the decision of all Seminoles. Different leaders and families made different choices. Some agreed to depart, persuaded by the combination of pressure, payments, and the promise of reunion with relatives in the West. Others, including those associated with Abiaka and other elders, refused to leave and withdrew deeper into the Everglades and the Big Cypress. Families weighed the dangers of continued flight against the costs of exile, and they did so under conditions of hunger and fear. The sources, produced mainly by army officers, record numbers and movements but say little of these deliberations, and Seminole oral traditions emphasize the choice of those who stayed.

The departure of 1858 did not result in the universal removal that the government had sought. The number who remained is uncertain: contemporary and later estimates often suggest approximately two hundred people, with considerable uncertainty about counting isolated families. The army and the state had no practical means of finding and removing them without a renewed campaign for which there was no political appetite, and the outbreak of the Civil War soon diverted national attention. The Seminoles who remained in Florida thus became the ancestors of the people who live there today.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Seminole Political and Military Strategies, 1817–1858

What Changed

  • The scale of Seminole forces and communities declined from thousands of people in 1817 and 1835 to a few hundred in 1855
  • The geography of resistance shifted southward, from the Apalachicola and Suwannee country in 1818, to the Withlacoochee in 1835 and 1836, to the Everglades and Big Cypress by the 1840s and 1850s
  • Black Seminole participation, central in 1816, 1818, and 1836, diminished as deportation, emigration, and flight removed many from Florida
  • American strategy changed from raids and invasions to large armies, then to fort networks, and finally to a war of attrition against subsistence
  • Seminole leadership moved from the hereditary and town leaders of the Alachua period to war leaders and dispersed band leaders in the later wars

What Stayed the Same

  • The reliance on dispersal, mobility, and knowledge of the landscape to avoid decisive defeat
  • Consensus-based, decentralized decision-making, with leaders who negotiated without surrendering independence
  • Refusal to accept removal as a legitimate settlement and the insistence on remaining in Florida
  • Pressure from slaveholders and settlers for land and for the control of people, and the use of diplomacy under force by the United States
  • The role of women, families, and agriculture as the foundation of resistance

Why the pattern matters: The change was uneven and often forced, and Seminole communities faced it with markedly different resources at different times. The combination of continuity and change explains why the Seminoles could survive in reduced numbers while the United States repeatedly adapted without achieving removal. Students should note who experienced change most directly: families in the path of campaigns and Black Seminoles subject to enslavement felt the most drastic transformations, while the core strategy of dispersal and persistence showed the greatest continuity.

Division V · Section 67.20

Unconquered: Seminole Survival, Sovereignty, and Historical Memory

Taken together, the three conventional wars form a long struggle over Florida, Indigenous land, freedom, and political independence. The First Seminole War extended American power into Florida and destroyed communities that provided refuge from slavery. The Second was an effort to compel an entire people to leave their homeland, and it failed to achieve that aim at enormous cost to both sides. The Third was an attempt to complete what the Second had not finished, and it too stopped short. The wars ended with large numbers of Seminoles and Black Seminoles removed and their communities in Florida greatly reduced. They also ended with a surviving core of Seminole people still in the peninsula, who had never signed a treaty of surrender.

The people who remained under leaders such as Abiaka, who is reported to have died around 1860, endured in the Everglades and Big Cypress. During the rest of the nineteenth century they lived in small camps, hunted, farmed, fished, and traded at settlements, often in conditions of poverty and isolation, and they preserved their languages, Mikasuki and Muscogee, as well as clan structure, ceremonial life, and political independence. Later decades brought new pressures, including the drainage of the Everglades, the plume trade and other commerce, tourism, and the expansion of Florida's population, topics that belong to later chapters. The cultural and political continuities maintained during and after the wars provided the foundation for the later institutional history of the Seminole peoples.

The Descendant Communities

Three principal modern tribal governments descend in different ways from the communities discussed in this chapter. The Seminole Tribe of Florida, whose members trace their ancestry largely to those who remained in Florida, organized a constitutional government in 1957, an episode treated alongside the broader history of tribal government reorganization in Chapter 97, and its later economic history, including its role in the growth of tribal gaming, is treated in Chapter 122. The Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida, composed largely of Mikasuki-speaking people also descended from those who remained, gained federal recognition in 1962 and maintains its own government, language, and traditions. The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma descends from those who were removed to Indian Territory, where they established a distinct nation, and its history includes the experiences of Black Seminoles and their descendants, whose status and relationships within the nation have been contested and are discussed further in Chapter 144. Black Seminole descendants also live in communities in Oklahoma, Texas, Mexico, and the Bahamas, each with its own historical memory. The later institutional histories of these communities are not narrated in this chapter.

What It Means to Be Unconquered

The word unconquered has special significance in Seminole self-understanding, and the Seminole Tribe of Florida uses it prominently in expressions of its history. It rests on the fact that the Seminoles never signed a treaty ending the wars, that their ancestors were never compelled by force to leave Florida in its entirety, and that their political independence endured. It does not claim that the Seminoles suffered no losses or that all resisted successfully. It recognizes the deportations, the deaths, and the destruction. It also recognizes that a people who lost thousands of their kin and much of their land nonetheless remained.

Students should therefore distinguish three things: the military results of the wars, in which the United States had far greater resources and removed most of the Seminole population; the persistence of Seminole communities in Florida; and the Seminole understanding of themselves as a people who were not defeated. These are not contradictory. The first describes what the army did, the second describes what happened to a community, and the third describes how a people has remembered and interpreted its history. A history that accepts only the first would reproduce the perspective of the conquerors, and a history that accepts only the third would leave out the suffering and the losses. A full account requires all three.

Historical Debate

Three Separate Wars or One Prolonged Struggle?

The question: Should the conflicts of 1817–1818, 1835–1842, and 1855–1858 be understood as three separate wars, as the conventional United States numbering implies, or as phases of a substantially continuous struggle against encroachment and removal, as the Seminole Tribe of Florida and many historians emphasize?

Interpretation A — Three distinct wars: The conventional framework follows the periods of declared United States military campaigns, the congressional appropriations and orders that framed them, and the official declarations of their endings. It captures real differences of scale, objective, leadership, and context. The First War was a short invasion directed at the frontier and Spanish Florida, the Second was a protracted campaign of removal, and the Third was a smaller conflict that followed a period of relative stability.
Interpretation B — One prolonged struggle: Seminole historical accounts and scholars influenced by them argue that pressure on Seminole communities never ceased. The wars were punctuated episodes in a long struggle over land and freedom, with the same underlying causes, including territorial expansion, the interests of slaveholders, and the intent to remove the Seminoles. The intervals were periods of restriction, treaty pressure, and violence, and the Seminoles did not experience formal endings as final.
Interpretation C — Variation in how the long conflict is bounded: Some scholars begin the conflict earlier, with the Patriot War and the destruction of Alachua in 1812 and 1813, or treat the Second War as the central event with the others as prelude and epilogue. Others emphasize the shifting meaning of "war" in a conflict that combined raids, treaties, deportations, and administrative coercion.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: army records, treaties and congressional documents, travelers' and officers' accounts, Spanish and territorial records, and Seminole oral tradition and tribal historical research. They differ in how they weigh evidence of continuity, such as the persistence of leaders, communities, and grievances across the intervals, against evidence of discontinuity, such as changes in scale and in the composition of the forces involved.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology, terminology, and interpretation, and less the underlying facts. It involves whose categories should organize the narrative, those of the army that fought the wars or those of the communities that endured them, and how to define a war in a conflict with no clear beginning or end.

Why it matters: The choice affects how students understand responsibility, agency, and outcome. A three-war framework can make the conflicts appear as separate frontier episodes and can imply that peace prevailed between them. A single-struggle framework emphasizes continuity and the persistence of Seminole sovereignty, but it can obscure the real distinctions among the phases and the particular choices made in each. This chapter uses the conventional numbering for clarity while treating the larger pattern as essential to understanding why the Seminoles survived.

Then identify what evidence, such as additional Seminole sources or records from the periods between the wars, would help determine which framework better explains the historical record.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Seminole Wars as a long political and military struggle over Florida, Indigenous sovereignty, freedom from slavery, and the reach of an expanding settler state. Its central argument is that Seminole and Black Seminole communities resisted conquest and removal over decades through diplomacy, alliances, ecological knowledge, and armed action, and that the United States, despite escalating its military operations, never achieved the complete removal of the Seminole people from Florida.

The Seminoles were not a single people who came to Florida ready-made. Their communities formed during the eighteenth century from Muscogee-speaking and Mikasuki-speaking migrants, surviving older Florida peoples, and people who had escaped slavery, in a borderland contested by Spain, Britain, and the United States. Political authority resided in towns and councils, and leaders such as Micanopy held prestige without commanding all Seminoles. Black communities, descended from fugitives and from Spanish Florida's tradition of sanctuary, lived in alliance with Seminole towns in relationships that ranged from autonomous maroon settlements to tributary arrangements and kinship, and American slaveholders regarded them as a threat to the institution of slavery.

The First Seminole War grew from the collision of American territorial ambition, the interests of slaveholders, and the arrival of Red Stick refugees after the Creek War. The destruction of the fortified settlement at Prospect Bluff in 1816, the attack on Fowltown in 1817, and Andrew Jackson's invasion of Spanish Florida in 1818 established a pattern of intervention across a border and demonstrated the weakness of Spanish authority. The Adams–Onís Treaty and the transfer of Florida in 1821 followed from several causes, of which Jackson's campaign was one, and they placed Seminole communities under United States territorial claims.

Under territorial government, the Treaty of Moultrie Creek confined many Seminoles to an interior reservation with poor land, and the Treaty of Payne's Landing and the Fort Gibson agreement, whose authority and consent were disputed, provided the legal basis for removal. Seminole leaders including Micanopy, Jumper, Alligator, Abiaka, Coacoochee, and Osceola, together with Black Seminole leaders such as Abraham and John Horse, decided to resist, and the decision was collective and strategic. The attacks of December 28, 1835, at Fort King and on Dade's column opened the Second Seminole War.

The Second War lasted until 1842 and was the largest of the three. American commanders from Gaines and Scott to Jesup, Taylor, Armistead, and Worth assembled large forces, built chains of forts, and tried a succession of strategies, but the army could not bring the Seminoles to a decisive defeat. Florida's wetlands, rivers, hammocks, and prairies imposed severe limits on conventional operations, and Seminole knowledge of the land, dispersal of families and settlements, and capacity for ambush and withdrawal turned geography into a strategic resource. The Battle of Lake Okeechobee in 1837 illustrated the difference between a tactical success and the achievement of a strategic aim.

Black Seminoles played military, diplomatic, and political roles throughout the war, as fighters, scouts, interpreters, and negotiators. Their fate was at the center of the conflict, since American victory threatened them with enslavement. The collapse of negotiated assemblies, as when slaveholders appeared at the Tampa Bay camps in 1837, and the seizure of leaders under a flag of truce in the autumn of that year, deepened distrust of American diplomacy. Osceola's death in captivity transformed him into a symbol, but resistance continued under Coacoochee, Abiaka, and other leaders operating through dispersed groups.

American strategy shifted toward attrition. Under Worth, the army attacked the subsistence systems on which families depended, destroying fields, settlements, and cattle, and capturing civilians. The consequences for women, children, and elders were severe, and the effect was to reduce the number of Seminoles in Florida through surrender and deportation. The war was declared over in 1842 without a negotiated peace. It had cost the United States roughly fifteen hundred soldiers, mostly to disease, and very large sums, while thousands of Seminoles and Black Seminoles had been removed. A small number remained, and the government tolerated them while retaining its intention to remove them.

The years after 1842 were not peaceful. The Armed Occupation Act, Florida statehood, and the Swamp Land Act extended settlement and signaled plans to develop the wetlands that sheltered the remaining Seminoles. The Third Seminole War, which began in December 1855 after a survey party damaged the gardens of Billy Bowlegs's community, was a smaller conflict fought against a much reduced population. It ended in 1858 with the departure of Bowlegs and part of his community, but not with universal removal, and Seminole families who remained withdrew deeper into the Everglades and Big Cypress.

Historians divide these conflicts differently. The conventional framework follows United States campaigns and declarations, while the Seminole Tribe of Florida and many scholars emphasize a continuous struggle against encroachment and removal. Both frameworks capture something real, and evidence of continuity across the intervals, in leadership, grievances, and strategy, supports the second without erasing the distinct character of each war.

Finally, the survival of Seminole communities is essential to evaluating the wars' consequences. The Seminole Tribe of Florida, the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida, and the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma descend in different ways from the communities discussed here, and Black Seminole descendants maintain their own histories in several countries. The concept of remaining unconquered rests on the absence of a surrender and on the persistence of a people, and it must be distinguished from the military results of the wars, in which the United States removed most of the Seminole population at great cost. The chapter thus connects the conflicts of removal treated in Chapters 65 and 66 to the continental wars of resistance that follow in Chapter 68 and the chapters beyond it.

Select one of the following: the destruction of Prospect Bluff (1816), the executions of Arbuthnot and Ambrister (1818), Dade's battle (1835), the Battle of Lake Okeechobee (1837), the seizure of Osceola and Coacoochee (1837), the Macomb agreement (1839), or the outbreak of the Third War (1855).
  2. Gather sources. Using library and archival resources, collect at least four sources representing at least three perspectives, such as army reports, congressional documents, newspaper coverage, the account of a Seminole or Black Seminole participant recorded by an American officer, tribal historical materials, and modern scholarship.
  3. Evaluate each source. For each, identify the author, date, audience, purpose, and limits, and note whose perspective is missing.
  4. Map the episode. Create a map or sketch showing locations, forces, and routes, noting where sources disagree about numbers or places.
  5. Write findings. In a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, reconstruct the episode, explain where sources agree and disagree, and use careful language such as "accounts differ" or "the evidence suggests" in place of unqualified assertion.

Library resources to use: Published treaty and congressional collections, army records in printed editions, Seminole tribal historical publications and museum resources, the map collections of university libraries, and the MGU History Library's resources on Florida, Seminole, and Black Seminole history.

Chapter Reflection

The Seminole Wars matter because they show, in unusually concentrated form, how several of the great forces of nineteenth-century North America converged on a single homeland: the expansion of a slaveholding republic, the political ambitions of settler states, the logic of removal, and the determination of Indigenous communities and people escaping slavery to govern themselves and remain free. The conflicts cannot be reduced to a frontier security problem, to the career of any one leader, or to the sequence of American campaigns. They were struggles over land, sovereignty, and freedom in which Seminoles and Black Seminoles acted as strategic and political agents.

The chapter also reveals the limits of military power exercised without a clear political settlement. The United States had overwhelming material advantages, yet it spent years and enormous resources in Florida without attaining its stated purpose. That outcome did not spare Seminole families the destruction of towns, the loss of fields, deportation, and death, and the costs of attrition fell heaviest on those least visible in the military record. Resistance preserved a presence, and it did so at great price.

The wars shaped what followed. They contributed to the pattern of removal and treaty pressure examined in Chapters 65 and 66, and they demonstrated tactics, including the targeting of subsistence and the seizure of leaders under truce, that recur in later campaigns of conquest. Their legacy lies, too, in the separate yet related histories of the Seminole Tribe of Florida, the Miccosukee Tribe, the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma, and Black Seminole descendant communities, each of which holds its own historical memory.

For the larger textbook, the chapter prepares the transition from the southeastern theater of removal to the wider continental conflicts of Volume VI, beginning with the borderland powers of Mexico and Texas in Chapter 68. It also offers a model of historical method that students should carry forward: to read official declarations alongside the experiences of the communities they describe, to distinguish battlefield outcomes from strategic ones, and to recognize that a people's survival is itself a historical outcome that demands explanation.
