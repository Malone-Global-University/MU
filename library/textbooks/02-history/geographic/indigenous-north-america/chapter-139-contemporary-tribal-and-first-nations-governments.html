Contemporary Tribal and First Nations Governments

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

A government is recognized less by what it claims than by what it does: it enacts laws, collects and spends revenue, resolves disputes, registers its citizens, administers land, and keeps working through changes of leadership. This chapter asks how Indigenous nations in North America do these things in the twenty-first century.

Previous chapters traced the long political history that makes this question possible: the treaty relationships of earlier centuries, the Indian Reorganization Act and the Indian Act, the policy reversals of the self-determination era, land claims and modern treaties, tribal courts, economic development, and Indigenous environmental stewardship. Those chapters explained where Indigenous governmental authority comes from and how it was constrained, recognized, and contested. This chapter turns from those origins to operation. It examines the councils, executives, courts, departments, budgets, and intergovernmental agreements through which Indigenous nations govern, and it asks what institutional capacity is needed to turn political authority into public services, enforceable laws, and decisions about a nation's future.

The documentary record for such a study is unusually direct. The Cherokee Nation's constitution, drafted by a constitutional convention in 1999 and ratified by Cherokee citizens in 2003, sets out a government with legislative, executive, and judicial branches. The Nisg̱a'a Final Agreement took effect in 2000, and the Nisg̱a'a Constitution provides for a national government, village governments, and urban locals. The Navajo Nation Council, the Greenlandic parliament Inatsisartut, the First Nations Health Authority in British Columbia, and the Purépecha community government of Cherán in Michoacán each publish laws, reports, or governing documents. Historians of this period can therefore read many Indigenous governments through their own institutional records, supplemented by court decisions, statutes, treaties, audits, and the research of Indigenous and non-Indigenous scholars.

Those records also reveal how varied Indigenous government is. A tribal government in the United States may be a federally recognized political entity with a written constitution, a land base held in trust, and a compact with federal agencies to run its own programs. A First Nation in Canada may govern under the Indian Act, under a leadership-selection code of its own, or under a modern treaty. An Indigenous community in Mexico may govern through a communal assembly operating within a municipal and agrarian legal order. Greenland's public government is a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark, and its institutions serve all residents, not only members of a particular nation. These are not variations on one model. They rest on different constitutional foundations, different histories, and different relationships to surrounding states.

The chapter's central argument is that Indigenous governments are neither cultural organizations nor advocacy groups, and they are not merely administrative arms of other governments. They are continuing political institutions. Their authority, resources, and legal standing vary widely, and the gap between recognized authority and practical capacity is a recurring theme. A nation may have legal authority it lacks the revenue to exercise, or substantial revenue and limited jurisdiction over the people who live within its territory. The chapter therefore pays attention to law, money, personnel, and administration as much as to principle.

To organize that analysis, the chapter introduces a six-dimension Indigenous Governance Capacity Framework: political authority, legal capacity, administrative capacity, fiscal capacity, territorial authority, and institutional legitimacy. The framework is an instructional device of this textbook. It is not an externally validated index, and it is not a tool for ranking nations. Its purpose is to give students a consistent set of questions to ask of each institution they encounter.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines the foundations of contemporary government: political authority, constitutions and customary law, citizenship, and leadership. The second explains the mechanics of executive administration, lawmaking, justice, and public finance. The third compares governance in the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland, so that the American tribal model is not mistaken for the whole continent. The fourth examines governing responsibilities: services, enterprise, land and infrastructure, and emergencies. The fifth turns to intergovernmental relations, accountability, nation-building, and a continental synthesis.

The evidence has limits. Governments publish what they choose to publish, and external agencies publish what law requires. Annual reports and audited statements describe revenue and spending but not always the quality of services or the confidence citizens place in institutions. Court decisions show where jurisdiction is contested but not how daily administration works. Several developments discussed here, including the status of Métis self-government legislation and the implementation of Mexico's 2024 constitutional reform, were still unfolding at the November 2026 editorial horizon, and the text marks them as such.

Historical Context

Indigenous peoples governed themselves for millennia before the modern states surrounding them existed. Earlier chapters examined confederacies such as the Haudenosaunee (Chapter 26), village and clan governments in the Southwest (Chapters 18 and 30), the diplomacy of the Covenant Chain (Chapter 56), and the written constitutionalism of the nineteenth-century Cherokee Nation (Chapter 64). Contemporary Indigenous government is the continuation of that political history under conditions that colonial and settler states reshaped. This chapter takes up only as much of it as is needed to explain present institutions.

Three bodies of law and policy set the conditions under which most Indigenous governments now operate. In Canada, the Indian Act of 1876 created a system of band administration that placed First Nations under federal supervision and defined legal "Indian" status (Chapter 85). In the United States, the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 encouraged tribes to adopt written constitutions and elected councils, often on templates supplied by federal officials (Chapter 97). Termination in the 1950s then attempted to end federal recognition of some tribes (Chapter 104), and the self-determination legislation that followed, beginning with the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, allowed tribes to contract and later compact to administer federal programs themselves (Chapter 114). In Greenland, Home Rule in 1979 and Self-Government in 2009 created a public government within the Kingdom of Denmark, and in Mexico, constitutional recognition of Indigenous peoples evolved through reforms in 1992 and 2001 and, most recently, 2024.

These histories produced governments with mixed origins. Some nations adopted institutions that outside governments proposed, and some modified them, resisted them, or maintained customary institutions alongside them. Others negotiated modern treaties that created new constitutional governments. The chapter therefore avoids two simplifications: that contemporary tribal governments are only creations of colonial law, and that they are simply unbroken survivals of precolonial government. Most combine inherited political traditions with institutions shaped by the legal settings they entered.

A second piece of context is the difference between governing power and governing capacity. Legal recognition does not itself provide revenue, trained staff, roads, water systems, or courthouses. Indigenous governments frequently assume responsibilities, such as health care or child welfare, that are financed largely by other governments through contracts, grants, or transfer agreements. Whether this arrangement is administration of a federal program or an exercise of self-government depends on who designs the program, who sets priorities, who holds legal authority, and who controls the money. The chapter returns to this distinction repeatedly.

A third piece concerns geography and population. Many Indigenous nations govern territories that are large and sparsely populated, such as the Navajo Nation, which spans parts of Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah, or the dispersed villages of Alaska. Others govern small land bases. In both situations many citizens live away from the homeland, a subject Chapter 140 develops, and governments must serve and represent them with limited territorial jurisdiction. Chapter 144 treats the identity questions that underlie membership. Here those subjects appear only as governmental problems.

This chapter uses a six-dimension framework, following the analytical approach of Chapters 137 and 138, to compare how Indigenous governments turn authority into action. It is a proposed MGU instructional framework, not an externally validated governance index. It does not rank nations, and no nation is expected to score evenly across the dimensions. Different nations have different resources and priorities, and the framework's purpose is to ask consistent questions.

Section 139.1

Indigenous Nations as Contemporary Political Communities

The starting point is a distinction that is easy to blur. "Indigenous peoples" can refer to a demographic population: everyone in a country who identifies as Indigenous or is counted as such by a census. An Indigenous nation is something different: a political community with a history, a territory or homeland, citizens or members defined by its own rules, institutions that make collective decisions, and interests that belong to the community as a whole. A census category cannot enact a law, sign a treaty, or operate a school. A nation can. The governments examined in this chapter are the institutions of nations, not of populations.

This distinction explains why a government of an Indigenous nation differs from a cultural organization or an advocacy group. A cultural society may preserve language and ceremony, and an advocacy organization may press a political agenda before legislatures and courts. Both can be valuable and both may be closely connected to a nation. A government, by contrast, claims authority to make binding decisions for a defined community, to regulate conduct, to hold and manage collective property, and to represent the community in relations with other governments. In practice, many organizations blur these categories. Intertribal bodies such as the National Congress of American Indians and the Assembly of First Nations in Canada advocate for governments but are not themselves governments of the nations they serve. Regional service organizations may deliver health or social programs without possessing general governmental authority. Understanding who holds which role is a first task of institutional analysis.

Four distinctions clarify the source of governmental power. Inherent authority originates in the Indigenous political community and predates external recognition. Recognized authority is acknowledged by another legal order; acknowledgement is not itself a grant. Delegated authority is conferred by another government for a defined purpose. Negotiated authority is specified or protected by an agreement or treaty. These categories can overlap: a treaty may recognize an existing power and specify how it is exercised, while a service contract may finance a responsibility without transferring legislative jurisdiction. In the United States, federal law recognizes and limits retained tribal powers. In Canada, the Indian Act enumerates certain powers, while First Nations assert inherent self-government and may negotiate constitutionally protected arrangements. The precise scope of asserted inherent rights remains subject to legal and political dispute.

These distinctions matter practically because the source of authority affects what a government can do and how secure its powers are. Powers granted by an ordinary statute can be amended by the legislature that created them. Powers protected in a treaty or constitutional instrument are harder to change unilaterally. Powers claimed as inherent may be asserted without waiting for external authorization but may be contested in court. Contemporary Indigenous governments often rely on a layered structure: inherent authority for legitimacy, treaties or statutes for recognition, and agreements or contracts for particular programs.

The recent history of how these governments reached their present form can be told briefly. The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries brought severe restrictions on Indigenous governance in both the United States and Canada: federal agents or Indian agents supervised councils, the Indian Act limited band powers and suppressed customary leadership in many communities, and removal, allotment, and residential schooling disrupted political institutions (Chapters 85–88). The Indian Reorganization Act then established, in 1934, a federal framework for tribal constitutions, though the model constitutions offered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs encouraged uniformity and often left significant authority subject to federal approval (Chapter 97). Termination and relocation followed in the 1950s (Chapters 104 and 105). From the late 1960s, federal policy moved toward self-determination, and the legislation of 1975 permitted tribal governments to administer programs previously run by federal agencies (Chapter 114). In Canada, the 1982 constitutional recognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights, and later negotiations of self-government agreements and modern treaties, created additional pathways (Chapter 117).

It would be a mistake to read that history as the invention of Indigenous governments by legislation. Many nations governed themselves through councils, clans, chiefs, assemblies, and customary law during the periods when external states denied them recognition, and many used the formal structures offered to them for their own purposes. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy's Grand Council remained active through the twentieth century, and the Cherokee Nation maintained a political community through the dissolution attempts of the early twentieth century and re-established a formal government. The point is not that external law was irrelevant, but that the relationship between recognition and political existence is more complicated than a single origin story allows.

Historical Vocabulary
Nation, Government, Sovereignty, and Jurisdiction

Nation refers here to a political community with its own history, territory or homeland, citizens, and institutions. It does not imply a fully independent state in the modern sense. Government refers to the institutions through which a nation or other polity makes and enforces collective decisions. A nation can exist without a formally recognized government, and a government can exist, as in a corporation-like service body, without exercising general political authority.

Sovereignty describes the claim to ultimate political authority within a sphere. In this textbook, inherent sovereignty is the claim that Indigenous nations hold political authority that predates colonial states. Legal systems recognize it differently: United States law speaks of tribes as "domestic dependent nations" with retained but limited sovereignty; Canadian law speaks of Aboriginal and treaty rights and an inherent right of self-government under section 35; Mexican law has recognized self-determination within the unity of the state; Greenland's Self-Government Act recognizes the Greenlandic people under international law.

Jurisdiction is the legal power to decide and to govern over particular subjects, persons, or places. A government can claim sovereignty and still face limits on jurisdiction, for example over non-members, over particular crimes, or over land it does not own. Potential misunderstanding: sovereignty, jurisdiction, and capacity are not the same thing. A nation may be sovereign in principle, have limited jurisdiction in law, and lack the funds to exercise its jurisdiction fully.

Section 139.2

Constitutions, Customary Law, and Indigenous Political Traditions

Indigenous governments rest on several different foundations. Some operate under written constitutions adopted through formal votes. Some operate mainly under customary law, transmitted through ceremony, clan relationships, and oral tradition. Many combine the two. A constitution may be a single document or a collection of enacted laws, treaties, and customary understandings. It is therefore misleading to classify nations simply as "constitutional" or "traditional." The more useful question is how each government identifies authority, defines offices, and settles disputes about the limits of power.

Several customary models persist. Hereditary leadership, in which offices pass through families or clans, continues in many Northwest Coast and Haudenosaunee communities. Clan mothers in Haudenosaunee systems traditionally nominate and may remove chiefs. General assemblies, in which community members deliberate and decide, appear in a number of Pueblo communities and in Indigenous communities in Mexico, where the communal assembly is often the highest authority. Consensus-based councils operate in some Subarctic and Plains communities and in elders' councils that advise or constrain elected officials. Chapter 26 and Chapter 30 examined the historical roots of some of these traditions.

In the United States, the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 pushed many tribes toward a standard pattern of a constitution, an elected council, and a chairperson or president. Many tribes adopted IRA constitutions, others adopted constitutions outside the Act, others retained customary or traditional governments, and some, such as the Seneca Nation of Indians and the Cherokee Nation, have constitutional histories that predate or diverge from the 1934 model. In Canada, the Indian Act's band-council system, with elections under the Act's provisions, was imposed on many communities that had hereditary or customary governance; some of those communities continue to maintain traditional government alongside the elected band council. The Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario is a well-known case in which the Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs Council and the elected council each assert authority in different matters. This is a durable example of legal pluralism: the coexistence of more than one legal and political order within one community.

The Cherokee Nation's constitution offers the clearest documentary example of a contemporary written constitution drafted by a nation for itself. The Cherokee Nation had earlier constitutions, including one adopted in 1827 and one in 1839 after removal (Chapter 64), and a 1976 constitution adopted under federal supervision after the dissolution policies of the early twentieth century. In 1999 a constitutional convention of Cherokee citizens produced a new draft. Cherokee voters ratified it in 2003. The document establishes a tripartite government: a Principal Chief and Deputy Principal Chief in the executive branch, a Tribal Council in the legislative branch, and a judicial branch headed by a Supreme Court. It also contains provisions on citizenship, elections, and amendment, and it defines the relationship between the Nation and the United States as a government-to-government one. The Council includes seats for districts within the Nation's jurisdiction and seats for citizens living outside it, an example of institutional design responding to a dispersed citizenry.

By contrast, the Navajo Nation's institutions rest on a body of statute, the Navajo Nation Code, and on Diné fundamental law, which the Council recognized in the 2002 Diné Bi Beenahaz'áanii Act as principles of Navajo culture and values that guide governance. The Navajo Nation's government never adopted a conventional constitution in the 1930s; its structure was built largely through Council legislation, with a major governmental reform in 1989 establishing separate branches. In the Pueblos of New Mexico, governing structures differ from pueblo to pueblo: some operate through governors and councils chosen by religious or traditional leaders, some by elections, some under written constitutions, and some under unwritten customary law. Information about internal religious and political selection is often not published, and scholars rely on what the pueblos choose to make public. This is a reminder that opacity to outsiders does not mean absence of institutions or of accountability within the community.

Primary Source Window

Context: Earlier Cherokee constitutions dated to the nineteenth century, and a 1976 constitution had been adopted under federal approval. The text is published by the Cherokee Nation government. Paraphrase of the structure: The constitution assigns legislative power to an elected Tribal Council, executive authority to an elected Principal Chief and Deputy Principal Chief, and judicial authority to Cherokee courts, with provisions for elections, citizenship, and amendment. It is a paraphrase of the document's overall organization, not a quotation of any provision. Students should consult the published text for specific article language. Analysis: The constitution shows an Indigenous nation designing a modern separation of powers by its own process, rather than adopting a federal model. It treats judicial review and an independent judiciary as part of legitimate government. Limitations: A constitution states formal design, not practice. It does not represent perspectives of citizens who opposed provisions, nor does it show the federal government's view of the Nation's authority.

Section 139.3

Citizenship, Political Membership, and Representation

Membership defines the political community. Who counts as a citizen of an Indigenous nation determines who votes, who may hold office, who receives services, and whose collective interests the government represents. The chapter treats membership here as a governmental matter and leaves the deeper questions of identity, descent, and blood quantum to Chapter 144.

Terminology differs by country. In the United States, tribes determine their own membership criteria, and tribal citizenship is political membership. Federal Indian law has treated certain classifications tied to tribal membership as political rather than racial, notably in the context of federal Indian affairs; that reasoning is not a blanket rule for every classification or dispute. Criteria vary widely. The Navajo Nation requires a minimum degree of Navajo ancestry, the Cherokee Nation bases citizenship on lineal descent from persons on the Dawes Rolls without a minimum blood requirement, and other nations use residency, lineal descent, or matrilineal or patrilineal rules. In Canada, Indian status is a federal legal category defined by the Indian Act, while First Nation membership may be determined by the First Nation itself if it has assumed control of its membership list under the Act's provisions or under departmental Band Lists where it has not assumed membership control. The Indian Register, which records status, is distinct from these lists. Many people have status without being members of a particular First Nation's list and the reverse. Treaty beneficiary enrollment under modern treaties is determined by the treaty's enrolment provisions. In Mexico, belonging to an Indigenous community is generally expressed through self-identification, community membership, and recognition under the community's own normative system, rather than through a national register. Citizenship in the surrounding state is a separate matter: Indigenous people are, in most cases, also citizens of the United States, Canada, or Mexico. [49]

Political participation follows membership but depends on institutional design. Most tribal governments hold elections in which enrolled citizens of voting age vote, with residency requirements in some cases. The Cherokee Nation's Council includes at-large representation for citizens living outside the Nation's jurisdictional area, and the Nation has pursued a treaty-based claim, arising from the Treaty of New Echota of 1835, to a delegate in the United States House of Representatives, a claim that Congress had not acted on as of this edition's research. Some Canadian First Nations allow members living off-reserve to vote in band elections, a right that the Supreme Court of Canada required in its 1999 Corbiere decision for Indian Act band elections. Nisg̱a'a citizens living in urban areas participate through urban locals, discussed in Section 139.10.

The relationship between population distribution and governmental organization is a persistent challenge. In several nations, a majority of citizens live outside the territory over which the government has direct jurisdiction. A government may provide scholarships, health services, language programs, or voting access to those citizens, yet it generally cannot regulate conduct, tax residents, or run courts in distant cities in the way it can within its own land base. Its responsibilities toward citizens therefore extend beyond its territorial jurisdiction, while its legal authority does not. Service delivery to dispersed citizens often depends on agreements with state, provincial, or municipal governments, on federal funding formulas that favor on-reserve residents, and on community organizations. Chapter 140 returns to these arrangements.

The Cherokee citizenship example also requires attention to the descendants of Cherokee Freedmen. The 1866 treaty and subsequent disputes about citizenship show that the political authority to establish membership operates within a particular treaty and constitutional history. The Nation's own historical account recognizes full citizenship, including eligibility to hold office, for descendants of Cherokee Freedmen. Membership administration is thus not simply a genealogical task; it requires records, an adjudicative process, and compliance with the nation's governing law and treaty obligations. The significance of the case is that the rules defining the electorate affect every other institution, from representation on the Council to the composition of the next generation of leadership. Chapter 144 examines the historical controversy more fully. [3]

Section 139.4

Political Leadership, Elections, and Community Authority

Indigenous governments select leaders in many ways. Elected chiefs, presidents, chairpersons, and council members predominate in the United States and in Canada. Hereditary chiefs, clan representatives, and elders' councils continue to hold authority in numerous communities. In Mexico, community assemblies and communal authorities exercise governance in a very large number of communities. Executive administrators, who run the departments that implement policy, are often appointed professionals rather than elected officials. The relationship among these roles varies by nation and cannot be summarized with a single formula.

In the United States, tribal constitutions typically provide for elections at regular intervals, often every two to four years, with provisions on eligibility, recall, and removal. Elections are administered by tribal election boards or commissions, and disputes may go to tribal courts, which have sometimes been called on to resolve contested elections. The Cherokee Nation's Election Commission and judiciary, for example, are part of its constitutional structure. In some tribes, leaders are chosen by the council from among its members rather than by popular vote, a practice found in several nations.

In Canada, the leadership-selection landscape is especially varied. Under the Indian Act's election provisions, a First Nation elects a chief and councillors, historically for two-year terms, although the Act's terms have since been joined by an alternative. The First Nations Elections Act of 2014 provides an opt-in system with four-year terms and more detailed procedures for nominations and appeals. Many First Nations select leaders under custom systems, determined by community election codes or by hereditary and customary processes; self-government agreements and modern treaties establish leadership provisions in their own constitutions. Indigenous Services Canada describes these categories of Indian Act, First Nations Elections Act, custom, and self-government agreement leadership selection as the principal alternatives. This variety is itself a form of governance diversity: the same country contains elected councils, hereditary systems, and treaty-based national governments.

Electoral disputes and succession are an ordinary feature of political life and occur in every system. They have arisen in contested tribal elections in the United States, in disputes about the validity of band elections in Canada, and in conflicts between elected and hereditary leadership. Such disputes do not show that Indigenous governments are unusually unstable; they show that leadership selection depends on legitimacy, which different communities source differently. Gender representation also varies. Women have historically held influential positions in many Indigenous political traditions, including the selecting and counseling roles of Haudenosaunee clan mothers, while colonial administrative systems often marginalized women's political roles. Contemporary elected councils include women as chiefs, presidents, and council members, though levels of representation differ from nation to nation, and Indian Act status rules long affected women's membership rights, as Chapter 117 noted.

Customary and elected leadership are not necessarily incompatible. A nation may have hereditary chiefs who hold responsibility for territory, ceremony, and law, and an elected council that administers programs and negotiates with governments. These institutions may cooperate, as they do in many Northwest Coast communities, or conflict, as in disputes over resource development in which elected councils and hereditary leaders have taken different positions. The more productive analytical question is the division of responsibility: which body holds authority over which matters, and how are conflicts settled. Contemporary administrative demands, such as managing budgets, complying with funders' reporting rules, and negotiating agreements, tend to be assigned to elected councils and professional staff, while traditional authority often retains responsibility for culture, land stewardship, and law. Where these roles are clearly defined, they can be complementary.

Multiple Perspectives

Different Sources of Political Legitimacy

The viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing positions documented in scholarship and public debate. They are not quotations from any single individual.

An elected council member administering programs: Legitimacy comes from an electoral mandate and from compliance with the nation's constitution and funding requirements. Elections provide a regular, transparent way to hold leaders accountable and to meet the reporting duties that funders and courts expect.
A hereditary or clan-based leader: Legitimacy comes from a leader's obligations to land, lineage, ceremony, and law, which continue across generations and do not depend on electoral cycles. From this perspective, an elected body created by outside legislation may administer programs but cannot replace the responsibilities of customary authority.
A citizen living in a distant city: Legitimacy depends on whether the government hears and serves citizens wherever they live. Access to voting, information, and services may matter more than whether the leadership system is elected or customary.
A federal or provincial official: A government that is clearly constituted, with defined offices and procedures, is easier to deal with in funding, consultation, and agreement-making. This view risks treating administrative convenience as equivalent to legitimacy and has historically been used to impose uniform structures.

These perspectives do not hold equal power. External officials have long had the ability to recognize or refuse to recognize particular institutions, while community members have had to work within the structures that result. The purpose of comparing them is to show why disputes over legitimacy are rarely only technical.

Section 139.5

Executive Government and Public Administration

Executive institutions carry out the decisions of a government and manage its daily operations. In Indigenous governments the executive may be a president, principal chief, chairperson, or chief, sometimes with a vice president or deputy, an executive council or cabinet, and a set of administrative departments directed by appointed professionals. In some nations the council as a whole serves as the executive, and a chair or chief presides without a separate branch. The variety reflects constitutional design, the size of the nation, and the history of its administrative institutions.

Two large governments illustrate the differentiated model. The Navajo Nation has a President and Vice President elected by the people, an executive branch organized into divisions and departments, a Council that legislates, and a judicial branch. The 1989 reform of Navajo government created this separation of powers; before then the Council had played much of the executive role. The Cherokee Nation, under its 2003 constitution, has a Principal Chief and Deputy Principal Chief, who lead an executive branch with departments that include health services, education, housing, natural resources, public safety through the Cherokee Nation Marshal Service, and commerce. The Cherokee Nation's health system is among the largest tribally operated systems in the United States.

The functions that Indigenous departments perform resemble those of other governments. They include health clinics and hospitals, schools and scholarship programs, housing authorities, finance and accounting offices, natural-resource and environmental management, roads and transportation, public safety, social services, and community development. What distinguishes them is often their funding structure and the range of responsibilities that fall on a single small government. A tribal government may simultaneously act as a school board, a health department, a housing authority, a land agency, and an employer, with revenue streams that differ for each function and that carry different reporting requirements.

A further distinction concerns elected leadership and professional administration. Elected leaders set policy and represent the community, but programs depend on professional staff: nurses, accountants, engineers, teachers, planners, lawyers, and police officers. Many tribal governments are major employers in their regions, and their civil service systems, personnel policies, and procurement rules are matters of public law and political importance. Tribal personnel and procurement codes often include Indigenous or tribal member preference in hiring, which is a legally recognized practice in the United States under federal law. How effectively a government keeps professional staff, shields routine administration from electoral turnover, and trains citizens for senior administrative positions is central to administrative capacity, a point developed in Section 139.19.

From policy to administrative delivery

Public administration links a decision made at the political level to the experience of a citizen requesting a service. A housing policy, for example, requires eligibility rules, an application process, assessment of demand, budgeting for construction or repair, procurement, and continuing maintenance. The administrative organization must assign responsibility for each of these steps. If a program is funded through an external grant, its staff must also reconcile local eligibility rules with the conditions attached to that funding. A central government with a housing department may deliver the program itself, authorize a housing authority to deliver it, or allocate resources to local institutions. Each method produces a different chain of accountability.

The Cherokee Nation's public directory illustrates the differentiation of functions in a large tribal government. It identifies administrative responsibilities for transportation and infrastructure, health, education, language, real estate, citizenship registration and other functions. Its financial resources office publishes budgets and financial reports. Its executive, legislature and judiciary are distinct from the authorities and business entities associated with the nation. These records allow the historian to distinguish a department that carries out an appropriation from a corporate entity that has its own governing board or a commission that regulates an activity. The organizational chart is therefore a map of responsibility, not simply a list of officials. [4]

Professional continuity is particularly important when elected leadership changes. Personnel policies, records retention, procurement procedures and financial controls preserve the knowledge required to keep a clinic open or a construction contract moving while a new executive establishes priorities. These systems do not remove political responsibility; they make it possible for the new leadership to govern without recreating the entire administrative apparatus. Smaller governments may assign several functions to one official or contract for specialized assistance. A comparison of staff numbers alone therefore tells students little about the quality of administration. The important questions concern the division of work, the reliability of funding and the accessibility of services.

Section 139.6

Legislative Councils, Lawmaking, and Public Policy

Legislative bodies make the rules that an Indigenous government enforces. They go by many names: tribal councils, business committees, general councils, legislative assemblies. Most adopt laws through ordinances, which are general and lasting rules, and resolutions, which express decisions or direct action on particular matters. Some collect their laws in codes, such as the Navajo Nation Code or the Cherokee Nation's codified laws. Some operate by the assembly of all eligible citizens, and some, particularly where customary law governs, make decisions through consensus without a formal legislature. Not every Indigenous government has a separate legislature, and the textbook does not treat the American model as the norm.

The Navajo Nation Council is a useful institutional example. Before the reform the Council had 88 delegates. Voters approved a reduction to 24 in December 2009, implemented through the 2010 election and the ensuing council term. The change reorganized representation rather than abolishing chapters. The delegates are elected from groups of chapters, and the Navajo Nation's 110 chapters remain the local units of government. The Local Governance Act, adopted in 1998, allows chapters to become certified to exercise a broader range of local powers, including planning, budgeting, and managing community resources. The Council works through standing committees, with the Naabik'íyáti' Committee serving as a key body for matters involving the President and for coordinating legislation. The President may sign or veto legislation, and the Council may override a veto by a supermajority.

Lawmaking follows constitutional or statutory rules. Constitutions may limit what a council can do: requiring a referendum for certain changes, protecting citizens' rights, limiting borrowing, or requiring that some decisions be made by an assembly. Public hearings, committee review, legal review by the nation's attorney general or legislative counsel, and executive approval are common steps. In the United States, some tribal constitutions and laws historically required Secretarial approval of amendments or certain actions by the Secretary of the Interior, a federal oversight role that many tribes have worked to remove through constitutional amendment.

The point of studying these procedures is to understand how policy becomes operational. A law that is passed but not funded, not published, or not enforced leaves the community's problem unchanged. Capacity therefore depends not only on council authority but on legislative staff, legal drafters, accessible codes, and executive implementation. Indigenous governments have addressed this through publishing codes online, creating legislative service offices, and supporting training. The Navajo Nation Office of Legislative Services and its public records are examples of this institutional infrastructure.

A documented legislative mechanism

The Navajo Nation's 2009 vote on legislative representation demonstrates a form of lawmaking in which citizens themselves initiated institutional change. The old council size and the number of local chapters were not identical. Changing the council from 88 to 24 seats required electoral districts that combined multiple chapters, whereas the chapter institutions continued to exist. The Office of Legislative Services identifies the resulting Council as a body of 24 delegates representing 110 chapters. The reform therefore changed the number and geographic constituency of lawmakers without erasing the local political communities they represented. A student should distinguish the vote authorizing the change, the electoral arrangements necessary to implement it, and the subsequent work of the smaller legislature. [5] [6] [45]

The Council's published 2026 legislative register also shows how a proposal becomes a specific authorization. One entry concerns an emergency appropriation of US$6 million from the Unreserved, Undesignated Fund Balance for assistance to the 110 chapters, with a waiver of specified financial law provisions. The text identifies the legislative action, source of money, beneficiaries and proposed legal exception rather than just announcing an emergency policy. The register is evidence of the legislative record, not proof that each chapter spent the authorized funds. Implementation would be established by allotments, payment records and subsequent reporting. This distinction applies equally to ordinances, resolutions and appropriations in other governments. [7]

Section 139.7

Tribal Courts, Justice Institutions, and Legal Administration

Indigenous justice institutions include courts, appellate bodies, prosecutors and defenders, police and other enforcement agencies, and processes that do not rely on adversarial litigation. In the United States, hundreds of tribal justice systems operate, ranging from fully developed court systems with trial and appellate levels to small courts with a single judge. The Navajo Nation has one of the largest tribal court systems, with district courts and a Supreme Court. The Cherokee Nation has a District Court and a Supreme Court, which replaced the earlier Judicial Appeals Tribunal after the 2003 constitution. Many courts apply written codes, tribal common law, and customary law, and tribal judges are appointed or elected according to each nation's constitution.

Alternatives to litigation are well developed. The Navajo Peacemaker Court, established in the early 1980s, uses community-based dispute resolution grounded in Navajo concepts of relationship and harmony, led by respected community members rather than judges. Other nations operate healing-to-wellness courts, restorative justice programs, and elders' councils. In Canada, community justice programs, Gladue courts, and Indigenous-led justice initiatives operate in many places, and the Nisg̱a'a Final Agreement provides the Nisg̱a'a Lisims Government with law-making authority and the capacity to establish courts. In Mexico, community justice is a long-standing practice in many Indigenous communities, and in the state of Guerrero the community police system known as the CRAC-PC, created in 1995, was later given recognition in state legislation. In Greenland, courts operate within the Danish judicial system, and the Self-Government Act allows the transfer of some justice responsibilities by agreement.

Understanding jurisdiction is central. Civil jurisdiction concerns noncriminal disputes such as contracts, family law, and property. Criminal jurisdiction concerns offenses and punishments. Territorial jurisdiction depends on location. Personal jurisdiction depends on who is involved, such as whether a defendant is a tribal member, a member of another tribe, or a non-Indian. In the United States, federal law restricts tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, a rule rooted in the 1978 Supreme Court decision in Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe and partially modified by Congress, most notably in the Violence Against Women Act reauthorizations of 2013 and 2022, which restored tribal criminal jurisdiction over some non-Indian offenders in specified cases. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 expanded tribes' sentencing authority when specified procedural protections are present. The Supreme Court's 2020 decision in McGirt v. Oklahoma and 2022 decision in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta shifted the balance of federal, state, and tribal responsibility in eastern Oklahoma, increasing both the demand on tribal courts and the complexity of coordination. Chapter 118 treats the doctrinal history and Chapter 130 the history of criminal justice. This section addresses their administrative consequences.

Jurisdictional gaps affect daily operations. When a tribal police officer encounters a non-Indian suspect, when an offense occurs on land with mixed ownership, or when a state claims concurrent authority, institutions must coordinate through cross-deputization, memoranda of understanding, and information-sharing. In Public Law 280 states, where Congress transferred certain jurisdiction to states in 1953 (Chapter 104), tribal courts and state courts may overlap in ways that complicate funding and responsibility. In Canada, policing of First Nations communities has relied on funding agreements under a federal policing program, and shortages of resources and the lack of a clear legislative basis have been subjects of continuing policy discussion. These examples show that legal authority is not the only determinant of justice administration: staffing, funding, facilities, and coordination arrangements shape what courts and police can actually do.

In practice, jurisdiction must be resolved before a case can be prosecuted. In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the United States Supreme Court held that Congress had not disestablished the Muscogee reservation for purposes of the Major Crimes Act. Later proceedings considered other reservation territories. Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) held that the state and federal government have concurrent jurisdiction to prosecute certain crimes committed by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country. Neither decision made all offenses exclusively tribal matters. The 2022 Violence Against Women Act, meanwhile, recognizes participating tribes' special criminal jurisdiction over specified covered offenses, including stalking and sexual violence, subject to statutory requirements and procedural safeguards. It is not unrestricted criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. [8] [48]

These distinctions create administrative consequences. Police, prosecutors and victim advocates must identify the relevant forum, preserve information for transfer between agencies and coordinate protective services. A court may have authority under a statute but need additional judges, counsel, record systems and detention agreements to exercise it. The Department of Justice's reimbursement and grant programs for special tribal criminal jurisdiction illustrate the connection between legal recognition and the costs of implementation. The legal power to hear a case does not, by itself, supply the personnel or operating budget required to do so. [9]

Public finance determines what a government can actually do. Indigenous governments finance their operations from a mix of sources, and the mix differs sharply among nations. The principal categories, which this section examines in turn, are own-source taxation, intergovernmental transfers, enterprise income, natural resource revenue, investment income, restricted grants and contracts, and borrowing.

Taxation is the clearest expression of fiscal self-determination, but its scope is constrained. In the United States, tribes have inherent authority to tax within their jurisdiction, recognized by the Supreme Court in cases such as Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe (1982) and Kerr-McGee Corp. v. Navajo Tribe (1985), but tribal taxation of non-members on non-Indian-owned land has been limited by later decisions. Tribal taxes also face competition with state taxes, producing disputes over double taxation that tribes and states have resolved through compacts in matters such as fuel and tobacco. Federal tax law treats tribes in some respects like states, allowing tribal governments to issue tax-exempt bonds for essential governmental functions. In Canada, First Nations may levy property taxes on reserve lands under the Indian Act's taxation provisions, and the First Nations Fiscal Management Act of 2005 created supporting institutions: the First Nations Tax Commission, which oversees taxation laws; the First Nations Financial Management Board, which sets financial standards and certifies financial management systems; and the First Nations Finance Authority, which pools the borrowing of participating First Nations to finance infrastructure at lower cost, secured by tax and other revenue streams. This is an unusual example of a legislated, Indigenous-governed financial system in which First Nations opt in.

Intergovernmental transfers dominate many budgets. In the United States, tribes receive funds through Bureau of Indian Affairs and Indian Health Service programs, housing block grants under the Native American Housing Assistance and Self-Determination Act, and programs of other federal agencies. Under self-governance compacts, tribes receive funding in a single agreement and have more discretion in how they use it than under contracts that require line-by-line compliance. In Canada, funding flows through Indigenous Services Canada and Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada under grants, contribution agreements, and, since 2019, ten-year grants for eligible First Nations that provide more predictable funding. Self-governing nations negotiate fiscal financing agreements. Greenland's annual block grant from Denmark is a large part of Greenland's public budget, and the Self-Government Act contains rules on how mineral revenues affect it.

Enterprise income can be substantial. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, net revenues from tribal gaming must be used for specified purposes, including funding tribal government operations and programs, promoting economic development, and supporting charitable causes and local government agencies, and payments to individual citizens require an approved plan. Chapter 122 treated gaming as an economic development subject. The governmental issue here is how revenue becomes budgeted public money. Some nations treat enterprise distributions as general revenue, some dedicate them to specific programs, some place a portion in permanent funds. Natural resource revenue from coal, oil, gas, minerals, timber, or fisheries has historically been important for nations such as the Navajo Nation and has also exposed budgets to commodity price swings and resource depletion. Investment income from trust and reserve funds can provide stability, and some nations adopt constitutional or statutory rules limiting how much principal may be spent.

Budgeting follows several distinctions. Operating budgets cover recurring costs such as salaries and services; capital budgets cover infrastructure and equipment; restricted funds must be spent for specified purposes, while unrestricted funds can be allocated by the government. Borrowing is a capital financing tool, and public debt must be serviced from future revenue. Financial reserves protect against shortfalls, procurement rules govern purchasing, and independent audits test whether statements are reliable. In the United States, tribal governments that spend federal funds above a threshold are subject to the federal single audit requirement, and in Canada, First Nations submit audited consolidated financial statements to funders. A related Canadian statute, the First Nations Financial Transparency Act of 2013, required publication of audited statements and remuneration, and its enforcement has been the subject of political debate since 2015; its statutory requirements and administrative enforcement policy should be distinguished.

A basic point follows. Substantial business revenue does not necessarily eliminate fiscal dependence or guarantee sufficient resources for public services. A nation may have a profitable enterprise whose revenue is small relative to the cost of health, housing, water, and roads for a large and dispersed population, or whose income is volatile. Conversely, a small nation with few enterprises may govern effectively using transfer funds and shared services. Fiscal capacity is therefore about the reliability, flexibility, and sufficiency of revenue, not just its size.

Section 139.9

Tribal Governments in the United States

Tribal governments in the United States are diverse in size, structure, and history. Federal law recognizes them as political entities with inherent rights of self-government, exercised subject to the legal circumstances governing their exercise. The Bureau of Indian Affairs maintains the official list of federally recognized tribes. The list published in December 2024 counted 574 entities: 347 in the contiguous states and 227 in Alaska. In January 2026 the Department of the Interior published an updated list adding the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina following a statute enacted in December 2025 that recognized the Lumbee while setting conditions on the tribe's eligibility for federal services; the statutory conditions include verification of the tribe's roll. The January 30, 2026 list contains 575 entities, according to the Bureau of Indian Affairs. Older or lagging publications may still report 574; the dated official list controls the count used here. The shift also illustrates that recognition can occur by administrative acknowledgment, by act of Congress, or by other federal action, and that the political process of recognition continues.

Federal recognition carries a government-to-government relationship, eligibility for many federal programs, and the legal status of tribal governmental powers. Reservations are lands set aside for tribes by treaty, statute, or executive action; trust lands are held by the United States in trust for a tribe or individual Indians; and fee lands are held under ordinary private ownership, sometimes within reservation boundaries. The relationship between these categories shapes jurisdiction and regulation, as Section 139.15 explains. Tribal governments also manage treaty relationships, where treaties continue to define rights to land, hunting, fishing, and services. Service administration is carried out through contracts and, for tribes that choose it, self-governance compacts under the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act as amended in 1994 and 2000. A self-governance compact is a negotiated agreement transferring responsibility and funding for programs to a tribe with greater flexibility than a contract.

Several case studies show the range of institutions.

The Navajo Nation has a central government in Window Rock, Arizona, with executive, legislative, and judicial branches and a land base spanning parts of three states. Its Council of 24 delegates legislates, and 110 chapters provide local administration, community meetings, and a direct connection between residents and the central government. The chapter system is a clear example of how a large, dispersed territory can combine central authority with local governance.

The Cherokee Nation illustrates a written, three-branch constitution, a large professional civil service, and a government-owned enterprise structure. Its jurisdiction in northeastern Oklahoma, including land recognized as reservation after McGirt, and its large population of citizens living across the country, make it a case study in governing both territory and diaspora.

Haudenosaunee institutions show the coexistence of traditional and elected governments. The Haudenosaunee Grand Council of hereditary chiefs, with clan mothers who nominate and counsel them, continues to function in the Onondaga Nation and in other communities, while the Seneca Nation of Indians has since 1848 operated an elected council and president, and several Mohawk communities have elected governments alongside traditional structures. These differences reflect nineteenth- and twentieth-century political histories in which some communities adopted elected systems to protect land and self-government, while others kept chiefs' councils.

Pueblo governments are community-specific. The nineteen Pueblos of New Mexico have their own governments, with governors, councils, and religious or customary officials whose roles differ from pueblo to pueblo. Many pueblo governors carry canes of office associated with Spanish colonial and later American recognition, including canes presented in 1863 (Chapter 30). The All Pueblo Council of Governors provides intergovernmental coordination on issues such as health, water, and federal policy. Much of the internal political structure is not public, and outsiders should avoid assuming that formal governmental offices exhaust a pueblo's political life.

Alaska Native village governments govern small, geographically dispersed, often road-inaccessible communities. Alaska has the largest number of federally recognized entities, 227 in the 2024 list. Many village tribal councils are small, with limited local revenue, and rely on regional nonprofit tribal organizations to provide health, social services, and other programs. Large Alaska Native health organizations manage compacts at regional scale. A crucial distinction concerns Alaska Native corporations, created by the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 (Chapter 115): they are principally state-chartered, for-profit regional and village corporations owned by Alaska Native shareholders; many hold land and capital, but they are not tribal governments. Tribal governments hold political status and the government-to-government relationship, and the two kinds of institution have different legal powers. The United States Supreme Court's 1998 decision in Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government held that the particular ANCSA lands at issue did not constitute a dependent Indian community within the statutory definition of Indian country. The decision does not extinguish tribal political status or resolve every question of jurisdiction in Alaska.

A named example of a smaller government is the Jamestown S’Klallam Tribe in Washington State. Its health department operates services for citizens and the surrounding community, including primary care and an out-of-area benefits program. The Indian Health Service identifies the Tribe as a participant in tribal self-governance since 1994. It is also a member of the Northwest Portland Area Indian Health Board, an intertribal institution that provides technical assistance, health policy coordination and training to member tribes across Washington, Oregon and Idaho. These arrangements show that a nation can retain decision-making authority while obtaining specialist support at regional scale. Its government, its commercial ventures and the regional board are different institutions with different mandates. The size of the citizen population does not determine the size of the workforce or the complexity of its service responsibilities. [15] [16] [17]

State recognition and non-recognition also matter. Several states recognize tribes that do not hold federal recognition, which can provide limited state-level benefits but not the federal government-to-government relationship. Congress recognized six Virginia tribes by statute in 2018. Communities without federal recognition, including some whose status was ended during termination or never acknowledged, may govern themselves and pursue recognition without access to federal programs. Recognition can be gained through the Part 83 administrative process, congressional action, or judicial or other routes, and the political and financial costs of the process have been substantial.

Federal recognition and self-governance compacts should also be distinguished. The official list recognizes the governmental relationship; it does not mean that each listed entity has negotiated a compact or operates the same range of services. The 2026 addition of the Lumbee Tribe is an example of recognition by legislation and subsequent administrative listing. A nation may then need to establish or expand program administration under the applicable laws and funding rules. The status of its political institutions must not be inferred from the number of facilities operating in a given year. [1] [2]

Alaska particularly illustrates the distinction between political membership, corporate shareholding and service eligibility. A person may be a citizen of a village tribal government and a shareholder in an Alaska Native corporation, but those two relationships confer different rights. A regional nonprofit organization can also administer services across several villages under agreements without becoming the government of every member village. The institutional landscape was shaped by the 1971 land settlement and by the subsequent expansion of tribal administration, not by the simple replacement of governments with corporations. [19]

Section 139.10

First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Governance in Canada

Canada's Indigenous peoples are constitutionally recognized as First Nations, Inuit, and Métis, and their governments differ in legal foundation. Treating them as one category obscures real differences. A Canadian student must therefore keep several levels separate: the Indian Act band council, the First Nation that may govern under a custom code or a self-government agreement, the modern treaty government, the tribal council or regional service organization, the Inuit land-claim organization, the public territorial government, and the Métis government.

Most First Nations, roughly six hundred communities, have governments operating under the Indian Act or under alternatives to it. An Indian Act band council holds powers enumerated in the statute, including bylaws on a limited range of subjects, subject to ministerial oversight in some respects. Many First Nations have moved to increase their authority by adopting custom election codes, by assuming control of land management under the Framework Agreement on First Nation Land Management, by opting into the First Nations Fiscal Management Act, or by negotiating sectoral self-government agreements in education or other fields. Others have negotiated comprehensive self-government agreements or modern treaties. Tribal councils and regional organizations, such as health authorities, school boards, and political-territorial organizations, provide services and political coordination but are generally not the governments of their member First Nations. The Assembly of First Nations is a national advocacy organization.

The Nisg̱a'a Nation is the principal case study. The Nisg̱a'a Final Agreement, the first modern treaty in British Columbia, took effect on May 11, 2000, after decades of political and legal struggle that included the Calder case of 1973 (Chapter 117). The treaty transferred about two thousand square kilometres of land in the Nass Valley to Nisg̱a'a ownership and gave the Nisg̱a'a Lisims Government law-making powers over matters such as citizenship, culture and language, land and resource management on Nisg̱a'a lands, and some aspects of policing and administration of justice, alongside provincial and federal law. The Nisg̱a'a Constitution, which came into effect with the treaty, provides for a national government, four village governments, and three urban locals, serving Nisg̱a'a citizens in Vancouver, Terrace, and the Prince Rupert area. The structure places elected officials and traditional institutions in a single constitutional order, and the urban locals respond to the same problem of dispersed citizens raised in Section 139.3.

Distinctions are essential. Indian Act governance is statutory administration with limited enumerated powers; negotiated self-government gives a nation defined legislative authority through an agreement or treaty. First Nation membership is not identical to Indian status, as Section 139.3 noted. Indigenous governments differ from regional service organizations, which may be governed by delegates of First Nations and deliver programs without being governments of the people they serve.

Inuit governance in Canada is organized under land claim agreements across Inuit Nunangat. The Inuvialuit Final Agreement (1984) created the Inuvialuit Regional Corporation; the Nunavut Agreement (1993) created Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated; Nunavik Inuit are represented through Makivik Corporation and the Kativik Regional Government under the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (1975); and the Labrador Inuit Land Claims Agreement took effect in 2005, creating the Nunatsiavut Government with an elected Assembly. Nunavut is a distinct case. The territory was created in 1999 as a public territorial government elected by all residents, operating through a consensus-style legislative assembly without political parties. Inuit make up the large majority of its population, roughly 85 percent in recent censuses, but its political membership is not restricted to Inuit. The Nunavut Agreement's land and resource rights belong to Inuit beneficiaries, represented by Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated, and co-management boards share public responsibility. Therefore Nunavut combines a public government accountable to all residents with Inuit treaty institutions accountable to Inuit beneficiaries, a dual structure that Section 139.15 and Section 139.20 revisit. Chapter 116 treated the creation of Nunavut.

Métis governance also has distinct institutions. The Métis are recognized as one of the three Indigenous peoples in section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, and Canadian courts, notably in the 2003 Powley decision and the 2016 Daniels decision, have clarified Métis rights and federal responsibility. Alberta's eight Métis Settlements have a unique land-based governance system created by provincial legislation in 1990 and a General Council. Métis governments such as the Manitoba Métis Federation, Métis Nation of Ontario, Métis Nation–Saskatchewan, and Otipemisiwak Métis Government (formerly the Métis Nation of Alberta) have negotiated recognition and self-government agreements with the federal government. Canada signed self-government recognition and implementation agreements with the three provincial Métis governments in February 2023, and Bill C-53, introduced in 2023 to give them legislative recognition, did not receive royal assent before Parliament was prorogued in January 2025. In February 2026 the federal government introduced Bill C-21 to give legal effect to the Red River Métis Self-Government Recognition and Implementation Treaty with the Manitoba Métis Federation, and the bill was debated at second reading on April 22, 2026; the available parliamentary record still lists second reading as its stage at the editorial cutoff. The legal status of Métis self-government arrangements therefore differs among the Métis governments and was still developing at this edition's horizon. Several First Nations governments and organizations, particularly in Ontario, objected to aspects of the earlier legislation, which illustrates that recognition of one Indigenous government can raise disputes with others.

The Nisg̱a'a constitutional arrangements also show how representation can be designed around migration. The national government's executive includes officers, chief councillors of each village government and representatives of the urban locals. The constitution establishes four village governments and three urban locals, enabling participation outside the Nass Valley. The arrangement creates channels for political representation, but it does not make every city in which a Nisg̱a'a citizen resides subject to Nisg̱a'a territorial law. The treaty defines lawmaking powers and relationships with provincial and federal law. Its institutional design therefore connects dispersed citizenship to a territorially grounded political community. [20] [21]

The Red River Métis treaty, signed on November 30, 2024, offers another lesson in the distinction between agreement and implementation. Bill C-21 was introduced in February 2026 to give effect to that treaty. The parliamentary record available for this edition identifies debate at second reading on April 22 and does not establish royal assent. Bill C-53, which concerned different Métis governments, did not become law in the preceding Parliament. A signature, a bill's introduction, parliamentary passage, royal assent and commencement are separate milestones. The Canadian examples therefore cannot be described as a uniform, completed transition from representative organizations to treaty governments. [22] [23] [47]

Section 139.11

Indigenous Community Governments and Autonomy in Mexico

Mexico's Indigenous governance operates within a constitutional order that differs sharply from the United States and Canada. Indigenous peoples do not hold a nation-to-nation treaty relationship with the state in the same way, and their governmental institutions are expressed through communities, municipalities, and agrarian bodies. Communal assemblies, local normative systems, communal land institutions, municipal authorities, and customary offices carry out functions that elsewhere might be associated with tribal governments. Mexican law distinguishes municipalities, which are the basic level of local government, from agrarian communities and ejidos, whose members hold land under the social property regime. Roughly half of Mexico's land is social property, and Indigenous communities hold much of it, so land institutions and local government overlap closely. Chapter 14 and Chapter 141 treat the historical and regional background.

The legal foundation of Indigenous autonomy was built through successive reforms. A 1992 amendment recognized Mexico as a pluricultural nation, and the 2001 reform to Article 2 recognized rights to self-determination and autonomy and the use of normative systems in internal conflicts, subject to the Constitution, but left to state legislation many details of implementation. The 1996 San Andrés Accords, negotiated between the government and the Zapatista movement, were an important reference in these debates, though many of their commitments were not carried into law as originally negotiated. Indigenous communities in Oaxaca, Michoacán, Guerrero, Chiapas, and elsewhere developed institutions in the gap between recognition and implementation.

Oaxaca provides a major example. The state has 570 municipalities, and 418 of them elect authorities through Indigenous normative systems, often called usos y costumbres, in which community assemblies choose municipal authorities according to local rules, often tied to a system of community service. Oaxaca's electoral institute, which supervises elections, administers and validates these systems alongside party-based elections. The system has generated both recognition of community self-government and debates about participation, particularly concerning women's rights to vote and hold office, which Oaxacan courts and electoral authorities have addressed.

The Purépecha community of Cherán in Michoacán is the other principal case. In April 2011, residents of Cherán rose against illegal logging and violence that had affected the community's forests and security. They set up community checkpoints and assemblies and dismissed the existing municipal authorities. After litigation, the federal electoral tribunal ruled in November 2011 that the community had the right to choose its authorities according to its own customary system. Cherán then established a communal government centered on a council of twelve members, the Concejo Mayor, with representatives from the community's neighborhoods, in place of party-based elections, and a communal police force. The case shows Indigenous community governance operating within, and sometimes against, existing municipal law, and the role of courts in recognizing it.

On September 30, 2024, a constitutional reform to Article 2 concerning Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities was published in the Diario Oficial de la Federación. It recognizes Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities as subjects of public law, with legal personality and their own patrimony, and affirms collective rights including self-determination and autonomy, subject to the constitutional framework. The reform provides a stronger constitutional foundation than earlier recognition, since public-law status allows communities to act as legal subjects rather than only as beneficiaries of policy, and it points toward direct allocation of public resources to communities. However, implementation depends on secondary legislation, state and municipal laws, funding arrangements, and court interpretation. As of this edition's editorial horizon, scholars, communities, and officials were still working through how the reform would affect elections, financing, jurisdiction, and relations with municipalities and states, and no single national outcome should be assumed. Students should distinguish constitutional recognition from practical implementation. Chapter 141 follows the subject into northern Mexico and the borderlands.

From public-law recognition to fiscal implementation

Mexico's reform acquired a concrete financial dimension in 2025. A January 16 agreement led to the creation of FAISPIAM, the Indigenous and Afro-Mexican component of the federal Social Infrastructure Contributions Fund. The published allocation agreement specified MXN 12,374,279,195 for 2025, representing ten percent of the broader fund. The 2026 allocation agreement specified MXN 13,506,070,241, also ten percent of the fund. These are statutory program allocations, not a measure of unrestricted community revenue or of completed construction. [24] [25]

Under the 2025 guidelines, participating communities administer resources and execute qualifying social infrastructure projects directly, using committees selected through their assemblies for administration and oversight. The mechanism creates a practical connection between community decisions, financial responsibility and public works. It also introduces administrative obligations: projects must meet the program's conditions, expenditures must be documented, and oversight must be organized. This is a form of fiscal capacity within a defined program, not the creation of independent general taxation authority. The allocation agreements and guidelines establish the design of the policy; the number, quality and completion of projects require separate implementation evidence. [26]

Oaxaca's electoral institute, meanwhile, identifies 418 municipalities governed through Indigenous normative electoral systems, compared with 152 under party-based elections. The updated catalogue illustrates why administrative counts should be dated. Electoral recognition does not establish that all 418 communities follow identical procedures. Local norms differ in the frequency of elections, forms of assembly and procedures for resolving disputes. The institute's validation role demonstrates the interaction between community authority and the state's electoral law. [27]

Section 139.12

Greenlandic Self-Government and the Arctic Political Order

Greenland's government is structurally different from tribal and First Nations governments. Greenland is a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark, and its public institutions serve all residents. Most residents are Greenlandic Inuit, and the Self-Government Act recognizes the Greenlandic people as a people under international law, but the government is a public one, not a citizenship government whose membership is defined by descent. Chapter 100 followed the twentieth-century history of Inuit North America, and Chapter 148 returns to Greenland's future position.

The historical path began with Greenland's incorporation into the Danish state and its status as a Danish county after 1953. Home Rule took effect on May 1, 1979, after a 1978 act and a referendum in Greenland. It established an elected parliament, a Home Rule government, and the transfer of responsibilities such as local government, education, health, and social affairs, while Denmark retained broad powers including foreign affairs and defense. The Self-Government Act took effect on June 21, 2009, after a referendum in Greenland in November 2008. It recognized Greenlandic as the official language, provided for assumption of mineral-resource authority, which Greenland took over in 2010, and provided a mechanism by which Greenland could take over additional areas of responsibility from the Danish state by agreement, including some in justice administration. Denmark retained powers including constitutional matters, foreign, defense, and security policy, and currency policy, with provisions for Greenland's involvement in foreign-policy matters affecting it.

The institutions are straightforward. Inatsisartut is the elected parliament, with 31 members, and adopts Greenlandic laws. Naalakkersuisut is the executive government, led by a premier and composed of ministers who are accountable to Inatsisartut. Municipalities provide local administration and services; Greenland now has five, Sermersooq, Kujalleq, Qeqqata, Avannaata, and Qeqertalik. The Kingdom of Denmark retains responsibilities set by the constitutional relationship, and Greenland elects two members to the Danish parliament, the Folketing. After the March 2025 election the Demokraatit party led the vote and Jens-Frederik Nielsen became premier, leading a broad coalition.

Public finance reflects the relationship. Greenland's budget is financed by local taxes, fishing and other revenue, and an annual block grant from Denmark that is a large share of public revenue. The Self-Government Act provides that Greenland's mineral revenue beyond a threshold reduces the block grant, an incentive structure that links resource development to the question of fiscal independence. Policy competencies, including language policy, have been transferred over time, and natural resources are a decisive area of public policy. Land in Greenland is public, with rights of use allocated, unlike private fee ownership, an example of how different land systems shape governance.

Questions of independence require care. The Self-Government Act contemplates that the Greenlandic people may decide on independence, through a process involving a referendum in Greenland, negotiation with Danish authorities, and consent from the Danish parliament. Political leaders and parties in Greenland hold different views on timing and conditions, and international attention increased sharply in 2025. This chapter distinguishes political proposals and statements, which are not constitutional changes, from accomplished arrangements: Greenland remains a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark. Chapter 148 treats the future of sovereignty arrangements.

The distinction between assumed and transferable powers is essential to interpreting the 2009 legislation. Greenland may assume responsibility in eligible fields under the arrangements established by the Act; certain fields require negotiation before transfer. Once a field is assumed, Greenland also takes responsibility for financing it. Other matters, including the constitutional order and major elements of defense and foreign policy, remain with the Kingdom. Thus a statutory list of potentially transferable functions is not an inventory of completed transfers. The Danish Prime Minister's Office maintains a separate overview of responsibilities already assumed. This mechanism ties expanded political authority to administrative personnel, financial obligations and institutional preparation. [28]

Section 139.13

Health, Education, Housing, and Social Services

Public services are where governmental authority becomes visible to ordinary citizens. Indigenous governments operate clinics and hospitals, schools and scholarship programs, language education, housing authorities, water and sanitation systems, social assistance, elder services, and child welfare programs. The scale varies from a single community clinic run by a small nation to health systems employing thousands. The analytical question is not only whether a service exists but who designs it, who controls the budget, and who holds the legal authority behind it.

Three arrangements should be distinguished. First, a government may administer an externally funded program: a federal or provincial department designs the program and funds it, and the Indigenous government carries it out under contract or contribution agreement, following the funder's rules. Second, a government may design an Indigenous-controlled service system: the Indigenous institution sets priorities, structures services according to its own values, and holds meaningful control over funds, though the legal authority may still rest on federal legislation or agreements. Third, a government may exercise legislative jurisdiction over a policy area, enacting its own laws that govern the service and, in some cases, that have force in the surrounding legal system. The three differ in the degree of self-determination they embody. The same service area can move from the first to the third over time.

In the United States, tribal health care is carried out through the Indian Health Service, tribally operated facilities under self-determination contracts and self-governance compacts, and urban Indian health programs. Many large tribal governments operate hospitals and clinics under compacts. Education includes tribally controlled schools, Bureau of Indian Education schools, scholarships, and tribal colleges (Chapter 124). Housing is largely financed through the Native American Housing Assistance and Self-Determination Act's block grant and operated through tribal housing authorities, and persistent shortages remain. Social services include child welfare programs governed in part by the Indian Child Welfare Act, which the Supreme Court upheld against constitutional challenge in Haaland v. Brackeen in 2023 (Chapter 131).

Two Canadian developments illustrate institutional transfer in different ways. The First Nations Health Authority in British Columbia took over the programs and responsibilities of Health Canada's regional First Nations and Inuit health office on October 1, 2013, following a 2011 tripartite framework agreement among First Nations in British Columbia, the province, and Canada. It is governed through a structure linked to the province's First Nations: it serves about two hundred First Nations in the province, within a governance structure that also includes the distinct First Nations Health Council and First Nations Health Directors Association, and it designs programs in partnership with First Nations communities, integrates with provincial health services, and has emphasized culturally grounded approaches. Its legal basis is agreement rather than a First Nations statute, so it shows an Indigenous-controlled service system built by negotiation rather than by assertion of legislative jurisdiction.

The second development concerns child and family services. In 2019 Parliament enacted the federal statute respecting First Nations, Inuit and Métis children, youth and families, commonly called Bill C-92, which came into force on January 1, 2020. It sets national standards, such as the principle of the best interests of the child and a priority on keeping children with their families and communities, and it affirms that the inherent right of self-government recognized in section 35 includes jurisdiction over child and family services. It allows Indigenous governing bodies to enact their own laws and, under defined conditions, gives those laws priority over conflicting provincial law. Quebec challenged the statute's constitutionality, and the Quebec Court of Appeal found parts of it beyond federal power. On February 9, 2024, the Supreme Court of Canada, in a reference decision, held that the statute is constitutional, upholding the statute, including its affirmation and the mechanisms giving qualifying Indigenous laws force as federal law. The judgment did not definitively determine the constitutional scope of the inherent right under section 35. The Cowessess First Nation in Saskatchewan was among the first to enact its own child and family services law and conclude a coordination agreement under the statute. The decision confirmed a legal basis for the third arrangement, legislative jurisdiction, though practical implementation, including funding, remains negotiated.

Education and language are similar. Many Indigenous governments run immersion schools and language programs (Chapter 123) and negotiate education authority, as in some self-government agreements. Elder care, housing maintenance, water treatment, and community social programs are among the most demanding responsibilities because they require continuous funding for staff and infrastructure. Capacity limits, such as difficulty recruiting health professionals to remote communities, affect all arrangements.

The British Columbia health transfer was not simply the replacement of a federal office with one new elected government. The 2011 tripartite agreement preceded the 2013 transfer. In the resulting structure, the First Nations Health Council provides political leadership and represents First Nations health priorities; the First Nations Health Directors Association supports the professional and technical capacity of health directors; and the First Nations Health Authority administers services and undertakes system transformation. Canada and British Columbia remain partners in the broader health governance relationship. Distinguishing these institutions helps students see how policy representation, expertise and service delivery can be assigned to separate bodies while serving a shared purpose. [29] [30]

The child welfare framework provides a contrast. The Supreme Court of Canada's 2024 judgment upheld Parliament's statute under federal legislative authority, including its incorporation and priority mechanisms. It did not adjudicate the complete constitutional content of every Indigenous nation's section 35 right. An Indigenous governing body developing a law under the statute must address not only the rules of child placement and protection, but also case management, workforce training, records, financing and arrangements with provincial or territorial agencies. Thus a change in legislative authority can require a parallel expansion of administrative systems. [31]

Financial records also show why service delivery deserves institutional analysis. The Cherokee Nation's FY2025 popular report describes substantial spending on health services and a network of outpatient and inpatient facilities. Its detailed budget and annual reporting make it possible to distinguish service expenditure from capital investment. A hospital building requires capital financing, while maintaining clinical staff, supplies, patient records and equipment requires recurring revenue. Tribal control over service priorities has meaning only when the institution can sustain both kinds of expenditure over time. [10]

Section 139.14

Economic Development and Government-Owned Enterprises

Economic development is a governmental responsibility when governments own businesses, hold natural resources in trust for citizens, invest public funds, or regulate economic activity on their lands. Earlier chapters examined Indigenous commercial enterprises (Chapter 121) and gaming (Chapter 122). This section addresses the governance of economic development: how a government relates to the businesses it owns.

Indigenous governments own or hold interests in a wide variety of enterprises: gaming operations, tourism and hospitality, utilities, agricultural operations, energy and resource ventures, real estate development, financial institutions, construction and services companies, and partnerships with private firms. Some are very large. The Navajo Tribal Utility Authority, created in 1959, supplies electricity, water, and other utility services across the Navajo Nation. The Navajo Agricultural Products Industry is a large farming enterprise. The Cherokee Nation's holdings are organized in a government-owned holding company, Cherokee Nation Businesses. In Canada, First Nations such as the Membertou First Nation in Nova Scotia and the Whitecap Dakota First Nation in Saskatchewan have developed diversified enterprises, and self-governing nations such as the Nisg̱a'a have corporate entities as well. In Alaska, the Native corporations created under ANCSA are large enterprises, but they are corporations, not governments.

The principal analytical distinction is between government and enterprise. A government makes laws, provides services, and answers to citizens as a political community. An enterprise sells goods or services to earn a return. When the two are not separated, political leaders may make day-to-day business decisions, business losses may threaten public services, and business employees' jobs may depend on election results. Separation is a common design response. Under it, the government owns the enterprise, often through a charter or corporate law enacted by the nation, and a board of directors appointed under a charter oversees professional management. The government sets the mission and rules, appoints board members, receives distributions, and supervises, but does not run the business directly. Profits are distributed to the government according to a policy, reinvested in the enterprise, or placed in reserves.

The government decides how much profit is reinvested versus distributed, whether the enterprise serves non-commercial goals such as employment or language revitalization, how much risk to accept, and who sits on the board. Sovereign immunity adds a legal dimension in the United States. Tribal enterprises may be chartered to waive immunity for commercial contracts, balancing the need for creditors and partners to have recourse against the government's interest in protecting public assets. Canadian First Nations do not enjoy an equivalent doctrine and may be sued like other legal entities, which shapes how they structure agreements and corporate vehicles.

Section 139.15

Land Administration, Infrastructure, and Territorial Planning

Governing territory means keeping land records, granting and enforcing leases, regulating land use, protecting water and environment, building and maintaining roads and utilities, and planning for growth. These responsibilities are grounded in land tenure, but ownership, regulatory authority, and responsibility for infrastructure are not always held by the same institution.

Land tenure arrangements differ widely. In the United States, reservations may include trust land, held by the federal government for a tribe or individual Indians (roughly 56 million acres nationwide), and fee land, held under ordinary private title, including land owned by non-Indians within reservation boundaries. Trust status limits sale and affects taxation and jurisdiction. Allotment, discussed in Chapter 86, left many reservations with fragmented ownership, and individual allotments descended to multiple heirs, complicating leasing and development. Programs to consolidate fractionated interests, including the buy-back effort funded after the Cobell settlement, and the HEARTH Act of 2012, which allows tribes with approved regulations to approve certain leases without secretarial review, address these problems. In Canada, reserve lands are held by the Crown for First Nations under the Indian Act, with administration by the band council or, for participating nations, under the Framework Agreement on First Nation Land Management, which allows First Nations to adopt their own land codes. Modern treaty settlement lands, such as the Nisg̱a'a lands, are owned and governed under treaty terms, and Inuit-owned lands in Nunavut are held under the Nunavut Agreement, with Inuit holding surface title to roughly 18 percent of the territory and subsurface rights on about 2 percent. In Mexico, communal lands and ejidos are held collectively under agrarian law and governed by assemblies. In Greenland, land is public with allocated rights of use.

Administration requires institutions. Land offices maintain records and title, which is the basis for leasing and housing finance. Cadastral mapping documents boundaries. Zoning and land use plans allocate land for housing, agriculture, industry, and conservation. Environmental regulation requires technical staff, and some tribal governments have been authorized by the federal government to administer environmental programs. Housing development depends on land availability, financing, and utilities. Water and wastewater systems, electricity, broadband, and roads often cost more per household in remote or dispersed communities. Long-term community plans coordinate these investments.

Because ownership, regulation, and infrastructure may sit in different hands, institutional overlap is common. A reservation may include land the government does not own, subject to state or county authority; a trust parcel may be leased to a company whose operations are regulated by multiple agencies; a utility may be owned by an enterprise rather than the government; a road may be maintained by a county. This is why the territorial authority dimension of the framework asks what authority a government can exercise, not what it owns.

The 2026 Nunavut land use planning decision illustrates overlapping responsibilities in a modern treaty setting. Under the Nunavut Agreement and the Nunavut Planning and Project Assessment Act, a land use plan for the Nunavut Settlement Area must be formulated by the Nunavut Planning Commission and then accepted jointly by three parties: the Government of Canada, the Government of Nunavut, and Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated. The Commission submitted a Recommended Nunavut Land Use Plan in 2023 after many years of work, covering roughly one-fifth of Canada's land mass. On July 3, 2026, the three parties announced a decision on the plan, and public reporting described it as a decision not to approve the plan as submitted and to require further work; all three parties said more work was needed. The federal government also noted that two First Nations holding section 35 rights in the region should be consulted on revisions. The episode shows a public territorial government, a federal government, and an Inuit treaty organization each holding a decision-making role in the same territory, none able to approve the plan alone, and the difficulty of making a consensus decision under three-party approval. Chapter 116 treated the Nunavut Agreement, and Chapter 138 treated northern climate and resource pressures.

The July 3, 2026 joint statement on the Nunavut plan further identifies April 1, 2027 as the scheduled date for the Government of Nunavut to assume administration and control of Crown lands, natural resources and inland waters under the Devolution Agreement. That is a future scheduled milestone at this chapter's editorial cutoff, not a completed transfer. It will alter the institutional setting of subsequent planning, without dissolving the treaty role of Inuit institutions or eliminating the need for coordination with other rights holders. The planning decision and devolution agreement illustrate that a territory's system of land administration can change while a treaty-based approval mechanism remains in force. [32]

Section 139.16

Emergency Management, Public Health, and Community Resilience

Emergencies test governments. Floods, wildfires, storms, infrastructure failures, epidemics, food shortages, and climate-related displacement require declarations, coordination, money, communication, and recovery planning, often across several jurisdictions. Chapter 138 examined northern climate adaptation and Indigenous responses to environmental change. This section concentrates on the governmental institutions responsible for implementing emergency responses.

The COVID-19 pandemic was a major test. Navajo Nation infection rates in spring 2020 were among the highest in the United States, and the government imposed stay-at-home orders, curfews, and public health measures that were stricter than those in surrounding states, while working to bring water, food, and supplies to households. Many tribal governments restricted access to their lands, ran their own testing and vaccination campaigns, and in several cases achieved vaccination rates above those of surrounding populations, drawing on their health systems and community networks. Federal pandemic recovery legislation in 2021 allocated a dedicated block of funds to tribal governments through the American Rescue Plan Act, about twenty billion dollars, which tribal governments were able to use for governmental services; this was a significant direct fiscal transfer to tribes. Canadian First Nations also used community-based public health measures, and the First Nations Health Authority coordinated responses in British Columbia.

Wildfire and flood evacuations illustrate other challenges. In Canada, First Nations communities have repeatedly been evacuated because of wildfire and flooding, for example the recurring flood evacuations of Kashechewan in northern Ontario and the large wildfire evacuations of 2023. Evacuations require a coordination arrangement among the First Nation, provincial authorities, and the federal government, which has had responsibility for emergency management on reserves funded through Indigenous Services Canada, with provinces or First Nations delivering services under agreements. Disputes about who pays, who coordinates, and how long evacuees may be displaced are recurring policy problems. In the United States, tribes face similar issues in wildfire, drought, and flooding, with additional strains on infrastructure.

Institutional frameworks have been changing. In the United States, the Sandy Recovery Improvement Act of 2013 amended the Stafford Act to allow federally recognized tribal governments to request a presidential emergency or major disaster declaration directly, rather than only through a state governor, or to choose to be included under a state's request. This change recognized the government-to-government relationship in disaster management and removed a dependency on state officials. A tribe must still have the capacity to prepare an emergency plan, assess damages, and manage federal funds, and smaller tribes may need technical assistance. Emergency management involves emergency declarations, mutual aid and coordination agreements, disaster financing from federal, provincial, and tribal sources, public communication in Indigenous languages and through local media, evacuation planning, and long-term recovery. In Mexico, communities in Oaxaca, Michoacán, and elsewhere have used communal assemblies, tequio or community labor traditions, and local police to coordinate responses to disasters and security threats.

Food insecurity and climate displacement raise longer-term questions. Governments responsible for remote communities must plan for supply disruption, thawing permafrost, coastal erosion, and relocation, matters examined in Chapter 138. The governmental issue is whether institutions have authority to make relocation decisions, funds to carry them out, and agreements with other governments that provide resources. Emergencies reveal both capabilities, such as local knowledge, trust, and the ability to mobilize quickly, and limitations, such as chronic underfunding, aging infrastructure, and dependence on other governments' decisions.

Section 139.17

Government-to-Government Relations and Intergovernmental Agreements

Indigenous governments do not govern in isolation. They deal continually with federal, state, provincial, territorial, and municipal governments, and with each other. The relationships are shaped by treaties, statutes, executive policy, court decisions, and negotiated agreements, and they create ongoing governmental responsibilities.

Intergovernmental relations differ from ordinary interest-group lobbying. A lobbyist seeks to persuade a government that holds authority over the matter. An Indigenous government is itself a government negotiating over jurisdiction, funding, and obligations, often under treaty or constitutional rights that the other party is legally required to respect. The practical forms include treaty implementation, consultation, service agreements, self-governance compacts, taxation agreements, infrastructure partnerships, law enforcement coordination, and revenue-sharing agreements. In the United States, Executive Order 13175, issued in 2000, directs federal agencies to consult with tribal governments on policies with tribal implications, and many states have adopted their own frameworks, such as Washington State's 1989 Centennial Accord, a government-to-government agreement between the state and the federally recognized tribes within it. Tribal-state compacts govern gaming, fuel and tobacco taxation, and law enforcement cooperation. In Canada, the federal government has negotiated modern treaties and self-government agreements, adopted an inherent-right policy in 1995, and enacted the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act in 2021, and the duty to consult and accommodate, developed by the Supreme Court of Canada, governs Crown dealings affecting asserted or established rights (Chapter 117). Chapter 143 treats the international dimension.

Canada's modern treaties provide the clearest examples of how negotiated relationships create continuing obligations. A modern treaty is not a one-time transaction. It establishes governments, land and resource regimes, fiscal arrangements, and co-management institutions that must be implemented over decades, with periodic review of financial agreements and disputes over whether commitments have been met. Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada reports on implementation of these agreements, and Indigenous signatories have repeatedly argued that implementation has fallen short. The Nunavut experience, including disputes over the funding of Inuit treaty obligations, the Nunavut Lands and Resources Devolution Agreement signed on January 18, 2024, and the land use plan discussed in Section 139.15, shows how agreements generate implementation work and sometimes disputes.

It would be wrong to treat recognition of a government-to-government relationship as establishing equal constitutional authority or ending political conflict. In the United States, Congress retains broad power over Indian affairs, and courts have limited tribal jurisdiction in several areas. In Canada, treaty and self-government agreements operate within the Constitution and alongside federal and provincial law, and the federal government's reliance on negotiated rather than legislated recognition has been criticized by some Indigenous leaders. Indigenous governments may also disagree with one another, as in disputes over overlapping territory, or over the recognition of Métis governments, which some First Nations leaders contested. Relationships between tribes and state or local governments can be cooperative on matters such as emergency response and contentious on taxation or jurisdiction. Intergovernmental relations are an ongoing political practice, not a settled status.

Agreements can be understood as administrative interfaces between legal orders. A health compact, for example, may specify funding, scope of service, reporting and dispute resolution while leaving other governmental powers unaffected. A land-use agreement may instead establish consultation, shared review or procedures for dealing with competing legal requirements. The historian must ask which institution signs, what obligation is created, how long it lasts, and whether the agreement alters legal jurisdiction or only the delivery of a program. The Nunatsiavut fiscal financing agreement explicitly requires audited reporting by both the government and the Inuit Community Governments, illustrating how negotiated funding and accountability operate together. Section 139.18

Transparency, Accountability, and Institutional Legitimacy

Accountability sustains public confidence in any government. Indigenous governments use many mechanisms: elections and recall, constitutional limits on power, financial disclosure, independent audits, ethics codes and conflict-of-interest rules, legislative oversight, judicial review, community assemblies, public participation, and administrative transparency. The mix depends on the governmental system. In a constitutional government, courts may review actions for compliance with the constitution. In a customary or hereditary system, accountability may operate through clan obligations, the power of clan mothers to remove leaders, the oversight of elders, and the requirement that decisions be reached by community consensus. In consensus-based or assembly systems, accountability may rest on direct participation and public deliberation. These are different systems of accountability and should not be judged only by the criteria of one of them.

Two kinds of accountability must be separated. Accountability to citizens is the responsibility of a government to the community it represents, enforced through elections, courts, assemblies, and customary processes. Reporting to external governments or funders is the obligation to account for funds received, enforced by funding agreements, audit requirements, and statute. The two overlap but do not coincide. A government may submit complete reports to funders while citizens lack access to information, and a government may be highly accountable to its citizens through customary processes while producing documentation that external reporting systems do not recognize. Many Indigenous governments argue that heavy, multiple, program-specific reporting requirements consume administrative capacity without improving accountability to citizens.

External legal frameworks also affect accountability. In the United States, the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 applies several civil rights protections to tribal governments and provides federal habeas corpus review of detention, while the Supreme Court's 1978 decision in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez left enforcement of most other provisions to tribal forums. Tribes that spend federal funds above statutory thresholds are subject to federal single audit requirements. Tribal governments have adopted ethics codes, financial controls, auditor offices, and inspector-general-type bodies. In Canada, funding agreements require audited statements, and the First Nations Financial Transparency Act of 2013 required public posting of audited statements and chief and councillor compensation; the enforcement of its requirements was suspended in 2015, and its future has been debated. The Financial Management Board offers an Indigenous-governed alternative: First Nations may obtain certification of their financial management systems and financial performance, which can support access to capital through the First Nations Finance Authority.

It would be inaccurate to present Indigenous governments as inherently more or less susceptible to administrative failure than other governments. Failures of financial management, conflicts of interest, and contested elections occur in governments of all kinds, and Indigenous governments have often been held to additional scrutiny by external agencies because of their reliance on public funds. Equally, strong institutions exist in many nations. The relevant historical question is how institutional legitimacy is built and sustained: through clear rules, independent institutions, citizen access to information, stable administration, and processes that fit the nation's own political traditions.

Historical Debate

External Oversight or Indigenous Institutional Accountability?

The question: Who should hold Indigenous governments accountable for their use of public funds and their governance: external governments and funders through audit and reporting rules, or Indigenous institutions and citizens through the nation's own constitutional and customary mechanisms? The question arises in policy debates over transparency legislation, funding conditions, and the structure of financial oversight bodies.

Interpretation A — External oversight is necessary: Supporters argue that governments that receive public funds from taxpayers should meet uniform reporting and transparency standards, that citizens of Indigenous nations benefit from public disclosure, and that external audit can detect problems that internal processes miss.
Interpretation B — Accountability should rest primarily with Indigenous institutions: Critics of external requirements argue that uniform, program-by-program reporting is burdensome and often not designed to serve citizens, that non-Indigenous governments are not subject to comparable requirements in every respect, and that accountability to one's own citizens, through Indigenous-governed institutions such as financial management boards, is more meaningful and consistent with self-determination.
Interpretation C — The mechanisms can be combined: Some analysts and Indigenous institutions argue that citizen-facing accountability and external reporting are not mutually exclusive, and that Indigenous-designed standards, certification systems, and published statements can satisfy both purposes.

Evidence: The debate draws on audits and financial statements, legislation such as the 2013 Canadian transparency act and its enforcement history, funding agreements, reports of Indigenous financial institutions, and studies of governance and community outcomes. Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and policy design more than established facts: what counts as adequate transparency, who is the proper audience for disclosure, and whether differences in reporting burden reflect legitimate oversight or unequal treatment. Why it matters: The design of accountability systems affects administrative capacity, public confidence, and the meaning of self-determination in practice. Student task: Identify evidence that could clarify which mechanisms increase citizen confidence and which merely increase administrative burden, and explain how different governmental systems might apply the concept of accountability.

Section 139.19

Contemporary Challenges of Indigenous Nation-Building

Indigenous governments face structural challenges that differ in degree among nations but recur across North America. They include fiscal dependence and restricted revenue authority; shortages of trained administrative and technical personnel; infrastructure and housing deficits; jurisdictional disputes; geographically dispersed populations; uneven economic development; leadership succession and institutional continuity; and technological modernization.

Fiscal dependence is the most pervasive. Most Indigenous governments rely heavily on transfers whose terms they do not control, and their revenue-raising powers are limited by law, small tax bases, and land tenure that restricts taxation and borrowing. Shortages of trained personnel, such as engineers, accountants, health professionals, lawyers, and planners, affect small and remote communities most, and many governments invest in scholarships, training, and recruitment of citizens. Infrastructure and housing deficits, including inadequate water systems, overcrowded homes, and limited broadband, directly affect health and education. Jurisdictional disputes consume legal and administrative resources. Dispersed populations require service models that reach beyond the territory. Economic development is uneven: some nations have diversified enterprises and substantial reserves, while others have few commercial opportunities. Leadership succession and institutional continuity matter because frequent electoral turnover can disrupt programs unless professional administration and clear rules provide stability. Technological modernization, including data systems, cybersecurity, and records management, increases the administrative demands on governments with small staffs. Chapter 145 treats data and knowledge sovereignty.

Nation-building is the term, widely used in scholarship, for the continuing development of legitimate and effective political institutions. Research by the Harvard Project on Indigenous Governance and Development, led by Stephen Cornell and Joseph Kalt, studied American Indian nations from the late 1980s and concluded that sustained community outcomes were associated with practical decision-making control, capable institutions of governance, and institutions that fit a community's own political culture, alongside strategic thinking about long-term goals. The Native Nations Institute at the University of Arizona has developed training and research along related lines. These studies are a scholarly foundation, not a prescription. They arose from particular cases, primarily in the United States, and critics note that outcomes also depend on resources, location, history, and external policy, and that institutional models do not transfer automatically between nations or countries.

The chapter therefore avoids presenting any model as universally appropriate. A large, resource-rich nation, a small nation with shared services, a modern-treaty government, a territorial public government, and a Mexican community assembly face different conditions and have different priorities. Some emphasize territorial protection, others language, others economic diversification, others service delivery. Some Indigenous scholars and leaders emphasize that nation-building also means revitalizing Indigenous political traditions and law, not only building administrative capacity along conventional lines.

Institutional development may also proceed through shared specialist capacity rather than the construction of a separate department in every nation. The regional health board examined in Section 139.9 provides an example: member governments can pursue their own policy goals while drawing on common technical resources. Similarly, financial management institutions serving participating First Nations create a mechanism for common standards and pooled financing without making them a single government. Shared capacity can reduce duplicated administrative effort, but it requires clear arrangements for accountability, resource allocation and the protection of community-specific priorities. This comparison is more informative than a simple contrast between large and small governments.

Using the six-dimension framework, a student can ask of each: where does political authority come from, what legal capacity does the government have, can it administer services, can it finance itself, what territorial authority does it hold, and how does it sustain legitimacy.

The central conclusion is that sovereignty becomes operational through institutions. Political authority must be accompanied by the capacity to enact decisions, administer resources, provide services, protect rights, and maintain institutional continuity. Neither legal recognition without capacity nor capacity without recognized authority is sufficient. Indigenous nations pursue both: they assert and defend authority in courts, treaties, and legislatures, and they build the departments, budgets, courts, and records that make that authority real. This is why Indigenous sovereignty is not only a historical inheritance or a legal principle. It is a continuing governmental practice.

The chapter's cases also warn against simple conclusions. Institutions do not guarantee outcomes, models cannot be transplanted without regard to history, and external legal and fiscal conditions limit what even well-designed governments can do. At the same time, the record shows persistent institutional creativity: constitutions drafted by conventions, chapter governments, treaty governments, urban locals, community councils recognized by courts, and Indigenous-controlled health authorities. These developments occurred in different legal settings, but they share the work of turning political authority into public institutions.

The next chapter, Chapter 140, turns to a subject that tests these institutions especially severely: Indigenous communities whose social, economic, and political lives extend beyond reservations, reserves, and villages into cities and across borders. Governments must serve and represent citizens in places where they have limited jurisdiction, and the institutions of this chapter are therefore one half of the story. The other half is how Indigenous people build community and political life in urban North America.

The fiscal cases make the six dimensions concrete. A community assembly in Mexico may gain the ability to administer a defined infrastructure allocation without acquiring unlimited fiscal jurisdiction. A modern treaty government in Canada can negotiate transfers and tax arrangements while retaining its own constitutional institutions. A tribal government in the United States may exercise recognized jurisdiction while depending on grants to sustain courts and health services. Greenland may assume a field of responsibility and become responsible for financing it. In each case the mechanism differs, but the analytical distinction remains: political authority, legal capacity and financial means are mutually important, not interchangeable. The purpose of institutional comparison is to understand these relationships in historical context, not to measure legitimacy according to budget size.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Government Across Generations

What changed? The forms of Indigenous government changed substantially. Councils and chiefs under colonial supervision gave way to constitutions, professional administrations, courts, tax systems, enterprise holdings, and treaty institutions. Responsibilities expanded from limited local administration to health, education, housing, land, and emergency management. Governments in many nations now employ large professional workforces and manage multi-million-dollar budgets. What remained continuous? Claims to self-government persisted through periods of non-recognition. Customary institutions, clan systems, assemblies, and law continued in many communities and have been revived in others. Many nations kept or rebuilt a territorial base and a political community defined by their own membership rules. When did change occur? Change was uneven and gradual, with accelerations around the 1934 and 1975 United States laws, the 1982 Canadian constitutional recognition, the modern treaties from 2000, the Greenlandic reforms of 1979 and 2009, and the Mexican reforms culminating in 2024. Who experienced change most directly? Elected leaders and administrators faced new responsibilities; citizens in remote or dispersed communities depended most on whether services reached them; nations moving from administration to jurisdiction experienced the greatest institutional change. Who experienced greater continuity? Communities in which customary governance continued alongside elected structures, and communal assemblies in Mexico, often experienced more continuity in decision-making practices. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that contemporary Indigenous government is neither a vanished tradition nor a creation of colonial law alone. It is an adaptive set of institutions, shaped by both inherited political traditions and external legal settings.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how Indigenous nations in North America govern in the twenty-first century. Its central argument is that Indigenous governments are continuing political institutions, distinct from cultural organizations, advocacy groups, and administrative agencies, whose authority, resources, and legal standing vary widely among the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland.

The chapter began with the distinction between Indigenous peoples as demographic populations and Indigenous nations as organized political communities, and between inherent authority and authority delegated, recognized, or negotiated by surrounding states. Contemporary governments emerged from mixed histories: some adopted structures promoted by the Indian Reorganization Act or Indian Act systems, some adapted or resisted them, some maintained customary institutions, and some negotiated modern treaties. The chapter therefore avoided treating any government as simply a creation of colonial law or as an unbroken precolonial survival.

Foundations differ. The Cherokee Nation's 2003 constitution shows a written tripartite government drafted by the nation itself. The Navajo Nation shows a statutory government with local chapters. Haudenosaunee communities show the coexistence of hereditary and elected systems, and Pueblo governments show community-specific arrangements. Citizenship and membership are governmental questions, defined differently among tribal enrollment, Indian status, treaty enrolment, Mexican community membership, and Greenlandic residence, and leadership selection varies from elected councils to hereditary and consensus systems. Customary and elected authority can be complementary when responsibilities are clearly divided.

The chapter then examined how governments operate. Executive institutions combine elected leaders with professional administrations that deliver services. Legislative bodies enact ordinances and resolutions through processes that can be traced from proposal to implementation, as in the 2009–2010 reduction of the Navajo Nation Council. Justice institutions include courts, peacemaking, and community systems, but jurisdictional limits, particularly over non-Indians in the United States, affect their work. Public finance depends on a mix of own-source taxes, transfers, enterprise income, resource revenue, investments, restricted grants, and borrowing, and substantial business revenue does not necessarily mean fiscal autonomy.

The continental comparison showed the range of arrangements. United States tribal governments include large constitutional governments, small nations that pool services, and Alaska Native village governments that differ from ANCSA corporations. In Canada, Indian Act band councils, self-governing First Nations, the Nisg̱a'a modern treaty government, Inuit regional governments, the public territorial government of Nunavut, and Métis governments with differing legal status are distinct institutions. In Mexico, community assemblies, Oaxaca's normative systems, and Cherán's communal government operate within a constitutional order transformed in September 2024 by recognition of Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities as subjects of public law, with implementation still unfolding. Greenland's self-government within the Kingdom of Denmark is a public government of a different structure.

Governing responsibilities test capacity. Health, education, housing, and social services can be administered for another government, designed as Indigenous-controlled systems, or governed through Indigenous legislation, as the First Nations Health Authority and the 2019 child and family services statute, upheld in 2024, illustrate. Government-owned enterprises require a separation between government and business. Land administration shows that ownership, regulation, and infrastructure responsibility are often held by different institutions, and the 2026 Nunavut land use plan decision showed three parties each holding a necessary role. Emergencies, including the COVID-19 pandemic, evacuations, and floods, exposed both capability and limits.

Intergovernmental relations are negotiations between governments, not lobbying, and they create continuing implementation obligations without necessarily establishing equal constitutional authority. Accountability to citizens and reporting to external funders are different, and the design of accountability remains debated. Nation-building scholarship identifies decision-making control and capable, culturally fitting institutions as important, but it does not provide a universal model.

The chapter's six-dimension framework, political authority, legal capacity, administrative capacity, fiscal capacity, territorial authority, and institutional legitimacy, offered a consistent way to examine these cases without ranking nations. The comparison of eight governmental models confirmed that they differ in constitutional foundation, jurisdiction, finance, membership, territory, and responsibilities.

The central conclusion is that sovereignty becomes operational through institutions. Authority must be accompanied by the capacity to decide, administer, provide services, protect rights, and maintain continuity. Indigenous sovereignty is therefore not only a historical inheritance or a legal principle but a continuing governmental practice, which Chapter 140 continues by examining Indigenous communities whose lives extend beyond conventional territorial boundaries.

Documentary comparisons add an important qualification to the institutional framework. Cherokee Nation financial reporting separates governmental operations from the businesses that distribute income to it; Nunatsiavut's financial plan combines negotiated transfers, tax revenues and treaty-related resources; and Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated administers treaty-related resources distinct from the territorial public budget. Mexico's FAISPIAM program similarly illustrates how a defined allocation can strengthen community administration without conferring unrestricted fiscal autonomy. These cases show why a government budget, an audited financial statement, an enterprise account and a funding announcement must be read differently. Institutional development depends upon the capacity to translate resources into sustained services, while accountability requires clarity about which body is responsible for decisions, implementation and reporting. Changes in governmental structure are ongoing: the parliamentary process for Red River Métis treaty legislation and the scheduled Nunavut devolution transfer should not be mistaken for completed implementation.

Key Terms

Inherent sovereignty
The claim that Indigenous nations hold political authority that predates colonial states and does not depend on their grant; recognized in different forms in United States, Canadian, and other legal systems.
Self-determination
The ability of a people to determine its own political status and pursue its own development; in United States policy since 1975 also refers to tribal administration of federal programs.
Self-government
The exercise of governmental powers by an Indigenous nation, whether through inherent authority, treaty, statute, or agreement.
Tribal government
In the United States, the governing institutions of a federally recognized tribe or Alaska Native village.
Federal recognition
Acknowledgment by the United States of a tribe's political status and government-to-government relationship, obtained through administrative acknowledgment, statute, or other federal action.
Jurisdiction
The legal power of a government to make and enforce decisions over particular subjects, persons, or places.
Political citizenship
Membership in an Indigenous nation as a political community, determined under that nation's own rules, as distinct from ancestry alone or citizenship in the surrounding state.
Tribal enrollment
In the United States, the formal registration of a person as a member of a tribe under its membership criteria.
Constitution
A foundational legal document, or body of fundamental laws, establishing a government's institutions, powers, and limits.
Customary law
Law grounded in long-standing community practice and understanding, often transmitted orally, that governs conduct and institutions.
Hereditary leadership
A system in which leadership offices pass through lineages or clans, sometimes with selection or removal by designated clan members, as in Haudenosaunee systems.
General assembly
A body composed of all eligible community members that deliberates and decides, as in many Pueblo communities and Mexican communal systems.
Executive authority
The power to carry out laws and administer government, held by a president, chief, council, or other office.
Legislative council
An elected or selected body that enacts laws, such as the Navajo Nation Council or Cherokee Tribal Council.
Ordinance
A general, lasting law enacted by a tribal council or similar body.
Resolution
A formal expression of a legislative body's decision, often directing action or addressing a particular matter rather than establishing general law.
Judicial review
The power of a court to examine government action for consistency with a constitution or fundamental law.
Public administration
The organized management of government programs and services by professional staff under political direction.
Civil service
A government's professional workforce, with personnel rules governing hiring, promotion, and employment.
Fiscal autonomy
The capacity of a government to raise and allocate revenue according to its own priorities.
Own-source revenue
Revenue a government raises under its own authority, such as taxes, fees, or distributions from its own enterprises, as opposed to transfers.
Intergovernmental transfer
Funds provided by one government to another under statute, agreement, or program rules.
Operating budget
A budget for recurring costs such as salaries, programs, and routine services.
Capital budget
A budget for infrastructure, buildings, and equipment with long-term value.
Public debt
Money a government owes to lenders, to be repaid from future revenue.
Government-owned enterprise
A business owned by a government, often managed through a charter and board, that distributes returns to the government.
Sovereign immunity
In United States law, the general immunity of tribal governments from suit unless waived by the tribe or abrogated by Congress; Canadian law has no equivalent doctrine for First Nations.
Self-governance compact
In the United States, a negotiated agreement under the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act by which a tribe assumes responsibility and funding for programs with greater flexibility than under a contract.
Modern treaty
In Canada, a comprehensive land claims or self-government agreement, protected under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, such as the Nisg̱a'a Final Agreement.
Public government
A government whose institutions serve all residents of a territory rather than members of a particular nation, such as Nunavut or Greenland.
Legal pluralism
The coexistence of more than one legal and political order, such as customary and state law, within the same community or territory.
Institutional legitimacy
The public confidence and acceptance that a government's institutions hold, built through fairness, continuity, accountability, and fit with community values.
Administrative capacity
A government's ability to implement policy and deliver services through staff, systems, and organization.
Governmental accountability
The mechanisms by which a government answers for its decisions and use of resources, to its citizens and to external parties.
Nation-building
The continuing development of legitimate and effective political institutions by an Indigenous nation, as studied by the Harvard Project on Indigenous Governance and Development.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the textbook from the history of how Indigenous authority was contested to the practice of how it is exercised. Earlier chapters showed that treaties, constitutional recognition, courts, land claims, and self-determination policy created openings. The institutions examined here are where those openings are used or constrained: in a council debate, a department budget, a court hearing, a coordination agreement, or an emergency declaration.

The chapter also reveals a pattern across North America. Authority recognized in law and capacity to exercise it are different things, and the distance between them is where many contemporary struggles occur. The same pattern appears in a modern treaty that requires continuing implementation, a tribal court with limited reach, a First Nation health system dependent on negotiated funding, a Mexican community whose autonomy depends on secondary legislation, and a self-governing territory whose budget depends partly on a block grant. Recognizing the pattern does not diminish Indigenous political agency. It shows how much of that agency is expressed through the work of building and sustaining institutions.

Later chapters build on this foundation. Chapter 140 examines communities whose citizens live in cities and across borders, where governments serve people beyond their territorial jurisdiction. Chapters 141 through 143 consider Indigenous Mexico, international borders, and internationalism, each of which tests how governments relate to other states. Chapters 144 and 145 address membership and knowledge sovereignty, and Chapter 148 examines how the relationships among Indigenous nations and the governments of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland may continue to change.

The continuities are as important as the changes. Councils, clan systems, assemblies, and customary law persist alongside constitutions and professional administration. The chapter's cases are not stages on the way to a single model of government but different solutions to shared problems of legitimacy, finance, jurisdiction, and service. That is why the textbook's conclusion that Indigenous nations are continuing political communities depends on seeing them in action. Indigenous sovereignty is a continuing governmental practice, expressed through the institutions by which Indigenous nations govern themselves and determine their futures.
