The Ghost Dance and Indigenous Religious Revitalization

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On the first day of 1889, the sun was partly or wholly darkened over western Nevada. Later accounts connect that eclipse to the religious vision of a Northern Paiute man named Wovoka. Within two years, the teachings associated with him were being sung, danced, debated, and reinterpreted by Indigenous communities from the Sierra Nevada foothills to the Missouri River and south to Indian Territory. Many officials, newspapers, and later popular writers saw only a menace. The people who danced saw a way of mourning, hoping, and renewing the world.

The valleys of the Walker River and Mason Valley, in what is now western Nevada, were home to Northern Paiute people long before Nevada existed as a territory or a state. The region combined river bottoms, marsh, alkaline lakes, sagebrush country, and mountain pine-nut groves. By the 1880s it had also been shaped by mining, ranching, irrigation, railroads, and settler claims on land and water. Northern Paiute families continued to fish, gather, work for wages, and maintain ceremonies and kin networks. It was in this setting, and out of religious traditions that were already old, that the revelation commonly associated with the year 1889 took shape. The teachings that followed left the Great Basin in the memories and bundles of travelers, and they would be taken up in dozens of communities.

The Ghost Dance is among the most widely recognized and most widely misunderstood episodes in Indigenous history. In popular memory it is frequently fused with the Wounded Knee Massacre of December 1890, so that the religion appears to be only a prologue to a tragedy, a desperate "last stand" by a defeated people, or a militant cult. This chapter argues that such readings reverse the historical order of explanation. The Ghost Dance was a religious movement first. Its teachings drew on Indigenous traditions of prophecy, vision, healing, and ceremonial renewal. It spread through Indigenous communication networks and acquired different meanings in different places. It was interpreted by agency officials, military officers, missionaries, and journalists through assumptions of reservation administration and settler colonialism, and those interpretations, rather than anything in the dance itself, turned a ceremony into a perceived emergency.

The chapter's central historical question is how Indigenous prophetic teachings and ceremonial traditions developed into an intertribal religious revitalization movement, why different nations interpreted the Ghost Dance in different ways, and how American officials transformed an expression of religious hope into a perceived military emergency. To answer it, the chapter distinguishes two movements. The first, associated with the Northern Paiute prophet Wodziwob, emerged in the late 1860s and spread through the Great Basin and California in the early 1870s. The second, associated with Wovoka, emerged in 1889 and spread rapidly across the Great Basin, the Plains, and Indian Territory. The two movements shared important themes, especially the return of the dead, the renewal of the world, and the central role of circle dancing, but they were not identical, and modern scholarship treats the relationship between them as a matter for investigation rather than assumption.

The chapter also treats the movement as plural. Wovoka's message, as it survives, emphasized peace, honest conduct, and reunion with deceased relatives. Northern Paiute, Shoshone, and Bannock communities received it in light of their own ceremonial histories. Among the Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, Caddo, and neighboring peoples of Indian Territory it was woven into traditions of visions, songs, and healing. Among the Lakota, who experienced the breakup of the Great Sioux Reservation in 1889, it became associated with distinctive beliefs about sacred power, including shirts and dresses believed by some to protect their wearers. The chapter takes care not to let the Lakota case stand in for the whole, and not to let the best-known Lakota leaders stand in for the community of men, women, and children who danced.

The evidence is heavily mediated. No one wrote a manuscript in Wovoka's hand that has been authenticated as a source for his teachings. What survives are oral accounts, translated messages, letters written by or for intermediaries, songs transcribed by an ethnologist, agency correspondence, newspaper stories, photographs, drawings, surviving garments collected under colonial conditions, and later recollections. Each passed through interpreters, editors, or institutions with their own purposes. Learning to ask who is speaking, to whom, through whom, and why is therefore central to this chapter's method, and it is the explicit subject of its documentary studies.

Federal policy forms the background to the movement's reception. Chapter 84 examined the reservation order, Chapter 86 the Dawes Act and the assault on communal land, and Chapters 87 and 88 boarding schools and their human consequences. Together, these policies attacked Indigenous landholding, family life, language, and ceremony. Reservation administrators had in the 1880s restricted religious ceremonies and sought to replace them with Christianity and individual farming. In that setting, a gathering of Indigenous people in a circle, singing and praying for hours or days, could be received by a government agent as a challenge to authority even when its teachings forbade violence.

This chapter stops at the threshold of the Wounded Knee Massacre. The killing of Sitting Bull on December 15, 1890, and the confrontation at Wounded Knee Creek on December 29 are introduced only to explain the escalating context; the massacre, its victims, its aftermath, and its memory belong to Chapter 90. What this chapter offers is the history that popular accounts often omit: the religious thought, the ceremonial creativity, the communication networks, and the regional variety of an Indigenous movement whose significance does not rest on how the government responded to it. It also insists that the Ghost Dance did not end in 1890. Like other Indigenous ceremonial traditions in this period, it survived, adapted, and was remembered, a pattern consistent with the continental argument of the whole textbook.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Indigenous traditions of prophecy, vision, and ceremonial renewal existed before the Ghost Dance and provided foundations for it.
  2. Distinguish the Northern Paiute movement of Wodziwob (c. 1869–1870s) from the later movement associated with Wovoka (1889–1890), identifying both shared themes and differences.
  3. Describe the central teachings attributed to Wovoka, including peace, ethical conduct, reunion with deceased relatives, and world renewal, and explain how these teachings reach historians through mediated sources.
  4. Analyze the Ghost Dance ceremony, including circle dancing, song, prayer, and visionary experience, while avoiding the assumption that all communities performed it identically.
  5. Trace the routes and mechanisms through which the movement was transmitted, including delegations, kinship ties, interpreters, and letters.
  6. Compare how Northern Paiute, Shoshone, Bannock, Southern Plains, and Lakota communities received and adapted the teachings.
  7. Assess the relationship between material conditions on the Lakota reservations and the reception of the Ghost Dance, avoiding simple deprivation explanations.
  8. Evaluate Ghost Dance shirts as a Lakota religious adaptation rather than a universal feature of the movement, and explain the limits of interpreting surviving garments.
  9. Explain how Indian agents, missionaries, newspapers, and military officials interpreted the Ghost Dance and how those interpretations contributed to an escalating crisis in 1890.
  10. Contextualize the Ghost Dance among other Indigenous religious renewal movements, including the Dreamer tradition, the Indian Shaker Church, and peyote religion.
  11. Evaluate sources for the Ghost Dance, including Mooney's ethnography, agency reports, newspapers, songs, and photographs, with attention to authorship, translation, and mediation.
  12. Interpret the debate over whether the Ghost Dance is best understood as religious revitalization, anticolonial resistance, or a family of locally adapted movements in which religion and politics were inseparable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What older Indigenous traditions of prophecy, vision, and ceremony made the Ghost Dance intelligible and meaningful to the communities that adopted it?
  2. In what respects were the Ghost Dance of 1870 and the Ghost Dance of 1889–1890 the same movement, and in what respects were they different?
  3. How did a message from western Nevada travel across thousands of miles, and what happened to it as it was translated, interpreted, and carried by different people?
  4. Why did the Ghost Dance take such different forms among Great Basin, Southern Plains, and Lakota communities?
  5. How should historians weigh the role of political and economic conditions against the internal religious logic of the movement in explaining its appeal?
  6. Why did Indian agents, missionaries, newspapers, and military officers interpret the dance as a threat, and what interests and assumptions shaped those interpretations?
  7. How did Indigenous people themselves disagree about the Ghost Dance, and why does that disagreement matter?
  8. What can and cannot be known about Wovoka's teachings, given the sources that survive?
  9. What does the survival of Ghost Dance traditions after 1890 reveal about the relationship between political suppression and religious continuity?

Historical Context

By 1870 most Indigenous nations west of the Mississippi were living under pressure from railroads, mining rushes, military campaigns, settler agriculture, and treaties that were often broken or reinterpreted. Earlier chapters have traced this process: the removals of Volume VI, the California Gold Rush in Chapter 70, the Plains wars of Volume VII, and the destruction of the bison economy in Chapter 81. By 1890 the main military conflicts between Plains nations and the United States were over, reservations were the principal framework of administration, and the federal government was pursuing assimilation through allotment, schooling, and the regulation of religion, a policy examined in Chapters 84 through 88.

The Great Basin and Plateau had a different but connected history. The peoples of the Great Basin, including the Northern Paiute and the Western Shoshone, lived by seasonal movement through diverse ecological zones, gathering pine nuts, roots, and seeds, fishing, and hunting, and were organized in small kin-based communities that cooperated at seasonal gatherings. Miners, ranchers, and railroad builders in the 1850s through 1870s disrupted these economies by occupying springs, fisheries, and pinyon groves. Many Indigenous families responded by working for wages on ranches and in towns while sustaining ceremonies, family ties, and claims to land. Chapter 31 describes the Great Basin and Plateau before colonization; this chapter takes up the region in the second half of the nineteenth century.

Indigenous societies also possessed established religious institutions. Visions, songs, dances, healing practices, and relationships with sacred powers and ancestors were part of community life long before the Ghost Dance. Prophets had arisen in earlier periods, as Chapter 62 noted for Tenskwatawa and as the Seneca prophet Handsome Lake had earlier demonstrated in the Haudenosaunee world. The Ghost Dance belongs to this long tradition. At the same time, the federal government had since the early 1880s used agency regulations to restrict dances and ceremonies, and many agents regarded Indigenous religion as an obstacle to civilization. A religious movement that drew large gatherings therefore encountered an administrative apparatus already inclined to see ceremonial gatherings as disorder.

A final piece of context concerns how the history is known. The principal early synthesis, James Mooney's study for the Bureau of American Ethnology, was written by an outsider who conducted extensive fieldwork and treated Indigenous informants with unusual seriousness for his time, but who also worked within the assumptions of a federal scientific agency. Later scholarship, including studies of Wovoka's life, of the Shoshone and Bannock, of the Kiowa, and of the Lakota, has corrected, expanded, and sometimes challenged that account. Students should keep in mind throughout that the history of the Ghost Dance is a history reconstructed from sources that were created for many different purposes.

Division I

Indigenous Prophetic Traditions and the Northern Paiute Origins of the Ghost Dance

Section 89.1

Indigenous Religious Life, Colonial Disruption, and the Traditions of Spiritual Renewal

A history of the Ghost Dance must begin with Indigenous religious systems as living institutions of community life, rather than with the colonial disruptions that provoked particular responses. Across North America, religion was not a separate compartment of existence. It was woven into how communities understood land, food, kinship, healing, politics, and the dead. Ceremonies marked seasonal cycles and renewed relationships with the beings and powers on which a community depended. Songs were owned, inherited, received in dreams, and performed according to protocols. Healing practitioners treated illness by drawing on spiritual power. Relationships with ancestors continued after death, and the living bore obligations to them.

Great Basin peoples such as the Northern Paiute understood the world as animated by power, which Numic-speaking peoples commonly refer to with a term often rendered in English as puha. Individuals received power through dreams and visions, and the people who held it, often called shamans in older scholarship, used it for healing, for weather and food-related work, and for community protection. Seasonal gatherings for rabbit drives, pine-nut harvests, and fishing combined economic cooperation with dancing, singing, and the renewal of social ties. The round dance, in which participants move in a circle, was a familiar social and ceremonial form. On the Plains, religious life centered on vision-seeking, sacred bundles, and communal ceremonies such as the Sun Dance, in which individual sacrifice and collective renewal were combined. Lakota people spoke of wakan, a term often translated as sacred or mysterious power, a concept this chapter returns to in Section 89.11.

Societies differed in how they understood prophecy and religious leadership. In some, visionary authority was open to anyone who received a vision and could demonstrate its power through effective healing or accurate guidance. In others, it was bound to specific societies, bundle keepers, or lineages. Some prophets taught that disaster signaled a failure to fulfil obligations and that renewal required a return to proper conduct. Others announced a transformed world to come. Such prophets were not novelties of the colonial period. But the colonial period gave prophetic teaching new urgency and new content.

The nineteenth century brought disruptions that were severe and cumulative. Epidemics repeatedly struck Indigenous communities, as Chapter 40 and later chapters describe. Removal policies uprooted entire nations. Wars, treaty violations, and the creation of reservations restricted mobility and changed access to resources. On the Plains the collapse of the bison economy, treated in Chapter 81, removed both a food source and a foundation of ceremonial and social life. In the Great Basin, ranching, mining, and irrigation diverted waters and displaced gathering and hunting grounds. The federal government, with the cooperation of Christian missions, regulated religious practice. In 1883, regulations for Courts of Indian Offenses targeted specific ceremonies and the work of healers, and agents on many reservations discouraged or banned dances. Canada took a comparable path through amendments to the Indian Act, a subject treated in Chapter 85.

Against this background, scholars have long asked whether new prophetic movements should be read primarily as responses to crisis. The answer proposed in this chapter is that crisis shaped the content, urgency, and reception of teachings, but did not by itself generate them. Communities facing equally severe conditions did not all adopt the Ghost Dance, and those that did interpreted it according to their own religious traditions. Historians therefore distinguish continuity within established spiritual traditions from the appearance of new prophetic movements that adapted older ideas to changed circumstances. The Ghost Dance was the latter, but it drew on the former.

The concept students will encounter most often in the scholarship is revitalization. Anthropologist Anthony F. C. Wallace formulated a well-known model of revitalization movements in the 1950s and 1960s, and later scholars, notably Alice Beck Kehoe, have criticized the model for treating Indigenous movements as pathological responses to stress. This chapter uses "religious revitalization" descriptively, as a way to name movements that sought to renew a people's relationship with the sacred and with one another, and not as a diagnosis.

Historical Vocabulary
Religious Revitalization, Prophecy, Vision, and Ceremonial Renewal

Religious revitalization is a scholarly term for a movement in which a community deliberately seeks to renew or reshape its religious and social life, usually in response to changed circumstances, and often through new teachings said to have been received from a spiritual source. Prophecy, in this context, refers to teaching presented as revealed knowledge about the present or the future, which may concern moral conduct, expected events, or ceremonial practice. A vision is a religious experience, received in sleep, trance, or a vision quest, which may confer power, songs, or instruction. Ceremonial renewal refers to ritual action meant to restore relationships among people, the sacred, and the world.

Historical context: All four concepts are older than the Ghost Dance. The first, however, is a modern analytical term. Participants did not describe themselves as joining a "revitalization movement."

Why it matters: The terms allow historians to compare movements across regions and periods while keeping attention on Indigenous religious logic.

Potential misunderstanding: "Revitalization" can suggest that a culture was dying and needed revival. Many participants understood their ceremonies as continuing and renewing something living, and the older theories that treated such movements as symptoms of cultural breakdown have been widely criticized.

Section 89.2

Wodziwob and the Ghost Dance Movement of 1870

The first Ghost Dance, now often called the Ghost Dance of 1870 for the year in which it drew national attention, is associated with the Northern Paiute prophet Wodziwob, whose name is also written Wodzibob. Accounts place him in the area around Walker Lake and Fish Lake Valley, in west-central Nevada, in the late 1860s. Dates are approximate, and the exact chronology of the movement's growth and spread is debated. What is reasonably well established is that, from about 1869, teachings attributed to Wodziwob, and to other Northern Paiute religious figures associated with him, circulated among Great Basin communities and then into California.

The movement's setting was the world created by the 1860s. The Comstock Lode had drawn thousands of miners into Nevada in 1859. The Paiute War of 1860 around Pyramid Lake had ended in Northern Paiute defeat. The transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869 and ran through Northern Paiute and Western Shoshone country. Settlement displaced fishing and gathering sites. Epidemic diseases continued to take lives. In this environment, accounts of the movement's teachings emphasize the return of deceased relatives and the renewal of the world. Several recollections collected later report that the dead would come back, and some tie the expected renewal to dramatic events, such as an earthquake, though the surviving testimony is variable and its details should not be pressed too far.

The ceremonial form was the circle dance. Participants gathered, sang, and danced in a circle, often through the night, and communities that adopted the movement organized dances and sent news to neighbors. The movement spread among Northern Paiute communities and to neighboring Western Shoshone and Washo peoples, and then across the Sierra Nevada into California, where it was taken up and transformed by several Indigenous nations. Ethnographic research carried out later, notably by Cora Du Bois in the 1930s, documented the "1870 Ghost Dance" among northern California communities and described how its teachings and dance forms developed into regional religious traditions with their own names and leaders. Some of these, such as the Bole-Maru and earth-lodge traditions of northern and central California, developed into durable religious institutions rather than short-lived crazes.

Modern scholarship, especially Gregory E. Smoak's Ghost Dances and Identity, argues that the 1870 movement mattered for more than its religious content. It brought people from separate communities together in common ceremonial activity, encouraged travel and communication across tribal boundaries, and contributed to the development of broader Indigenous identities, a process Smoak describes in the language of ethnogenesis, the formation of new or enlarged collective identities. In the Great Basin, where political organization was often local and flexible, the movement offered a shared framework without requiring a centralized institution.

The 1870 movement did not become as widely known as the 1889 movement, and it is sometimes treated as a mere precursor. That view is misleading. The Ghost Dance of 1870 was a movement in its own right, with its own geographic reach, its own regional developments, and its own long afterlife in California. At the same time, there were continuities that allowed the 1889 revelation to find a hearing. Some of the same themes, the return of the dead, a renewed world, and the circle dance, recur. Some of the same families and communities were involved. Mooney believed that Wovoka's father, Tävibo, had been associated with the earlier movement, and Hittman and others have examined this connection. The 1889 movement was in this sense a revival, though its content, scale, and recipients differed.

Primary Source Window 1

James Mooney's Account of Wodziwob and the Earlier Ghost Dance

Creator: James Mooney, ethnologist, Bureau of American Ethnology · Date: research and fieldwork principally 1891–1893, with related recordings in 1894; published 1896 (some catalogues 1897) · Type: Retrospective ethnographic narrative, in The Ghost-Dance Religion and the Sioux Outbreak of 1890, Fourteenth Annual Report of the Bureau of American Ethnology

Consult the historical source: Mooney’s original published study (Project Gutenberg). These links do not convert the editorial paraphrase below into a verified original-language quotation.

Context: Mooney devoted part of his study to what he called the "Ghost dance of 1872" and traced its origins to Wodziwob among the Paiute. He assembled the account from interviews with Paiute and other informants, from documents, and from reports of earlier observers. He was not present in Nevada in 1869 and could not have witnessed the movement's origins.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): Mooney reports that a Paiute prophet named Wodziwob taught that the dead would return and that a transformation would renew the world, that the movement spread among Nevada Indian communities and into California, and that the practice centered on the circle dance. Mooney also notes that he considered the earlier movement relevant to the later one.

Analysis: The account is valuable as the earliest sustained scholarly effort to reconstruct the 1870 movement and as evidence that Mooney, and many of his informants, understood the later Ghost Dance as having antecedents. It records names, places, and teachings that otherwise would not have been preserved in a written source.

Limitations: The account is retrospective, written roughly twenty years after the events by an outsider relying on memory-based testimony passed through translation. It reflects Mooney's own questions, his reliance on particular informants, and his tendency to read the 1870 and 1889 movements as a single phenomenon. Later research, especially by Du Bois and Smoak, has added regional detail and corrected the sense of a simple lineage.

Perspective: The narrator is a federal ethnologist. Perspectives of Northern Paiute participants appear only as filtered through Mooney's selections and summaries.

Section 89.3

Wovoka, Northern Paiute Religious Traditions, and the Revelation of 1889

Wovoka, later known to many Euro-Americans as Jack Wilson, was a Northern Paiute man born in the 1850s, probably around 1856, though dates vary in the sources. He grew up in western Nevada, in the Walker River and Mason Valley region. His father, Tävibo, is described in the literature as a religious figure, and Mooney associated him with the earlier movement. Michael Hittman, whose Wovoka and the Ghost Dance is the major modern biography, has examined what can be known of Wovoka's life from interviews, local records, and the writings of Mooney and other visitors. When Wovoka was a young man he worked, and for a time lived, with the family of David Wilson, a rancher in Mason Valley; the English name Jack Wilson derives from that association. He worked on ranches, as did many Northern Paiute men and women, and he spoke English.

That work exposed Wovoka to Christianity. The Wilson family were devout, and scholars have noted echoes of Christian vocabulary and imagery in the way later accounts present his teachings. Scholars disagree about how to interpret this. Some emphasize Christian sources for specific elements, such as the language of a returning savior. Others stress that the central structure of the teaching, including its ceremonial form, its emphasis on reunion with the dead, and its ethical teachings, can be understood within Northern Paiute religious traditions and the earlier Ghost Dance, with Christian language available as one vocabulary through which the teachings were translated for non-Paiute audiences. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the sources do not permit a firm separation of the strands.

Wovoka also was, by most accounts, a religious practitioner in the Northern Paiute tradition, a person recognized as having power and reputed to be able to influence weather and heal. He apparently possessed the standing to teach before the revelation that made him famous. The traditional narrative of that revelation, preserved in Mooney's account, places it in connection with the solar eclipse of January 1, 1889. A total solar eclipse crossed the American West that day, and Mooney was told that Wovoka, ill with fever, was taken to the other world during the eclipse, where he saw God and the dead living happily and received instructions for his people. Hittman and other scholars have noted that the date has probably been simplified in retelling and that Wovoka's religious experience may have unfolded over a longer period. Students should treat "January 1, 1889" as the conventional date in the tradition, and as an anchor that scholars use with caution.

The reported content of the vision was consistent in outline across the surviving accounts. Wovoka was shown the deceased relatives of his people in a good place. He was told that the dead would return and that a renewed world, free from suffering, would come. His people were to live righteously and to perform a dance as the means by which they would take part in the transformation. The vision therefore combined elements of an older Great Basin tradition of visionary instruction with a distinctive emphasis on collective moral conduct and the dance.

Students must distinguish carefully between what Wovoka himself taught and what has been said about his teachings by others. Wovoka was frequently visited by people from other nations, most of whom did not share his language, and he spoke to them through interpreters or in English. Some visitors recorded what they were told. The Euro-American authors who wrote about him, including government agents, journalists, and ethnologists, described the teachings in categories drawn from Christianity, and sometimes from their own expectations of an Indian "messiah." The title Messiah, which became standard in newspapers, is in large part a creation of outside observers and of Indigenous people translating for them, and does not necessarily reflect Wovoka's own self-understanding or Paiute categories.

Wovoka, for his part, lived near Yerington (the Mason Valley area) for the rest of his long life, dying in 1932. He continued to receive visitors for years. He appears to have worked to correct perceptions that he had called for war, and he exercised caution about the claims attached to him, an aspect of his career that Hittman discusses. The movement he began did not depend on his continued leadership. It had taken on lives of its own in communities that had received his teachings, and the next sections examine what those teachings were and how they traveled.

Section 89.4

Wovoka's Message: Peace, Moral Conduct, the Return of Ancestors, and World Renewal

The theology of the movement, as it can be reconstructed, was less a system of doctrine than a body of teachings and practices linked by a hope. At its center was reunion: the dead would return, and living people would again be with their relatives. This promise was not abstract. It addressed communities that had suffered extraordinary losses from epidemics, wars, and forced removals, and in which grief for the dead was widespread and constant. A religion that offered a pathway to reunion engaged those losses directly, and Mooney and later scholars observed that mourning practices, including the question of when and how to grieve, were bound up with the teachings.

The second major theme was the renewal of the world. In many accounts the expected transformation would bring an end to illness and suffering and a restoration of conditions in which people could live as they had before the disruptions of colonization. Details varied widely. Some versions anticipated the return of game animals. Some looked to a specific date. Many Euro-American writers focused on a prediction that the white population would be removed, but the surviving evidence suggests that this theme was emphasized more in some regional adaptations, and in reports by outsiders, than in the core of the message attributed to Wovoka. Scholars debate how much of the apocalyptic content belonged to the original teaching and how much was developed in transmission.

The third theme was ethical conduct, and it is the most strongly attested in the surviving versions of the message. Participants were told not to fight, not to harm others, not to lie, and not to steal. They were told to live in peace, to work, and to do right. The teaching about peace stands in direct tension with the popular image of the Ghost Dance as a war religion. It does not follow that every participant was or remained peaceful, or that all communities emphasized this message equally, but it does mean that the movement's best-documented core is ethical and pacific. Hittman and others also emphasize that this stress on peaceful conduct should not be read as a simple accommodation to white authority. For a people who had experienced repeated violence, moral discipline could also be understood as a condition of the world's renewal.

The practices that expressed the teachings were prayer, singing, feasting, bathing, and the dance itself. According to the versions that survive, the dance was to be performed repeatedly, in a pattern of consecutive days and nights, and participants were to wash or bathe, often in a river, at its end. These practices, discussed in the next section, were the means through which a community took part in what was hoped for. The moral and the ceremonial were bound together.

Christian terminology appears in some surviving versions. In Mooney's presentation, the message refers to Jesus as present and returning, and observers across the Plains referred to a Messiah. How to interpret this has been an enduring problem. Alice Beck Kehoe and others have argued that the Ghost Dance was rooted in Indigenous religious logic and that Christian terms were adopted or supplied in the course of translation. Others see more deliberate engagement with Christianity, including by Wovoka. A fair reading is that teachings in Northern Paiute and in English, transmitted among peoples with very different religious traditions, were available to multiple interpretations, and that the movement's capacity to be heard on its own terms by many different communities was part of what let it spread.

An essential documentary distinction applies to the best-known text. Wovoka's message of 1891 does not survive as a manuscript written by Wovoka. It survives through a chain of oral communication, transcription, interpretation, and later editorial presentation. A version was carried by a Cheyenne and Arapaho delegation that visited Wovoka, was preserved through the Cheyenne man Black Short Nose, and was reproduced by Mooney. Other versions, with variations, were recorded by Mooney from other communities. Differences among them reflect the realities of oral transmission and translation. A teaching spoken by Wovoka in one language, rendered into English by an interpreter, carried in memory to Indian Territory, translated into Cheyenne or Arapaho, and later transcribed and edited into English prose was not a single fixed text at any stage.

Section 89.5

The Ghost Dance Ceremony: Circle Dancing, Sacred Songs, Visions, and Collective Participation

The Ghost Dance was above all a ceremony, and a ceremony is something people do together. Its basic form, in the accounts of the nineteenth century, was a circle of participants moving slowly, usually sunwise or counter to it depending on the community, often with hands linked or joined, and singing songs in the language of the dancers. Mooney, who observed dances in several communities in the early 1890s, described men, women, and children participating together. The circle was a familiar form of Indigenous social and ceremonial life, particularly in the Great Basin, where round dances had long marked gatherings. Its symbolism in this setting was bound up with ideas of community and of cyclical renewal, though students should be cautious about assigning a single meaning that participants themselves did not articulate.

Songs were central. Many were reportedly received in visions during or after the dance, and each community that took up the dance developed its own repertoire. Songs conveyed religious ideas in compact, repetitive, and memorable form: images of the returning dead, of animals, of winds and birds, of the renewed earth. The repetition of a short song through many rounds, with changing words, produced a collective rhythm and concentration, and it allowed songs to be learned quickly by large groups. Mooney collected and transcribed songs from several nations, work discussed in Section 89.8 and again in 89.19. Because songs belong to specific communities and carry spiritual significance, historians who use them must respect that the songs were not composed for outside publication.

Prayer, speeches, and instruction accompanied the dancing. Leaders often addressed gatherings to explain the teachings, to recount visions, or to organize the dances. Meals and feasts were part of the gatherings, and in some communities dancers prepared by washing or purifying themselves. The pattern of repeated days and nights of dancing recorded in the message of 1891 was adapted locally. Some communities danced for several consecutive days. Others held shorter or more frequent dances. Dancing could continue for hours, and in some places for much of the night. The length and frequency of dances varied with the season, with work obligations, and with the circumstances of each reservation.

Visionary experiences were an expected part of many dances. Participants sometimes entered trance-like states, falling to the ground, remaining motionless, or experiencing visions in which they saw deceased relatives, heard songs, or received messages. Participants regarded such experiences as religious events, as encounters with the dead and with sacred power, consistent with long-established traditions of vision. Many Euro-American observers described the same phenomena in medicalized or dismissive terms, as hysteria, hypnosis, fits, or frenzy, and some wrote with fascinated horror. Students should recognize that these descriptions say as much about the observers' assumptions as about the dancers. A sober modern reading treats the visions as part of the ceremony's religious meaning, as hopes of reunion made experientially present.

It would be a serious error to describe a single choreography or ritual sequence as if all participating nations observed the dance identically. Evidence shows variation in the direction of movement, the posture of the dancers, the presence or absence of a central pole or tree in some communities, the use of paint, feathers, and special clothing, the number of days and nights, and the role of leaders. Some communities, such as the Lakota, developed forms that visitors from Nevada might not have recognized. The Southern Plains communities blended the dance with their own songs and with practices of healing. Observers who attended a dance in one place and generalized from it to others produced much of the confusion about the movement in the popular literature.

The meaning of the ceremony cannot be reduced to spectacle. For participants, it was a collective act of prayer and hope. For families that had lost children, parents, or spouses, it offered an occasion in which grief could be expressed and anticipation of reunion held in common. The ceremony also reaffirmed community identity and social ties at a time when administrative policy pushed toward the division of communities into individual households. A government agent looking at the same gathering from outside saw a large assembly of Indigenous people not working and not under direct supervision. Both descriptions are historically meaningful, and the gap between them is central to the history that follows.

Section 89.6

Indigenous Messengers, Delegations, Oral Communication, and the Transmission of Religious Teachings

A striking feature of the Ghost Dance of 1889–1890 is its speed. Within roughly two years teachings associated with a man in western Nevada were being taught in communities across the West. They traveled without telephones or newspapers under Indigenous control, through channels that were already in place. By this period railroads, wagon roads, and the telegraph and postal system also existed, and Indigenous travelers used the trains and even, on some occasions, obtained free or discounted travel, but the principal mechanisms of the movement's spread were human relationships.

The most important mechanism was the delegation. Communities that heard of Wovoka's teaching, from visiting kin or traveling neighbors, selected representatives to go to Nevada and see for themselves. Delegations included leaders, religious practitioners, and respected men, and in some cases they were accompanied by others. Some traveled long distances, from Indian Territory, from Wyoming and Montana, and from the Dakota reservations. They visited Wovoka, listened to his teaching, participated in dances, and then returned home to report to their communities and to organize the dance. The decision to send a delegation, to select its members, and to accept or reject its report was made by the home community. This point matters: communities were not passive recipients of an order issued from Nevada. They investigated, evaluated, and made their own decisions.

Kinship, intermarriage, and older exchange relationships supplied the infrastructure. Peoples of the Great Basin and the Plains had long traded and visited. Shoshone and Bannock communities were linked to the Northern Plains through marriage and trade. Arapaho, Cheyenne, and Lakota communities had long-standing relations. Intertribal contacts forged during the buffalo-hunting era and in the context of reservations, including through boarding-school networks that brought students from many nations together, supplied opportunities for contact and shared languages, a point developed in Chapters 87 and 88. Sign language and English served as trade languages.

Interpreters and written messages also played a part. Wovoka's teachings were relayed through English by Wovoka or by interpreters. Some messages were written down by bilingual Indigenous people, often former students, and carried by hand or sent by mail. Mooney and later scholars noted letters in English, sometimes written by educated Indigenous people on behalf of the prophet or the delegates. This created the possibility, and in some cases the certainty, of change. A teaching moved from Northern Paiute into English, from English into Cheyenne or Lakota, and from spoken word to memory, written note, and back again.

Students should distinguish among a message's original formulation, its transmission, and its reception. The original formulation, Wovoka's own teaching, is accessible only through reports. Transmission involved delegates who recalled, summarized, or emphasized elements of what they had heard. Reception involved communities that interpreted the message in light of their own religious ideas. A delegate who returned to a community where visions were highly valued might emphasize the visionary experience. A delegate returning to a community that had suffered recent military defeat might emphasize the promise of renewal. In each case the message was heard through what was already meaningful. The Ghost Dance was therefore a process, in which leaders actively interpreted, modified, and transmitted the teachings, and not simply the distribution of a fixed doctrine.

Section 89.7

The Great Basin, California, and the Shoshone-Bannock Experience

The history of the Ghost Dance is too often told as a movement that began in Nevada and mattered only when it reached South Dakota. Historians such as Gregory E. Smoak have argued against that narrowing. In Ghost Dances and Identity he treats Shoshone and Bannock religious history as more than an intermediate stage between Nevada and Wounded Knee. Great Basin communities were not simply transmitters. They had their own religious histories, their own reasons for taking up the dance, and their own stakes in what it meant.

For Northern Paiute communities, the Ghost Dance was a development within their own tradition. Wovoka's neighbors and kin heard his teachings first, and for them the dance connected to older ceremonies of renewal and to relationships with ancestors. At the same time the local setting was changing quickly. Many families depended on wage work on ranches, on seasonal labor, and on diminishing access to traditional resources. Reservations at Walker River and Pyramid Lake had been established, but many Northern Paiute people lived off-reservation near towns and ranches. The dance offered a way to gather, to affirm common ties, and to invoke a world in which the losses of previous decades would be reversed.

Among the Shoshone and Bannock, particularly at Fort Hall in Idaho, the Ghost Dance had its own career. Smoak examines how the Shoshone and Bannock of Fort Hall, whose reservation administrators restricted their movement and their access to hunting and gathering, responded to the teachings. Dancers and leaders at Fort Hall participated in the movement, and Fort Hall also served as a hub through which news and teachings moved to the Northern Plains, partly through kinship with Shoshone at Wind River in Wyoming. The movement contributed to the formation of expressions of shared identity that crossed community and tribal boundaries, and Smoak connects this to a longer pattern in which prophetic religion helped Indigenous communities in the region define themselves in relation to one another and to American power.

The relationship between the Ghost Dance and existing religious leaders is a recurring theme. In some communities, established religious figures became leaders of the dance. In others, new leaders arose from among those who had visited Wovoka or received visions. Authority could be contested, and communities sometimes divided over the dance. The presence of Christian missionaries and, at Fort Hall and elsewhere, of Indian agents who took a dim view of the dance created pressures that shaped how openly communities danced. Reports from agents reflect those tensions, and they also suggest that many communities continued to hold dances in spite of administrative disapproval.

California connections extend this picture. The Ghost Dance of 1870 had contributed to long-lived California religious traditions, and California communities in the later nineteenth century did not stand outside the later movement. Practices and interpretations differed from those of the Northern Plains. In some California communities the dance and its associated teachings were integrated into ongoing traditions of dreamer-prophets and ceremonial houses, rather than being a short-lived response to a new message. Chapter 32 treats California before the Gold Rush; the post-conquest California of Chapter 70 provides the setting in which these traditions developed. The California material is a reminder that the Ghost Dance was not a single movement with a single geography.

This attention to Great Basin and California communities serves a larger historical responsibility. A narrative focused on the Lakota in 1890 rests on documents created because the government and the press were alarmed. The Great Basin story, by contrast, was less visible to the authorities and less sensational for newspapers, and it is therefore less documented. That imbalance in the sources should not be taken as an imbalance in historical importance. Communities in these regions were central participants in the movement, and the movement's long persistence in some of them suggests that it was woven into religious life in ways that the attention of outsiders did not capture.

Section 89.8

The Southern Plains: Arapaho, Cheyenne, Kiowa, Comanche, Caddo, and Other Communities

The Southern Plains offer a comparative study of how a single set of teachings was received by different nations. After the military conflicts of the 1860s and 1870s, the Southern Cheyenne, Southern Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, Kiowa-Apache, Wichita, Caddo, and neighboring peoples lived on reservations in Indian Territory under agents who controlled rations, annuities, and access to land. The Plains buffalo had been largely destroyed by the late 1870s, as Chapter 81 describes. The Dawes Act and related policies, including the sale of surplus lands, were moving toward the allotment of reservations in the territory, a process in which these nations later lost land. The Ghost Dance arrived in these years.

Among the Cheyenne and Arapaho, delegations visited Wovoka in 1889–1891, and Mooney recorded detailed accounts of how the movement developed. The Arapaho, in particular, took up the dance with great energy. Arapaho leaders, including the man known as Sitting Bull (the Arapaho Sitting Bull, not to be confused with the Hunkpapa Lakota leader), played prominent roles in bringing back and interpreting the teachings. The Arapaho developed an extensive body of songs. Cheyenne people took part as well, with Northern Cheyenne also participating in dances in Montana. The Arapaho and Cheyenne communities held dances regularly during the early 1890s, and Mooney attended some of them.

Local religious leaders combined Wovoka's teachings with existing traditions. For the Arapaho, who had a strong tradition of sacred objects and ceremonial leadership, the Ghost Dance was added to an existing set of religious institutions rather than replacing them. Visions received in the course of the dance were interpreted in light of established ways of understanding sacred experience. Songs drew on imagery drawn from the Plains world, animals, birds, wind, and the sacred directions, as well as from the central themes of the new teachings. Healing remained a major concern, as it had been in earlier practice, and some dancers and leaders became known for curing.

Among the Kiowa, Benjamin R. Kracht's research on Ghost Dance, Christianity, and peyote traditions shows a more complicated relationship between the dance and other religious options. The Kiowa had a rich ceremonial history, including the Sun Dance, which had become difficult or impossible to hold by around 1890 because of the loss of the bison and federal restrictions. The Kiowa also had Christian missionaries among them, and they were adopting peyote religion, which spread through Indian Territory in these decades. Kiowa people sent individuals to learn about the Ghost Dance, and the movement attracted a substantial number of participants, but it did not command the field. Some Kiowa leaders regarded the dance with skepticism, and as Kracht shows, many people moved among traditions or combined elements of them. The result was that the Ghost Dance coexisted with other forms of religious practice and was reshaped by that coexistence.

Comanche, Caddo, Wichita, and Pawnee communities also participated, and in some cases for much longer than elsewhere. The Caddo in particular maintained Ghost Dance traditions well into the twentieth century, and Mooney and later scholars noted the Caddo and Wichita as active participants. The communities differed in their degrees of reservation confinement, religious restriction, and economic pressure. Where agents were more tolerant, dances were more openly held. Where agents and missionaries were hostile, dancers might hold ceremonies in remote locations or on occasions when supervision was light. In the Southern Plains the government did not, on the whole, respond with a military mobilization like that in the Dakotas. That contrast is itself a historical fact needing explanation, and it supports the view that official reaction depended less on the dance itself than on the local administrative and political circumstances.

Section 89.9

Lakota Society after the Bison Collapse, Reservation Confinement, and Land Division

The Lakota, the western division of the Oyate known to outsiders as the Sioux, had been among the most powerful nations on the Plains. As Chapters 75 through 77 recounted, they had resisted U.S. expansion in Red Cloud's War and the Great Sioux War, negotiated the Treaty of Fort Laramie, and defended the Black Hills. By the end of the 1870s, however, military defeat, the seizure of the Black Hills in 1877, and the near-extinction of the bison had ended the autonomy of the camps and bands. By 1890 the Lakota lived on reservations carved from the Great Sioux Reservation, under agents who controlled the distribution of rations, regulated movement, supervised schools, and promoted farming and Christianity.

The bison economy had been the foundation of Lakota material and ceremonial life. Bison provided food, shelter, clothing, tools, and the occasion for communal hunts that organized social relations and reinforced authority. Their destruction, treated in Chapter 81, replaced a self-sufficient economy with dependence on government rations. Rations were supposed to be provided under treaty and agreement, but they were administered by agents with discretion, and reductions in the late 1880s caused hardship. Reports from agents, from missionaries, and from Lakota leaders in these years document hunger, and drought and crop failures in 1889 and 1890 made farming unreliable on the semi-arid reservations. Epidemics, including measles, influenza, and whooping cough, were reported in the same period and added to mourning in many families. The precise scale of these losses is difficult to determine from the surviving records and varies from agency to agency.

Land and political authority were also at issue. The Act of March 2, 1889, authorized the division of the Great Sioux Reservation into several smaller reservations, including Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, Cheyenne River, and Lower Brule, with Crow Creek set apart as well, and opened roughly nine million acres to non-Native settlement, according to many accounts. The commission that obtained the required Lakota signatures, headed by General George Crook, used persuasion, promises, and pressure, and many Lakota leaders opposed the agreement. The terms of the agreement, and the speed with which promised compensation proved inadequate, produced deep resentment. This is the land division treated in Chapter 86 in the broader context of allotment, and it greatly intensified the sense that the future held further loss. Reductions in rations followed shortly afterward, which Lakota leaders connected to the agreement.

The assimilation program also confronted Lakota religious life directly. Agents and missionaries discouraged the Sun Dance, the Lakota's most prominent communal ceremony, and the government's regulations on ceremonies applied. Christian churches, Catholic and Episcopal among them, had established missions on the reservations, and many Lakota people had become Christians, some while maintaining older practices. Boarding schools, the subject of Chapter 87, had taken many Lakota children away from their families. Lakota communities had responded in various ways. Some embraced aspects of the new order. Others held to older practices quietly. Many did both. Lakota people were not uniformly resistant or uniformly accommodating, a point that matters for understanding the divisions of 1890.

Against this background, the promise of the return of ancestors and the restoration of the buffalo carried profound significance. The dead were numerous, and the lost world of the hunt was within living memory for older people. A teaching that spoke to those losses did so at a level beyond political calculation. But the argument that material deprivation automatically produced the Ghost Dance among the Lakota should be avoided. Other nations that endured severe hardship did not adopt it, and some Lakota communities, and some Lakota individuals, did not. Moreover, the Ghost Dance first reached Lakota communities as a religious message with its own theological content, and the political conditions shaped how that message was received and adapted. A reasonable account recognizes both: a religious message with its own logic arrived in a time and place that made it matter intensely.

Section 89.10

Lakota Delegations to Wovoka: Kicking Bear, Short Bull, and the Transmission of the Message

News of the movement reached the Lakota through intertribal visiting. Accounts, including Mooney's and later studies by Raymond J. DeMallie and Rani-Henrik Andersson, indicate that in 1889 Lakota men traveled to Wind River in Wyoming, where Northern Arapaho and Shoshone communities were already discussing the teachings, and that Lakota delegations went on to Nevada in 1889 and 1890. The precise composition and chronology of the journeys are difficult to reconstruct, and the sources disagree on individual names and dates. Among those most often named are Kicking Bear, a Miniconjou Lakota associated with the Cheyenne River Reservation, and Short Bull, a Brulé Lakota associated with Rosebud. Other Lakota men, including Good Thunder and Mash-the-Kettle, appear in the accounts as participants in the delegations.

Lakota delegates visited Wovoka, heard his teaching, and took part in dances. They returned in 1890, and after reports to their communities, Lakota people began to dance. The role of councils, speeches, and instruction was considerable. Kicking Bear and Short Bull explained the teachings in public addresses, organized dances, and instructed participants in songs and ritual practice. Relationships mattered: Kicking Bear is reported to have visited the Grand River camp near Standing Rock in October 1890, and the ties of kinship and acquaintance among Lakota communities shaped how the movement spread from Cheyenne River and Rosebud to Pine Ridge and Standing Rock.

Lakota messengers interpreted the promise of renewal in the context of the Lakota people's own territorial and political losses. Reports of their speeches describe teachings about a transformed earth, the return of the dead, and the restoration of the bison, as well as a time of change expected in the near future, sometimes given as the spring of 1891. Such reports must be handled with care. Many of the most dramatic statements attributed to Kicking Bear and Short Bull survive only through Indian police, interpreters, federal agents, journalists, or later historians, many of whom had strong interests in emphasizing the threat. Some accounts that circulated in 1890 suggested that the whites would be destroyed or that the renewed earth would cover them. Other accounts of what the leaders said are more restrained. The historian's task is to ask for each report who recorded it, who interpreted it, and for what audience.

The Lakota emphasis can also be distinguished from what the surviving Nevada message attributed to Wovoka. The Lakota leaders' own elaborations, including statements that dancers might be protected, and the intensity of expectation about a specific date, appear to have been Lakota developments rather than components of the Nevada teaching. This does not make them inauthentic. It shows religious interpretation at work. Lakota leaders drew on Lakota ways of thinking about prophecy, vision, and sacred power and made the message meaningful in terms their communities could act on. Wovoka himself later reportedly told Mooney and others that he had not taught some of the doctrines attributed to him.

Lakota responses were not uniform. Some communities embraced the dance quickly. Others, including many Christian Lakota, held back. Some leaders were wary of the consequences of defying agents. These divisions are explored in Section 89.13. What is clear from the record is that Lakota people were not following orders from Nevada, and that their leaders made choices, about what to say, whom to teach, and how to relate to agency authority, within a situation of limited power.

Section 89.11

The Lakota Ghost Dance, Sacred Shirts, Protective Power, and Religious Innovation

Among the most distinctive features of the Lakota Ghost Dance was the use of specially made shirts and dresses, which many dancers wore and which some believed could protect them from harm, including bullets. The garments, often made of cloth and painted or decorated with symbols such as birds, stars, the moon, the sun, and other images, and sometimes with feathers or fringe, were produced in the context of the dance and typically blessed or consecrated in ceremony. Surviving examples, held today by museums, display a range of designs. They have drawn the attention of historians and the public more than almost any other aspect of the Ghost Dance, and they are central to the popular impression that the movement was warlike. That impression needs to be examined carefully.

The first necessary distinction is between Lakota religious practice and Wovoka's teachings. Wovoka did not originate the Lakota belief in the protective power of such garments. Mooney, who inquired about the matter, reported that the Nevada prophet's teachings did not include bullet-proof shirts, and later scholarship supports the view that the shirts were an adaptation developed among the Lakota. The protective shirts were not a universal requirement of the Ghost Dance. Southern Plains dancers, Shoshone and Bannock dancers, and Northern Paiute dancers generally did not wear them, though many dressed for the dance in special clothing and paint. This is a primary example of how the same movement could produce different material cultures in different communities.

The second distinction is between religious meaning and military purpose. For Lakota people, holy clothing was integrated with long-standing beliefs in sacred power, or wakan. Warriors had worn protective medicine, painted designs derived from visions, and sacred objects into battle for generations, and the designs on holy garments connected to the traditional repertoire of vision imagery. The belief that a sacred garment, properly made and consecrated, could protect a wearer is therefore best understood as a development within Lakota religious thought, reinterpreted in the setting of the dance, rather than a plan for armed resistance. Sacred clothing possessed religious meaning in itself. The belief did not make the Ghost Dance a military organization, and the evidence for a coordinated military plan among the dancers is weak. Some Lakota people may have anticipated defending themselves from violence if attacked. The historical record does not support the idea that the dancers were preparing a war.

The third issue concerns the interpretation of objects. Many surviving Ghost Dance shirts and dresses entered museum and private collections through purchase, trade, and seizure in circumstances shaped by coercion and by the crisis of 1890 and its aftermath, as Chapter 90 will note. Garments that were collected as curiosities or souvenirs often came with little reliable information about the maker, the owner, the occasion, or the meaning of the designs. Collectors' labels, including the claims they make about use and provenance, may be unreliable. Historians interpreting the iconography of these objects must therefore proceed carefully, relying on Lakota testimony and the expertise of descendant communities, who in recent decades have pursued the return of some objects, an issue addressed in Chapter 125 in the context of repatriation.

Interpretation of the garments also reveals the diversity of Lakota thought. Some accounts say that wearers were told they could be shot without harm, others that the shirts offered protection only if worn with proper faith and conduct. Not every Lakota dancer wore one, and not every Lakota religious leader endorsed the belief. Some Lakota people were skeptical, and the belief that the shirts could protect their wearers was later contested within Lakota communities when events seemed to contradict it. Students should be cautious, therefore, about generalizing the belief or about assuming that all participants held it with the same confidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Sacred Power, Syncretism, Ghost Dance Shirt, and Ritual Adaptation

Sacred power refers to spiritual force or potency recognized in many Indigenous traditions, which can be received through visions, held by people and objects, and used for healing, protection, or guidance. Among the Lakota the concept is often connected with wakan. Syncretism is a scholarly term for the combination of elements from different religious traditions. Ghost Dance shirt refers to the decorated garments worn by some Lakota dancers and associated with beliefs in protective power. Ritual adaptation refers to the modification of ceremonies to local meanings and circumstances.

Historical context: These concepts help distinguish Lakota innovations from the Nevada teaching and show how Indigenous religion operated as a creative process.

Why it matters: They allow historians to explain variation without implying that one version was more authentic than another.

Potential misunderstanding: "Syncretism" is sometimes used as though a "pure" tradition were corrupted by mixture. Historians of religion regard mixture and adaptation as normal features of living traditions.

Section 89.12

Women, Families, Elders, and Community Participation in the Ghost Dance

Narratives of the Ghost Dance often center on a handful of prominent men: Wovoka, Kicking Bear, Short Bull, Sitting Bull. These figures matter, but the movement was carried out by communities. Participation was broad and included women, children, elders, and men of many ages. Mooney and other observers noted that large numbers of people took part in dances, and that families attended together. The evidence is uneven, since most observers who wrote about dances were men, outsiders, and often hostile or condescending. But enough survives to show that the Ghost Dance was a collective practice.

Women participated in the circle and sang, and many reportedly entered visionary states. In several communities women were among those who experienced prominent visions, and their reports were attended to. Women prepared food for gatherings, made and decorated garments, cared for children and elderly relatives during long dances, and sustained the households that made extended dances possible. In Lakota communities, women made and painted dresses and shirts. These contributions are less visible in the sources because observers focused on spectacle and on male leaders, so careful reading is required to recover them.

The movement's significance for families who had experienced death, displacement, or separation is hard to overstate. Parents who had lost children to disease, families separated by removal or by the boarding school system, and elders who remembered the lost world of the hunt all had reasons to want the world renewed. A ceremony that promised reunion, held with kin and neighbors, could provide a framework for expressing those losses. Historians must be cautious in reconstructing individual emotions, but the structure of the teachings and the testimony of participants indicate that grief and hope were central.

Dances also related generations to one another. Elders brought knowledge of older songs, ceremonial practice, and the memory of earlier prophetic movements. Younger people, including those who had attended boarding schools and could speak English, often served as interpreters, letter writers, and intermediaries. These roles could generate tension, since returned students were sometimes regarded by agents as models of progress and by others as bridges to the old ways. The Ghost Dance thus involved age groups in different ways and illustrates how assimilation policies could produce, unintentionally, new forms of Indigenous communication.

Participation could strengthen community ties even when beliefs differed. Within families there were disagreements. Some relatives danced, and others did not. Some Christian Lakota attended dances while retaining church membership, and others condemned the dances. Neighboring groups held differing views. The Ghost Dance was thus a source of unity for those who adopted it and, in some places, a source of friction. A historian who assumes that a community acted as a single unit will mischaracterize this complexity.

Section 89.13

Lakota Political Divisions, Sitting Bull, and Competing Responses to the Ghost Dance

Lakota communities in 1890 were not united in their response to the Ghost Dance. Divisions that existed before the movement shaped how it was received, and the movement in turn sharpened them. Some Lakota people adopted the dance with conviction. Others remained skeptical, among them many who had become Christians, who had accepted the reservation economy, or who judged that the dance would invite punishment. Still others sought to avoid confrontation with federal authorities by moderating their participation or by moving their dances out of sight. These positions did not map neatly onto categories such as "traditional" and "progressive," which agents and journalists used freely and which obscure more than they reveal.

Sitting Bull (Tatanka Iyotake), a Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man, lived on the Grand River in the Standing Rock Reservation. He had led Lakota resistance in the 1870s, had gone into exile in Canada after the Great Sioux War, had returned and surrendered in 1881, and had spent years in dispute with federal officials, above all with the Standing Rock agent James McLaughlin. He had also remained a figure of exceptional standing, and he refused some of the agency's programs while selectively engaging others. He had even briefly toured with a Wild West show in the 1880s. His relationship to the Ghost Dance has been the subject of much historical debate, and it needs to be understood in terms of his own political and religious situation.

The evidence suggests that Sitting Bull was interested in the movement, that people who followed the Ghost Dance gathered in the area of his camp, and that he did not forbid them. Accounts differ on how enthusiastic he was and on how far he took part in leading the dance. Some reports describe him as skeptical about specific claims, and others as sympathetic. It is clear that agent McLaughlin saw Sitting Bull as the principal political obstacle on his reservation and was inclined to treat the dance as a vehicle of that opposition. It is equally clear that Sitting Bull's political authority and Wovoka's role as prophet were different things. Sitting Bull did not found or command the Ghost Dance. He was a Lakota leader whose relationship with the movement must be examined separately from his reputation, and not every Lakota participant followed his direction, nor did every follower of his join the dance.

Reservation police further complicated the picture. The Indian police, many of them Lakota, were employees of the agency, paid and armed, and they formed a body whose members had mixed loyalties. Some officers regarded the dance with suspicion, some were kin to dancers, and some felt obliged to carry out agency orders at any cost. Lakota Christian leaders, mission-educated men, and others also expressed varied views. Agents in turn differed. At Pine Ridge, the new agent Daniel Royer, who had little experience with the Lakota, was soon alarmed, and his reports to Washington stressed the danger. Earlier in the decade, the experienced agent Valentine McGillycuddy had taken a more measured view of religious gatherings, a contrast that illustrates the importance of individual officials' judgment. Other agents considered the dance harmless or likely to burn itself out.

These disagreements matter for the argument of this chapter. They show that there was no single "Lakota response," and that the escalating crisis was not an outcome that dancing alone made unavoidable. People in the same community weighed risks differently, and decisions by individual officials and individual leaders affected whether tension rose or fell. The historical record also shows a repeated pattern in which those who believed the dance dangerous drew inferences from the number of dancers and from rumors rather than from observed hostility.

Section 89.14

Indian Agents, Missionaries, and the Government Interpretation of Indigenous Religion

Reservation officials assessed the Ghost Dance through a layered administrative apparatus. An agent on each reservation wrote to the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, who reported to the Secretary of the Interior. Agents drew on information from Indian police, interpreters, missionaries, traders, and informants, and sometimes from their own attendance at dances. Missionaries wrote their own reports and exerted their own pressure. Letters, telegrams, and reports crossed constantly, and what reached Washington was a selection. The Office of Indian Affairs, to which these reports went, was responsible both for the welfare of the people under its authority and for the promotion of assimilation.

The language of these accounts shows the framework of interpretation. The Ghost Dance appeared in agency correspondence under terms such as "craze," "delusion," "superstition," "fanaticism," and "excitement," and in some reports as a precursor of "outbreak." Such language reflected a widespread assumption that Indigenous religion was a form of error to be replaced, and a belief that ceremonial excitement could be dangerous. It was also tied to the administrative regulation of ceremonies already in place. By treating dancing as incompatible with farming and progress, agents defined an assembly of dancers as a failure to work. Several agents also read the dance as a threat to their own authority, since large gatherings occurred outside their control.

Not all officials shared this view, and the record contains dissenting opinions. Some agents, and some observers who visited dances, concluded that the gatherings were peaceful and the teachings were religious, and some advised against the use of force. The Southern Plains agents, as noted in Section 89.8, mostly did not call for military intervention. But the voices of alarm carried the greater weight in the key months, especially on the Sioux reservations, where the history of war, the recent land agreement, and the fear of settlers shaped the response.

One of the most influential documents is the report of Standing Rock agent James McLaughlin of October 17, 1890. McLaughlin, who had been at Standing Rock since 1881 and had a long record of conflict with Sitting Bull, described the dance as he understood it, interpreted what he regarded as its religious content in terms of Lakota beliefs, and expressed concern about its effect on Standing Rock's population and about Sitting Bull's role. The Minnesota Historical Society holds McLaughlin's relevant correspondence collections, which allow historians to compare the report with other letters. The report is especially valuable precisely because it shows how an experienced agent assembled information from Indigenous intermediaries and shaped it to serve administrative purposes.

Reading such a document requires attention to its structure. Some claims are descriptions of things the author observed or was told, such as that dances took place at certain locations. Some are inferences, such as claims about the intentions of the dancers or about Sitting Bull's aims. Some reflect the author's interests: McLaughlin had reasons to want Sitting Bull removed, to persuade his superiors that the situation required action, and to show that he was in control. Much of the information came through Lakota intermediaries, including Indian police, whose own positions and interests may have influenced what they reported. A careful reading separates these layers.

Section 89.15

Newspapers, Public Panic, Federal Decision-Making, and the Deployment of Troops

By the autumn of 1890 the Ghost Dance had become a national news story. Newspapers in the Dakotas, Nebraska, Minnesota, and the major eastern cities carried reports of the "Messiah craze," and correspondents were dispatched to the reservations. Many had little knowledge of Lakota religion or language and relied on agents, officers, interpreters, settlers, and one another. Stories often featured sensational headlines and drew on rumors of imminent outbreak. The Library of Congress's historical newspaper collections, including those digitized in Chronicling America, make it possible to compare headlines and descriptions from late 1890, and doing so shows how quickly a religious gathering could be recast as a menace.

The press did not invent the fear, which was rooted in memories of the wars of the 1860s and 1870s, in the settlers' anxiety on the borders of the newly reduced reservations, and in the real concerns of officials. But newspapers amplified it. Stories circulated from reporters to settlers, territorial officials, Indian agents, the Interior Department, and military officers, and then back again, with each repetition adding authority to claims whose origin was often a single uncertain report. Accounts of Lakota speeches in particular passed through translation and compression and then were published as direct statements. Few newspapers carried the voices of dancers themselves, and fewer still described the dances as religious ceremonies.

Within the administration, alarm rose through the fall. Agent Royer at Pine Ridge requested military protection in November. Other agents reported on dances and on the possibility of trouble. Officials in Washington weighed these reports against the views of agents who considered the dance harmless. In the same period the Interior Department and the War Department negotiated their respective responsibilities. On November 13, 1890, President Benjamin Harrison ordered troops to the Sioux reservations, acting amid requests for military protection and consultations among Interior and War Department officials. Dispatches and troop arrivals followed during the second half of November; Brigadier General John R. Brooke directed forces in the field, while Major General Nelson A. Miles exercised higher regional command. The precise sequence of agency recommendations, interdepartmental consultations, and individual deployments requires distinguishing the November 13 presidential decision from later troop movements. Mooney's published documentary notes also identify Harrison's November 13 order. The escalation was therefore a series of administrative and military steps, not a single event around November 20.

The arrival of the troops did not defuse the situation. Several hundred, and then several thousand, soldiers were eventually deployed in the region. For Lakota communities, whose experience of soldiers was of war and coercion, their presence was frightening. Some people who had been dancing near agencies moved to more remote places, such as a refuge area in the badlands, in part because they feared arrest or violence. Others came in to the agencies to demonstrate peaceful intentions. Soldiers and agents in turn read the movement of Lakota people as evidence of hostile intent. Surveillance, rumors, and uncertainty fed one another. The dynamic is characteristic of situations in which armed force is introduced before intentions are understood, and it was recognized by some contemporaries, including a few officers and agents, who warned against escalation.

The point for this chapter is the direction of causation. The Ghost Dance did not itself create a military emergency. Officials, driven by alarm, by incomplete information, by institutional interests, and by their own assumptions about Indigenous religion, defined the dance as an emergency, and the response they chose created conditions in which violence became more likely. This is not to say that no Lakota person contemplated resistance or that no dancer spoke defiantly. It is to say that the evidence for a planned uprising is weak and that the decision to mobilize troops was a political choice, with consequences that Chapter 90 will examine.

Cause and Effect

How Religious Misinterpretation, Administrative Conflict, and Military Mobilization Escalated the Crisis

Long-term causes

A history of war and coercion between the United States and the Lakota; the framework of reservation administration; official hostility to Indigenous ceremonies; settler anxiety; and the division of the Great Sioux Reservation.

Short-term causes

Rapid spread of the dance in 1890; alarmed agency reports; press sensationalism; disputes between agents and Lakota leaders, especially at Standing Rock; and conflicts within the federal administration.

Immediate trigger

Requests for military protection from agents and the November 1890 decision to place the situation in the hands of the army, with troops arriving at the agencies.

Immediate effects

Fear among Lakota communities; movement of some groups away from agencies; heightened suspicion among soldiers and settlers; polarization within Lakota communities.

Long-term consequences

The violence of December 1890 (Chapter 90); further suppression of ceremonies; and a durable public image of the Ghost Dance as a war religion.

Historical complexity

The escalation resulted from decisions by multiple actors, not from the dance alone, and alternatives existed, as shown by agents who advised patience and by the nonviolent course of the movement elsewhere.

Why this cannot be reduced to a single cause: Religion, administration, press, politics, and military institutions interacted. No one of them explains the crisis.

Section 89.16

December 1890: The Death of Sitting Bull and the Road toward Wounded Knee

This section concludes the immediate narrative of the Ghost Dance crisis and establishes the transition to Chapter 90. It does not retell the massacre, its casualties, burials, or the military controversies that followed. Those subjects belong to the next chapter.

In the first half of December, Agent McLaughlin and military authorities at Standing Rock were preoccupied with the prospect of arresting Sitting Bull. Arrangements involved the agency's Indian police, with the army available nearby. Accounts differ on the planning, including on whether the arrest was to be made by police alone and on what contingency measures were taken. Early on December 15, 1890, Indian police officers, led by Lieutenant Bull Head, entered Sitting Bull's cabin on the Grand River to arrest him. A crowd of his supporters gathered. In the confrontation that followed, shots were fired, and Sitting Bull was killed, along with several of his followers, including his son Crowfoot, and several members of the police. The details of who fired first are disputed in the sources, which come mostly from participants and from officials with interests in the outcome. What is not in dispute is that a Lakota leader was killed while in the custody of agency police during an arrest sought by the agent, and that the event sent shock through Lakota communities.

The killing deepened fear and division. Many of Sitting Bull's followers fled Standing Rock, some going to join Miniconjou communities on the Cheyenne River. Others were pursued or watched by troops. In the days that followed, the authorities directed attention to other Lakota groups regarded as potentially hostile. Chief among those was the community led by Spotted Elk, commonly called Big Foot, a Miniconjou leader on the Cheyenne River Reservation. Spotted Elk had been active as a negotiator and was reportedly ill. His community, which included many women, children, and elders, had experience of both the dance and the pressures of the period, and their movements in late December were followed closely by the army.

The people who left the agencies in these weeks were responding to fear as well as to religious conviction. Some were seeking safety. Some were attempting to reach other Lakota communities, and some were attempting to reach the Pine Ridge Agency, where they hoped to find protection or negotiation. Soldiers and officers interpreted such movements as signs of intent to resist. The record shows a recurring gap between what Lakota people said they were doing and what officials believed. The record also contains efforts by Lakota leaders, including some who did not dance, to mediate between the communities and the army.

On December 28, 1890, a detachment of the Seventh Cavalry intercepted Spotted Elk's community as it traveled toward Pine Ridge and escorted the group to an encampment beside Wounded Knee Creek. The wider cavalry force assembled there by nightfall. The massacre took place the next day, December 29. Keeping these dates separate distinguishes the decision to intercept and confine the travelers from what happened during the following morning's attempted disarmament. The circumstances, casualties, contested testimony, and aftermath of the massacre belong to Chapter 90 and are not narrated here.

Historical responsibility requires two cautions. First, the events of December 1890 should not be presented as the inevitable outcome of the Ghost Dance, or as proof that it was a war religion. Between the dance and the killings stood a series of decisions: the agents' requests for troops, the military mobilization, the plan to arrest Sitting Bull, and the choices made by officers and soldiers on the ground. Second, the fact that the dance was associated with the crisis should not obscure that the great majority of Ghost Dance communities, on the Southern Plains and in the Great Basin and among most Lakota, never experienced violence and continued their religious practices. The Ghost Dance and the massacre are connected in history, but they are not the same history.

Indigenous Religious Continuity, Historical Evidence, and the Legacy of the Ghost Dance

Section 89.17

Other Indigenous Religious Renewal Movements: Dreamers, Indian Shakers, and Peyote Traditions

The Ghost Dance was one of several Indigenous religious renewal movements of the late nineteenth century. Placing it in this wider setting corrects the impression that revitalization was a single event confined to western Nevada and South Dakota, and it shows that religious creativity was a widespread feature of Indigenous modernity. It is essential, however, not to treat the other traditions as branches of the Ghost Dance. Each had its own Indigenous foundations, sacred practices, and history, even where the broader conditions and certain themes overlapped.

On the Columbia Plateau, the Wanapum religious teacher Smohalla, who lived at Priest Rapids on the Columbia River, taught what became known as the Dreamer or Washani tradition. Smohalla taught that the earth was a mother who should not be wounded by plowing and that Indigenous people should hold to their own ways of living, and his teaching emphasized dreams and the maintenance of ceremonial practice. Dreamer communities, including non-treaty bands, rejected reservations and the agricultural program associated with them, and Dreamer teachings were part of the religious setting of resistance in the Plateau in the 1870s, including among Nez Perce people, as discussed in Chapter 78. The Dreamer religion was not a precursor of Wovoka's teaching and did not depend on it, but both movements shared a conviction that Indigenous ways should be maintained and that spiritual power could guide a people through catastrophe. Dreamer practice continued after the 1890 crisis and survives in forms that are still practiced on the Plateau.

In the Pacific Northwest, the Indian Shaker movement began in the early 1880s on Puget Sound among Coast Salish people. Its origin is associated with John Slocum, a Squaxin Island man who is reported to have died and returned to life in 1881 or 1882 and to have received instruction to build a church, and with his wife, Mary Thompson Slocum, whose trembling or shaking is associated with the movement's healing practice. The Indian Shaker Church combined elements of Indigenous healing traditions, including the use of spiritual power to diagnose and cure illness, with Christian forms such as a church building, bells, candles, and prayer. Its adherents experienced harassment from some agents and missionaries. A congregation of approximately forty adherents formally organized the church at Mud Bay on June 7, 1892; formal incorporation under Washington State law came separately in December 1910. Organization and state incorporation must not be treated as one event. This history exemplifies an Indigenous religious institution that adapted Christian forms while remaining distinct from missionary churches, a path somewhat different from that of the Ghost Dance.

On the Southern Plains, peyote religion was developing in the same decades. The ritual use of peyote, a small spineless cactus, by Indigenous peoples in northern Mexico and the Rio Grande region is of long standing, and in the later nineteenth century a ceremonial complex, centering on an all-night ceremony, prayer, and the sacramental use of peyote, spread among Kiowa, Comanche, Apache, and other peoples of Indian Territory. As Benjamin Kracht's research shows, the Kiowa case illustrates how peyote, the Ghost Dance, and Christianity were available to people as religious options, and how individuals and families moved among them. Peyote religion grew in the following decades into a major intertribal religious tradition, later institutionalized in the twentieth century, a subject for later volumes.

The four traditions, Ghost Dance, Dreamer, Shaker, and peyote, can be compared in terms of prophecy, healing, ceremonial practice, political authority, and responses to colonial interference. Prophecy was central to Ghost Dance and Dreamer teaching, while healing was central to the Shaker and peyote traditions, although all of them involved both. Political authority differed: Dreamer teachers were connected with communities that resisted reservation life, while Shaker and peyote adherents often sought legal accommodation. Responses to colonial interference ranged from refusal to adaptation. These differences show that Indigenous religious innovation did not follow one pattern. What the movements shared was a basic fact of the period: Indigenous people were building new religious institutions, often in the face of official opposition, as part of their efforts to maintain community life.

Section 89.18

Religious Survival after 1890: Suppression, Adaptation, and Continuing Ceremonial Traditions

The Ghost Dance did not disappear after December 1890. This basic point is sometimes obscured by the dramatic character of the Wounded Knee Massacre, which is often presented as the end of the movement. In fact, dances continued in many communities. Mooney reported that the dance remained active when he prepared his major study in the 1890s, and later scholarship documents additional adaptations and continuities in several regions. The timing and form of continuation differed from place to place.

On the Lakota reservations, the aftermath of the violence brought heavy pressure. Public dancing became dangerous, and many dancers stopped, withdrew, or went underground. Agents and the army kept watch, and some leaders were arrested or jailed. The fear generated by the events of 1890 made open ceremony hazardous for years. Yet the evidence does not indicate that the religious ideas simply vanished. Songs, memories of visions, and relationships formed in the dance continued, and some Lakota people continued to hold the dance in private or in modified form. For others, the experience of the massacre reshaped their understanding of the teachings, and in many families it became part of the memory of loss.

Among other nations the government's response was less severe, and dances continued openly for longer. Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, Caddo, Wichita, Pawnee, and other communities in Indian Territory held Ghost Dances through the 1890s and into the early twentieth century, adapting songs, schedules, and organization. Caddo communities maintained the tradition for especially long, and in some communities elements persisted for generations. In the Great Basin, the Ghost Dance continued among Northern Paiute, Shoshone, and Bannock communities, and in California the traditions that had developed from the 1870 movement continued to evolve. Wovoka himself lived until 1932 and continued to receive visitors for years, though the movement's national visibility declined.

Government policy played an important role in the decline of public ceremony. Restrictions on Indigenous religious practices remained in force, in different forms, into the twentieth century, as Chapters 95 and 97 will discuss. Agents continued to discourage dances. Allotment, schooling, and the pressures of wage labor made community gatherings harder to sustain. The expected transformation that some Ghost Dance teaching had predicted did not occur, and some followers reinterpreted or revised their expectations. For some the failure of specific predictions weakened the movement's appeal. For others, teachings about the renewal of the world, the return of the dead, and proper conduct continued to carry meaning and were reinterpreted as hopes for a future beyond the present generation, or as religious principles that did not depend on a particular date.

The distinction between a movement's declining political visibility and the disappearance of its religious teachings is vital. A movement that has stopped attracting national attention may continue as a ceremonial tradition within communities. Commemorative and renewed uses of Ghost Dance traditions occurred later in the twentieth century and into the present, in forms that belong to the later volumes of this textbook, including the Indigenous activism and memory work discussed in Chapters 112 and 132. This chapter introduces them only briefly. Students should keep in mind that the Ghost Dance, like other Indigenous ceremonial traditions that officials expected to disappear, survived through adaptation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Revitalization before, during, and after the Crisis of 1890

What Changed

  • The scale and geography of the movement, from regional (1870) to intertribal (1889–1890)
  • Local forms, including Lakota sacred shirts and varied Southern Plains songs and schedules
  • The visibility of the dance to outsiders, which rose sharply in 1890 and then fell
  • The expectations attached to it, as specific predictions went unfulfilled and were reinterpreted
  • Government response, from restrictions on ceremonies in the 1880s to military mobilization in 1890 to continued surveillance afterward

What Stayed the Same

  • The central themes of reunion with the dead, the renewal of the world, and ethical conduct
  • The circle dance as ceremonial form, with song, prayer, and communal participation
  • The role of vision and sacred power in Indigenous religious life
  • The use of kinship and intertribal networks for transmission

Pace and experience of change: Change was uneven. Lakota communities experienced the most abrupt disruption, while many Southern Plains and Great Basin communities experienced gradual adaptation. Participants who lost relatives in 1890 experienced rupture. Those whose dances continued quietly experienced continuity.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that revitalization was a long process and not a brief episode, and that the crisis of 1890 is only one chapter in it.

Section 89.19

James Mooney, Indigenous Testimony, Historical Photographs, and the Interpretation of Ghost Dance Sources

No historian of the Ghost Dance can avoid James Mooney (1861–1921), an ethnologist employed by the Bureau of American Ethnology, a federal research body associated with the Smithsonian Institution. Mooney, who had earlier worked among the Cherokee, was sent in 1890 to investigate the movement. Between 1890 and 1893 he visited a number of Indigenous communities, interviewed religious leaders, attended dances, consulted government documents, and collected songs. He traveled to Nevada in January 1892 to meet Wovoka. The results appeared in The Ghost-Dance Religion and the Sioux Outbreak of 1890, printed in the Bureau's Fourteenth Annual Report. The title page is dated 1896, though some modern catalogues list the work under 1897.

Mooney's study was unusual for its time in several respects. It took Indigenous religious ideas seriously, treating them as intelligible responses to circumstances. It compared the Ghost Dance with other religious movements, including Christian ones, and implicitly challenged the idea that Indigenous prophecy was a pathology. It also criticized government policy, and in that sense it was an early attempt to explain the crisis from a point of view other than the army's. It remains the most extensive early source. Subsequent scholarship cannot be understood without it.

The study also has limitations that students must grasp. Mooney was an outsider, employed by the federal government, whose questions and categories reflected the ethnology of his day, including evolutionary assumptions that placed Indigenous religions in a lower stage. He relied on interpreters and on selected informants, and he did not have command of most of the languages involved. His account of the 1870 movement was retrospective. His narrative of the Sioux "outbreak" drew on official sources and on the testimony of those who spoke to him in the aftermath of the violence, when they had reasons for caution. He selected, arranged, and edited what he published. Nor did he present everything as he had received it: his texts of songs and messages are translations and edited versions.

The sound recordings require special attention. In July 1894 Mooney made phonograph recordings of Ghost Dance songs, which are held by the Library of Congress. The Library notes that these recordings were likely sung by Mooney himself rather than being direct recordings of Indigenous ceremonial performers. They are therefore historical representations of the songs, as an ethnologist remembered and performed them, and they cannot be treated as recordings of the original 1890 ceremonies. Students should not assume that a recording of a Ghost Dance song made in 1894 preserves how dancers sounded in 1890. A related caution applies to photographs. Some images labeled as Ghost Dance scenes were posed, staged for a photographer, or taken at moments unrelated to the ceremony, and captions added later may be unreliable. Indigenous pictorial sources, such as the Ghost Dance pictograph reproduced by North Dakota Studies and associated with Stone Man, offer another viewpoint and allow comparison with government reports and newspaper illustrations. Sacred objects, ceremonial photographs, and recordings also raise questions of cultural protocol, and they should be reproduced or circulated only with attention to provenance and to the wishes of descendant communities.

Students should therefore learn to distinguish different kinds of evidence: field observation, in which an observer describes what he saw; interpreted testimony, in which an informant's words are filtered through an interpreter; translated song, in which a text is rendered from one language into another; photograph, in which an image preserves a moment but also a photographer's decisions; and retrospective scholarship, which synthesizes these sources and adds interpretation. A good historical argument identifies which of these a given claim rests on.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnography, Oral Testimony, Transcription, Translation, and Source Mediation

Ethnography is the descriptive study of a people's way of life, typically through fieldwork. Oral testimony is information conveyed by speech, rather than written, by a participant or witness. Transcription is the conversion of speech or song into written form, and translation is the rendering of one language into another. Source mediation refers to the series of people and processes that stand between an original event or statement and the document a historian reads.

Historical context: Mooney's study is an ethnography. The statements in it by Indigenous informants are oral testimony that he transcribed and had translated.

Why it matters: Each layer of mediation can add, omit, or change meaning. Recognizing the layers allows historians to use the sources while stating their limits.

Potential misunderstanding: A published English "message" or "song text" can appear to be the speaker's own words. In the case of the Ghost Dance it is usually an edited rendering.

Section 89.20

The Ghost Dance as Religious Revitalization, Indigenous Resistance, and Historical Continuity

This concluding section draws together the movements of 1870 and 1889–1890 and evaluates the relationship between religious teaching and political conditions. The two movements shared a vocabulary of reunion with the dead and a renewed world, and a ceremonial form centered on the circle dance. They differed in scale, in the routes of transmission, in the communities they reached, and in the circumstances of reception. The first, associated with Wodziwob, spread through the Great Basin and California and fed enduring regional traditions. The second, associated with Wovoka, expanded through delegations and kinship networks to the Plains and Indian Territory and drew a national response. The two were connected by people, places, and themes, but neither was a mere copy of the other, and each must be explained in its own terms.

The relationship between religious teaching and political conditions is a theme running through this chapter. The conditions of the 1880s, including dispossession, confinement, hunger, grief, and restrictions on ceremony, gave the Ghost Dance its urgency and shaped the forms it took. But the content of the teaching, drawn from Indigenous traditions of vision and renewal, cannot be derived from those conditions. Communities in similar conditions made different choices. Where the dance was adopted, it was interpreted according to local religious ideas: among the Lakota, in terms of wakan and protective power; among the Southern Plains peoples, in terms of songs, healing, and other religious options, including peyote and Christianity; among the Shoshone and Bannock, in terms of identity and kinship across tribal boundaries. The movement thus provided frameworks of meaning, hope, mourning, identity, and renewal during profound historical disruption.

The role of government misunderstanding and coercive administrative power is equally central. The idea that the Ghost Dance was a precursor to war was the product of interpretation, by agents, newspapers, and military officers working within assumptions about Indigenous religion and the need to maintain control. Those assumptions did not arise from the dance. They arose from the structure of reservation administration and the history of settler colonialism. The result was that a religious movement whose documented core stressed peace was treated as an emergency, and the response created conditions for the violence of December 1890. It would be wrong, however, to treat the officials as acting from simple malice or as the only actors. They included individuals who counseled restraint, and the Lakota community included its own divisions.

Finally, the interpretation that Indigenous religion was merely an irrational response to suffering must be rejected. That view, once common, treats Indigenous prophetic beliefs as symptoms, while treating comparable expectations in other societies, such as Christian hopes for resurrection and a transformed world, as legitimate parts of history. A consistent historical method does not apply different standards. The Ghost Dance was an expression of Indigenous religious thought, with its own ethical teachings, theology, and ceremonial practice, and it belongs in the history of religion as well as in the history of colonialism.

Historical Debate

Was the Ghost Dance Principally a Religious Revitalization Movement, a Form of Anticolonial Resistance, or a Family of Locally Adapted Movements?

The question: Historians have long debated how best to characterize the Ghost Dance, and the question has consequences for how the crisis of 1890 is explained.

Interpretation A — Religious revitalization: Some scholars emphasize that the movement was fundamentally religious, rooted in Indigenous traditions of prophecy and ceremony, and aimed at renewal of the community's relationship with the sacred and with the dead. On this view, political meanings were secondary and largely supplied by outsiders. This approach also draws on the language of revitalization theory, which has been criticized for treating Indigenous movements as responses to cultural stress.
Interpretation B — Anticolonial resistance: Others stress that, in a setting of conquest and religious suppression, practicing a prohibited ceremony and anticipating a world without colonial domination was itself a form of resistance, even if nonviolent. Some scholars, including those who criticize the older view of the dance as a "desperate" reaction, nevertheless emphasize its significance as an assertion of Indigenous cultural autonomy.
Interpretation C — A family of locally adapted movements: A third approach, found in studies of particular nations, such as Smoak's work on the Shoshone and Bannock, Kracht's on the Kiowa, and studies of the Lakota by DeMallie, Andersson, and others, emphasizes variation. On this view, the Ghost Dance was a set of related religious practices, each with local meanings, and in which religion and political meaning were inseparable, since religion in Indigenous communities was already bound up with questions of land, kin, and authority.

Evidence: Participants' own accounts are limited and mediated. Wovoka's surviving message stresses peace and ethical conduct. Lakota sources show distinctive features, including sacred shirts. Agency records show official fear and interpretation. Sources from the Southern Plains and the Great Basin show different patterns of practice and official response.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely over interpretation and terminology, such as what counts as "resistance" and whether a religious practice that officials sought to suppress should be classified by its intent or its effect. There is also disagreement about evidence, especially the reliability of reports of Lakota speeches.

Why it matters: If the dance was primarily religious, then the crisis of 1890 was largely a product of official misreading. If it was a form of resistance, the government's alarm has to be understood differently. If it was a family of local movements, no single explanation will do, and historians must proceed community by community.

Student task: Select one community discussed in this chapter and evaluate which interpretation best fits the evidence for it. Then identify a type of evidence that would help test your conclusion.

The three interpretations are not strictly exclusive, and this chapter's own conclusion draws on all of them. The Ghost Dance was a religious revitalization movement in the sense that it renewed a people's relations with the sacred and the dead. It was a form of cultural assertion under colonial conditions in the sense that its performance affirmed Indigenous values and community in the face of efforts to suppress them. And it was a family of local movements in the sense that the meaning of the dance was shaped by the people who performed it. What it was not was a plan for war or a symptom of collective irrationality.

Chapter 90 turns to the events of December 29, 1890, at Wounded Knee Creek. Students should read it with the understanding that the Ghost Dance, as the preceding pages have shown, did not cause the massacre. The massacre came from decisions made by officials, officers, and soldiers who read a religious ceremony as a military threat. The religion itself, with its songs, its hope of reunion, and its call to live rightly, continued in the communities that had embraced it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Ghost Dance cannot adequately be explained as a desperate reaction to military defeat or as a movement inevitably leading to violence. It emerged from existing Indigenous religious traditions, developed through intertribal communication, acquired distinctive local meanings, and was interpreted by federal authorities through the assumptions of reservation administration and settler colonialism.

Division I established the religious foundations. Indigenous religious life was a living institution in which land, ancestors, vision, song, healing, and ceremony were interwoven, and prophecy and ceremonial renewal had deep roots before the nineteenth century. Epidemics, removals, reservation confinement, resource loss, and restrictions on ceremony gave renewal new urgency without by themselves producing the content of the teachings. The Northern Paiute prophet Wodziwob's Ghost Dance of c. 1869–1870s spread through the Great Basin and into California, where it fed durable regional traditions, and it fostered intertribal relationships that later scholarship, especially Smoak's, connects to the growth of broader identities.

Wovoka, a Northern Paiute religious practitioner of Mason Valley who was also known as Jack Wilson, became associated with the revelation traditionally dated to the eclipse of January 1, 1889. The core of the teaching attributed to him emphasized reunion with deceased relatives, a renewed world free from suffering, ethical conduct, and peaceful behavior, expressed through prayer, dance, feasting, and bathing. Christian terms appear in the surviving versions, and scholars disagree about how to weigh Indigenous and Christian sources. The best-known text, the message of 1891, survives only through a chain of oral communication, transcription, translation, and editorial presentation, and cannot be treated as Wovoka's own manuscript.

Division II examined ceremony and transmission. The dance was a communal ceremony of circle dancing, song, prayer, and visionary experience, performed with significant local variation. Participants regarded trance and vision as religious experience, while many outside observers described them in medicalized or dismissive terms. The teachings traveled through delegations, kinship, trade relationships, interpreters, and letters, and communities actively evaluated, interpreted, and modified them. The movement's reception among Northern Paiute, Shoshone, and Bannock communities, and its California connections, show that it was not simply a stage on the way to the Plains. The Southern Plains peoples, especially the Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, and Caddo, combined the teachings with their own songs, healing traditions, and religious options, including peyote and Christianity.

Division III turned to the Lakota. After the destruction of the bison economy, reservation confinement, ration reductions, epidemics, and the 1889 division of the Great Sioux Reservation, the Ghost Dance acquired distinctive meaning among Lakota communities. Delegations, including those associated with Kicking Bear and Short Bull, brought teachings home, and Lakota leaders elaborated them in terms of their own historical losses and their ideas about sacred power. Ghost Dance shirts and dresses, believed by some to protect wearers, were a Lakota adaptation, not a universal feature of the movement and not a teaching of Wovoka. They had religious meaning and did not make the dance a military organization. The movement was a collective practice involving women, families, and elders, whose contributions are underrepresented in the sources.

Division IV explained how a religious movement became a perceived emergency. Lakota communities were divided in their responses. Sitting Bull, a Hunkpapa leader in long conflict with agent McLaughlin, was neither the founder nor the commander of the dance. Agents, missionaries, and the Office of Indian Affairs read the gatherings through a framework of superstition, fanaticism, and potential outbreak. The press amplified rumors, and in November 1890 federal authorities, including President Harrison, authorized military involvement, with troops arriving at Pine Ridge and Rosebud. The killing of Sitting Bull on December 15, 1890, and the movement of frightened Lakota communities set the stage for the confrontation at Wounded Knee Creek on December 29, the subject of Chapter 90. The escalation resulted from decisions by many actors and was not an inevitable outcome of the religion.

Division V situated the Ghost Dance in the wider history of Indigenous religious renewal. The Dreamer (Washani) tradition of Smohalla, the Indian Shaker movement associated with John Slocum, and the peyote traditions of the Southern Plains were distinct movements with their own foundations, and they demonstrate that religious creativity was a widespread feature of Indigenous modernity. After 1890, the Ghost Dance persisted in many communities in adapted forms, even as public participation declined under suppression and as some expectations were reinterpreted. The distinction between declining political visibility and the disappearance of religious teaching is central.

The chapter also stressed the problem of evidence. The principal early synthesis, James Mooney's study published in the Bureau of American Ethnology's Fourteenth Annual Report (title page 1896), combines careful fieldwork and sympathy with the limits of an outsider's perspective and the assumptions of his time. His 1894 song recordings, according to the Library of Congress, were likely sung by Mooney himself. Agency reports such as McLaughlin's October 1890 report reveal official interpretation more than Lakota belief. Photographs, objects, newspapers, and later recollections all require attention to authorship, translation, and mediation.

Historiographically, the Ghost Dance has been described as a religious revitalization movement, a form of anticolonial resistance, and a family of locally adapted movements in which religion and politics were inseparable. This chapter's synthesis draws on all three while rejecting the view that Indigenous religion was an irrational reaction to suffering, a standard that is not applied to comparable hopes in other societies.

The chapter's central contribution is to restore the movement's religious, ceremonial, and regional history, so that the Ghost Dance is not reduced to its association with a massacre. Its significance lies in Indigenous religious creativity, collective identity, and cultural continuity under conditions of dispossession and assimilation, themes that continue through the later chapters of this volume.

Chapter Reflection

The Ghost Dance matters because it shows Indigenous people acting as religious thinkers in the middle of conquest. Facing loss on a scale that official policy treated as a transition to civilization, communities across a large part of the continent gathered, sang, prayed, and worked out together what the world could still become. That creativity is easily overlooked when the movement is remembered chiefly through its association with a massacre.

The chapter also shows how interpretation can become a historical force. The meaning officials assigned to a ceremony shaped what they did, and what they did shaped what happened. The study of the Ghost Dance is therefore as much a study of how power reads culture as it is a study of a religion, and it prepares students for the documentary problems of the chapters that follow.

Later developments drew on the continuities the movement revealed. Indigenous communities maintained ceremonial traditions under restriction, developed new religious institutions, and later pressed for religious freedom and the return of sacred objects. These themes appear in Chapter 97 and the chapters on repatriation, memory, and cultural resurgence in Volume X.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter reinforces an argument made in Chapter 1: that Indigenous history did not end with conquest, that Indigenous peoples were historical actors, and that the evidence for their history must be read with attention to who produced it. The Ghost Dance was one expression of that continuing history, and Chapter 90 turns to the violence that has often overshadowed it.
