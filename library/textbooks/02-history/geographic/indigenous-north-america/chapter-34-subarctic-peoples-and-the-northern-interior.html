Subarctic Peoples and the Northern Interior

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

The northern interior of North America was not an empty wilderness crossed by scattered people in search of food. It was a known, named, and politically organized landscape in which movement itself was a skill, a form of knowledge, and a way of holding rights and relationships across distances measured in hundreds of kilometers.

High in the mountains of the southern Yukon, small permanent patches of ice and snow have been shrinking in recent decades. As they melt, they release objects that were frozen into them long ago: dart shafts, fragments of arrows, and layers of caribou dung. The oldest of these finds is thought to date back some 9,000 years. Caribou climb to such patches in summer to escape heat and biting insects, and the objects show that hunters understood this and returned to the same high places across many generations. Archaeological work at these sites has been carried out in partnership with Yukon First Nations, whose own knowledge of the mountains and of caribou adds to what the preserved weapons alone can show.

The ice patches offer an opening image for this chapter because they illustrate its central subject: the accumulated knowledge that allowed people to live well in a demanding environment of long winters, shifting animal populations, and vast distances. That knowledge was not static. The weapons recovered from the ice record technological change, including a shift from throwing darts to bows and arrows roughly twelve centuries ago. But they also record a striking continuity of purpose and place. The northern interior has a very deep human history, and the people who lived there were never outside history.

This chapter studies the Indigenous peoples of the Subarctic and the northern interior: the Cree, Innu, and Naskapi peoples, who speak Algonquian languages, and the many Dene or Athabaskan-speaking peoples, including the Dënesųłıné, Tłı̨chǫ, Sahtu, Dehcho, Dane-zaa, Tse'khene, Gwich'in, Hän, Tutchone, Kaska, Koyukon, Ahtna, Tanana, and others. Their homelands stretch from the interior of Labrador and Quebec, across the Hudson Bay and James Bay lowlands, the Canadian Shield lake country, the Mackenzie basin, and the Yukon uplands, into interior Alaska. This is one of the largest continuous forest and lake regions on Earth, and it was occupied by peoples who spoke languages as different from one another as English is from Mandarin.

The chapter advances two arguments. The first is that mobility was a sophisticated strategy rather than a sign of rootlessness. Families and communities moved through known territories according to seasonal cycles, maintained rights and relationships across enormous landscapes, and connected the forests, river systems, tundra margins, Plains, Arctic, Great Lakes, and Pacific Coast through trade, marriage, diplomacy, and shared knowledge. The second argument is that low population density does not mean low historical complexity. Northwest Coast societies, examined in Chapter 33, expressed complexity through permanent villages, ranked houses, and concentrated resource ownership. Northern interior societies expressed it differently: in enormous geographic knowledge, flexible kin networks, transportation technology, seasonal coordination, and political relationships maintained over great distances.

The evidence for this history is uneven and requires care. Archaeology, historical linguistics, geology, and the oral traditions of northern communities provide the main windows into the centuries before sustained European contact. Written sources begin in the seventeenth century in the east and much later in the far northwest, and they were produced by outsiders, usually missionaries, traders, and explorers. Detailed ethnographic descriptions, on which many textbook generalizations about northern life rest, mostly date from the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Reading these later sources back into earlier periods is a persistent temptation that this chapter tries to resist.

Older writing often described northern peoples as "nomadic hunters" and treated the Subarctic as a marginal place whose history began with the fur trade. This chapter rejects that framing. Indigenous trade networks carried copper, stone, shell, hides, and foods across the interior long before French or English traders arrived. European commerce entered and transformed those systems rather than creating them. The chapter therefore ends at the threshold of the commercial transformations examined in Chapter 46, rather than narrating them.

The chapter is bounded by its neighbors in the textbook. Chapter 11 owns the deep-time archaeology of northern settlement, which is bridged here but not repeated. Chapter 33 treats the Northwest Coast, Chapter 35 the Inuit, Yupik, Unangan, and Arctic maritime worlds, Chapter 27 the Great Lakes and Anishinaabe peoples, and Chapter 29 the Plains before the horse. Where northern interior peoples interacted with those regions, the interaction appears here, but the neighboring societies are not absorbed into this chapter. Métis ethnogenesis belongs to Chapter 52, and the numbered treaties to Chapter 82.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geographic extent and environmental variety of the Subarctic and northern interior, and explain why it cannot be treated as environmentally uniform.
  2. Distinguish Algonquian-speaking northern peoples from Athabaskan-speaking Dene peoples, and explain why "Athabaskan" and "Dene" are not perfect synonyms.
  3. Explain why no single Cree, Dene, or Innu polity existed, and why terms such as "band" can mislead when applied to flexible northern communities.
  4. Compare the principal regional worlds treated in this chapter, from Labrador to interior Alaska, in terms of resources, travel routes, and political relationships.
  5. Analyze seasonal mobility as an organized system of territorial knowledge rather than random wandering.
  6. Evaluate the importance of caribou while explaining why fish, moose, hare, waterfowl, and plant foods were equally essential in many districts.
  7. Assess northern technology, including snowshoes, toboggans, watercraft, nets, clothing, and shelters, as accumulated knowledge.
  8. Interpret forms of leadership and kinship without forcing them into the categories of either centralized chiefdoms or timeless bands.
  9. Trace the exchange routes that linked the northern interior to the Northwest Coast, Arctic, Plains, and Great Lakes before sustained European commerce.
  10. Evaluate the strengths and limits of archaeological, linguistic, oral, ethnographic, and early documentary evidence for northern history.
  11. Connect the northern interior to the larger continental history developed across this textbook.

Historical Context

The northern interior was opened to human settlement as the great continental ice sheets retreated. Deglaciation proceeded unevenly: parts of the western interior were available to people earlier than the Hudson Bay lowlands and the Labrador plateau, which were among the last areas to be uncovered, over a span of several thousand years. Chapter 11 examines the human adaptations of this deep past, including early settlement, hunting systems, and technological innovation. This chapter picks up from that foundation and asks how the long history of northern adaptation connects to the peoples named in later historical sources, without assuming that any ancient archaeological tradition corresponds directly to a particular modern nation.

By the centuries immediately before sustained European contact, the northern interior was home to peoples organized in small, flexible local groups tied together by kinship, marriage, shared language, and recurring seasonal gatherings. They depended on a mixture of caribou, moose, fish, waterfowl, small game, and plant foods, with the balance differing sharply from region to region. They moved by canoe, snowshoe, toboggan, and on foot over networks of lakes, rivers, portages, and trails. Their homelands adjoined those of the Anishinaabe peoples of the Great Lakes, the Plains peoples of the grassland edge, Inuit and other Arctic peoples along the tundra and coasts, and the Tlingit and other Northwest Coast peoples beyond the mountains.

Historians face two opposite errors in studying this region. The first is the image of the northern forest as a timeless, isolated hunting ground, "unchanged" until traders arrived. The second is to treat the fur trade as the beginning of northern history and to assume that the arrangements recorded by early traders were created by commerce. Neither is accurate. The region has a long record of technological change, population movement, climatic variation, and exchange. At the same time, European demand and European goods, which reached many northern communities before Europeans did, altered those older patterns in ways Chapter 46 examines.

A further problem is that the standard categories in older scholarship were created by outsiders. "Subarctic" is itself a twentieth-century scholarly convention that groups peoples by environment. "Band," "tribe," and "nomad" are labels from a nineteenth- and twentieth-century anthropological vocabulary that often froze flexible communities into artificial units. This chapter uses the names peoples use for themselves where those are established, identifies familiar exonyms where students will meet them in older sources, and treats the political organization of northern societies as a historical question rather than a settled classification.

Section 34.1

The Northern Interior as an Indigenous World

The zone examined in this chapter runs from the interior of Labrador and Quebec, west across the Hudson Bay and James Bay lowlands and the forests and lakes of the Canadian Shield, through the northern parts of the prairie provinces and the Mackenzie valley, into the mountains and river basins of the Yukon, and on to the interior of Alaska. Its southern edge meets the Great Lakes forests and the Plains parkland; its northern edge meets the treeless tundra of the Arctic coast; its western edge reaches the mountains that separate it from the Pacific Coast. Scholars call this environment the Subarctic, a twentieth-century convention for the boreal forest and its margins. The term is useful for organizing discussion, but it should not suggest that the region possessed a single culture or a single history.

The peoples of the region had their own names for their homelands, and those names express a very different geography from that of modern maps. The Innu call their homeland Nitassinan, "our land." The East Cree speak of Eeyou Istchee, "the people's land." Many Dene use Denendeh, "the land of the people." Each term describes a territory defined by use, relationship, and responsibility rather than by a surveyed boundary. In each case the land was known through named rivers, lakes, portages, hills, crossings, fishing places, and camps, so that a landscape that European observers sometimes described as trackless was, to the people who used it, thickly marked with meaning.

Rivers and lakes provided the principal highways. The Mackenzie flows north to the Arctic Ocean; the Yukon flows west to the Bering Sea; the Churchill, Nelson, and Saskatchewan systems flow east toward Hudson Bay; and many smaller rivers drain the Labrador plateau toward the Atlantic and the St. Lawrence. A traveler who knew the portages could cross from one great drainage to another. This network of water is the reason the northern interior could be a connected world, and it explains why exchange and diplomacy, treated in section 34.19, are structural themes of this chapter and not an afterthought.

Section 34.2

From Ancient Adaptations to Historically Identifiable Peoples

Chapter 11 describes the long northern record of settlement, hunting systems, mobility, and technological innovation. The question this section addresses is different: how do historians connect that deep past to the named peoples who appear in later sources? Archaeologists identify traditions or complexes on the basis of stone tools, hearths, camp layouts, and other material remains. Examples include the Shield Archaic in the eastern and central Canadian Shield and the Taltheilei tradition, dated from roughly 2,500 years ago until a few centuries ago, in the barren grounds and northern forest of the central Subarctic. Taltheilei assemblages show technology suited to caribou hunting, and many archaeologists propose that they represent the ancestors of later Dene peoples. That proposal is reasonable, and it is supported by continuity of land use, but it remains an interpretation. Material remains alone cannot show what language the toolmakers spoke or what they called themselves.

Other lines of evidence help. Historical linguistics shows that the Athabaskan languages form a family with a broad northern distribution and with offshoots on the Pacific Coast and in the Southwest, where the Apachean languages, discussed in Chapter 30, developed. Scholars generally place the earliest Athabaskan-speaking communities in the northwestern interior, though the details of dispersal remain debated. The Algonquian languages, which include Cree, Innu, and Anishinaabe languages, form another large family, and the Cree and Innu varieties are closely related. Neither family can be mapped directly onto an archaeological tradition without further evidence.

Geology and oral tradition sometimes illuminate one another. Mount Churchill, in the St. Elias Mountains, erupted violently at least twice during the first millennium CE, spreading a layer of volcanic ash across the Yukon and eastern Alaska that archaeologists call the White River Ash. The ash fell in two lobes: recent Bayesian redating places the northern lobe at roughly AD 260–390, and tree-ring and tephra studies place the eastern lobe at about AD 846–848. Earlier textbooks often gave older approximate dates for both events, so students should expect the chronology to continue to be refined. Some Athabaskan oral traditions describe a period of ashfall and darkness, and some scholars have proposed that such an event contributed to population movements, including the movements that eventually led Apachean peoples to the Southwest. That hypothesis is debated, and ash alone is not a sufficient explanation for migration. The example nonetheless shows how geological evidence and oral tradition, discussed in general terms in Chapter 1, can be read together.

Written evidence begins in the east in the seventeenth century, with French Jesuit accounts of the Innu and Cree, and in the far northwest much later. Each early source reflects the view of an outsider with specific purposes. Detailed ethnography begins in the late nineteenth century and expands in the twentieth, and it describes societies already deeply changed by trade, epidemic disease, missionary activity, and government policy. The most careful approach to the centuries before sustained contact combines these sources while recognizing that each answers different questions.

Section 34.3

Boreal Forests, Rivers, Lakes, and Seasonal Geography

The boreal forest is dominated by spruce, tamarack, pine, birch, and aspen, interspersed with muskeg, the waterlogged peatlands that cover large parts of the lowlands. Soils are thin and often poor, permafrost is discontinuous across much of the region, and the Canadian Shield contributes countless lakes and bare rock outcrops. North of the forest lies the forest–tundra margin, a transitional zone of stunted trees and open ground, where caribou spend part of the year. Winters are long and cold, summers are short but have very long daylight, and the whole system is regulated by the freeze-up and break-up of rivers and lakes. Ice made winter travel across lakes possible, but during freeze-up and break-up water travel was impossible and ice travel dangerous, so these were periods when families usually stayed at established camps.

Fire is a recurring feature of the boreal forest. Burned areas regenerate into young growth that provides browse for moose and habitat for other animals, so fire shaped resource distribution over decades. Biting insects in summer are a significant constraint, and animals and people both respond to them by seeking breezy ground, ice patches, or water. The animals themselves are variable. Snowshoe hare populations rise and fall in cycles of roughly a decade, with predators following. Caribou herds fluctuate over longer periods and can shift their routes. Fish populations are comparatively more reliable in many districts but vary by lake and river. Northern peoples responded to this uncertainty with diversified diets, flexible movement, extensive sharing, and detailed knowledge of alternative resources.

The environment was not uniform, and students should resist treating the Subarctic as a single landscape. The Labrador plateau and the north shore of the St. Lawrence differ from the flat, wet Hudson Bay lowlands, which differ from the lake country of the Shield, the Mackenzie basin, the Yukon uplands, and the river valleys and mountains of interior Alaska. At the same time, environment did not mechanically determine culture. The same forest supported Cree, Innu, and Dene communities with different languages, institutions, and ceremonial lives. Geography created possibilities and constraints, and people responded to them in historically specific ways.

Section 34.4

Languages, Names, and Northern Political Diversity

Two great language families dominate the northern interior, and they are not mutually intelligible. The Algonquian family includes the Cree, Innu, and Naskapi languages, which belong to a closely related dialect continuum, along with the Anishinaabe languages of the Great Lakes. The Athabaskan family, also spelled Athabascan, includes the languages of the Dene and related peoples across the northwestern interior, from the Dënesųłıné and Tłı̨chǫ to the Gwich'in, the Tutchone and Kaska languages, and the Koyukon, Ahtna, and Tanana languages in Alaska. Related languages are also spoken on the Pacific Coast and in the Southwest. Neighboring peoples therefore frequently spoke languages with no common ancestry and relied on bilingualism, interpreters, and trade languages in exchange and diplomacy.

Names raise difficult questions. Many exonyms in older sources were given by neighboring peoples and later adopted by Europeans, and some had pejorative origins. "Chipewyan" is generally understood as a Cree-derived exonym, whereas the people call themselves Dënesųłıné. "Slavey" derives from a Cree word for captives and has long been criticized, and many people now use Dene Tha', Dehcho Dene, or more specific names. "Dogrib" is the older name for the Tłı̨chǫ, and "Loucheux" for the Gwich'in. "Montagnais" is a French name for the Innu of the south, and "Naskapi" has been used for northern groups, though the peoples themselves use their own names. Cree names for themselves vary by dialect, among them nēhiyawak and Eeyou.

Historical Vocabulary
Athabaskan, Dene, and Related Names

Definition. Athabaskan is a linguistic classification for a family of related languages. Dene, and its cognates in different languages, is a word meaning "people" in many Athabaskan languages and is widely used by the Dene peoples of northern Canada as a self-designation, as in Denendeh.

Historical context. The cognate word appears in many languages: Dene in Dënesųłıné, Dane in Dane-zaa, Dena in Dena'ina, and Diné among the Navajo of the Southwest. Linguists and community members use the connected terms in different ways. Many peoples of the Yukon and Alaska identify through their own language names, such as Gwich'in or Koyukon, and not through "Dene."

Why it matters. Using "Athabaskan" and "Dene" as perfect synonyms obscures differences between a linguistic family and the political and cultural self-understandings of particular peoples.

Potential misunderstanding. Neither "Dene" nor "Cree" nor "Innu" designates a single centralized historical polity. Each label covers many communities with their own histories and leaders.

Political diversity matches linguistic diversity. Northern societies were organized around local groups of related households, regional networks of kin and marriage, and seasonal gatherings. Some regional identities, such as those of the Tłı̨chǫ or the Gwich'in, were durable and recognized, but they did not correspond to a centralized state. Chapter 1 cautioned against treating "tribe" as a generic category, and the same caution applies strongly here. Section 34.16 returns to the question of how political community actually worked.

Section 34.5

Innu and Naskapi Worlds of Labrador and the Eastern Subarctic

The Innu, whose name in Innu-aimun means "human being" or "person," are the Indigenous people of Nitassinan, "our land," which includes the interior of the Quebec–Labrador peninsula and the coasts of the Gulf of St. Lawrence, the Strait of Belle Isle, and Labrador. Students should not confuse the Innu with the Inuit. The names resemble one another in English, but the peoples, languages, and histories are distinct: Innu-aimun is an Algonquian language closely related to Cree, while Inuktitut belongs to an entirely different family, and Chapter 35 treats the Inuit. The French called the southern Innu Montagnais, and older writers sometimes used "Naskapi" broadly for northern Innu groups. Today the two names should be distinguished. The Mushuau Innu of northern Labrador, often glossed as the people of the barrens, are Innu, whereas the Naskapi Nation of Kawawachikamach in Quebec identifies as a distinct people with a language closely related to Innu-aimun and East Cree.

Nitassinan is drained by large rivers that flow south to the St. Lawrence, east to the Atlantic, and north toward Ungava Bay, and these rivers were the arteries of Innu travel. The country includes the forested southern interior, the open lichen woodland and tundra of the northern plateau, and a coast that offered fish, sea mammals, and birds' eggs. Innu life followed a seasonal movement between coast and interior, but the balance varied sharply. Southern groups, with access to the Gulf coast, often spent the open season near the shore and moved inland to hunting grounds in the fall. Northern groups, above all the Mushuau Innu, organized the year around the movements of the George River and other migratory caribou herds on the plateau. Travel in the open season was by canoe, with bark canoes in the south where suitable bark was available and other watercraft in the sparsely forested north, and in winter by snowshoe and toboggan.

Caribou hunting among the northern Innu was a cooperative undertaking. Ethnographic and early historical sources describe killing swimming animals from canoes at river crossings, communal drives, and snaring, and caribou supplied meat, fat, marrow, hides for clothing and tents, sinew, and antler. A communal feast known as makushan involved carefully crushing and boiling caribou bones to render marrow fat, which was eaten with caribou meat amid drumming, dancing, and communal sharing, and it expressed obligations of sharing and respect toward the animals. Hunters also read the cracks in a burned caribou shoulder blade to guide the hunt, a practice recorded in the seventeenth-century Jesuit sources. Southern Innu relied more on moose, beaver, porcupine, hare, and river and lake fish, and coastal groups used seals, fish, and seabirds. The picture is of a people who diversified according to place rather than a single caribou economy.

Innu political life was built from small units of related households that wintered together, regional groups tied to particular river systems, and summer gatherings that brought larger numbers together for fishing, marriage, trade, and ceremony. Leaders earned influence by hunting skill, knowledge, generosity, and judgment, and they could not compel others to follow, as the Le Jeune source in section 34.16 illustrates. Seasonal gatherings at places such as Tadoussac, at the mouth of the Saguenay, also linked the Innu to neighbors. Upland routes connected Innu and Cree peoples at the height of land, including the routes between the Saguenay and the Mistassini region, and relations with Algonquin and Anishinaabe peoples extended southwest. Relations with Inuit along the Labrador coast, documented mainly from the sixteenth century onward, included both trade and conflict, and from the sixteenth century European fishers and whalers also visited the Strait of Belle Isle and Gulf coasts, where Indigenous people traded with them. The consequences of those encounters are beyond the scope of this chapter.

The evidence for Innu history requires care. The archaeology of Labrador is rich, including the far older Maritime Archaic and later Indigenous traditions treated in Chapters 7 and 11, and Ramah chert from northern Labrador was carried over very long distances in the Archaic. But archaeologists cannot assume that the makers of those traditions were ancestors of the Innu in any simple way. Written sources begin in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and are mostly by outsiders, while detailed ethnography belongs to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The Innu themselves distinguish genres of oral knowledge, including tipatshimun, accounts of events and experience, and atanukan, sacred narratives, and historians working with Innu knowledge need to respect these distinctions instead of treating all oral tradition as a single kind of source.

Section 34.6

Cree Worlds of the Eastern and Central Boreal Forest

Cree languages form a dialect continuum across a vast area, and the peoples who speak them were never a single political nation. The East Cree of Eeyou Istchee live on the eastern side of James Bay and inland toward Lake Mistassini. The Swampy Cree and Moose Cree of the lowlands, sometimes called the Omushkegowuk, "people of the muskeg," live along the western and southern coasts of the bays and the rivers flowing into them, including the Moose, Albany, Attawapiskat, and Severn. Other Cree communities extend through the forests of northern Ontario and Manitoba and beyond. Each region had its own resource base and history. The Atikamekw, in the upper St-Maurice valley, speak a language closely related to Cree and are generally identified as a distinct people.

Cree economies combined hunting, fishing, and gathering according to season and place. In the lowlands and along the bay coasts, the spring and fall goose migrations were major events, and families gathered at goose camps to hunt and preserve food. Sturgeon, whitefish, and other fish were important at lakes and rapids, and beaver, muskrat, moose, hare, and, in the north, caribou provided meat and hides. Coastal communities also took seals and other marine resources. The eastern rivers, including the Rupert, Eastmain, and Nottaway, carried people inland to upland hunting grounds, and portage routes led across the height of land to the St. Lawrence and Saguenay drainages, where the Cree met Innu and Algonquin neighbors. Travel by birchbark canoe in the open season and by snowshoe and toboggan in winter linked camps over great distances.

Social and political life centered on kin-based hunting groups that came together at seasonal gatherings. Later ethnographic descriptions of the East Cree record hunting territories each overseen by a family-based steward responsible for the careful use of the land, a system whose antiquity is debated, as the Historical Debate in section 34.7 explains. Whatever its age, the system expresses a concern for stewardship that appears in many northern communities. Cree communities also formed part of regional networks of marriage and exchange with Anishinaabe peoples to the south, treated in Chapter 27, and with Innu to the east.

Cree religious life included dreams and relations with spiritual helpers, ritual specialists, and the stories of Wîsahkêcâhk, a figure who shapes the landscape and instructs people through both wisdom and folly. The earliest French accounts of the Cree, in the Jesuit Relations of the 1640s, describe communities between Lake Superior and James Bay as already active in regional exchange. Those records show Cree participation in networks that existed before the written record; they do not describe the origin of the Cree, and the question of where particular Cree communities lived before the seventeenth century remains partly a matter of inference from linguistic, archaeological, and oral evidence.

Section 34.7

Cree Worlds toward the Western Forest and Plains Borderlands

West of the Hudson Bay lowlands, the Woods Cree and related communities lived in the forests and lake country of northern Manitoba and Saskatchewan along the Churchill, Nelson, and Saskatchewan river systems. Rock Cree communities of the Shield country along the Churchill River, and Woods Cree communities extending through northern Manitoba and Saskatchewan, including the Reindeer Lake and Lac La Ronge regions, relied on fish, moose, caribou in the north, beaver, and waterfowl, and moved by canoe and portage across a landscape of thousands of lakes. Their homelands lay across a transition from the boreal forest to the aspen parkland and grassland to the south. This forest–grassland margin was an economic frontier: forest peoples could gain access to bison products from Plains peoples, while Plains peoples could obtain forest goods, and families sometimes moved between the two environments. Chapter 29 treats the Plains before the horse, and Chapter 49 the equestrian transformation, so this section addresses only the borderland.

The western Cree interacted with several other peoples. Assiniboine communities to the south, speakers of a Siouan language, and Anishinaabe communities to the southeast were important trading partners and, at times, rivals. To the northwest, the Cree met Dene communities along the line of the Churchill and Athabasca watersheds, and the relationships were similarly mixed, including trade, marriage, and conflict. Cree and Dene speakers relied on interpreters, bilingual intermediaries, and shared knowledge of travel routes to manage these relations. The Plains Cree, who later became a distinct equestrian people, are a historical formation of a later period connected to horses and trade, not an ancient division of the Cree.

Farther northwest, in the Peace River country of northwestern Alberta and northeastern British Columbia, lived the Dane-zaa, "real people," called Beaver in older sources. The Peace River, whose Dane-zaa name differs from its English name, drains a landscape of river valleys, boreal forest, muskeg, and open country that supported moose, wood bison, fish, and waterfowl, and the Dane-zaa moved seasonally among hunting and fishing places. Wood bison, a forest-dwelling relative of the Plains bison that ranged across northern Alberta and the southern Northwest Territories, were an important resource for the Dane-zaa and for neighboring Dene and Cree, a reminder that northern hunters did not depend on caribou alone. The Dane-zaa were a frontier people, adjoining the Cree to the east and south, other Dene peoples to the north and west, and the Tse'khene in the mountains.

Dane-zaa tradition includes the Dreamers, prophets who in dreams travel a trail to heaven and return with songs and teachings that guide the community. The tradition is documented in historical and modern sources, and scholars discuss how it relates to older Dane-zaa ideas of dream power and to later Christian influences; its antiquity should therefore not be assumed. The English name of the Peace River is associated with a tradition of peace between Cree and Dane-zaa, often dated to the later eighteenth century, which lies beyond this chapter's main period but shows how relations on the frontier were negotiated. The Cree–Dene frontier on the Peace and Athabasca rivers is among the most important meeting zones in the western Subarctic, and it receives further attention in section 34.19.

Historical Debate

How Old Are the Territorial Patterns Recorded in Early Sources?

The question: Early traders and later ethnographers recorded patterns of territory, family hunting areas, and the range of particular peoples. Did these arrangements reflect ancient practice, or were they substantially shaped by the fur trade and the movements it set in motion? The question has been debated for the Cree, the Innu, and the Dene, in connection both with family hunting territories in the eastern forest and with the apparent westward and northward expansion of some Cree communities in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Interpretation A: Substantial continuity. Some scholars emphasize continuity of land-use arrangements and relationships, supported by long archaeological records of repeated use of the same places, Indigenous oral traditions about territory and movement, and the logic of environments in which stewardship of dependable resources would have been valuable long before traders arrived.
Interpretation B: Substantial reshaping by the fur trade. Other scholars argue that the trade's demand for furs, new goods, and trading posts intensified, and in some places created, particular territorial arrangements, drew communities toward posts, and changed the relative power of neighboring peoples. The documentary record that begins in the seventeenth century captures a world already responding to European commerce.
Interpretation C: Variation by region and period. Many historians stress that neither extreme holds everywhere. Some arrangements evidently predate Europeans, others were modified or intensified by trade, and the balance differed among regions and over time.

Evidence: The debate rests on archaeology, early written sources, linguistic evidence, later ethnography, and oral tradition. The principal problem is that the sources that describe territorial arrangements most fully, ethnographies of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, describe societies that had already been transformed by trade, disease, and government policy.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology and causation, and it involves the interpretation of evidence rather than the existence of territorial knowledge, which all sides accept.

Why it matters: If territorial arrangements were ancient, European commerce entered an established system; if they were largely newly formed, northern societies appear more dependent on the trade than the evidence of long-distance exchange suggests. The answer also affects how treaty and land-use histories, examined in later chapters, are understood.

Student task: Identify which kinds of evidence could distinguish ancient from trade-era arrangements in a particular region, and explain why a regional answer may differ from a continental one.

Section 34.8

Dënesųłıné and the Athabasca–Great Slave Lake World

The Dënesųłıné, called Chipewyan in older literature, occupied a broad homeland, Dënesųłıné Nëné, extending from the west coast of Hudson Bay through the barren grounds and forest edge of what are now northern Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and the Northwest Territories, to the Athabasca and Great Slave Lake regions. The name "Chipewyan" is generally understood to come from a Cree word, and the people's own name is the one this chapter uses. The homeland included the open barren grounds, the forest edge, and the lake country, and different groups emphasized different resources. Names such as Ethen-eldeli, commonly rendered "caribou eaters," are associated with those whose lives were most closely tied to barren-ground caribou, while groups in the Athabasca and Great Slave Lake region relied more heavily on fish and on moose and wood bison in the southern forest.

Barren-ground caribou herds migrate between summer calving grounds on the tundra and wintering areas in the forest edge, and Dënesųłıné movements were tied to those migrations. Hunters took caribou at water crossings, in drives using fences or converging lines of stones and brush, and by other methods, and families used the meat, marrow, fat, hides, sinew, and bone. Dried and pounded meat and fat were stored for lean seasons. In summer, many families moved onto or near the barrens to follow the herds; in winter, they retreated to the shelter of the forest edge, where fuel, and fish from lakes, were available. Because caribou movements can shift from year to year and herds fluctuate, hunger was a real danger, and early outsider records describe years of hardship as well as plenty.

Lakes and rivers, including Lake Athabasca and Great Slave Lake, provided fish that could be caught with nets under the ice and in open water, dried, and stored, and fishing places drew families together in the open season. Knowledge of alternative fisheries and the practice of sharing helped communities survive failures of the hunt. Social life was organized around households and local groups tied by kinship and marriage into regional networks. Leadership depended on skill in hunting, knowledge of the land, and the ability to organize cooperative drives and to represent a group in dealings with neighbors, and, as in other northern societies, it rested on persuasion rather than coercion.

The Dënesųłıné interacted with several neighboring peoples. To the south, they shared a frontier with Cree peoples whose movement into the Churchill and Athabasca watersheds changed relationships over time, and sources and traditions describe both exchange and warfare. To the north and east, they came into contact with Inuit along the Hudson Bay and Arctic coasts, again with trade and conflict, and to the northwest with the Yellowknives, Tłı̨chǫ, and other Dene communities, with whom they shared language and kinship but also competed for resources. The western shore of Hudson Bay was a meeting zone for Dene, Cree, and Inuit. These relations are better documented from the eighteenth century, when traders and explorers began to write about them, than before, and the earlier picture has to be reconstructed with caution from archaeology, oral tradition, and later sources.

Evidence for the Dënesųłıné thus combines the Taltheilei archaeological record, which many archaeologists tentatively connect to ancestral Dene, with oral histories held by Dene communities and with later documentary records. The story of the Dënesųłıné in the commercial era belongs to later chapters. They remain a clear example of a northern people whose seasonal movements followed an organized logic tied to herds, fisheries, and relationships, and not the wanderings of "nomads."

Section 34.9

Tłı̨chǫ, Sahtu, Dehcho, and the Mackenzie Basin

The Mackenzie River, called Deh Cho, "big river," by Dene speakers, and its tributaries and lakes form one of the largest Indigenous corridors in the north. Several distinct Dene peoples lived within and beside it. The Tłı̨chǫ, formerly called Dogrib, live between Great Slave Lake and Great Bear Lake. The Sahtu Dene include the people of Great Bear Lake, the Hare people (K'áhsho Got'ı̨nę) along the middle Mackenzie, and the Mountain Dene of the Mackenzie Mountains. The Dehcho Dene occupy the Mackenzie and Liard valleys, including the confluence of the two rivers, which was a meeting place. Farther south, the related Dene Tha' are centered in northwestern Alberta, with traditional territory extending into the southern Northwest Territories, so that they overlap with Dehcho territory without being a Dehcho subgroup. The term Slavey, sometimes applied to Dehcho and related peoples, derives from a Cree exonym that many now reject.

The Yellowknives, T'atsaot'ine in the official spelling used by the Northwest Territories, with a modern community spelling rendered Tetsǫ́t'iné, historically lived on the north shore of Great Slave Lake and the country to the northeast, and they are associated with access to copper from the Coppermine region, which they used and traded. Copper was hammered into tools and ornaments, and it moved among Dene and Inuit communities and beyond. Oral tradition and later records describe periods of conflict between the Yellowknives and the Tłı̨chǫ and later peace between them, though these accounts belong chiefly to a later period than this chapter's main focus.

The basin's resources were varied. Lakes provided fish, including whitefish, lake trout, pike, burbot, and inconnu, taken with nets, weirs, and hooks. The forests provided moose, beaver, hare, and waterfowl, and in some areas wood bison. The barrens to the north and east supported caribou, including the herds that the Tłı̨chǫ and Yellowknives followed seasonally between forest edge and tundra. The mountains of the Sahtu region provided sheep and other game. The Mackenzie corridor made it possible to travel by canoe in summer and over ice in winter between distant places, and tributaries such as the Peel and the Liard connected the Dene to the Gwich'in to the northwest and to the Arctic coast, where Inuvialuit lived. Chapter 35 treats the Arctic peoples, and the relationship is discussed here only as an exchange frontier.

Landscape and story are closely tied. The culture hero Yamǫǫzha, known as Yamoria in Sahtu and Dehcho traditions, is said in many narratives to have traveled through the land, removing dangerous beings and establishing the conditions in which people could live, and the stories attach his deeds to particular places. Oral traditions of this kind, recorded in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and still told today, function as a geographic record, a moral guide, and a statement of relationship to land. They are historical sources in the sense described in Chapter 1, but they require interpretation and, in many cases, the permission of community knowledge holders for their retelling.

Political life in the Mackenzie basin was regional rather than centralized. Local groups of related families gathered at fisheries and caribou crossings, regional leaders were respected for their hunting and for their judgment in disputes and in dealings with neighbors, and relationships among the Dene peoples were maintained through marriage, shared language ties, and exchange. The distinct names and territories described above were real and durable, but they did not constitute states, and the boundaries between them were negotiated and flexible. Reconstructing earlier arrangements from the better-documented nineteenth and twentieth centuries remains a methodological risk, as the Historical Debate in section 34.7 explains.

Section 34.10

Gwich'in, Hän, Tutchone, Kaska, and the Yukon Interior

The Gwich'in live across the northern Yukon, the northwestern part of the Northwest Territories, and northeastern Alaska, in the Peel, Porcupine, and Mackenzie delta regions. Gwich'in communities are identified by river valley and region, and include the Teetł'it Gwich'in of the Peel and Rat river country, the Vuntut Gwitchin of the Old Crow Flats, and the Gwichya Gwich'in of the Mackenzie delta area. Their lives have long been bound to the Porcupine caribou herd, which crosses river valleys and mountain passes, and Gwich'in communities used caribou fences, water crossings, and other methods to harvest it. Fish, moose, and waterfowl were also important, and the wetlands of the Old Crow Flats supported muskrat and waterfowl. The Gwich'in had relations with the Dene to the south and with the Inuvialuit and Iñupiat along the Arctic coast, with trade, marriage, and conflict all recorded in later sources.

Farther south and west, the Hän lived along the Yukon River near the mouth of the Klondike (Tr'ondëk) River, where the annual salmon runs supported seasonal fish camps. The Northern and Southern Tutchone peoples live in the central and southern Yukon, along rivers such as the Pelly, Stewart, and Klukshu, and in the mountains where the ice patches described at the opening of the chapter lie. The Kaska live in the southeastern Yukon and northern British Columbia, in the Liard and Pelly country. These communities depended on a mix of caribou, moose, mountain sheep, fish, and small game, and their territories extended from river valleys to alpine hunting grounds. Salmon fisheries on the Yukon and its tributaries were major seasonal resources in some districts, while lake fish mattered more in others, and fish camps brought families together in summer.

South of the Kaska, in the Rocky Mountain Trench and the valleys of the Finlay and Parsnip rivers of northern British Columbia, live the Tse'khene (Sekani), whose name is commonly glossed as "people on the rocks." Their mountain country supported moose, caribou, mountain sheep and goats, and fish, and it lay between the Peace River world of the Dane-zaa to the east and the Carrier (Dakelh) and other peoples toward the Pacific side of the mountains. The Tse'khene thus show that the Dene world did not end at the Yukon: it reached down through the mountain valleys to the northern interior of British Columbia, where Athabaskan-speaking peoples were in contact with peoples of the Pacific watershed. Their history is less fully documented than that of some of their neighbors, and archaeology, oral tradition, and later records need to be used together.

The Yukon interior was linked to the Pacific Coast by mountain passes. Southern Tutchone and Tlingit communities exchanged goods and married across the mountains, and Southern Tutchone social organization includes matrilineal clans with similarities to Tlingit systems, evidence of long interaction. The Tahltan of the Stikine plateau, an Athabaskan-speaking people, lived near the volcanic source of Mount Edziza obsidian, which was carried widely across the northwest. Coastal Tlingit families often controlled the principal passes and acted as intermediaries, a role discussed in section 34.19. Chapter 33 treats the Tlingit and other Northwest Coast peoples, and they appear here only in their role as exchange partners. This region also shows that the Subarctic was not culturally homogeneous: some interior societies had clan systems and memorial ceremonies resembling coastal ones, while others did not.

Section 34.11

Dene Peoples of Interior Alaska

Alaska's Indigenous history was not solely maritime or Arctic. The interior was home to Athabaskan-speaking peoples, including the Koyukon of the Koyukuk and middle Yukon rivers, the Tanana peoples of the Tanana valley, the Ahtna of the Copper River, the Deg Hit'an and Holikachuk of the lower Yukon and Innoko regions, the Upper Kuskokwim peoples, and the Dena'ina of Cook Inlet and its hinterland, whose territory combined inland and coastal resources. These peoples are among those described collectively as Alaska Native, the term introduced in Chapter 1, and their modern political and economic institutions are examined in Chapter 115. They spoke related but distinct languages and had distinct regional histories, so that "Alaskan Athabaskan" is a linguistic grouping, not a single people.

Large rivers supported extensive inland worlds. The Yukon, Tanana, Koyukuk, Kuskokwim, and Copper rivers carried salmon runs and other fish that could be caught with weirs, traps, dip nets, and other gear and dried for storage, while caribou, moose, bear, small game, and waterfowl added to the diet. Fish camps on the rivers were centers of summer activity, and winter housing in some districts included semi-subterranean structures. The Koyukon, on the Koyukuk and middle Yukon, are among the best documented Alaskan Athabaskan peoples. Ethnographic work records elaborate rules of respect toward animals and a system of regional bands and matrilineal kin groups, and it shows how fishing, hunting, and the sharing of food were tied to moral obligations, though this documentation is from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and cannot be read back uncritically.

The Tanana peoples, divided among the Lower Tanana, Tanacross, and Upper Tanana, lived along the Tanana River and its tributaries, where salmon, whitefish, caribou, and moose supported seasonal movement and where trade routes ran toward the Yukon to the north and the Copper River to the south. The Ahtna occupied the Copper River basin, where large salmon runs supported intensive fishing, and they were organized in regional groups with matrilineal clans. The Ahtna worked native copper from the Copper River region into tools and valuables that were traded to coastal peoples, including the Tlingit, an example of the coast–interior connection discussed in section 34.19, and the river takes its English name from this copper. Some interior peoples, including the Koyukon and Ahtna, held memorial feasts, often described in English as potlatches, for the dead; their antiquity is uncertain, and they are known mainly from nineteenth- and twentieth-century observation.

Farther west and south, the Deg Hit'an and Holikachuk of the lower Yukon and Innoko rivers and the Upper Kuskokwim peoples lived in smaller-scale inland communities that depended on fish, caribou, moose, and small game, and the Dena'ina of Cook Inlet used salmon, sea mammals, and inland game. Ethnographic sources describe the Dena'ina as having ranked leaders, called qeshqa, and a system of clans and feasting, an example of the variety of social organization within the Athabaskan-speaking world. The lower Yukon and Kuskokwim rivers marked a zone where Athabaskan-speaking peoples met Yup'ik communities, who are treated in Chapter 35, and exchange and intermarriage across this frontier were part of regional life. Russian contact reached parts of Alaska from the late eighteenth century, which lies beyond this chapter's scope. The aim here is to show that interior Alaska had a long history of its own, organized around rivers, fish, caribou, and relationships to neighbors.

Section 34.12

Caribou Worlds

Caribou deserve a thematic treatment because they linked ecological knowledge, mobility, food, clothing, tools, social organization, and ritual obligation. Biologists distinguish several kinds. Barren-ground caribou form very large migratory herds that travel between tundra calving grounds and forest wintering grounds; woodland caribou are dispersed in smaller groups in the forest; and mountain caribou occupy upland ranges. Peoples who relied on migratory herds, such as the northern Innu, the Dënesųłıné, and the Gwich'in, organized their year around expected movements, while those who depended on woodland caribou used a more dispersed hunting strategy.

The animal supplied meat, fat, and marrow, and also winter hides with the hair on, which provide exceptional insulation, as well as skins for tents, sinew for thread, rawhide lacing called babiche, and bone and antler for tools. Hunters took caribou in several ways: by killing swimming animals from canoes at river crossings, by driving herds into pounds or along fences and lanes, by snaring, and, at places such as the ice patches, by stalking. These methods required cooperation among households, knowledge of herd behavior and terrain, and rules for distributing the catch. Hunting was subject to ritual obligation and respect, discussed in section 34.18.

The chapter must nevertheless reject the stereotype that all northern peoples depended primarily on caribou. Coastal Cree communities relied on geese and fish, many Dene of the Mackenzie basin on fish and moose, and the peoples of interior Alaska on salmon. Even caribou-centered peoples faced herd shifts and failures that could lead to hunger. Caribou were one of a set of resources, and the most successful strategies were diversified. The continuing importance of caribou to Indigenous communities, and the effects of habitat and climate change on herds, are treated in Chapters 135 and 138.

Section 34.13

Moose, Fish, Waterfowl, Small Game, and Plant Foods

Northern historical narratives often overemphasize big-game hunting. In many districts, fish were a more dependable food than large mammals. Whitefish, lake trout, pike, walleye, burbot, sturgeon, and inconnu were taken in lakes and rivers with nets made of babiche or twisted willow-bark fibers, with weirs and traps, with hooks, and by spearing. Winter fishing involved setting nets beneath the ice. Fish could be dried or smoked, stored, and fed to dogs, and fishing stations drew families together in the open season. In Alaska and the Yukon, salmon runs provided large seasonal harvests that supported dense seasonal settlement.

Moose were important in many forest districts, especially areas of regenerating growth after fire, and they provided meat and large hides. Beaver and muskrat were hunted for food and for their fur, and hares and grouse supported households through the winter, though hare populations rise and fall in cycles and could collapse. Waterfowl, especially geese in the lowlands, concentrated in the spring and fall and made seasonal camps possible. Preserved foods, including dried meat and fat mixtures, allowed travel and stored surplus for lean periods.

Plant foods and medicines also mattered, although they are less visible in the sources. Berries such as blueberries and cranberries were gathered and sometimes dried, roots and inner bark were used for food and fiber, and the leaves and roots of many plants had medicinal uses known to specialists in each community. Women, who most often gathered and processed these foods, held much of this knowledge. The overall picture is one of diversification: a household's well-being depended on the ability to shift between resources as conditions changed, and on relationships that allowed it to call on others in lean years.

Section 34.14

Snowshoes, Toboggans, Canoes, Dogs, Clothing, and Northern Technology

Northern technology represents accumulated knowledge of materials and environment. Snowshoes, built with wooden frames and lacings of babiche, came in forms adapted to different snow conditions, and they allowed hunters to move across deep snow and to pursue animals. Toboggans, flat-bottomed sleds of wooden boards, carried loads over snow, pulled by people, and also by dogs, which also served as pack animals. The word toboggan itself comes from an Algonquian language. Travel in the open season used the birchbark canoe in the east and center, where large birch grow, a light and repairable craft that could be carried over portages. In regions with less suitable birch, such as parts of the northwest, people built canoes of spruce bark and boats of moose hide.

Clothing and shelter made winter survival possible. Tanned moose and caribou hides, often smoked to make them supple and water-resistant, were sewn into parkas, trousers, mittens, and footwear. Caribou hide worn with the hair on gave exceptional warmth, and robes of woven hare skin provided insulation. Shelters ranged from conical lodges covered with hides or bark and domed lodges to semi-subterranean houses in parts of Alaska. Fire-making, food caching, and the preparation of dried and stored food were equally technical skills. Native copper, hammered into awls, knives, and ornaments, and stone from specific sources supplied tools, as section 34.19 explains.

These technologies were not isolated inventions. They moved among communities and were adapted locally, and craft knowledge, held especially by skilled makers, was transmitted through practice. A good snowshoe or a well-sewn parka represented hours of skilled labor, and the labor was divided among household members, as section 34.17 shows. The technological repertoire was also open to change: the shift from darts to arrows documented in the Yukon ice patches, and the later incorporation of metal and cloth through exchange, show a willingness to adopt what worked.

Section 34.15

Seasonal Mobility, Camps, Trails, and Territorial Knowledge

This section is the chapter's central analytical argument: seasonal movement was organized rather than random. Families followed known routes between recurring resource places, and their movements were scheduled around predictable events: the spring arrival of waterfowl, the break-up of lake ice, the summer concentration of fish, the fall movement of caribou, and the winter dispersal into smaller hunting groups. The details varied by region. In the Hudson Bay lowlands, goose camps in spring and fall structured the year; on the barren grounds, caribou crossings did; in the Yukon and Alaska, salmon runs did. Summer was commonly the season of larger gatherings, when marriages were arranged, news exchanged, and ceremonies held, and winter the season of smaller groups spread across hunting grounds.

Mobility did not mean rootlessness. The land was known through names, stories, and trails. Place-names recorded the history of events, the location of resources, and the deeds of ancestors and culture heroes, so that a person traveling a river was moving through a narrated landscape. Oral geographies could be very detailed, and in the eighteenth century European traders and explorers gathered maps drawn by Indigenous travelers and relied heavily on Indigenous guides. Communities recognized rights of access to particular fishing places, hunting areas, and routes, though the form of those rights varied and was often flexible, shaped by kinship, reciprocity, and the need to share in hard times.

Historical Vocabulary
Seasonal Round and Territory

Definition. A seasonal round is the yearly pattern of movement among resource places. Territory, as used here, refers to land and water used and cared for by a community, family, or network, according to recognized relationships.

Historical context. The expression "nomadic," common in older writing, implies wandering without destination. Northern movement followed established routes and schedules and was guided by knowledge passed between generations.

Why it matters. Treating mobility as organized restores northern peoples as the holders of land-based knowledge and political relationships.

Potential misunderstanding. Territory in northern societies was often fluid and overlapping, and it should not be imagined as a fenced boundary of the European kind.

Environmental knowledge also guided risk management. People tracked the condition of animals, the thickness of ice, the course of storms, and the quality of snow. Caches of food at known places allowed travelers to cross difficult country. These practices are examined in the context of environmental governance in Chapter 133. The system was resilient but not immune to failure: severe winters, failures of caribou or hare populations, and epidemics could cause hunger and death, and recorded accounts describe such episodes.

What Changed

  • Weapons and tools changed, including the shift from darts to bows and arrows suggested by the Yukon ice-patch record
  • Climate and forest conditions shifted over thousands of years, altering the distribution of animals and fish
  • Languages dispersed and diversified, and communities moved, as in the Athabaskan expansions
  • Later, European goods, trading posts, and epidemic disease altered the pattern of movement and the balance among neighbors

What Stayed the Same

  • The reliance on detailed knowledge of land, water, and animals passed between generations
  • The organization of the year around recurring resources and gatherings
  • The importance of kinship and sharing as protections against uncertain resources
  • The use of rivers, lakes, and portages as travel routes

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual in some respects, abrupt in others, and regionally uneven. The eastern forests felt European contact earlier than the far northwest, while all regions shared a long earlier history of adaptation. Those who lived most directly through technological and climatic change were hunters and the households that depended on them, while the logic of seasonal mobility continued for millennia.

Section 34.16

Household, Kinship, Leadership, and Political Community

The basic unit of northern life was the household, usually a nuclear or extended family, which in turn belonged to larger local groups, often composed of related households who camped and hunted together. These groups were flexible: they could grow or divide according to resources and relationships, and individuals could move between groups through marriage or kinship. Marriage ties connected distant communities, and cooperation in hunting and sharing of food rested on these ties. In some societies, notably Ahtna, Tanana, and Tutchone, matrilineal clans organized marriage and ceremonial life; in many Cree, Innu, and Dene communities, kinship was traced through both parents.

Leadership usually depended on knowledge, skill, generosity, persuasion, and the confidence of others. Respected hunters, elders, healers, and those with ritual knowledge could guide decisions about when and where to move, hunt, and gather, and leaders emerged in particular circumstances, such as caribou drives, trade, diplomacy, or war. Their authority was commonly limited to what others were willing to follow. This is not the same as lacking political organization. Decisions were made through discussion and consensus within and among groups, and these methods are well suited to dispersed, flexible communities. Later European traders sometimes appointed "chiefs" and "trading captains" for their own purposes, which makes it difficult to treat the leaders recorded in trade-era sources as straightforward survivals of older institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Band and Local Group

Definition. "Band" was an anthropological label for a small, mobile, kin-based community, later also a legal term in Canadian law. "Local group" is used here for a set of households that camped and hunted together at a given time.

Historical context. Twentieth-century scholars often presented bands as fixed units with defined membership. Evidence from northern societies suggests that groups changed in size and composition through the year and across years.

Why it matters. Treating flexible communities as permanent bands can lead to mistaken reconstructions of earlier political life.

Potential misunderstanding. "Band" under the Indian Act, discussed in Chapter 85, is a legal category created by the Canadian state and is not the same as an older local group.

Primary Source Window

Paul Le Jeune among the Innu, 1633–1634

Context: Paul Le Jeune, a French Jesuit and the superior of the Quebec mission, spent the winter of 1633–1634 traveling with a hunting group of Innu, whom the French called Montagnais, in the mountains and valleys south of the lower St. Lawrence, as part of his effort to learn their language and to promote conversion. He described his experience in his annual report to his superiors, written in 1634 and published in Paris in 1635, later collected in the series known as the Jesuit Relations.

Source type: Missionary report written by an outsider, for a European audience, intended in part to encourage support for the mission.

Paraphrase of the account: Le Jeune describes a winter of constant movement as hunters followed moose and other game, of periods of severe hunger when the hunt failed, and of the labor of women in moving camp and preparing food. He observes that those the French called captains could advise and persuade but could not compel others to obey. He also describes ritual specialists, whom he called sorcerers, conducting ceremonies for the hunt, and he records his own hostility toward them. This is a paraphrase, not a quotation.

Analysis: The report is valuable as a detailed eyewitness account of seasonal movement, dependence on hunting, gendered labor, and leadership by persuasion in an eastern northern society in the early seventeenth century. It supports, for this group, several patterns this chapter describes, including flexible leadership and organized seasonal movement.

Limitations: Le Jeune was a newcomer with limited fluency, a missionary who regarded Innu religion as error, and a writer seeking to persuade his European audience. The Innu he lived with were already in contact with French traders, and the account concerns one hunting group south of the lower St. Lawrence, not the interior of Labrador or the other northern regions of this chapter. It cannot establish what the Innu thought of their own leaders or ceremonies.

Perspective: The source represents the view of a French Jesuit man. The views of Innu women, hunters, and ritual specialists are not recorded in their own words.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about winter life, leadership, and the work of women?
  2. Who created it, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped how Le Jeune described Innu leaders and ritual specialists?
  4. What can historians learn from it about seasonal movement and political authority?
  5. What can they not establish from this source alone?
Section 34.17

Gender, Labor, Family, and Everyday Life

Work in northern households was divided, but not always rigidly. Men commonly undertook the hunting of large game and much of the fishing, trapping, and canoe and tool construction, while women commonly prepared hides, sewed clothing, made and laced snowshoes, dried and smoked meat and fish, set up camp, carried loads, gathered plant foods, and cared for young children. Women also snared small game and fished, and some hunted. Skilled sewing was essential to survival, because well-made clothing could mean the difference between life and death in winter, so women's labor was central to the household economy. The division of work differed across the Subarctic and should not be assumed to have been identical for all peoples or for all periods.

Everyday Life and Social History

Children, Elders, and the Rhythm of the Year

Children learned by participating. They carried small loads, learned to set snares and handle fish, watched as hides were scraped and clothing sewn, and absorbed place-names and stories while traveling. Elders held knowledge of routes, animals, medicine, and narrative, and their guidance mattered in decisions about movement and in the education of the young. Adoption and fostering moved children among related households, and marriages created ties among groups that could be called on in times of need.

The yearly cycle structured social life. Summer gatherings at fisheries, goose camps, or caribou crossings were occasions for marriages, trade, storytelling, games, and ceremonies, and winter dispersal meant smaller groups and longer isolation. Life was also marked by hardship, including seasons of hunger, injuries on the trail, and illness. Care of the elderly and the sick depended on kin, and sharing food was both a moral obligation and a practical means of survival. Most of what is known about everyday life comes from outside observers or from later ethnography, so historians draw also on oral histories, material culture, and the testimony of present-day community members.

Section 34.18

Animals, Spiritual Power, Hunting Ethics, and the Moral Landscape

Northern religious life was bound up with relations among people, animals, and places. Many communities understood animals as beings with their own intelligence and agency who gave themselves to hunters who behaved properly. Success in hunting depended on respectful conduct, including sharing the catch, treating the remains of animals with care, observing prohibitions, and avoiding waste. Practices such as returning certain bones to the water or the land, or refraining from giving them to dogs, are recorded in different forms across the region, though specific rules varied by people and place. Hunting was therefore also a moral activity.

Dreams were an important source of knowledge and power. Cree traditions speak of dream-visitors or helpers, often called pawākan, and Dene communities understood "medicine," a form of power acquired through dream experience, as the basis of the abilities of healers and hunters. Among the Innu and Cree, ritual specialists performed the shaking tent, a ritual in which the specialist, inside a closed lodge, communicated with spirit beings to learn about distant events or the location of game. Innu hunters also divined the course of a hunt by burning the shoulder blade of a caribou and reading the cracks, a practice recorded in early French sources, and held feasts in which caribou bones and marrow were treated ceremonially. Innu accounts speak of a master or great spirit associated with animals.

Narratives conveyed moral and historical knowledge. Wîsahkêcâhk among the Cree and Yamǫǫzha among the Dene are figures who shape the land and teach through example, and Raven stories are told among the Athabaskan peoples of Alaska and the Yukon. These cannot be reduced to a single religious system, and "animism" is not a sufficient description of the many northern ways of relating to the world. Religious life changed over time as new ideas moved between communities and, later, through missionary activity, treated in Chapter 54. The sacred places of the northern landscape and the knowledge that governs them remain important to communities today.

Section 34.19

Rivers of Exchange: Trade, Diplomacy, Warfare, and a Connected Northern Interior

The northern interior was a corridor of exchange long before the arrival of European traders. Native copper, from sources such as the Coppermine region and the Copper River in Alaska, was hammered into tools and valuables, and it moved among Dene, Inuit, and coastal peoples. Obsidian from volcanic sources in the northwest was carried long distances, and dentalium shells from the Pacific Coast reached the interior, where they were valued. Hides, furs, dried foods, babiche, and finished goods such as clothing and snowshoes moved along rivers and across passes, and from the Great Lakes, copper and other materials circulated in older periods, as Chapters 7 and 12 explain. Knowledge, songs, ceremonial practices, and marriage partners moved with goods, so trade corridors were also diplomatic and political corridors.

Several exchange systems can be distinguished. In the northwest, Tlingit families controlled the major passes between the coast and the Yukon interior and acted as intermediaries between Pacific Coast goods, such as dentalium and the products of coastal trade, and interior goods, such as furs, hides, and copper; Chapter 33 treats the Tlingit themselves. In the Mackenzie delta and along the Arctic coast, Gwich'in and Dene communities exchanged with Inuvialuit and Iñupiat peoples at seasonal meeting places, trading goods from the forest for goods from the coast. In the east, Innu, Cree, and Anishinaabe communities exchanged goods along the Atlantic coast, the St. Lawrence, and the Great Lakes, linking to the world described in Chapter 27. Cree and Assiniboine communities traded with Plains peoples at the grassland margin, as described in Chapter 29.

Exchange was accompanied by diplomacy and sometimes by conflict. Accounts and oral traditions record alliances sealed through marriage and ceremony, truces, and also warfare between neighboring peoples, including Cree and Dene, Dene and Inuit, and Gwich'in and Inuit. Students should avoid stereotypes: conflict was one dimension of relationships that also included long periods of trade and intermarriage, and our knowledge of earlier warfare is limited by sources that mostly postdate European contact. When European goods began to arrive, they traveled along existing routes, sometimes reaching peoples long before Europeans did, as Chapter 1 observed. Indigenous intermediaries, including Cree and Assiniboine communities, often controlled access to goods, and the effect of the trade on northern societies was shaped by these existing systems. Chapter 46 treats the fur trade as a historical system, Chapter 52 the emergence of Métis communities, and Chapter 82 the numbered treaties that later affected much of the western Subarctic.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Shaped Northern Exchange?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns scholars and communities have documented. They are not quotations from any named individual.

Interior Dene and Tutchone communities: Exchange brought needed materials, such as shell, copper, and obsidian, and maintained relationships with coastal partners. It was part of a world of kin and obligation, and reciprocity and marriage were as important as the goods themselves.
Coastal Tlingit families: Control of passes and trading routes gave coastal families wealth and influence, and their role as intermediaries was protected through alliances, marriage, and ceremony. They had an interest in keeping others from reaching interior partners directly.
Inuvialuit and Iñupiat communities on the Arctic coast: Seasonal meetings supplied inland goods, such as hides and wood products, in return for goods of the sea, and they also created chances for conflict, so that trust had to be maintained through established practices.
Cree and Assiniboine intermediaries in the east and center: Position between forest and grassland, and later between Europeans and interior peoples, gave them influence over the movement of goods, which they tried to keep.
Later European observers: Traders and explorers described exchange mainly in terms of what interested them, such as furs and routes, and often overlooked its older social and ceremonial dimensions. These groups did not have equal power or information, and the source record privileges the outsiders' view.
Cause and Effect

A Connected Northern Interior

Long-term causes

Resources, such as copper, obsidian, shell, and certain foods, were unevenly distributed; river and lake systems made travel possible; and flexible kin networks and mobile lifeways created habits of travel and relationship.

Short-term causes

Seasonal gatherings, marriage alliances, and the demand for particular goods created occasions for exchange, and fluctuating local food supplies encouraged reliance on distant partners.

Immediate trigger or context

The arrival of European goods and traders, beginning on the eastern edge in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, added new items and new incentives to systems already in operation.

Immediate effects

Existing routes carried new goods, sometimes ahead of the Europeans themselves, and intermediary peoples gained influence, shifting the balance among neighbors.

Long-term consequences

The trade reshaped northern economies, movement, and relationships in ways examined in Chapters 46 and 52 and later chapters on treaties and land.

Historical complexity

No single cause produced northern exchange. Ecology, technology, kinship, diplomacy, and choice all contributed, and later European demand cannot be treated as its origin.
Section 34.20

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the northern interior was a politically and geographically organized Indigenous world in which mobility was a strategy. Before moving on, students should be able to explain how Cree, Innu, Dene, and other northern communities used seasonal movement, kinship, technology, and knowledge of land to live and to maintain relationships across great distances; why the labels "band," "nomad," and "Athabaskan" must be used with care; and how exchange connected the interior to the Northwest Coast, Arctic, Plains, and Great Lakes before sustained European commerce. Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Indigenous peoples of the Subarctic and northern interior of North America, a region stretching from Labrador to interior Alaska. It has argued that this region was not an isolated wilderness but a known, named, and politically organized world in which mobility was a sophisticated strategy, and that the low population density of the region does not imply low historical complexity.

The chapter began with the deep history of the region, bridging from Chapter 11. Evidence from archaeology, including the Yukon ice patches and the Taltheilei tradition, from linguistics, from geology, and from oral tradition shows long continuity of land use together with technological and environmental change. Historians must be careful not to assign ancient archaeological traditions mechanically to later named peoples, and not to read twentieth-century ethnography back into earlier centuries.

The environment of the boreal forest, with its rivers, lakes, muskeg, fire, long winters, and fluctuating animal populations, created both opportunities and constraints, and it varied greatly from the Labrador plateau to the Hudson Bay lowlands, the Mackenzie basin, the Yukon uplands, and interior Alaska. Northern peoples responded with diversified diets, flexible movement, and extensive sharing, not with a single environmental formula.

Two language families, Algonquian and Athabaskan, structure much of the region's human geography. The Innu, Naskapi, and Cree speak related Algonquian languages, and the Dene peoples speak Athabaskan languages. The chapter has emphasized that neither Cree nor Dene nor Innu denotes a single centralized polity, and that "Athabaskan" and "Dene" are not perfect synonyms. Names given by outsiders and names used by communities need to be distinguished.

The regional sections surveyed the Innu and Naskapi, the eastern, central, and western Cree, the Dënesųłıné, the Tłı̨chǫ, Sahtu, and Dehcho of the Mackenzie basin, the Gwich'in, Hän, Tutchone, and Kaska of the Yukon, and the Athabaskan peoples of interior Alaska. Each had its own resource base, relationships, and history, and each was linked to neighbors by rivers, trails, and kinship.

The thematic sections examined caribou, fish, moose, waterfowl, small game, and plants, and corrected the tendency to overemphasize big-game hunting and caribou in particular. They also treated northern technology, including snowshoes, toboggans, canoes, nets, clothing, and shelter, as accumulated knowledge held and transmitted by skilled people.

The chapter's central analytical section argued that seasonal mobility was organized, with families following known routes between recurring resources and holding rights and relationships through a landscape that was named and narrated. The corrective is that mobility did not mean rootlessness. Leadership, kinship, and political community rested on flexible local groups, marriage networks, and the authority of knowledge, skill, and persuasion, an organization that the category "band" can obscure.

Gender, labor, family life, and spiritual life were also treated. Women's work in hide preparation, sewing, food processing, and camp life was central to survival, and children and elders were integral to the transmission of knowledge. Religious life involved relationships with animals and places, dreams, ritual specialists, narratives, and hunting ethics, and it varied among peoples.

The synthesis section traced the rivers of exchange that linked the interior to the Northwest Coast, the Arctic, the Plains, and the Great Lakes through copper, obsidian, shell, hides, foods, knowledge, and marriage. Trade routes were diplomatic corridors, and European commerce entered and transformed older systems rather than creating northern trade.

The chapter's Historical Debate asked how old the territorial patterns recorded in early sources are, and the Primary Source Window used Paul Le Jeune's account of a winter with an Innu hunting group to illustrate both the value and the limits of outsider evidence. The chapter ends at the threshold of the commercial transformation, which Chapter 46 treats, and leaves Arctic peoples to Chapter 35 and Métis ethnogenesis to Chapter 52.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a persistent image: the northern forest as an empty space waiting for traders. The Indigenous peoples of the Subarctic lived in a world they knew in great detail, and they organized their lives, relationships, and political communities around movement through it. Their history shows that complexity can take forms other than permanent towns and ranked houses.

The chapter also reveals how connected the continent was. The same rivers that carried families between fishing places carried copper, shells, hides, and ideas between the forests and the Pacific Coast, the Arctic, the Plains, and the Great Lakes. Understanding the northern interior as part of this web prepares students for Chapter 36, which synthesizes Indigenous North America as a continental system.

Finally, the chapter points forward. The commercial and political changes of later centuries, treated in Chapters 46, 52, and 82, entered a world with its own institutions and its own knowledge, and many northern communities today draw on that knowledge in managing land, animals, and water. Continuities of place and relationship survived the transformations of colonization, and they remain visible in the continuing stewardship of caribou, fisheries, and landscapes. The northern interior is therefore not a prelude to someone else's history but a continuing Indigenous world.
