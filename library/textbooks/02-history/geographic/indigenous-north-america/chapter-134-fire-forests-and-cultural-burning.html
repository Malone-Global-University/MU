Chapter Opening

On February 27, 2025, the Karuk Tribe of northern California and the California Natural Resources Agency announced the first agreement of its kind under a new state law recognizing tribal authority to conduct cultural burns. The announcement was new. The practice it concerned was not: for generations before any state government existed, Karuk families had set fire to the landscapes of the mid-Klamath River country in order to feed, clothe, heal, and govern themselves, and for generations afterward colonial dispossession, fire restrictions, and loss of access constrained their ability to continue that practice.

That sequence, from governing fire, to being forbidden to use it, to negotiating the legal terms on which it may be used again, is the subject of this chapter. It is one of the clearest windows available into how Indigenous environmental authority was built, dismantled, and is now being reconstructed. Fire is easily imagined as a pure force of destruction, and in the twenty-first century the images that dominate public memory are of burned towns, smoke-filled valleys, and evacuations. Yet across much of North America, fire was also a tool. Indigenous communities applied it deliberately, in particular seasons, in particular places, and for particular reasons, to encourage food plants, to renew the shoots used in weaving, to open travel routes, to improve forage and hunting grounds, to clear brush near villages, and to fulfill ceremonial responsibilities. In this sense fire was not only an ecological process acting on Indigenous societies. It was part of how Indigenous societies acted on, and cared for, the land.

Chapter 133 examined the knowledge systems through which Indigenous peoples observed, understood, and governed environments. This chapter follows one of the most consequential applications of that knowledge through time. It begins with the long history of Indigenous fire stewardship and the ecological conditions that made it varied from region to region. It then follows the colonial encounter: the interpretation of managed landscapes as untouched wilderness, the passage of laws and the creation of institutions that restricted Indigenous burning, and the rise of scientific forestry and fire suppression in the United States, Canada, and Mexico. It examines what happened to ecosystems, to subsistence, and to the transmission of knowledge when fire was excluded, and it turns finally to the contemporary resurgence of Indigenous-led cultural burning and the legal and political struggles over who holds the right to burn.

The chapter's central argument is that the contemporary restoration of cultural burning is both an ecological transformation and a reconstruction of Indigenous environmental authority. Returning flame to a hillside is not by itself the whole story. What must also be returned, or built anew, are the relationships, knowledge, institutions, training, and legal standing through which Indigenous peoples historically decided where, when, why, and by whom a landscape would be burned. This is why cultural burning cannot be reduced to a technique for reducing wildfire risk, valuable as that effect may be, and why this textbook treats it in a volume devoted to sovereignty and resurgence.

The chapter also asks students to practice careful historical reasoning about a subject that attracts both exaggeration and dismissal. Some popular accounts imply that Indigenous peoples created every open meadow and prevented every large fire; some official histories treated Indigenous burning as primitive carelessness. The evidence supports neither extreme. Lightning, climate, soils, and grazing animals also shaped fire regimes, and fire effects differ sharply between a ponderosa pine forest, a tallgrass prairie, a coastal rainforest, and a boreal spruce stand. Historians and ecologists reconstruct past fire from tree rings, charcoal in lake sediments, archaeological sites, early written accounts, and Indigenous oral histories and living practice. Each source has strengths and limits, and the chapter examines them explicitly in section 134.18.

The history told here is continental. It draws on case studies from California, the Pacific Northwest and Plateau, the Great Plains and Eastern Woodlands, British Columbia and other parts of Canada, and the Sierra Madre of Mexico, while respecting the fact that the United States, Canada, and Mexico developed different legal frameworks for land, forests, and Indigenous peoples. The chapter takes care not to treat any one region as representative of all. Karuk and Yurok cultural burning receives extended attention because it has been unusually well documented and has been central to recent legal change, but it is one regional case among many.

Students should also note what this chapter does not attempt. It does not offer a comprehensive ancient history of California or the Plains (Chapters 9 and 29), a full ethnographic account of regional peoples (Chapters 32 and 33), or a general treatment of Indigenous ecological knowledge (Chapter 133). It does not provide a history of bison destruction (Chapter 81), of reservation and reserve administration (Chapter 84), or of climate adaptation in the Arctic (Chapter 138). Nor is it a forestry manual. It is a history of fire as a cultural, ecological, and institutional subject, and of the Indigenous communities that have lived with fire, been barred from it, and are working to bring it home.

Finally, the chapter prepares students for what follows. Fire-managed landscapes were also habitats, and Chapter 135 turns from fire to the stewardship of fisheries, wildlife, and treaty-protected living resources, while Chapters 136 through 138 examine extraction, infrastructure, and climate change. Together they show that the contemporary struggle over the right to burn belongs to a wider pattern in which Indigenous nations assert environmental jurisdiction not as a metaphor but as an exercise of political authority over land, water, and life.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Indigenous peoples used deliberately applied fire to shape landscapes, food systems, and settlements, and why precolonial North America cannot accurately be described as untouched wilderness.
  2. Distinguish among cultural burning, prescribed burning, wildfire, agricultural burning, fire stewardship, fire suppression, and fire exclusion, and explain why the distinctions are historical and political as well as technical.
  3. Compare Indigenous fire stewardship across major ecological regions, including California, the Pacific Northwest and Plateau, the Plains, the Eastern Woodlands, Canada, and Mexico.
  4. Describe the fire-related ecology of major North American ecosystems, including the difference between fire intensity and fire severity.
  5. Analyze how colonial laws, land administration, and reservation and reserve systems restricted Indigenous burning, distinguishing explicit prohibitions from other institutional barriers.
  6. Explain the economic, scientific, and political motives behind the rise of institutional fire suppression, including the significance of the Great Fire of 1910.
  7. Trace the social and cultural consequences of fire exclusion for Indigenous food, medicine, weaving, hunting, ceremony, and the transmission of knowledge.
  8. Assess the interacting causes of modern wildfire, avoiding explanations that attribute every large fire to a single factor.
  9. Evaluate the strengths and limits of fire-scar, charcoal, archaeological, documentary, and oral evidence for reconstructing past fire regimes and their human origins.
  10. Describe the institutions of the contemporary cultural fire resurgence, including the Karuk and Yurok programs, the Cultural Fire Management Council, and the Indigenous Peoples Burning Network.
  11. Interpret the legal and political significance of California Senate Bill 310 and of Canadian provincial and federal reforms, distinguishing recognition from the exercise of decision-making authority.
  12. Connect Indigenous fire stewardship to the broader themes of sovereignty, nationhood, and environmental governance in Volume X.

Historical Context

Three earlier developments in this textbook form the immediate background to the chapter. The first is the long history of human adaptation to North American environments traced in Volumes I through III. By the time agriculture, towns, and regional exchange networks had spread across much of the continent, Indigenous communities had accumulated many centuries of observation about seasons, plants, animals, and weather, and had developed specialized economies suited to California oak woodlands, Plains grasslands, Northwest Coast rainforest, Southwestern pine forest, and Eastern Woodland river valleys. Fire was one of the tools, though not the only one, through which such knowledge was applied. The second development is the colonial and national expansion examined in Volumes IV through VII, which removed Indigenous communities from much of their territory, restructured land tenure, and placed forests, grasslands, and parks under the authority of settler governments. The third is the assimilation and administration of Indigenous communities examined in Volumes VIII and IX, which further constrained Indigenous economic and ceremonial life.

Volume X shifts attention to the period in which Indigenous nations have pursued self-determination, legal recognition, and cultural revitalization. Within Part XXIII, Chapter 133 introduced Indigenous environmental knowledge as a system of observation, responsibility, and governance rather than a collection of isolated facts. Chapter 134 applies that framework to a single, highly visible domain. This arrangement is deliberate. Fire shows with unusual clarity how a land-management practice can be simultaneously ecological, economic, spiritual, and political, and how its suppression can have effects extending far beyond the vegetation it targets.

Students should keep several ecological points in mind from the start. Fire is a natural process in many North American ecosystems, ignited by lightning and shaped by climate, fuel, and topography. Human ignition, whether Indigenous or later, interacted with those natural processes rather than replacing them. A fire regime, which describes the typical frequency, seasonality, intensity, and extent of fire in a given ecosystem, varies enormously: frequent low-intensity surface fires characterize some pine forests and grasslands, while some coastal rainforests and boreal stands burn rarely but severely. Consequently, the same act, whether burning or excluding fire, can have very different consequences in different places. The chapter returns to these distinctions repeatedly, because the most common errors in popular accounts, whether romanticizing Indigenous burning or condemning it, arise from ignoring them.

A second set of points concerns evidence and language. Many Indigenous communities maintain oral histories, place names, ceremonies, and living practices that carry knowledge about fire. Written records, including accounts by explorers, missionaries, surveyors, and officials, sometimes describe Indigenous burning but were written by outsiders with their own purposes. Natural archives such as tree rings and lake sediments record that fire occurred without necessarily recording who lit it or why. The vocabulary used for burning also carries history. Terms such as "prescribed fire" and "fuel reduction" belong to forestry and emergency management, whereas "cultural burning" and "fire stewardship" are used by many Indigenous practitioners and some governments to name practices defined by Indigenous knowledge, purposes, and authority. This chapter tries to use each term carefully and to explain the stakes of the distinctions.

Third, the chapter is continental but not uniform. In the United States, Indigenous nations hold a distinct political status described in federal Indian law (Chapters 93 and 118), and cultural burning intersects with federal land agencies, state air-quality regulation, and tribal trust lands. In Canada, the relevant frameworks include Aboriginal and treaty rights, provincial jurisdiction over forests and wildfire, and the federal management of national parks (Chapter 117). In Mexico, the agrarian institutions of ejidos and Indigenous communities, along with national forestry and fire policy, form a distinct setting (Chapter 141). The chapter keeps these differences visible instead of applying the legal vocabulary of any one country to the others.

Core Vocabulary Preview

The following terms are defined here for orientation and revisited in the Key Terms section. Students should be alert to the distinction between technical forestry vocabulary and the cultural and political concepts Indigenous practitioners use.

Cultural burning
Intentional application of fire guided by Indigenous knowledge, values, purposes, and authority, with cultural as well as ecological objectives.
Fire stewardship
The broader relationship of care, responsibility, and management between a community and fire-influenced landscapes, of which burning is one part.
Prescribed fire
Fire applied under a plan for stated objectives, such as fuel reduction or ecological restoration, usually under agency or landowner authority.
Fire regime
The characteristic frequency, season, intensity, severity, and extent of fire in an ecosystem over long periods.
Fire suppression
The active extinguishing or control of fires, institutionalized in twentieth-century forestry agencies.
Fire exclusion
The prolonged keeping of fire out of a landscape, whether by suppression, prohibition, or loss of those who burned it.
Fire ecology
The scientific study of relationships between fire and living organisms and environments.
Cultural landscape
A landscape shaped by sustained interaction between human communities and natural processes.
Traditional ecological knowledge
Place-based knowledge of environments developed, tested, and transmitted within Indigenous communities across generations; a term used by scholars and some communities.
Ecological succession
The sequence of changes in plant communities after disturbance.
Fire intensity
The energy released by a fire, often measured at its flaming front.
Fire severity
The effects of fire on an ecosystem, such as tree mortality and soil change.
Fuel load
The quantity of combustible living and dead vegetation available to burn.
Fire-adapted ecosystem
An ecosystem whose species and structure have evolved with recurring fire.
Landscape mosaic
A patchwork of vegetation at different stages of recovery, created by fires of varied timing and extent.
Oak savanna
An open landscape of scattered oaks over grassland or prairie, often maintained by recurring fire.
Scientific forestry
The systematic management of forests as renewable resources, emerging in Europe and adopted in North America from the late nineteenth century.
Environmental dispossession
The loss of lands, resources, and management authority on which a people's way of life depended.
Wildland–urban interface
The zone where human development meets or intermingles with wildland vegetation.
Co-management
Shared decision-making authority between governments and Indigenous nations over lands or resources; distinct from consultation.
Dendrochronology
The dating of events through the study of tree rings.
Fire-scar analysis
Study of scars in tree rings to reconstruct dates and frequency of past surface fires.
Environmental jurisdiction
Legal authority to make and enforce rules about land, water, air, and living resources.
Ecocultural restoration
Restoration that aims to renew both ecological conditions and the cultural relationships and uses connected to them.
Intergenerational knowledge
Knowledge transmitted across generations through participation, teaching, story, and practice.
Division I

The Indigenous History of Fire

Fire as an instrument of landscape management, knowledge, and everyday life in Indigenous North America.

Section 134.1

Fire and the Making of Indigenous North America

Fire existed in North America long before people did. Lightning ignites fires in forests and grasslands every year, and the continent's vegetation bears the marks of millions of years of burning. The question for historians is therefore never whether fire was present, but how human beings entered an already fiery world and what they did with it. For Indigenous communities, the answer varied with place and time. In some environments, people relied chiefly on the natural fire cycle and sought to avoid or adapt to it. In others, they added deliberate ignitions to natural ones, shifting the timing, frequency, location, and effects of burning in ways that served particular purposes. Distinguishing naturally occurring fire from deliberately applied fire is one of the basic analytical tasks of this chapter.

Where Indigenous communities did apply fire, the purposes were practical, ecological, and cultural at once. Burning could renew grasses that attracted game, stimulate straight shoots of shrubs used in weaving, keep oak woodlands open so that acorn crops were easier to gather, reduce undergrowth that sheltered pests and hid enemies, and clear brush from trails and village margins. It could also reduce the buildup of dead vegetation that otherwise fed larger, hotter fires. Many of these effects were observed, tested, and refined across generations. Fire stewardship, as this chapter uses the term, therefore describes not a single act of ignition but a long relationship of attention, timing, and responsibility between people and particular places.

The seasonal rhythm of burning is a useful way to understand this relationship. In many regions, the choice of season determined the result. A burn set in late summer or autumn, when fuels were dry and the weather turning, would produce different effects from one set in spring when plants were beginning to grow. Practitioners paid attention to moisture, wind, the condition of particular plants, the behavior of animals, and the needs of communities. Because families and kin groups often held recognized responsibilities for particular places, decisions about burning were embedded in social relationships. The knowledge involved was neither accidental nor uniform. It was developed locally, adjusted over time, and, in many communities, taught through direct participation.

The consequences for landscapes were substantial in many places, though historians and ecologists debate their extent. Repeated burning can maintain open woodland and savanna, favor fire-tolerant trees such as oaks and certain pines, sustain prairie openings within forest, and create patchwork mosaics of vegetation at different stages of recovery. Such mosaics supported diverse plants and animals, including species that people hunted and gathered. Many of the landscapes that European observers described as parklike, open, or naturally productive had been shaped, in part, by this kind of management. The landscapes Europeans encountered were thus often cultural landscapes: places formed by long interaction between ecological processes and human decisions.

This insight undercuts the long-standing image of precolonial North America as untouched wilderness, a theme developed further in section 134.9. At the same time, the chapter resists the opposite exaggeration. Not every open landscape was created by Indigenous burning. Climate, soils, topography, lightning, and grazing animals also produced and maintained grasslands, savannas, and forest openings. In some regions, such as parts of the Pacific coastal rainforest, large fires were infrequent and human burning played a smaller role. Historians therefore speak of Indigenous influence on fire regimes as variable, strongest where evidence of repeated, purposeful burning is combined with ecological conditions that make such burning effective, and uncertain where the evidence is thin.

Historical evidence for these patterns comes from several sources, which later sections examine individually. Oral histories, ceremonies, place names, and living practices preserve Indigenous knowledge of burning and its purposes. Early written accounts by explorers, traders, missionaries, and surveyors sometimes record fires lit by Indigenous people, though often with limited understanding of their purposes. Tree rings and lake sediments record when fires occurred. Archaeological sites reveal what plants and animals people used and where. No single source can establish the whole story, and the strongest conclusions arise where several independent lines of evidence converge.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultural Landscape

Definition. A landscape whose ecological character has been shaped through sustained interaction between human communities and natural processes, so that its vegetation, wildlife, and layout reflect human knowledge, decisions, and labor as well as climate and soil.

Historical context. Indigenous communities across North America maintained cultural landscapes through burning, harvesting, planting, pruning, tending, and the management of access. European colonists often failed to recognize such management and described the resulting open woodlands, meadows, and prairies as natural abundance.

Why it matters. The concept allows historians to treat Indigenous land use as a form of environmental history and governance rather than as mere occupancy of a pristine natural world, and it explains why removing Indigenous management could change landscapes.

Potential misunderstanding. "Cultural landscape" does not mean that every feature of a landscape was created by people. It describes a relationship in which natural processes and human practice are both at work, in proportions that vary by place.

Section 134.2

Cultural Burning as an Indigenous Knowledge System

To understand why cultural burning has become central to Indigenous resurgence, it is essential to see it as a knowledge system, not merely a method. A knowledge system includes observation, concepts, vocabulary, techniques, rules of conduct, teaching methods, and the social authority that determines who may act. In the case of fire, these elements together answer questions that a purely technical manual would treat separately: when a place is ready to burn, who has the responsibility to burn it, which plants and animals depend on the result, what ceremonies or obligations accompany the act, and how the next generation will learn.

Environmental observation is the foundation. Practitioners attend to the state of vegetation, the moisture of leaves and soil, the phase of plant growth, the direction and strength of wind, the behavior of birds and animals, and the way fire has moved on the same ground in earlier years. This knowledge is highly local. A hillside above one village may require different timing and technique from a similar-looking slope a day's walk away, and the same practitioner may adjust burning from year to year as conditions change. Such precision is one reason Indigenous practitioners and some scientists stress that cultural burning cannot be simply transplanted from one place to another.

Authority and responsibility form the second pillar. In many communities, rights and duties regarding burning were associated with families, lineages, or particular roles, and decisions followed from relationships among people and places as well as from technical judgment. Some practitioners describe fire as part of reciprocal obligations to the land and to other living beings, in which people care for plants and animals that in turn sustain them. Language often carries these ideas. The Karuk word pikyav, often translated as "fix it," is cited by Karuk leaders to express the idea that people have an active responsibility to repair and maintain the world, and to do so through practice. Students should understand such terms as expressions of how particular communities understand fire, not as universal Indigenous doctrine.

Education and intergenerational transmission form the third. Children and young adults learned by accompanying elders, by gathering and processing the plants fire produced, by observing burns, and by participating in ceremonies and seasonal work. Knowledge was transmitted through stories, songs, place names, and direct practice. When laws and policies later barred communities from burning, they interrupted this chain of transmission as well as the burning itself, a consequence examined in section 134.12.

The vocabulary used in public discussion can obscure these dimensions, so the distinctions among terms deserve careful attention. Cultural burning, as used by many Indigenous practitioners and, increasingly, by governments, refers to the intentional application of fire guided by Indigenous knowledge, cultural purposes, and authority. Prescribed burning is a forestry and land-management term for fire applied under a plan, usually with defined objectives such as reducing fuels or restoring ecological conditions, and usually conducted under agency or landowner authority. Wildfire is unplanned fire. Agricultural burning refers to fire used to clear fields or crop residue, including the swidden or milpa systems of some Indigenous farming communities. Fire stewardship is the broader relationship of care, of which burning is one part. Fire suppression is the active extinguishing of fires, and fire exclusion is the broader condition in which fire is kept out of a landscape for long periods, whether through suppression, prohibition, or the loss of those who burned it. Parks Canada, for instance, now distinguishes cultural burning, described as Indigenous-led and knowledge-driven, from prescribed fire, described as agency-driven and guided by hazard reduction or ecological objectives.

These distinctions matter in practice. An Indigenous nation and a forestry agency may both light fire on the same slope on the same day, and the ecological result may look similar. But the legal authority, the purposes, the decision-making, and the relationship to community may differ completely. A government burn conducted to protect timber is not thereby a restoration of Indigenous stewardship, and a cultural burn conducted to nurture basketry materials may also reduce fuels without being reducible to that outcome. Chapter 134's argument depends on keeping these distinctions in view.

Finally, knowledge systems are not static. Indigenous fire knowledge has always adapted to changing climates, species, technologies, and social conditions. Contemporary practitioners use drip torches, radios, and weather forecasts alongside older methods, and they integrate observations of new conditions, such as invasive plants, altered wildlife, and hotter, drier seasons. Adaptation does not diminish authenticity; it reflects the experimental character of living knowledge, as Chapter 133 emphasized.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultural Burning

Definition. The intentional application of fire to land guided by the knowledge, values, purposes, and authority of Indigenous communities. Definitions vary among nations and legal systems, and Indigenous practitioners often emphasize that cultural objectives, such as nurturing food, medicine, and weaving plants, and fulfilling ceremonial and territorial responsibilities, are inseparable from ecological ones.

Historical context. Cultural burning was practiced in many forms across North America for generations before colonization, was restricted or prohibited by colonial and national governments from the nineteenth century onward, persisted in family and community practice, and has been revived through Indigenous-led programs since the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries.

Why it matters. The term identifies a practice in which authority and purpose are Indigenous. That is why the recent legal recognition of cultural burning in California and British Columbia raises questions of sovereignty rather than only of forestry.

Potential misunderstanding. Cultural burning is not simply "prescribed burning by Indigenous people," nor is it a synonym for fuel reduction. Using the terms interchangeably can erase the cultural and political dimensions that give the practice its meaning.

Section 134.3

Fire Ecology and the Diversity of North American Landscapes

Fire ecology is the study of how fire interacts with living things and environments. A basic grounding in it is necessary because the effects of fire, whether lit by lightning, by Indigenous practitioners, or by settlers, depend on the ecosystem. The central concept is the fire regime, a description of how fire typically behaves in a given area over long periods, including its frequency, seasonality, intensity, severity, and spatial extent. Regimes differ so widely that a practice beneficial in one ecosystem may be damaging in another.

Two terms are often confused. Fire intensity refers to the energy released by a fire, often measured at the flaming front. Fire severity refers to the effects of fire on an ecosystem, such as the proportion of trees killed or the degree to which soil is altered. A fast-moving surface fire in dry grass may be intense for a brief moment but have low severity for the trees above it, while a crown fire that kills most of a forest's trees has high severity. These distinctions help explain how frequent low-severity burns may protect an ecosystem from rarer high-severity fires.

Other concepts follow. Fuel load is the amount of combustible material, living and dead, available to burn. Ecological succession describes the sequence by which plant communities change over time after disturbance. Fire-adapted ecosystems contain species that tolerate or depend on fire, such as thick-barked trees that survive surface fires, serotinous cones that open in heat, or resprouting shrubs and grasses. A landscape mosaic is a patchwork of vegetation at different ages and conditions, produced by fires of varying timing and extent. Understory refers to the vegetation beneath the main tree canopy, which frequent surface fire tends to thin.

Applying these ideas to a continental survey shows the range. In the coniferous and mixed forests of the Pacific Northwest, the western side of the Cascades and the coastal rainforest are often characterized by long intervals between fires, many of them severe, while drier interior and southern valley forests experienced more frequent fires. California's oak woodlands, grasslands, and mixed-conifer mountain forests include areas historically characterized by frequent, relatively low-severity fire, as well as chaparral shrublands that naturally burn at high severity at longer intervals. Southwestern ponderosa pine forests are widely studied as systems adapted to frequent surface fires. Great Plains grasslands depend on a combination of fire, grazing, and drought to keep woody plants in check. In the Southeast, longleaf pine ecosystems are among the most fire-dependent communities on the continent. Eastern woodlands included oak and pine communities that benefited from periodic burning, alongside forests where fire was less frequent. Canada's boreal forest is characterized by large, often severe fires at long intervals, a very different pattern from the surface-fire regimes of drier pine forests. In Mexico, the pine-oak forests of the Sierra Madre Occidental include regions where research documents frequent surface fire regimes.

These contrasts have an important implication for the history of Indigenous burning. The potential for Indigenous ignition to shape vegetation is greatest where ecological conditions already permit frequent fire and where plants of cultural importance respond to it. In ecosystems where fires are naturally rare and severe, as in some boreal or coastal forests, Indigenous communities used fire for specific local purposes, such as clearing travel routes, berry patches, or camp areas, but it would be inaccurate to describe regional forests as engineered by burning. The ecological background therefore sets limits on the claims historians can responsibly make.

Historical Vocabulary
Fire Regime

Definition. The characteristic pattern of fire in an ecosystem over long periods, including frequency, season, intensity, severity, size, and spatial pattern.

Historical context. Scientists reconstruct historical fire regimes from tree rings, charcoal, and other records, and from written and oral accounts. Human activity, including Indigenous burning and later fire exclusion, has modified regimes in many places.

Why it matters. The concept explains why fire exclusion can be harmful in a pine forest that evolved with frequent surface fire, yet may have little effect in a forest that historically burned at long intervals.

Potential misunderstanding. A regime is a statistical pattern, not a rule for any given fire. Climate variability can shift regimes over time, and natural and human influences may overlap.

Section 134.4

Fire, Food Systems, Materials, and Everyday Life

The meaning of fire stewardship becomes concrete when we look at the work it supported. Burning was embedded in the annual rounds of gathering, hunting, farming, craft production, and ceremony through which communities lived. Because much of this work was carried out by women, children, elders, and specialists whose labor rarely appears in official documents, social history requires reading evidence about fire alongside evidence about plants, tools, kinship, and seasonal movement.

Food production is the clearest example. Acorns, a staple in much of California and parts of the Pacific Northwest, came from oak and tanoak trees whose productivity and accessibility depended partly on the condition of the surrounding woodland. Burning could reduce competing vegetation, limit certain insect pests in nuts, clear the ground for gathering, and favor oaks over encroaching conifers. Berry-producing plants such as huckleberries and blueberries responded to periodic fire in several regions, as did camas and other edible bulbs in prairie and savanna settings. Seeds, greens, and root crops were encouraged in burned patches. In agricultural communities, fire cleared fields and returned nutrients to soil, whether in the swidden systems of southern Mexico or in the fields of Eastern Woodland farming towns.

Medicinal and ceremonial plants were also tied to burning. Many communities used plants that thrive in recently burned or periodically burned places and relied on their availability for healing and ritual. Fire could create the conditions for such plants to grow in accessible patches rather than scattered thinly across dense forest. The loss of fire, as later sections show, could therefore reduce access to medicines even when the plants themselves had not disappeared entirely.

Craft materials were a third domain. Basket weavers across California and the Pacific Northwest depended on straight, pliable shoots, roots, and grasses of particular qualities. Hazel sprouts that emerged after fire, for example, can be long and straight, suitable for the foundational structure of baskets, and weavers of the Karuk, Yurok, Hupa, and other nations have described burning as essential to securing materials of the right quality. Research on Karuk and Yurok communities has examined these links between cultural burning and basketry resources. Basketry was not only craft: baskets stored and processed food, carried water, served as ceremonial regalia and gifts, and expressed identity and artistry across generations.

Fire also shaped the use of land for animals. Burned meadows and forest edges often produced fresh growth that attracted deer, elk, and other game, concentrating them where hunters could find them, and open ground improved sightlines and mobility. In grasslands, burning could renew forage for grazing animals. Travel corridors and gathering places were kept open, and the clearing of brush around settlements reduced hazards from large, uncontrolled fires and from concealed enemies or animals. Ceremonial grounds and sacred places were likewise maintained through the care of fire.

People of different ages and roles participated differently. Elders held and shared knowledge of timing, plants, and place. Women, who commonly were the primary gatherers and weavers in many communities, had direct and detailed knowledge of the plants that burning supported, and contemporary Indigenous women's leadership in cultural fire, discussed in section 134.16, builds on this foundation. Hunters understood the responses of animals to burned ground. Children learned by helping gather, watching burns, and absorbing stories and rules. Specialists, including ceremonial leaders and persons recognized for particular knowledge, might guide when and where fire was set. The interdependence of these roles meant that the interruption of fire practice would ripple outward through food, craft, health, and ceremony.

This is also the point at which social history should avoid romantic simplification. Burning involved risk, labor, and sometimes disagreement. Fires could escape, weather could shift, and communities had to coordinate with neighbors whose territories might be affected. Some societies had rules about responsibility for escaped fire, and burning in some places was shared among communities in ways that required negotiation. Fire stewardship was a demanding and skilled practice, not an effortless harmony with nature.

A patch of woodland is burned in a particular season; the following year, hazel shoots emerge straight and flexible; weavers gather and prepare them; baskets are made that will later hold acorn flour, carry water, or be given in ceremony. Meanwhile, the same burn clears ground for acorn gathering, encourages berries or bulbs, and draws game to fresh growth. Each step involves different people: those who decide when to burn, those who tend the fire, those who gather, those who process, and those who teach the young.

Because historical documents rarely describe this chain in detail, historians reconstruct it from ethnography, archaeology, oral history, and the testimony of present-day practitioners, taking care to distinguish what is documented for a particular community from what is inferred by analogy. Regional Systems of Indigenous Fire Stewardship

Case studies from California, the Pacific Northwest and Plateau, the Plains and Eastern Woodlands, Canada, and Mexico.

Section 134.5

Cultural Burning in California

California is among the best-documented settings for the study of Indigenous fire stewardship, partly because of the richness of the ecological record and partly because of the sustained work of Indigenous communities and researchers. The state's environmental diversity, ranging from coastal redwood forests and interior oak woodlands to Sierra Nevada conifer forests and Mediterranean-climate shrublands, was matched by an extraordinary variety of Indigenous nations and languages (Chapters 9 and 32). When the United States acquired California from Mexico in 1848 under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, and California subsequently entered the Union in 1850, the region was home to hundreds of Indigenous communities, and each had its own relationship to local landscapes. It is therefore important to avoid speaking of a single "California Indian" fire practice.

Within that diversity, certain patterns recur. Oak and tanoak woodlands provided acorns that, once processed, supplied a major portion of the diet for many communities. Grasslands and woodland margins supplied seeds, bulbs, greens, and game. Mountain forests supplied materials, medicines, and hunting grounds. Burning, applied at appropriate seasons and intervals, helped sustain these resources. Historians and ecologists have drawn on ethnographic records, early travelers' descriptions, tree-ring and charcoal studies, and the knowledge of present-day practitioners to reconstruct such systems, and the case for substantial Indigenous influence on fire in many California landscapes is among the stronger ones, though the balance of human and lightning ignition varies by location and period.

The Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa peoples of the Klamath and Trinity River regions illustrate how fire, subsistence, and craft were bound together. Their homelands include steep forested mountains, river corridors, prairies, and oak and tanoak stands. Research involving Karuk and Yurok communities has examined how burning supported basketry materials such as hazel and beargrass, acorn-bearing tanoak and oak stands, and other plants of nutritional, medicinal, and ceremonial importance. Karuk leaders emphasize that burning was integrated with fishing, gathering, ceremony, and village life along the Klamath River, and that fire stewardship formed part of the legal and social order, not merely a side practice.

The governance dimension deserves emphasis. Karuk natural resource leaders have argued that cultural burning is an exercise of responsibility to ecological relationships and a right rooted in inherent sovereignty, an argument that undergirds the political demands examined in section 134.19. For the historian, the important point is that fire practice in these communities was inseparable from social organization, ceremony, and territorial responsibility. The ceremonial cycle, the fishing and gathering seasons, and the maintenance of landscapes proceeded together.

California Indigenous burning did not, of course, exist in a vacuum or in unchanging form. Populations fluctuated; climate shifted; communities migrated, traded, and intermarried. The landscapes encountered by Spanish missionaries along the coast beginning in the late eighteenth century, and by Anglo-American migrants after the Gold Rush, were products of this long history. What followed, in the middle of the nineteenth century, was a catastrophic rupture: violence, disease, forced labor, and displacement during and after the Gold Rush (Chapter 70) removed or killed enormous numbers of Indigenous people and separated survivors from their places. State and federal law, and later forestry institutions, then restricted the return of fire, a story told in Division III. For now, students should hold in mind the scale of what had existed before this rupture and the fact that fire knowledge persisted, in families and communities, through it.

Regional Case Study

Indigenous California's Fire-Managed Landscapes

The mid-Klamath region offers a case in which fire, food, craft, and governance can be examined together. Evidence includes oral history and the testimony of practitioners; ethnographic accounts of basketry and acorn use; tree-ring records of historical fire frequency; and, in recent decades, collaborative research with Karuk and Yurok communities on the ecological effects of burning on basketry resources and other cultural plants.

What the case shows. Frequent, locally timed fire supported specific plant communities on which households depended, and the people who tended those communities held recognized knowledge and responsibilities.

What the case does not show. It does not prove that all open vegetation in California resulted from Indigenous burning, nor that every community used fire in the same way. Historians therefore pair the Klamath case with other regional evidence from the Sierra Nevada foothills, coastal prairies, and Central Valley margins before generalizing.

Section 134.6

Fire Stewardship in the Pacific Northwest and Interior Plateau

North of California, fire stewardship took different forms as the landscape changed. The Pacific Northwest includes wet coastal rainforests, inland valleys, mountain ranges, and the dry interior Columbia Plateau. In the rainforests west of the Cascades, large fires are historically infrequent and often severe, and Indigenous communities there, including many Northwest Coast peoples treated in Chapter 33, relied principally on marine resources, cedar, and other forest products, using fire locally for specific purposes. By contrast, in the inland valleys and lowlands, and in the drier forests and mountain meadows east of the crest, burning was more consequential.

The Willamette Valley of Oregon is the most frequently cited example. Early nineteenth-century observers described open prairies and oak savannas, and later ecological and historical studies, together with Kalapuya oral history, indicate that these landscapes were maintained in part by repeated burning. The Kalapuya and neighboring peoples relied on camas bulbs, tarweed seeds, hazelnuts, acorns, and game that flourished in these habitats. Without periodic burning, Douglas fir and other conifers tend to invade and shade out prairie and oak savanna, which is part of the historical interpretation of what happened when burning ceased. A similar logic applies to prairies and Garry oak savannas of the Puget Lowland and southern Vancouver Island, where Coast Salish communities and others used and tended camas meadows and other food-producing prairies.

On the Columbia Plateau and the eastern slopes of the Cascades, Indigenous communities such as the Yakama, Nez Perce, Warm Springs peoples, and others used fire in association with seasonal rounds that took families from river fisheries to upland root-digging grounds and berry fields. The mountain huckleberry, a prized food, is often described as responding to fire and being maintained in open subalpine meadows, and Indigenous communities in the region have long regarded huckleberry fields as places of gathering, ceremony, and social meeting. Burning in these contexts was seasonal and purposeful, and the fields were also sites of intertribal gathering, which reminds us that fire-maintained landscapes served social as well as economic functions.

Hunting was another consideration. Open woodlands and meadows improved visibility and forage, and burning could manage the movement and abundance of deer and elk. In some mountain areas, fire kept travel routes open, linking summer and winter settlements and connecting communities across ranges.

The historical argument for Indigenous maintenance of prairies and savannas in this region draws on several lines of evidence: early explorers' and settlers' descriptions of burning; ethnographic records; botanical and ecological studies showing that these vegetation types are quickly lost to conifer encroachment without disturbance; and Indigenous oral history and practice. The interpretation is widely accepted for the Willamette Valley, but even here researchers differ about the frequency and extent of burning and the relative contribution of climate and other factors. For other areas, evidence is thinner, and responsible claims are accordingly more cautious.

Fire, Prairie Ecosystems, and Indigenous Food Production

Prairies and oak savannas in the Pacific Northwest are species-rich habitats that support camas, wildflowers, grasses, and pollinators, and many are now among the region's most threatened ecosystems. Their persistence in the nineteenth century depended on processes that kept trees from taking over. Indigenous burning was one such process, and its decline, together with settlement, agriculture, and fire suppression, contributed to the shrinking of these habitats.

Today, Indigenous nations and conservation organizations in the region work on prairie restoration that includes prescribed or cultural burning, though restoration projects vary in who holds authority and in how much they involve Indigenous knowledge. Section 134.7

Fire, Grasslands, and the Eastern Woodlands

East of the Rockies, the relationship between fire and landscape involved different ecosystems and a different mixture of human and natural influences. The Great Plains are a grassland region shaped by climate, with limited rainfall favoring grasses over trees, by the grazing of bison and other animals, and by fire, both lightning-caused and human-set. Because grasslands regrow rapidly after burning, fire can renew forage, remove accumulated dead material, and discourage the spread of woody plants. Historical accounts by travelers record Indigenous burning of prairies, and many Plains nations understood and used the effects of fire on grazing animals. Ecologists today describe the interaction of fire and grazing as a key driver of grassland structure.

Plains burning raises the problem of attribution. Because lightning ignites many grassland fires and because the climate itself favors grassland, it would be inaccurate to say that Indigenous peoples created the Plains. The more defensible claim is that Indigenous burning influenced the timing, location, and frequency of fire within a grassland system already shaped by climate and grazing, and that this influence mattered most in the grassland margins where forest and prairie meet. Burning also served military, signaling, and hunting purposes, which were distinct from ecological management, and this chapter does not attempt to treat the Plains economy, covered in Chapters 29 and 49, in full.

In the Great Lakes region, fire interacted with pine, oak, and mixed forests and with openings used for gathering and agriculture. Anishinaabe, Menominee, Ho-Chunk, Potawatomi, and other communities used fire in various ways, including for clearing agricultural fields, maintaining blueberry grounds, and managing hunting areas. Dendrochronological and sedimentary research in the region has examined whether fire frequency rose and fell with human population and land use, and some studies have documented associations between Indigenous presence and fire history and between Indigenous depopulation and changes in forest composition. These findings are suggestive, but the relationships are complicated by climate variation, and researchers continue to refine the evidence.

Southeastern North America provides a further case. The longleaf pine ecosystem, one of the most fire-dependent communities on the continent, historically burned frequently, from lightning and from human ignition. Eighteenth-century naturalists and travelers such as William Bartram described Native burning in the region, and Muscogee, Cherokee, Choctaw, Seminole, and other peoples used fire to keep woods open, encourage game, and manage fields and berry patches. After the removals of the 1830s (Chapter 66), settler populations introduced their own burning traditions, including the woods burning practiced by rural white and Black southerners for grazing, and later the region became a focus of twentieth-century fire-control campaigns, in which burning was often portrayed as backward. The Southeast thus illustrates how fire practice persisted, changed hands, and became stigmatized.

In the Northeast and the broader Eastern Woodlands, early colonists commented on open, parklike forests near Indigenous villages and cornfields, often attributing them to Indigenous clearing and burning. Historians continue to debate how widespread and extensive such burning was and how much open land reflected agriculture, settlement, and natural conditions. The most careful assessments emphasize that Indigenous land use created a patchwork of cleared fields, regrowing forest, and managed woods around communities, whereas more distant forests were less altered. As elsewhere, the scale of Indigenous influence was real but uneven.

Section 134.8

Indigenous Fire Management in Canada and Mexico

A continental history must follow fire stewardship across the modern boundaries of Canada, the United States, and Mexico, because Indigenous peoples and ecosystems did not respect them. In Canada, First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities lived in environments ranging from Pacific coastal rainforest to prairie, boreal forest, and Arctic tundra. Fire stewardship was most extensive in southern and interior environments where grasslands, open forests, and berry grounds existed. In British Columbia, interior communities such as the Secwépemc, Syilx (Okanagan), Ktunaxa, Nlaka'pamux, and others used fire to maintain open forests, grasslands, and berry and root-gathering areas. The Ktunaxa territory of southeastern British Columbia has been the subject of tree-ring research documenting centuries of fire history, which interacts with Ktunaxa oral history and practice. In the prairie provinces, Blackfoot, Cree, Métis, and other communities used fire in grassland management, hunting, and travel. In boreal and northern regions, fire occurs on a larger and less frequent scale, and Indigenous communities such as the Dene and Cree used fire locally to improve berry and game habitat, to clear camps and trails, and to signal or manage movement, while recognizing and living within the dynamics of large natural fires.

In Mexico, Indigenous fire practice must be understood within a different history of land tenure and agrarian institutions. Many Indigenous communities hold land through ejidos or as Indigenous or agrarian communities, which gives them collective rights unlike those of Indigenous nations in the United States or Canada, and national forestry and fire policy has been developed through agencies such as the national forestry commission. Fire in rural Mexico has long been bound up with agriculture: swidden or milpa systems, in which fields are cleared and burned, rotated, and allowed to regrow, are widespread in southern Mexico, including in parts of Veracruz, Oaxaca, Chiapas, and the Yucatán Peninsula. Policy discourse has often treated such burning as a threat to forests, though research on swidden systems indicates considerable variation in their ecological effects and a significant role of local rules and knowledge.

The Rarámuri of the Sierra Madre Occidental, in the state of Chihuahua, provide an especially important case. Their homeland includes pine-oak forests that, according to fire-history research in northern Mexico, historically experienced frequent surface fires. Studies by ecologists and historians working in Rarámuri territory have examined relationships among fire, forest structure, and Indigenous land use, including agriculture, grazing, and the management of forests and fields, and they have noted that the region retains forests with fire-regime characteristics less disrupted by twentieth-century fire exclusion than many in the United States. This has made the Sierra Madre a valuable reference landscape for ecologists studying pine forests across the continent. The Rarámuri themselves, whose communities face pressures from logging, mining, violence, and drought, are not simply a reference for foreign researchers; their own governance of land and forest raises the same questions of authority and recognition seen elsewhere.

Comparisons across the three countries clarify what differs. In the United States, federal land agencies and state air regulators have been the principal institutional barriers, and tribal trust land provides one setting for tribal authority. In Canada, provinces control forest land and wildfire management, while the federal government manages national parks and reserves, and constitutional recognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights shapes arguments for Indigenous authority. In Mexico, agrarian law, community land tenure, and national forestry and fire norms form the main framework. The resulting differences affect who can burn, under what authority, and with what liability.

Section 134.9

European Settlement and the Idea of Untouched Wilderness

When European colonists and administrators encountered Indigenous-managed landscapes, they interpreted them through ideas of nature, property, and improvement that they brought with them. The ways in which those ideas shaped later fire policy are essential to the chapter's argument, because laws and institutions rested on assumptions about what land was, who had made it productive, and who had the right to manage it.

Many European observers did notice Indigenous modification of the land. Early accounts describe open woods, burned meadows, and cleared fields, and some colonists commented on the Indigenous use of fire. But in colonial legal and political thought, such management was frequently discounted. In many European traditions of the period, land ownership was justified by agricultural improvement, enclosure, fences, permanent buildings, and cultivated fields of a recognizable European type. Seasonal burning, gathering, and the tending of wild plants did not fit these categories, and colonial claimants could describe lands used in these ways as empty, wild, or unimproved, and therefore available for settlement. The idea of wilderness as land untouched by people thus served practical and ideological purposes in dispossession.

The idea took on additional force in the nineteenth century, when Romantic and conservationist movements in the United States and Canada began to portray wilderness as a sacred, pristine, and endangered inheritance. Early national parks and forest reserves were created in part to protect landscapes imagined as natural and uninhabited, and Indigenous peoples were often excluded from parks or constrained in their uses of them. This conception had paradoxical consequences. Landscapes that had been shaped by Indigenous management were protected as if they were unmanaged, so that the practices that had maintained them were forbidden in the name of preservation. Over time, the absence of burning altered those very landscapes, a consequence taken up in section 134.13.

It is important not to treat all colonial actors as sharing a single view. Spanish missionaries, French fur traders, English farmers, American foresters, Canadian surveyors, and Mexican agrarian officials differed in their economic interests and ideas about land. Some traders depended on Indigenous hunters and had incentives to preserve Indigenous land use. Some farmers adopted burning themselves to clear land and improve pasture. Some naturalists praised Indigenous knowledge. Colonial approaches also changed over time, so that the same government that tolerated burning in one decade might ban it in another. Understanding the diversity of colonial attitudes prevents the history from becoming a simple morality tale and helps explain why restrictions emerged unevenly.

The displacement of Indigenous peoples was itself an ecological event. Where communities were killed, removed, or confined to small reserves or reservations, their burning ceased across the lands they had formerly used, regardless of any specific law. Settlement and agricultural development changed fire patterns in other ways: plowing and grazing reduced fuels in some areas, roads and fields acted as firebreaks, and settlers' own fires, accidental or deliberate, added new ignitions. The decline of Indigenous burning thus resulted from a combination of dispossession, depopulation, legal restriction, and altered land use, rather than from any single cause.

Historical Debate

Was Precolonial North America an Untouched Wilderness?

The question: To what extent did Indigenous peoples transform North American landscapes before and during early colonization, and how should historians characterize the resulting environments?

Interpretation A — The "pristine wilderness" view (now largely abandoned in its strong form): Earlier scholarship and popular writing often portrayed precolonial North America as a vast, essentially natural forest and prairie in which Indigenous populations were small and their effects slight.
Interpretation B — The "managed landscape" view: Many historians, geographers, ecologists, and Indigenous scholars argue that Indigenous peoples shaped landscapes substantially through fire, agriculture, harvesting, and settlement, so that many environments were cultural landscapes in 1492 and for centuries thereafter.
Interpretation C — The "regional and qualified" view: Many researchers accept substantial Indigenous influence in some regions while stressing that its scale varied widely, that climate, lightning, and animals were also powerful forces, and that sweeping claims risk replacing one oversimplification with another.

Evidence: Early written descriptions; oral histories; archaeological evidence of settlement and plant use; tree-ring, charcoal, and pollen records; and comparison of forests before and after depopulation or fire exclusion.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns scale and extent (how much of the continent), attribution (how to separate human from natural causes), and interpretation (how to read early descriptions written by outsiders). Few scholars now defend the strong wilderness view.

Why it matters: The answer affects both history and policy. If landscapes were managed, then removing the managers changed them, and restoring them may require restoring management. If landscapes were untouched, then preserving them means keeping people out. The debate also bears on how readily colonial claims of "empty land" can be recognized as political arguments.

Section 134.10

The Criminalization and Restriction of Indigenous Burning

Restrictions on Indigenous burning took many forms, and historians need to distinguish them. Some were explicit legal prohibitions, in the form of statutes, regulations, or park rules that made certain kinds of fire illegal or required permits that were difficult for Indigenous communities to obtain. Others were indirect: they arose from the loss of territory, the confinement of communities to reservations or reserves, the transfer of land to private owners and public agencies, the criminalization of travel or gathering, or the cultural and religious suppression of Indigenous practices. Both kinds of restriction reduced burning, but they operated through different legal mechanisms and are documented in different kinds of sources.

Explicit legislation appears early in some colonial jurisdictions. British Columbia’s Bush Fire Act of 1874 is a frequently cited example. The provincial wildfire service describes its historical effect as a ban on cultural burning. A 2025 comparative study of fire governance, drawing on earlier forestry history, describes a more specific offense: leaving an open fire without extinguishing it, punishable by a fine of up to $100 or imprisonment for up to three months. These accounts address different aspects of the law and need not contradict one another. The original 1874 text has not been independently consulted for this chapter; the verified 1887 amendment below documents a subsequent change to its judicial provisions. Neither source warrants concluding that every Indigenous ignition was automatically criminal under the original enactment. General fire regulations, colonial land tenure and uneven enforcement could nevertheless constrain cultural burning severely. Historians must distinguish the text of a law, its administration and its practical consequences for Indigenous communities. See the comparative fire-governance study and the primary source below.

Other jurisdictions developed comparable rules through different instruments. In the United States, state and territorial governments and, from the early twentieth century, federal agencies adopted fire laws, permit systems, and forest regulations that limited burning on public and private lands. In California, the debate over "light burning," in which some landowners and observers advocated periodic low-intensity fire modeled on Indigenous practice, was largely settled in the early twentieth century in favor of fire suppression by federal and state forestry officials. In Canadian national parks, Parks Canada now acknowledges that cultural burning by Indigenous peoples was no longer allowed within the parks and that many communities lost knowledge of and access to cultural burning, with significant effects on landscapes. In Mexico, forestry and agricultural burning norms, adopted in the twentieth century, regulated the use of fire in forests and agricultural lands, with consequences for Indigenous swidden agriculture and forest management.

Indirect restrictions often had as much effect as explicit ones. Reservation and reserve systems (Chapters 84 and 85) confined communities to portions of their territories, and in some regions, such as the Karuk homeland, much of the ancestral land lies within national forests rather than within reservations, so that burning on those lands required access and permission from federal agencies. Allotment and the sale of reservation lands (Chapter 86) fragmented ownership, and the Canadian pass and permit systems limited movement off reserves. Boarding and residential schools (Chapter 87) removed children from the seasonal rounds through which fire knowledge was taught. Each of these developments, treated in detail in other chapters, contributed to the decline of burning even where no statute named it.

Enforcement was uneven, and Indigenous people did not always comply. Many families continued to burn quietly, at small scale, or in remote places, sometimes at personal risk. Others adapted by burning in ways less visible to authorities. This persistence complicates any narrative of simple disappearance and is central to section 134.12. Nor were all Indigenous people opposed to fire control; some communities, threatened by large fires, supported suppression and worked as firefighters, as discussed in the next section.

Section 134.11

Scientific Forestry and the Rise of Fire Suppression

The restriction of Indigenous burning coincided with, and was reinforced by, the rise of scientific forestry. In the late nineteenth century, concern about timber famine, watershed protection, and the destruction of forests by unregulated logging and fire led governments in North America and Europe to create forestry institutions. Foresters trained in European traditions of managed forests argued that forests were a renewable resource that required organized protection, and fire was widely seen as the principal enemy of that protection. This was not an irrational belief. Large, destructive fires had killed people, destroyed towns, and consumed enormous quantities of timber, often following logging operations that left heavy slash. Protecting communities, infrastructure, and timber resources was a legitimate and widely supported objective.

In the United States, the Forest Service was created in 1905 within the Department of Agriculture to administer the national forest reserves, with Gifford Pinchot as its first chief. Its leaders, building on conservationist ideas, regarded fire control as central to the agency's mission. Then, in August 1910, the Great Fire, also called the Big Blowup, swept across the northern Rockies of Idaho, Montana, and Washington, burning roughly three million acres and killing dozens of people, most of them firefighters. The disaster made a profound impression on the young agency and the public, and it strengthened the argument that fire should be fought everywhere and fought quickly. Later legislation, including the Weeks Act of 1911 and the Clarke–McNary Act of 1924, expanded federal support for fire protection and cooperation with states.

Over the following decades the doctrine hardened. In 1935 the Forest Service adopted what became known as the "10 a.m. policy," aiming to control any reported fire by ten o'clock the following morning. Public education campaigns, notably the Smokey Bear campaign beginning in 1944, taught generations of Americans that forest fires were caused by carelessness and were to be prevented. These campaigns were effective in reducing human-caused ignitions, but they also fixed in public consciousness a picture of fire as uniformly harmful, a picture at odds with the ecology of many fire-adapted ecosystems and with Indigenous understanding.

Canada developed parallel institutions. The federal government created forest reserves and a parks service in the early twentieth century, and provincial forest services, such as British Columbia's Forest Branch in the 1910s, took responsibility for fire protection on provincial Crown lands. Fire suppression became the dominant policy in national parks, which, as Parks Canada's own historical statement acknowledges, eliminated most wildfires and banned cultural burning. Mexico, too, established forestry institutions in the twentieth century, and its national policies regulating agricultural and forest burning reflected concern about forest loss. Though the institutional details differ, a common set of ideas, many drawn from European scientific forestry and the conservation movement, shaped all three.

The motives for suppression were thus economic (timber and agriculture), political (the authority and funding of new agencies), scientific (the forestry understanding of the day), and humanitarian (the protection of lives and settlements). The decisive point for this chapter is what the institutions left out. They did not consult Indigenous nations about fire, did not regard Indigenous knowledge as a form of forest science, and often treated Indigenous burning as the very carelessness they sought to prevent. Light-burning advocates in California who pointed to Indigenous practice were rebuffed by officials who feared that any accommodation of burning would weaken the argument for total protection. Meanwhile, Indigenous people were not absent from federal fire work: many served as firefighters, and by the middle of the twentieth century Native crews were prominent in wildfire suppression, a poignant irony given that their own burning had been forbidden.

Section 134.12

Indigenous Dispossession and the Interruption of Knowledge Transmission

For Indigenous communities, the loss of fire was not an abstraction. It meant fewer acorns that were harder to gather, scarcer basketry materials of lower quality, thicker brush overtaking berry patches and meadows, fewer medicinal plants within reach, changed hunting grounds, and the closing of ceremonial landscapes. These losses compounded the broader damage of dispossession. Environmental dispossession, the separation of a people from the lands, resources, and management authority on which its way of life depended, thus operated through ecological change as well as through the loss of title.

Food security was one dimension. Communities that had relied on a combination of managed and wild foods were pushed toward wage labor, rations, and purchased foods, often of poorer nutritional quality. Where fire-dependent foods declined, the transition was harder. Medicinal plants likewise became harder to obtain, with consequences for health care at a time when epidemic disease and poverty were already severe. Craft traditions such as basketry were strained when suitable materials were scarce or access to gathering places was denied, and weavers reported spending more time and travel to find materials or substituting lesser ones. In the Karuk and Yurok communities, advocates link these changes to declines in cultural practice as well as to ecological conditions.

Hunting and gathering changed as habitats shifted. The loss of open meadows and forest edges altered the distribution of game and plants. Sacred and ceremonial places were sometimes overgrown or inaccessible, or lay within lands controlled by agencies that restricted entry or modification. The reduction of community control over resources meant that decisions affecting these places were made by others, an issue developed in Chapter 119.

The effect on education may have been the deepest. Fire knowledge was taught through practice, season after season, and could not be fully preserved in a book. When elders could not burn, and when children were separated from families by boarding and residential schools, the chain of apprenticeship frayed. Some knowledge was lost, some was held by a few individuals or families, and some survived in fragments, stories, and place names. The historical record suggests that a generation or more might pass in some communities without large-scale cultural burning, making the work of revitalization today one of rebuilding practical competence as well as legal standing.

Yet this chapter avoids the claim that Indigenous fire knowledge disappeared and was later rediscovered by outside institutions. That narrative, though common in popular media, misrepresents the evidence. Families and communities preserved knowledge in multiple ways: by burning quietly in small patches; by teaching younger relatives about plants and places; by maintaining stories, songs, and ceremonies; by continuing to gather and weave; and by working within and around agencies to influence land management. Elders who held knowledge became the foundation of later revitalization. Researchers and agencies came to appreciate Indigenous fire knowledge largely because Indigenous people had kept it and spoke up. The process is more accurately described as the persistence of knowledge, constrained and partly interrupted, followed by renewed public recognition and support.

Section 134.13

The Ecological Consequences of Fire Exclusion

When fire is removed from an ecosystem that evolved with it, the effects accumulate over decades. In forests historically characterized by frequent, low-severity surface fires, such as many ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forests of the western United States and the pine-oak forests of northern Mexico, regular burning kept the understory thin and the canopy open. Without fire, young trees establish beneath older ones, shade-tolerant species increase, litter and dead wood accumulate, and the forest becomes denser. Fuels that were once discontinuous become continuous, and fire that does eventually occur can climb from the surface into the canopy, producing high-severity burns where the historical regime had favored low severity. This is one of the better-established findings of forest ecology, though its extent varies by forest type and location.

In grasslands and savannas, the absence of fire allows woody plants to encroach. Prairies and oak savannas may turn to shrubland or forest, and open meadows in mountain forests may close over. The Pacific Northwest prairies discussed in section 134.6 and the eastern savannas and woodlands where burning once maintained oaks and open conditions are examples. In the southeastern longleaf pine ecosystem, fire exclusion allows hardwoods to invade and reduces the diversity of understory plants.

Changes in species composition affect culturally significant plants. Several of the species that Indigenous communities rely upon, including hazel and beargrass for weaving, certain berries, camas, and acorn-bearing oaks, are described by practitioners and researchers as responding to periodic fire or being outcompeted without it. Communities report that the quality and accessibility of such plants declined where burning stopped. Wildlife habitat also changed: species adapted to open forests and grasslands lost habitat, while others benefited from denser cover. Hunting patterns, and the places where game could be found, shifted accordingly.

Altered succession and fuel patterns changed the behavior of wildfire. Where dense, uniform fuels replaced patchwork mosaics, large fires could spread across wider areas with fewer natural barriers. In fire-adapted forests, fire exclusion thus contributed, together with other factors, to conditions in which fires can burn with higher severity than under the historical regime. Conversely, it would be a serious error to generalize this result. In ecosystems where fires were naturally infrequent and severe, such as many coastal rainforests and boreal stands, the absence of fire for a century or more is not necessarily abnormal, and applying a frequent-fire model there would be mistaken. Fire exclusion must therefore be evaluated ecosystem by ecosystem.

Section 134.14

Modern Wildfires and the Reassessment of Forest Management

The wildfires of recent decades, including very large fires in the western United States and Canada and devastating fires affecting communities, have prompted widespread reconsideration of forest management. Understanding these fires historically requires attention to multiple, interacting causes, and the chapter urges caution about any account that assigns them to one factor alone.

Historical fire suppression is one factor, especially in frequent-fire forests where fuels accumulated. Climate change is another: warmer temperatures, earlier snowmelt, and prolonged drought dry fuels and lengthen fire seasons across much of western North America, and extreme weather events, such as high winds, can drive fire spread beyond what any fuel management could stop. Forest management history also matters, including logging practices, plantation forestry, and road building. Land-use change contributes: expanding residential development into fire-prone areas, the wildland–urban interface, places more homes and people in the path of fire and increases ignitions from power lines, vehicles, and equipment. Human-caused ignitions account for a large share of fires in many regions. The relative weight of these factors differs among fires, regions, and years, and scientific debate continues about the contribution of each to particular events.

Over the second half of the twentieth century, institutions began to revise their doctrines. The recognition that fire is an ecological process, together with experience of costly suppression and the buildup of fuels, led the National Park Service and the Forest Service to accept prescribed burning and, in some areas, to allow natural fires to burn under management. The large Yellowstone fires of 1988 provoked controversy and further review. Canadian agencies, including Parks Canada, likewise began prescribed burning programs. By the early twenty-first century, major agencies increasingly spoke of living with fire, restoring fire to ecosystems, and building community resilience. These reforms, however, were largely framed in the language of forestry and ecology. They did not generally recognize Indigenous authority, and they often treated Indigenous knowledge as a source of techniques to be incorporated rather than as a legal and governmental tradition.

For Indigenous communities, wildfire is not only an ecological or policy matter but a direct threat. The Karuk Tribe lost homes, including elder housing, in the Slater Fire of 2020, and in 2021 the village of Lytton in British Columbia, home to members of the Nlaka'pamux Nation, was devastated by fire. Indigenous communities frequently live in or adjacent to fire-prone forests, often with limited resources for evacuation and rebuilding. Their advocates argue that restoring cultural burning should be understood as part of community safety as well as cultural continuity, while cautioning that cultural burning is not a guaranteed protection against extreme fires driven by drought and wind.

Section 134.15

The Indigenous Cultural Burning Resurgence

The revival of Indigenous fire stewardship has not been a single movement with a single leader or date. It has emerged from many communities, often independently, as elders, practitioners, tribal natural resource staff, weavers, and youth sought to restore burning and the knowledge, relationships, and authority attached to it. Its sources include the persistence of fire knowledge through the suppression era, the self-determination policies examined in Chapter 114, the growth of tribal natural resource departments and environmental programs, and the changing attitudes of land agencies toward prescribed fire and collaboration.

Community initiatives take varied forms. Some begin with small family burns at gathering sites. Others involve tribal departments planning burns on tribal trust or fee lands, or negotiating access to burn on federal or state lands. Elder-led education brings younger people into the field and into ceremony, linking practice to language and story. Cultural fire practitioners, a recognized role in some communities, combine traditional knowledge with contemporary training in safety and communications. Indigenous environmental organizations, intertribal networks, and research partnerships provide support, funding, and a means of sharing experience across regions.

A critical feature of the resurgence is its integration of cultural and ecological aims. Practitioners often describe the revival of burning as simultaneously restoring food, medicine, and weaving plants, strengthening language and ceremony, reconnecting youth with place, and reducing hazardous fuels. These aims are inseparable in many communities' own accounts, and they distinguish cultural burning from programs whose sole objective is fuel reduction. Reconnecting younger generations with environmental responsibilities, through camps, apprenticeships, and burn crews, is as central to the movement as the fire itself.

Students should also distinguish Indigenous-led resurgence from government programs that incorporate selected Indigenous techniques. A government agency may adopt a practice, such as the use of particular ignition patterns or timing, because evidence suggests it is effective, and may credit Indigenous knowledge. But if the agency holds decision-making authority, sets objectives, and funds only what fits its mandate, the program remains agency-led, even when it draws on Indigenous knowledge. Indigenous leaders and the Canadian national parks agency itself have stressed that cultural burning should remain Indigenous-led so as not to misappropriate cultural practices. The distinction has practical consequences for funding, liability, permitting, and the transfer of knowledge, and it frames the legal debates in Division V.

Importantly, resurgence does not mean replicating the past. Contemporary practitioners work in landscapes changed by a century or more of exclusion, with denser fuels, altered species composition, new invasive plants, homes adjacent to the forest, roads, and a warming climate. Burns must often be planned more cautiously, sometimes beginning with mechanical thinning or small-scale test fires, than would have been necessary under a regime of frequent burning. Practitioners adapt traditional knowledge to these conditions, which exemplifies the continuing development of Indigenous knowledge systems rather than a return to a fixed tradition.

Section 134.16

The Karuk, Yurok, and Intertribal Fire Stewardship Movement

The mid-Klamath region of northern California has become the most closely studied example of the contemporary resurgence, and it shows how a local effort can build institutions with continental influence. The Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa peoples live in a mountainous region of great ecological diversity where, in the twentieth century, much of the land came under federal management as national forest. Karuk territory, in particular, lies largely within national forests rather than within a large reservation, which meant that Karuk burning depended on federal permission.

The Karuk Tribe's Department of Natural Resources, headed by Bill Tripp, has been a leading institution in the effort to restore cultural burning, to develop climate and land-management plans grounded in Karuk knowledge, and to forge partnerships with federal agencies and researchers. The Karuk have worked on collaborative restoration efforts in the Western Klamath Mountains and have advocated for policies that recognize tribal authority over cultural fire. Their position is that burning is an exercise of inherent responsibility and sovereignty, not a favor from agencies.

Among the Yurok, practitioners and families revitalized burning at community level, and the Cultural Fire Management Council, co-founded in Yurok territory by Margo Robbins and others, organizes burns and trainings that emphasize family participation, the needs of weavers, and the transmission of knowledge to young people. Hupa participants have likewise joined regional burning and restoration. Weavers have been central: their demand for high-quality materials gave concrete purpose to the effort and linked burning to the revival of a living art.

The Prescribed Fire Training Exchanges, or TREX, provided one important resource. First organized in 2008 by the Forest Service, The Nature Conservancy, and other agencies, TREX brings participants together for hands-on training in prescribed fire. Beginning in the early 2010s, Karuk and Yurok communities partnered with TREX to conduct burns on their lands and to train community members, adapting the model to tribal priorities. Out of these collaborations came the Indigenous Peoples Burning Network, formed in 2015 by Indigenous fire practitioners and culture keepers of the Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa, with support from The Nature Conservancy and funding from the Forest Service. Its leadership team and advisors are Indigenous practitioners, and its activities include planning, learning exchanges, Indigenous-led training, and convening. It has since grown to include tribes in several states, including the Klamath Tribes in Oregon, Ojibwe communities in Minnesota, and Pueblo communities in New Mexico, and it provides a documented example of intertribal cooperation to revitalize fire cultures and to strengthen Indigenous authority.

Women's leadership has been prominent. Karuk and Yurok women, as weavers and knowledge holders, have held major roles, and the first Karuk Indigenous Women’s TREX, in 2022, was designed as a training led by and for Indigenous women, with Indigenous practitioners answering to Indigenous organizers. Organizers described the training as prescribed burning with cultural objectives, distinguishing it from burning aimed purely at fuel reduction. The initiative illustrates how fire stewardship is linked to gendered knowledge and how it can create leadership opportunities in a field historically dominated by men.

Partnerships with scientific and governmental institutions have been both enabling and complicated. Collaboration with universities, the Forest Service, and conservation organizations has provided training, funding, and technical support, and Indigenous leaders have shaped research agendas. At the same time, the network and its partners have had to negotiate legal and administrative barriers: the need for burn plans and permits, air-quality rules, liability and insurance requirements, qualification standards for burn bosses, and limited access to federal lands. Leaders in the network have described long timelines before a tribe could burn without intensive agency monitoring, and they have insisted that Indigenous peoples have a right to self-determination in shaping their fire cultures. These barriers set the stage for the legislative developments of the next division.

Section 134.17

First Nations Fire Stewardship and Forest Restoration in Canada

In Canada, the resurgence of cultural burning has unfolded within a legal setting different from that of the United States. Provincial governments hold primary jurisdiction over wildfire on provincial Crown lands, the federal government manages national parks and historic sites, and Indigenous peoples assert rights grounded in Aboriginal title, treaties, and inherent jurisdiction, recognized in section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, and examined in Chapter 117. Cultural burning therefore involves negotiation among First Nations, provinces, and Parks Canada, and the question of who decides is as important as the question of how to burn.

British Columbia, the province whose 1874 act is the chapter's principal Canadian historical example, has become a center of reform. The province's Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, passed in 2019, commits it to aligning provincial laws with the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (Chapter 143). Within that framework the provincial Ministry of Forests and BC Wildfire Service have, in partnership with the First Nations Emergency Services Society (FNESS), developed a Cultural and Prescribed Fire Program. The province uses the phrase "cultural and prescribed fire" to acknowledge Indigenous cultural burning as distinct from prescribed burning, and states that while it does not itself undertake cultural burning, it has a responsibility to address some of the barriers to it. Provincial communications acknowledge that earlier prohibition and suppression increased wildfire risk and harmed biodiversity and forest health.

Legislative change followed. Amendments to the Wildfire Act, enacted on November 30, 2023, enable BC Wildfire Service staff to assist First Nations and other partners with burning projects, and B.C. Regulation 68/2024, deposited March 18, 2024, subsequently expanded the resource-management fire definition to include purposes identified by Indigenous peoples. Its section 23.1 permits an open-fire agreement under section 60 of the Wildfire Act to substitute for the specified burn-plan requirement; other applicable obligations are not automatically removed. A provincial factsheet on the amendments identifies the imbalance of power over who leads and controls burning as a historical barrier. These changes are significant, but their practical meaning depends on implementation, funding, and the willingness of agencies to share decision-making.

Parks Canada illustrates the federal side. Its own historical account acknowledges that cultural burning was excluded from national parks and that Indigenous peoples lost knowledge and access as a result. It now states a commitment to supporting Indigenous-led cultural burning by addressing barriers such as training and resources, establishing partnerships to revitalize burning in parks and historic sites, and supporting Indigenous Guardian initiatives. In April 2023 it established an Indigenous Fire Circle of Indigenous Parks Canada employees and external advisors to give advice on engagement, Indigenous-led burning, and the agency's approach to suppression. In Waterton Lakes National Park, prescribed fires in recent years have been carried out with the participation of the Siksikaitsitapi (Blackfoot Confederacy), and the park's 2022 management plan commits to working with the Blackfoot and other Indigenous peoples in park management. Elsewhere in the national parks system, Métis youth and families have taken part in cultural fire camps at Rocky Mountain House National Historic Site.

Questions of authority remain open in all these cases. Who may initiate a burn, who approves it, who pays for it, who is liable if fire escapes, who must be certified, and who owns the knowledge produced are matters set by law and agreement. Funding for First Nations burning often flows through provincial or federal programs with conditions attached, and regulatory requirements designed for agencies can burden small community programs. Some First Nations pursue agreements that preserve their authority, while others work through partnership programs, and many combine approaches. For the historian, the relevant question is whether the new frameworks shift decision-making authority or merely add Indigenous participation to agency-controlled processes.

Section 134.18

Indigenous Environmental Science and Contemporary Fire Research

Debates over the history and future of cultural burning depend on evidence, and historians need to understand what each type of evidence can and cannot establish. Contemporary fire research includes Indigenous knowledge systems and Western scientific methods, increasingly in combination. The goal of this section is to explain the evidence base clearly and to caution against treating any one source as decisive.

Oral histories and community knowledge document how communities understood and used fire, including purposes, seasons, plants, places, and responsibilities. They are often the only source for the cultural meaning of burning and can contain precise ecological observation passed over generations. They can also record events and relationships across long spans, though, like all historical sources, they are shaped by the purposes and circumstances of their telling. They are best interpreted in collaboration with the communities that hold them and under those communities' protocols.

Documentary sources, including early explorers' accounts, missionary and trader records, surveyors' notes, and government correspondence, sometimes describe Indigenous burning and the landscapes it produced. They provide dates and locations but reflect outsiders' perspectives and may misunderstand or exaggerate. Historical vegetation records, such as land survey notes that list tree species and densities, allow reconstruction of past forest composition, but they too were produced for settlement and land-sale purposes.

Tree-ring science, or dendrochronology, can date the growth of trees to the year. Fire-scar analysis examines scars left in the rings of trees that survive surface fires, producing a record of fire years and, with enough trees, estimates of fire frequency over centuries. For example, tree-ring studies in Ktunaxa territory in southeastern British Columbia have analyzed fire frequency across several centuries. Fire scars show that fire occurred and when, but they cannot, by themselves, tell who or what started it, and the surviving-scar record is biased toward trees that survived injury. Severe or stand-replacing fires may instead be reconstructed through tree establishment dates, mortality evidence, charcoal and other ecological records. Sediment charcoal from lake and bog cores records fire over thousands of years at a coarser resolution and across a wider area, usually showing changes in fire activity with climate and vegetation. Charcoal records are valuable for long-term context but typically cannot distinguish human from natural ignition. Pollen in the same sediments reveals changes in vegetation that may accompany shifts in fire.

Archaeological evidence, including charred plant remains, site distribution, tools, and food residues, can show what people ate and where they lived and, in some cases, suggest the use of fire-managed resources. But it rarely demonstrates deliberate burning directly. Geographic information systems, remote sensing, and ecological monitoring allow researchers to map vegetation change, measure fuels, and monitor the effects of burns, and they are widely used in tribal natural resource departments. Forest composition studies compare present and historical stands to estimate the effects of fire exclusion. Community-based research, in which tribal members help design, conduct, and interpret studies, integrates these methods with Indigenous knowledge and is increasingly regarded as best practice.

The key analytical distinction is between evidence of fire occurrence and evidence of fire origin, purpose, and cultural meaning. A scar or a charcoal layer demonstrates that fire occurred. To argue that Indigenous people set it requires additional evidence: a pattern of fire timing that does not match lightning or climate, proximity to settlement, correspondence with oral and documentary accounts, and consistency across multiple records. Even then, researchers describe human influence in terms of probability and degree. This is why responsible histories use phrases such as "associated with" or "consistent with" rather than "proves." It is also why the weight of evidence differs from place to place, and why claims about continental-scale Indigenous burning should be held with care.

Finally, communities may control access to sensitive environmental knowledge, including locations of culturally important plants and sacred sites, and may set conditions on how research is conducted, shared, and published. These issues of knowledge governance are treated in Chapters 133 and 145; here it is enough to note that responsible fire research, particularly research touching on cultural plants and ceremonial places, depends on consent, collaboration, and respect for community authority.

Section 134.19

The Right to Burn: Law, Jurisdiction, and Indigenous Sovereignty

By the early twenty-first century, the question of cultural burning had moved from forestry practice to constitutional and statutory law. At the center is a simple question with complex answers: who has the authority to decide whether, when, and how fire is applied to land? Indigenous nations argue that the authority is inherent, derived from their own laws and relationships to territory, and long predating the settler states that later regulated burning. Governments, by contrast, derive their regulatory authority from their own constitutions and statutes, and they have asserted it over fire, air quality, public safety, and land management.

Several kinds of barriers stand between Indigenous practitioners and the right to burn. Territorial access is the first: much ancestral land is now owned by governments or private parties, and permission to burn there depends on landowners or agencies. Land administration comes next, since federal, state, provincial, and park rules govern what may be done on public land. Government permits and burn plans, often designed for agency or commercial burners, can be costly and slow. Air-quality regulation limits smoke emissions and sometimes restricts burn days. Liability and insurance rules determine who bears the cost if fire escapes. Professional certification standards can require qualifications that traditional practitioners may not hold, even where their experience is extensive. Each rule may serve a legitimate public purpose, but together they can make cultural burning difficult for communities that were historically excluded from setting the rules.

Governments have developed several responses. Intergovernmental agreements and co-management arrangements establish shared decision-making over particular lands or programs. In the United States, federal policy has expanded opportunities for tribal co-stewardship, including Joint Secretarial Order 3403, issued by the Departments of the Interior and Agriculture on November 15, 2021, on tribal co-stewardship of federal lands and waters, though the implementation and scope of such measures vary. Tribes exercising authority over their own trust lands may establish their own fire programs. In British Columbia, as described above, section 60 agreements and Wildfire Act amendments create new options. In Mexico, community land governance and national fire norms form the setting, and the extent to which Indigenous communities can set their own fire rules depends on local tenure and negotiation with forestry authorities.

California’s Senate Bill 310, authored by Senator Bill Dodd and signed by Governor Gavin Newsom on September 27, 2024, took effect on January 1, 2025. The chaptered legislation added Public Resources Code section 4505, enabling the California Natural Resources Agency and, separately, local air districts to make written agreements with federally recognized California Native American tribes. These agreements can recognize tribal regulation of cultural burning within ancestral territory and modify specified state regulatory requirements within their terms. The Karuk Tribe sponsored the measure. During legislative consideration, the California Air Pollution Control Officers Association raised concerns about public notification and smoke modeling. Those concerns illustrate the competing responsibilities of tribal governments and public-health regulators, rather than a rejection of all cultural burning. On February 27, 2025, the Karuk Tribe and the California Natural Resources Agency announced the first agreement under the law. The state’s announcement described access to the prescribed-fire and cultural-burning claims fund without mandatory formal burn plans. The agreement framework does not authorize burning on another owner’s property without permission, and it does not provide immunity from specified recoverable costs for gross negligence. Section 4505 requires an identified geographic area within the tribe’s ancestral territory and, as enacted, schedules repeal of the article for January 1, 2030. These terms are not a blanket exemption from all fire, environmental or liability rules. The agreement mechanism also excludes communities without federal recognition, although other provisions of California law may apply to their cultural fire practitioners. See the chaptered bill, the legislative record and the agreement announcement.

Distinguishing recognition from authority is the crux. A government can recognize the value of cultural burning, change its vocabulary, and fund Indigenous participation without transferring decisions. Conversely, an agreement can transfer significant regulatory authority, as the SB 310 agreement is described as doing for regulatory compliance within its terms. Even so, such agreements are negotiated, limited in scope, subject to the terms of state law, and apply to particular lands. They do not alter federal land management on national forests, where the Forest Service retains authority, and they depend on continued political support. Evaluating them requires attention to who decides, who funds, who is liable, who may revoke, and what remedies exist.

Historical Debate

Does Government Recognition of Cultural Burning Restore Indigenous Environmental Sovereignty?

The question: When governments legalize, fund, or formally recognize cultural burning, does that restore Indigenous authority over fire and land, or does it incorporate a practice into settler regulatory systems?

Interpretation A — Recognition as meaningful restoration: Supporters point to agreements, such as the one under SB 310, in which a state agrees that tribal governments regulate cultural burns on their lands, as real shifts in authority that reverse a century of prohibition and create precedents.
Interpretation B — Recognition as incorporation: Critics argue that most programs keep authority with governments, which set eligibility, funding, certification, and liability rules. They warn that cultural burning could be absorbed into agency priorities, such as fuel reduction, leaving Indigenous purposes subordinate.
Interpretation C — Recognition as a step in an ongoing struggle: Others see recognition as neither full restoration nor mere incorporation, but as a contested and partial gain that creates openings for further assertion of authority while leaving unresolved issues such as access to federal land, funding, and unrecognized tribes.

Evidence: The text of statutes and agreements; the terms of funding and permitting; who signs and who may revoke; the practices of agencies in implementation; and the testimony of Indigenous leaders and practitioners.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation of legal texts, expectations about implementation, and different understandings of sovereignty itself, whether as inherent authority that governments can only acknowledge, or as authority exercised within the legal systems that surround Indigenous nations.

Why it matters: The answer shapes strategy. If recognition is restoration, the task is to replicate agreements widely. If it is incorporation, the task may be to resist or reshape programs. Either way, the debate shows that the right to burn is political as well as ecological.

Section 134.20

The Future of Indigenous Fire and Forest Stewardship

The history traced in this chapter can be summarized as a movement through several stages, which should not be treated as identical or inevitable across regions. Indigenous communities developed community-governed relationships with fire and landscape. Those relationships helped shape cultural landscapes: food systems, wildlife habitats, forests, and grasslands. Colonial dispossession removed territory, authority, and access. Institutional fire suppression and exclusion followed, changing vegetation, habitat, subsistence, and the transmission of knowledge. In the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries, Indigenous communities have revitalized knowledge and practice, built institutions, and begun to negotiate legal recognition of environmental authority.

The future of cultural burning is therefore about more than wildfire prevention. Cultural burning may reduce fuels, and evidence from many ecosystems supports the role of frequent low-severity fire in lowering the risk of high-severity fire. But no one should present it as a simple solution to large climate-driven fires, and Indigenous practitioners themselves emphasize its multiple purposes. What is at stake includes the reconstruction of Indigenous institutions, such as natural resource departments, practitioner networks, and training systems; the restoration of cultural responsibilities, including those carried by weavers, elders, and young people; the renewal of territorial relationships through access to ancestral lands; and the assertion of environmental authority within and across the legal systems of Canada, the United States, and Mexico.

Real challenges remain. Climate change is making fire seasons longer and fires more extreme in many regions, which narrows the windows for safe burning and raises the risks of any ignition. Funding is uncertain and often tied to short-term grants. Workforce capacity, including trained burn bosses and crews, is limited, and the legal and insurance structure may not support small community programs. Many Indigenous nations lack land bases or recognized authority sufficient to carry out burning at scale. Unrecognized nations may be excluded from programs available to federally recognized ones. Smoke management and public acceptance will continue to matter. And Indigenous practitioners must manage the risk that their knowledge will be appropriated or reduced to techniques while authority remains elsewhere.

Yet the trajectory is also one of strengthened Indigenous governance. Tribal and First Nations governments are building programs; intertribal networks provide mutual support; new laws open doors; younger generations are training in both traditional and contemporary skills. These developments connect to the broader themes of Volume X: self-determination, land and law, cultural resurgence, and environmental governance. They also connect to the next chapter. Fire-managed landscapes were habitats and food systems, linked to the animals, plants, and waters on which communities depend. Chapter 135 follows these connections into the stewardship of fisheries, wildlife, and treaty-protected resources, where Indigenous nations likewise assert management authority grounded in relationships older than the states that regulate them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the contemporary restoration of Indigenous cultural burning is both an ecological transformation and a reconstruction of Indigenous environmental authority. It began from the observation that fire in North America was never only a natural force acting on people. In many regions Indigenous communities applied fire deliberately, at particular seasons and places, to sustain food, medicine, craft materials, game, travel routes, and ceremonial landscapes, and in doing so helped shape cultural landscapes that European observers often misread as wilderness.

Cultural burning was presented as a knowledge system, not merely a technique. It combined detailed local observation of plants, weather, and animals with recognized responsibilities, intergenerational teaching, and, in many communities, spiritual and ceremonial obligations. For that reason the chapter distinguished cultural burning from prescribed burning, wildfire, agricultural burning, fire suppression, and fire exclusion, and insisted that the differences are political and cultural as well as technical.

Ecology set the limits within which these practices operated. Fire regimes differ widely across North America: frequent, low-severity fire characterized many pine forests, oak woodlands, grasslands, and longleaf pine ecosystems, while many coastal rainforests and boreal forests burned rarely and severely. The chapter accordingly cautioned against claims that Indigenous burning created every open landscape, stressing that lightning, climate, soils, and grazing animals also mattered, and that the strength of the case for human influence varies by region and by the evidence available.

Regional case studies illustrated this variety. In California, Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa burning supported acorn-bearing woodlands and basketry materials within systems of ceremony and governance. In the Pacific Northwest, prairie and oak savanna management, camas, and huckleberry fields linked fire to food and social gathering. On the Plains, in the Great Lakes, and in the Southeast, fire interacted with grazing, forest composition, and settlement in ways that researchers continue to debate. In Canada and Mexico, different land tenures and legal systems shaped Indigenous fire practice, and the Rarámuri of the Sierra Madre Occidental provided an important example of Indigenous land use in a frequent-fire forest.

The colonial period disrupted these systems through several mechanisms that the chapter kept distinct. Colonial ideas of property and improvement discounted Indigenous management and encouraged the image of untouched wilderness. Conquest, displacement, and the creation of reservations, reserves, parks, and forests removed access to burned lands. Explicit laws, such as British Columbia's Bush Fire Act of 1874, restricted burning, while indirect barriers, including schooling and land fragmentation, further eroded practice and teaching. Students examined the verified 1887 amendment alongside secondary accounts of the 1874 act, distinguishing statutory text from historical interpretation.

Scientific forestry and the institution of fire suppression gave these restrictions lasting force. The creation of the United States Forest Service in 1905, the Great Fire of 1910, the Weeks and Clarke–McNary Acts, the 10 a.m. policy, and the Smokey Bear campaign established suppression as the dominant doctrine, while Canadian and Mexican institutions pursued parallel forestry policies. The chapter acknowledged that protecting lives, communities, and timber was a legitimate objective and emphasized that the decisive omission was the exclusion of Indigenous knowledge and authority.

The consequences for Indigenous communities included reduced access to food, medicine, and weaving materials, changes in hunting and gathering, loss of access to ceremonial places, and strained transmission of knowledge. At the same time the chapter rejected the idea that fire knowledge vanished and was later rediscovered by outsiders. Families and communities preserved knowledge through quiet practice, teaching, ceremony, and advocacy, and they supplied the foundation for later revitalization.

Ecological consequences varied with ecosystem. In frequent-fire forests, exclusion contributed to denser stands and fuel accumulation; in grasslands and savannas, it permitted woody encroachment; in naturally infrequent-fire systems, its effects were smaller. Modern wildfires arise from interacting factors, including past suppression, climate change, drought, forest management, human ignitions, and residential expansion into the wildland–urban interface, so that no single explanation suffices. Agencies gradually reassessed doctrine in the later twentieth century, but largely without recognizing Indigenous authority.

The resurgence of cultural burning has been led by Indigenous communities through elder-led education, tribal natural resource departments, practitioner networks, and research partnerships. The Karuk, Yurok, and Hupa case demonstrated how a regional effort built institutions, including the Cultural Fire Management Council and the Indigenous Peoples Burning Network, drawing selectively on outside resources such as Prescribed Fire Training Exchanges while asserting Indigenous leadership. Women's leadership, linked to weaving and knowledge-holding, was prominent. The chapter distinguished such Indigenous-led resurgence from agency programs that incorporate selected techniques.

Evidence for past fire regimes comes from oral history, documents, fire scars, charcoal, archaeology, and monitoring technologies. Each can establish some things and not others, and the central lesson was that evidence of fire occurrence is not evidence of who lit the fire or why. Responsible interpretation uses multiple lines of evidence, acknowledges uncertainty, and respects Indigenous control over sensitive knowledge.

Finally, the chapter treated the right to burn as a question of law, jurisdiction, and sovereignty. Barriers include territorial access, permits, air-quality rules, liability, and certification. Responses in British Columbia, Parks Canada, the United States, and California's Senate Bill 310 and its first agreement with the Karuk Tribe show governments recognizing cultural burning to differing degrees. The debate over whether recognition restores sovereignty or incorporates Indigenous practice into settler systems remains open, and the answer depends on who actually holds decision-making authority.

The chapter's conclusion is that returning fire to the land involves returning the relationships, knowledge, institutions, and authority through which Indigenous peoples historically governed landscapes. That claim connects Chapter 134 to the surrounding chapters of Part XXIII, which follow Indigenous stewardship into fisheries and wildlife, extraction, infrastructure, and climate change.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparative History of Indigenous Fire Stewardship

This project asks you to compare three Indigenous fire stewardship systems from different ecological regions of North America and to explain similarities and regional differences using evidence.

  1. Select three systems. Recommended: California Indigenous cultural burning, First Nations fire stewardship in British Columbia, and Rarámuri forest management in Mexico. Other combinations may be approved.
  2. Document historical practice. For each, use scholarly and community-authored sources to describe historical fire practices, ecological conditions, and community responsibilities, noting the kind and strength of evidence.
  3. Trace colonial restriction. Identify laws, institutions, and other barriers, distinguishing explicit prohibitions from indirect restrictions. Consult statute texts where available rather than summaries alone.
  4. Assess consequences and restoration. Describe environmental and cultural consequences of fire exclusion and contemporary restoration programs, including who holds decision-making authority.
  5. Compare and conclude. In a 6–8 page report, explain similarities and differences, state clearly what the evidence establishes and does not, and use careful language where findings are uncertain.

Library resources to use: Tribal and First Nations natural resource department publications, provincial and national archives and legislative records, fire-history and tree-ring literature, government agency histories, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Indigenous environmental history.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 134 matters because it shows, through a single practice, how environmental management, cultural continuity, and political authority were bound together in Indigenous societies, and how their separation reshaped landscapes and lives. The history of fire is a history of who was recognized as a legitimate steward of land, and of how that recognition was withdrawn and is now contested.

The chapter also reveals a larger process visible throughout Volume X: knowledge and authority that were suppressed but not extinguished have been rebuilt by Indigenous communities themselves, with allies and often against institutional resistance. The persistence of fire knowledge through a century of prohibition is a clear instance of the continuity this textbook has emphasized from its first chapter, as is the adaptation of that knowledge to a hotter, more crowded, and more regulated world.

The chapter shaped, and is shaped by, the chapters around it. It builds on the foundations of Indigenous environmental knowledge in Chapter 133 and on the land and sovereignty struggles of Parts XX and XXI, and it anticipates the stewardship of fisheries and wildlife in Chapter 135, the extractive conflicts of Chapter 136, the land defense of Chapter 137, and the climate adaptation of Chapter 138. Each shows Indigenous nations exercising environmental jurisdiction grounded in older relationships.

Students should leave with a disciplined understanding of what the evidence supports. Indigenous burning shaped many landscapes, but not all, and not in identical ways. Fire exclusion has had serious consequences in some ecosystems and little in others. Modern wildfire has many causes. Government recognition can be meaningful and incomplete at once. Holding these complexities together is the work of historical reasoning.

The restoration of cultural burning, finally, is a reminder that environmental history is not only about landscapes. It is about the institutions, families, languages, and laws that give a landscape meaning. Returning fire to the land, in the chapter's central argument, means returning the relationships through which people have long cared for it, and that task belongs, above all, to the Indigenous nations whose homelands these are.
