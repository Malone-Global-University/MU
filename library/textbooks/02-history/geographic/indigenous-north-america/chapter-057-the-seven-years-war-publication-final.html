The Seven Years' War

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

The war that Britain and France fought for North America was also a set of Indigenous wars: struggles over river valleys, trade, alliances, captives, and the right to decide who would be allowed to build forts and settle on Native land. When the French flag came down in Canada, those struggles did not end. They entered a new and more dangerous phase.

In the late summer of 1760, as a British army advanced down the St. Lawrence River toward Montreal, delegations from the Indigenous communities of the St. Lawrence valley met with representatives of the British Crown. Documentary evidence shows that these communities, many of them Catholic mission towns long allied with New France, did not simply wait to learn the terms of a conquest. They conferred among themselves, exchanged messages and diplomatic gifts with the British superintendent of northern Indian affairs, Sir William Johnson, and negotiated understandings concerning peace, religion, land, movement, and trade. A separate agreement was concluded with the Wendat (Huron) community of Lorette in September 1760. These negotiations are one of the clearest windows into the argument of this chapter: Indigenous nations were political actors throughout the Seven Years' War, and the end of French power in Canada did not itself settle the political status or continuing authority of Indigenous peoples in relation to Britain.

The war itself is conventionally dated from 1754 to 1763, with the formal declarations of war between Britain and France coming in 1756 and the Treaty of Paris concluding the wider conflict in February 1763. In the United States it has long been called the "French and Indian War," a name that reflects the point of view of the British colonies, for whom the enemy was France and its Indigenous allies. Indigenous peoples did not experience the war as a single contest with two sides. Haudenosaunee nations made different choices from one another. Ohio Country Lenape (Delaware) and Shawnee communities moved from wary neutrality to war and then, in 1758, back toward negotiation. The Cherokee fought alongside Britain and then against it. The Wabanaki and Mi'kmaq defended homelands that British officials were already claiming. Each decision was made in councils, shaped by local conditions, and aimed at the survival and autonomy of particular communities.

This chapter reconstructs the war from those Indigenous vantage points. It follows the fighting, from Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity in 1754 to the Monongahela, Lake George, Oswego, Fort William Henry, Louisbourg, Fort Duquesne, Niagara, Quebec, and Montreal. But it treats campaigns as the visible outcome of Indigenous decisions about whether and why to take part. It also treats the war as a social and economic process that reached into households, cornfields, trading networks, mission communities, and the lives of captives and refugees, well away from any battlefield.

The evidence for this history is uneven, and students should learn to read it critically. Most surviving written records were produced by British, colonial, and French officers, officials, missionaries, and traders. Indigenous speakers are heard largely through interpreters and clerks who recorded, translated, and edited their words for purposes of their own. Wampum belts, oral traditions preserved in Indigenous communities, archaeological evidence from forts and towns, and the later writings of Indigenous authors supplement this record, but they cannot fill every gap. Many events, notably the killing of Joseph Coulon de Jumonville in 1754 and the violence following the surrender of Fort William Henry in 1757, survive in conflicting accounts, and this chapter says plainly where historians cannot be certain.

The chapter also resists two opposite temptations. One is to treat Indigenous nations as auxiliaries in a European war, valuable chiefly for scouting and ambush. The other is to imagine that Indigenous diplomacy could have decided the war's outcome regardless of European resources. The historical record supports neither extreme. Indigenous decisions repeatedly affected military outcomes, and British material and financial power grew substantially after 1757. The interaction between those two facts is the subject of Divisions III through V.

Finally, the chapter distinguishes European imperial claims from Indigenous sovereignty. In 1763 France transferred its claims in much of eastern North America to Britain, and Spain ceded Florida. Neither treaty was negotiated with, or signed by, the Indigenous nations whose homelands were affected. A claim made by one empire against another was not a voluntary transfer of Native political authority, and large parts of the continent east of the Mississippi remained under Indigenous control in 1763. The new situation opened questions about land, trade, and military security that Chapter 58 will examine in the form of the resistance movement of 1763. Chapter 57 prepares for that discussion by explaining how Indigenous peoples entered the postwar world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Seven Years' War is better understood as a set of interconnected Indigenous and imperial conflicts than as a two-sided contest between Britain and France.
  2. Describe the competing claims to the Ohio Country in the early 1750s and the political positions of France, Britain, Virginia land interests, the Haudenosaunee, and resident Ohio nations.
  3. Analyze the events of 1754, including Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity, with attention to Tanaghrisson's political objectives and to conflicting source accounts.
  4. Evaluate the roles Indigenous forces played in the Monongahela campaign of 1755 and in the northern campaigns of 1755–1757.
  5. Interpret the Fort William Henry episode of 1757 using conflicting evidence, distinguishing documented events from sensationalized retellings.
  6. Assess the social consequences of war for Indigenous and colonial communities, including captivity, displacement, food insecurity, and prisoner exchange.
  7. Compare the wartime strategies of the Haudenosaunee nations, the Seven Nations of Canada, Great Lakes peoples, Wabanaki and Mi'kmaq communities, Ohio Country peoples, and the Cherokee.
  8. Trace the causes, course, and outcome of the Anglo-Cherokee War of 1759–1761 as a conflict connected to, but distinct from, the wider war.
  9. Explain how Indigenous diplomatic realignment, especially the Treaty of Easton of 1758, contributed to the collapse of French strategic positions in the Ohio Country.
  10. Analyze the negotiated agreements of 1760 as evidence of Indigenous political agency during the transition from French to British power in Canada.
  11. Distinguish European imperial claims in the Treaty of Paris from the actual political and territorial control exercised by Indigenous nations in 1763.
  12. Contextualize the situation of 1763 as the background to the resistance movements examined in Chapter 58.

Historical Context

By the middle of the eighteenth century, eastern North America was a densely interconnected political landscape. Chapter 55 examined the institutions and regional systems of Indigenous diplomacy that structured relations among Native nations and between them and Europeans: councils, wampum, gift exchange, kinship metaphors, and the practice of balancing one empire against another. Chapter 56 traced the Covenant Chain, the alliance system connecting the Haudenosaunee with the English colonies, through the Albany Congress of 1754. This chapter assumes that background and asks how those systems functioned when war came.

Three European empires and a growing number of colonial governments had interests on the continent. New France controlled the St. Lawrence valley, maintained posts and missions in the Great Lakes region, and held a long, thinly settled claim along the Mississippi. Its power rested less on settler numbers, which were small compared with those of the British colonies, than on its alliances, gift-giving, and trade with Indigenous nations of the interior. The British colonies along the Atlantic seaboard were far more populous, and their growth created steady pressure on Indigenous land, but they were separate jurisdictions with competing interests and without a unified military command. Spain held Florida and large territories to the west and southwest, and entered the war only late, in 1762.

Indigenous nations were not peripheral to this arrangement; they were its foundation. In the Great Lakes region, the "middle ground" analyzed by historians depended on accommodation between Algonquian-speaking peoples and French traders and officials. In the Northeast, the Haudenosaunee had for generations used neutrality and selective alliance to protect themselves between New France and the English colonies, and had concluded treaties of peace at Montreal and Albany in 1701. In the Ohio Country, migrants from the east, including Lenape, Shawnee, and Haudenosaunee-affiliated peoples sometimes called Mingo, had established towns in the decades before the war, partly to escape colonial pressure. In the South, the Cherokee, Creek, Catawba, Choctaw, and Chickasaw dealt simultaneously with Carolina, Georgia, Virginia, French Louisiana, and one another.

Environmental and economic conditions mattered. The Ohio River and its tributaries linked the Great Lakes, the Mississippi, and the Atlantic watersheds across the Appalachian ridges. Fur, deerskins, and European manufactured goods moved along these routes, as did diplomats and war parties. Many Indigenous communities depended on firearms, powder, lead, cloth, kettles, and metal tools obtained through trade or diplomatic gifts, and the interruption of supply could affect their autonomy. At the same time, they remained farming and hunting peoples whose households produced food and whose councils had the authority to make or decline war. This combination of dependence on trade and retained political independence shapes every section that follows.

Section 57.1

The Seven Years' War as an Indigenous and Imperial Conflict

The Seven Years' War is usually described as the first truly global war. Fighting between Britain and France, and their respective allies, extended across Europe, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, the Philippines, and North America. Historians disagree about how best to rank these theaters, but most accept that the North American conflict began before the formal European declarations of 1756 and that colonial questions, especially the control of the Ohio Valley, helped set the larger war in motion. That alone would make the North American phase important. This chapter argues for a further point: the war on this continent operated at several scales at once, and Indigenous nations were principal actors at almost every one of them.

At the largest scale, the war was an imperial contest in which Britain and France sought advantage in trade, territory, naval power, and alliances. At a second scale, it was a series of conflicts among colonial governments and settlers, who disagreed with one another and with their own metropolitan authorities about land, military funding, and Indigenous policy. At a third scale, it was a set of Indigenous territorial struggles in which nations sought to prevent the building of forts and towns on their lands, to secure trade, to protect or recover people, and to defend the autonomy of their councils. At a fourth scale, it was a war of local defense, fought by villages and households against raiders and armies and for the safety of fields, kin, and captives. These scales intersected but were not identical. A decision made in London or Versailles could not command a Shawnee town council, and a Mohawk war party might act for reasons that had little to do with imperial strategy.

The name "French and Indian War" reflects the perspective of the British colonies. It identifies the enemy as France plus "Indians," a collective term that conceals the identities and differing objectives of the many nations involved. It also frames the conflict as a war with a single opposing coalition. In reality, there was no Indigenous bloc. Some Haudenosaunee men served with British forces while other Haudenosaunee men, living in Canadian mission towns, served with the French; some Cherokee fought for Britain before turning on the colonies that had armed them; many Indigenous communities adopted strategies of neutrality that were hard to maintain but not therefore meaningless. Using "Seven Years' War" as the chapter title does not erase the older name, which appears in the sources and in popular memory, but it avoids letting one participant's vocabulary define the whole.

A second reason to question simple frameworks is that Indigenous nations had their own wars during these years, only some of which were connected to the imperial contest. Intercommunity rivalries, struggles over hunting territories, and obligations of revenge or mourning shaped decisions to fight. European officers frequently misunderstood or ignored these motives, reading Indigenous behavior primarily as loyalty or disloyalty to an empire. When historians reconstruct why a particular group joined a campaign or went home, they need to consider the kinship obligations, council decisions, supplies, and reputational concerns that actually governed participation.

At the level of a single council, entry into the war required a calculation about authority and risk. A military leader could attract volunteers and persuade an allied community to send warriors, yet rarely possessed the power to conscript an entire nation for years of fighting. Warriors weighed the probable rewards of a campaign against the needs of planting and harvesting, the defense of their own towns, the availability of ammunition, and the danger that fighting alongside an imperial officer would make their families targets. The departure of a war party therefore did not necessarily signify the collective decision of every settlement identified by its nation’s name. Conversely, a council’s proclaimed neutrality might coexist with armed participation by individuals or bands pursuing older relationships. The task for a historian is to identify the decision-making community and its circumstances rather than convert every recorded “Indian ally” into a permanent soldier of France or Britain.

This problem of scale also changes how victory and defeat should be interpreted. A fort could fall while the surrounding Indigenous towns remained self-governing; an empire could claim a river valley while needing permission to move through it; a battlefield success might expose allied families to reprisals rather than bring lasting security. Indigenous diplomats assessed campaigns through these consequences, not solely through the raising or lowering of European flags. Written military reports record regiments, distances, and garrison strengths with considerable detail, but they often compress Indigenous participation into a few collective labels. Reading the record against that habit means asking who supplied intelligence, who negotiated access, who carried provisions, which councils authorized participation, and how the costs were borne after the officers departed. These questions connect conventional military history with the political history of the peoples on whose territories it occurred.

Historical Vocabulary
Imperial War, Alliance, Neutrality, and Sovereignty

Imperial war refers here to a conflict in which European states used overseas colonies and Indigenous alliances to pursue goals set largely by competition in Europe and across oceans. It describes the interests of the empires, not the full range of what happened on the ground.

Alliance in Indigenous diplomacy was usually not a permanent, unconditional commitment. It was a relationship maintained through councils, gifts, wampum, trade, and mutual obligation, and it could be renegotiated or suspended when one party failed to honor its side. Kinship language, such as "father" and "brother," expressed different expectations of reciprocity and authority and should not be read as literal descriptions of dependence.

Neutrality was an active diplomatic position, not simply abstention. Neutral nations had to defend their stance through messages, gifts, and sometimes force, and neutrality might be adopted by one community while neighbors fought. The Haudenosaunee used neutrality as a sustained policy after 1701, but in 1754–1763 it frequently broke down at the level of individual towns and warriors.

Sovereignty here means the political authority of Indigenous nations to govern themselves, make war and peace, and control their territories. It is a different matter from European claims to "sovereignty" or "dominion" over land, which empires asserted against one another without necessarily exercising effective control.

Potential misunderstanding: students should not assume that an Indigenous alliance with Britain or France meant political subordination to that power. Nor should they assume that neutrality signaled weakness or indecision. Both were tools used by sovereign communities.

Section 57.2

The Ohio Country and the Crisis of Territorial Authority

The Ohio Country, the broad region drained by the upper Ohio River and its tributaries, was in the 1740s neither a vacancy waiting for European settlement nor an undisputed Indigenous homeland. It was a zone of recent migration and overlapping political claims. Over the preceding decades, Lenape and Shawnee people had moved west from Pennsylvania and the Susquehanna valley, in part to distance themselves from colonial settlement and land purchases, and Haudenosaunee-affiliated families, usually called Mingo in colonial sources, had established communities along the river. Wyandot (Wendat) towns lay to the north near Lake Erie, and Miami communities to the west. These peoples formed their own councils and traded with whomever offered the best terms, which for many years meant Pennsylvania traders operating from the east.

Several outside claims intersected over this population. France asserted possession based on exploration, the Mississippi connection with Canada, and its alliances with Great Lakes nations, and it regarded the Ohio as the link between the St. Lawrence and Louisiana. In 1749 a French expedition under Pierre-Joseph Céloron de Blainville traveled down the Ohio, burying lead plates to proclaim French possession and warning British traders to leave. Britain's claims rested on colonial charters, which in some cases extended nominally to the Pacific, and on the Covenant Chain, since English officials argued that the Haudenosaunee had acknowledged British sovereignty over lands the Haudenosaunee claimed by conquest. Virginia land speculators organized the Ohio Company in the late 1740s and obtained a royal grant to land near the forks of the Ohio. The Haudenosaunee, represented by the Onondaga council, asserted that they held political authority over the resident nations and over land cessions, a claim the Ohio peoples themselves treated with considerable skepticism when it suited them.

The ambiguity of these claims explains much of the crisis. The 1744 Treaty of Lancaster, in which the Haudenosaunee released claims in Virginia, was read by Virginia officials as including lands to the west, while the Haudenosaunee understood it more narrowly. At Logstown in 1752 Virginia negotiated with delegates of the Haudenosaunee and Ohio nations over permission to build a fort and settle, and the participants left with different understandings of what had been agreed. Colonial officials thus claimed a legitimate title that many Indigenous participants did not recognize, and the ambiguity encouraged each side to build forts and send traders in order to establish facts on the ground.

French responses grew more forceful after 1750. In June 1752 a force of Odawa, Ojibwe, and French-Canadian fighters led by Charles Langlade destroyed the Miami town of Pickawillany, a major trading center that had developed a close relationship with Pennsylvania traders; its prominent leader Memeskia was killed. In 1753 and early 1754 France built a chain of forts south of Lake Erie, at Presque Isle, Le Boeuf, and Venango, and in 1754, after Virginians began a small fort at the forks of the Ohio, the French seized the site and built Fort Duquesne. These posts protected French communications, but to Ohio peoples they were also a new, armed presence in their homelands. Many leaders initially objected to French forts as strongly as to British ones, and some regarded the French advance as a threat to their ability to control trade and play the empires against each other.

The Ohio River system mattered to different participants for different reasons. For France, it connected Canada to Louisiana and kept British settlement east of the mountains. For Virginia investors, it offered land to sell. For Pennsylvania traders, it opened access to fur and deerskins. For the Haudenosaunee, it was a region over which their diplomats sought continued leverage. For Lenape, Shawnee, Wyandot, Miami, and Mingo communities, it was a home, a food-producing landscape, and a trading position that they wanted to keep free of fortified garrisons. The war that followed did not begin because one empire invaded an empty country but because several powers tried to convert ambiguous authority into effective control in a landscape already full of political communities.

Section 57.3

The Outbreak of War, 1754: Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity

In the autumn of 1753, the young Virginia officer George Washington carried a message from Governor Robert Dinwiddie to the French commander at Fort Le Boeuf, demanding that France withdraw from the region. Tanaghrisson, the Haudenosaunee-affiliated leader often called the Half-King, accompanied him for part of the journey. Tanaghrisson had been placed by the Haudenosaunee council to speak for its interests among the Ohio nations, and he had his own reasons for engaging with Virginia: he hoped to secure British support against the French advance and to reinforce Haudenosaunee authority, which was being challenged by French forts and by the growing independence of the Ohio towns. French officials rejected Virginia's demand. In spring 1754 Virginia sent forces westward, and Tanaghrisson and some followers joined Washington's column as it moved toward the forks.

On May 28, 1754, Washington, with a Virginia detachment and a small number of Tanaghrisson's followers, attacked a French party at a ravine now called Jumonville Glen. The French commander, Joseph Coulon de Jumonville, was killed along with about ten of his men, and others were captured. Beyond those basic facts, the sources diverge. The French claimed that Jumonville led a diplomatic mission carrying a summons asking the British to withdraw, and that he was killed while trying to read it. Washington and his officers maintained that the party was a scouting force sent to reconnoiter and that the summons was a cover. Several accounts, including testimony from French survivors, say that Tanaghrisson struck Jumonville down himself, perhaps in a deliberate act of political violence. Washington's own reports are contradictory in detail, and the confusion was compounded by language barriers and by the later political uses to which both sides put the incident.

Tanaghrisson's role deserves careful attention. He was not simply an auxiliary of Washington but a political actor with objectives that did not match Virginia's. His authority rested on his position as a Haudenosaunee representative, and he sought to commit the British to the defense of Ohio nations, to make the French leave, and to demonstrate his own capacity to act. The killing of Jumonville may have been intended to make a break with France irreversible and to compel the British to fight. It is also possible that violence escalated in a chaotic skirmish. Historians weigh the evidence differently, and the surviving documents come from men who had reasons to place responsibility elsewhere. Nor is it possible to say what individual followers of Tanaghrisson intended. What is clear is that Indigenous decisions and acts, rather than a European order, helped determine the moment at which skirmishing turned into a crisis.

After Jumonville Glen, Washington's force built a stockade at Great Meadows, called Fort Necessity, and prepared for a French response. Tanaghrisson and many of his followers left the area, reportedly because they were dissatisfied with Washington's leadership and doubtful of British ability to protect them, and some Indigenous families who had gathered near the Virginians withdrew with them. Tanaghrisson died in October 1754 while in Pennsylvania. His departure illustrates a recurring pattern: Indigenous allies assessed the competence and reliability of the power they were asked to support, and withdrew when they judged the risk too great.

In early July a French and Indigenous force under Louis Coulon de Villiers, brother of Jumonville, attacked Fort Necessity. After a day of fighting in rain, Washington negotiated a capitulation on July 3 and was permitted to march out. The surrender document, written in French, contained language describing Jumonville's death in terms that Washington, who did not read French, signed without understanding. The French then used it as evidence that the British had admitted to an assassination. The incident gave France a propaganda advantage, and its military result was that the French now controlled the forks of the Ohio with a garrison at Fort Duquesne. For Ohio Indigenous communities, the British failure at Fort Necessity was a significant piece of information about Britain's strength.

Both sets of papers were circulated and published by their governments to shape European opinion, and the dispute over the incident became a topic of diplomatic correspondence.

French accounts say the party came to deliver a message and that the attack was a violation of the rules protecting envoys. Several accounts describe Tanaghrisson's role in the death of Jumonville; the details differ. These are paraphrases of the sources, not quotations.

Tanaghrisson's own account survives, if at all, only through colonial interpreters and clerks. Perspective: The surviving record reflects British and French officers. Missing are the voices of the Indigenous participants as they would have described their own aims, and the viewpoints of the Ohio communities who watched the events unfold.

Section 57.4

Choosing War, Alliance, or Neutrality

In 1754 and 1755, Indigenous councils across the northeastern and Great Lakes regions weighed the same questions: whether the new fighting threatened their communities, which empire was more likely to respect their interests, whether to take part at all, and what neutrality might cost. Decision-making in Indigenous nations generally relied on consultation among village and clan leaders, women who held authority in matrilineal societies, and men with reputations as speakers and warriors. Councils could advise, but participation in a war party remained voluntary, and no council could compel a warrior to fight or prevent a family from seeking revenge. This structure gave Indigenous communities flexibility, and it also meant that decisions made by leaders often failed to bind all members.

The factors weighing on a council were concrete. One was security: whether an army moving through the region would respect a town's autonomy, and whether the town could be protected if it sided with one power. A second was supply. Powder, lead, cloth, kettles, and metal tools were needed for hunting and for defense, and an empire that could not supply them offered a poor alliance. A third was land. Nations that had watched colonial settlement push west considered how far a British victory would extend that pressure. A fourth was kinship and obligation: communities with ties to Canadian mission towns, or to Christian missionaries, or to Pennsylvania traders, weighed those relationships. A fifth was reputation, because leaders gained or lost influence through successful action and through reading events correctly.

These considerations produced distinct strategies. The Haudenosaunee Onondaga council, maintaining the policy of neutrality, tried to keep the Confederacy out of the war while its Mohawk and Seneca communities were under pressure from both sides, a subject treated in Section 57.9. Many Ohio Lenape and Shawnee at first held back, concluding after Fort Necessity that France controlled the region but unwilling to commit themselves. The Great Lakes nations with long-standing ties to French traders and officials, including Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, and Wyandot communities, began sending contingents to Fort Duquesne and other French posts, motivated by trade, gifts, and the strength of the French position. The Catholic mission communities of the St. Lawrence, whose members included Mohawk, Abenaki, Algonquin, and Wendat families, were bound to New France by religion, settlement, and the proximity of French armies, while preserving the option to negotiate. In the south, Cherokee leaders considered offers from both Virginia and the Carolinas, as Section 57.12 describes.

The key lesson of 1754–1755 is that Indigenous nations were not arrayed in two camps but were making provisional choices. The Braddock expedition of 1755 changed the balance of considerations for many Ohio nations. Before it, the British had little to show for their claims. After it, as Section 57.5 explains, British failure persuaded many Ohio Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo that French power was the better bet and that British settlements were exposed. Their decision to attack the Pennsylvania and Virginia frontier in autumn 1755 was not a general Indigenous act of war but a consequence of specific local conclusions about security and opportunity. Raids such as those on Penn's Creek in October 1755 and on the Moravian mission at Gnadenhutten in November 1755 followed from these calculations. Teedyuscung, speaking for Lenape communities on the Susquehanna, initially joined the fighting, then used it to press Pennsylvania on land claims and to negotiate peace.

Neutrality could not always survive, because armies and war parties did not respect it. A town that sheltered refugees or provided corn to one side risked punishment by the other. Warriors who joined a campaign could draw their families into the consequences. The choices available to Indigenous communities narrowed as the war went on, and Divisions II and III show how different groups handled that narrowing. At the same time, the possibility of reversing a decision remained an important source of leverage. As the next division shows, Indigenous military contributions were substantial in the early years, and Indigenous leaders understood that their participation was valuable and could be withdrawn.

Section 57.5

Braddock's Defeat and the Battle of the Monongahela, 1755

In 1755 Britain sent General Edward Braddock to North America with regular regiments and instructions to dislodge France from the Ohio. Braddock's column, supplemented by Virginia and other provincial troops, left Fort Cumberland in Maryland in the spring and cut a road across the Appalachian ridges toward Fort Duquesne. The expedition was large, slow, and heavily dependent on wagons and artillery, and it moved through country where Indigenous communities could observe it closely. Its progress was therefore known to the French at the forks long before it arrived, and it moved without extensive Indigenous scouting of its own.

The relationship between Braddock and potential Indigenous allies was poor. A few Ohio and Haudenosaunee-affiliated leaders, including Scarouady, joined the expedition, and Pennsylvania's trader George Croghan brought a small group of warriors. Braddock and colonial agents did attempt to recruit Indigenous support, including through meetings and diplomatic emissaries, but their efforts produced limited cooperation. Disagreements over land, gifts, treatment of envoys, and the authority of the commanding general alienated potential allies; the result was damaging alliance management, not an absence of recruitment. Testimony recorded afterward by colonial officials attributed to the Lenape leader Shingas an account that Braddock refused to give assurances about what land the Lenape would retain if the French were driven out, saying that no one would settle the land except the English. Such reports come at second hand and should be read with caution, but they accord with the pattern of British dealings with the Ohio nations and with the choices those nations made. Large numbers of potential allies stayed away or withdrew.

On July 9, 1755, the advance column of Braddock's army crossed the Monongahela River about ten miles from Fort Duquesne. French commander Daniel-Hyacinthe-Marie Liénard de Beaujeu led a mixed force of French marines, Canadian militia, and Indigenous warriors out from the fort. The contribution of Indigenous forces was large; modern estimates of their numbers vary, but most place them at several hundred, drawn from Great Lakes nations such as the Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, and Wyandot, together with Ohio Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo. Some accounts say that many warriors were initially reluctant to engage an army of this size, and that Beaujeu, an officer with experience among Great Lakes peoples, persuaded them to attack. Whatever the exact sequence, Indigenous and French fighters met the British advance in the woods before it could deploy artillery effectively.

The battle was brief and decisive. British regulars, trained to fight in close formation on open ground, were caught in a narrow road with wooded ridges on either side from which musket fire poured in. Beaujeu was killed early, and command passed to Jean-Daniel Dumas, who held the force together. Indigenous fighters moved along the flanks and rear while French and Canadian troops engaged the front, and the British column collapsed into confusion. Braddock was mortally wounded and died on July 13; a large proportion of his officers and men were killed or wounded, and the survivors retreated, with young Virginia officer Washington helping to organize the withdrawal. The British abandoned much of their baggage and artillery, which became booty for the victors.

It would be wrong to describe the battle as a European victory achieved with Indigenous help. The French and Indigenous allies were not under a single command, and Indigenous warriors fought for their own reasons: defense of their homelands, gifts and plunder, standing in their communities, and avenging earlier injuries. Indigenous fighters exercised their own tactical judgment in the woods, and their decision to attack, rather than withdraw, was decisive. At the same time, the French garrison and its logistical system made it possible for such a force to assemble at the forks. The victory was an alliance outcome.

The political consequences were large. The British failure left the frontiers of Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia open, and French and Indigenous war parties began raiding in the autumn. Ohio nations that had hesitated drew the lesson that British claims could not be enforced. The defeat also affected relations between Britain and the Haudenosaunee, who had been urged to support the expedition. Section 57.15 shows that Ohio Indigenous nations would change course again as the strategic situation changed.

Section 57.6

The Northern War: Lake George, Oswego, and the Campaigns of 1755–1756

The northern theater of the war, along Lake Champlain, Lake George, the Mohawk valley, and Lake Ontario, was a region where Indigenous diplomacy and military planning were closely entwined. These waterways formed the corridors between New France and New York, and they passed through or near lands claimed by the Haudenosaunee, the Abenaki, and the St. Lawrence mission communities. They were also the zone in which the Covenant Chain's wartime stresses were greatest, because Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee communities lived next to the fighting and had kin on the other side.

In 1755 the British planned several coordinated operations. One, led by William Johnson, aimed at Crown Point on Lake Champlain, and a second, under William Shirley, at Fort Niagara. Johnson had recruited several hundred Haudenosaunee warriors, mainly Mohawk, partly through his personal relationships with Hendrick and other leaders. French officials responded by sending Baron Dieskau, the commander of regular troops, with a mixed force of French regulars, Canadian militia, and Indigenous warriors drawn largely from the Canadian mission communities, including Kahnawake Mohawk. In September 1755, an ambush by this force on a British scouting party led by Ephraim Williams, remembered as the Bloody Morning Scout, killed Hendrick and many others, and was followed by the Battle of Lake George, in which Johnson's army repelled the French attack and captured the wounded Dieskau.

The Lake George campaign illustrates the problem of Haudenosaunee participation. Hendrick himself reportedly voiced doubts about the plan and about the size of the British column, and Mohawk fighters, aware that they might meet Kahnawake relatives in battle, were uneasy about the coming fight. Johnson's own reports record that many Haudenosaunee did not want to attack Indigenous kin, and the encounter reinforced a widely shared reluctance to fight men of their own nations. After the battle, Johnson was rewarded with a baronetcy, while the British failed to take Crown Point, and the French built Fort Carillon at Ticonderoga to block the route. The Haudenosaunee, having lost a major leader, became less willing to commit large forces in future years.

On Lake Ontario, the British post at Oswego was a trading center for the Haudenosaunee, especially the Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, and it also threatened French communications with the interior. In March 1756 a French and Indigenous raiding party under Gaspard-Joseph Chaussegros de Léry destroyed Fort Bull, a supply depot on the route to Oswego. In August 1756 Montcalm, with regulars, militia, and Indigenous allies, brought artillery from Fort Frontenac and captured Oswego after a short siege. For the Haudenosaunee, the loss was a serious blow to British prestige and to trade, and it showed the French capacity to strike effectively. Many Haudenosaunee drew the conclusion that neutrality offered better protection than a close tie to a British position that had been so easily taken.

Indigenous allies contributed to these campaigns in ways beyond combat. They served as scouts, couriers, guides, and interpreters, supplied meat and information, and handled the canoes and portages on which movement over waterways depended. French commanders relied on their allies' knowledge of routes and conditions, and on their ability to assess the strength of enemy positions without exposing a larger force. These services were valuable, which is why gift-giving was central to the alliance: officers who wanted allies to remain in the field had to provide food, clothing, powder, and recognition of status. When these were short, warriors left.

Diplomatic competition ran alongside the fighting. Both Johnson and the French sent messages to Haudenosaunee councils at Onondaga and elsewhere, urging them to commit to one side or to remain neutral, and each tried to portray the other as a threat to Indigenous land. In 1756 Johnson was commissioned by the Crown as superintendent of northern Indian affairs, putting a single official in charge of British dealings with the Haudenosaunee and their neighbors, rather than leaving the work to the colonies. The new office strengthened British capacity to coordinate gifts and councils, and reflected British recognition that Indigenous diplomacy was crucial. It did not guarantee that Indigenous nations would follow British direction.

Section 57.7

Fort William Henry, 1757: Victory, Surrender, and Contested Violence

In August 1757 Montcalm led an army against Fort William Henry, the British fort at the southern end of Lake George built after the 1755 battle. The expedition gathered a very large Indigenous contingent, well over a thousand warriors by most accounts, from many nations, including peoples of the western Great Lakes, some of whom had not previously joined a campaign in the east. They came in response to French invitations transmitted through Vaudreuil and through Indigenous diplomatic networks, and they brought their own aims: gifts, plunder, captives, and honor. The scale and diversity of the force made management difficult. French officers had to coordinate the movements of people who were allies rather than subordinates and who spoke different languages.

The siege lasted about a week. French artillery bombarded the fort, and Lieutenant Colonel George Monro, whose appeals for reinforcement from Fort Edward were ignored, agreed on August 9 to surrender. The terms of capitulation allowed the garrison to march out with honors of war, retaining their muskets but not ammunition, under French escort, to Fort Edward. This was a conventional European arrangement. It was negotiated by European commanders, however, and the Indigenous allies had not accepted its terms or been fully consulted. For them, the siege had been fought to gain spoils and captives, and the surrender denied them much of what they had expected.

On the morning of August 10, as the British column prepared to leave, some Indigenous warriors entered the camp, took goods, and seized people, including soldiers, women, and others associated with the garrison. As the column moved out, attacks occurred, with some British and colonial personnel killed and others taken captive. French officers, including Montcalm, attempted to intervene, and some captives were redeemed by the French afterwards. What happened over the following hours is difficult to reconstruct. Eyewitness accounts differ on the number of dead, the extent of organized violence, the role of particular nations, and the French response. Early British claims of hundreds or even a thousand killed circulated widely, but most modern scholarship, working with muster and burial evidence, estimates that fewer died than those first reports suggested, probably in the range of tens to a couple of hundred, while a larger number were captured. These numbers remain uncertain.

Historians try to avoid assigning uniform motives to the Indigenous participants. Many warriors were angry at what they saw as an unfair settlement. The terms had left them without the plunder customarily expected, and the capitulation barred the surrender of enemy property. Some may have acted in revenge for earlier losses; others may have wanted captives for adoption or ransom, practices central to many communities' ways of recovering from losses of population, as Section 57.8 explains. Some Indigenous leaders sought to restrain the violence, while others participated or stood aside. The warriors came from different nations and were not a single group with a single plan. Nor were French officers uniformly passive or complicit: they differed over what could be done, and some were criticized afterward by both British and Indigenous observers.

The aftermath carried further consequences. Some western warriors returned home with captives and the belongings of the dead, and later accounts, difficult to verify, link their return to the spread of smallpox in Great Lakes communities, though historians disagree about the extent and cause of that outbreak. French authorities spent considerable effort ransoming prisoners, and the episode strained relations with some allies who felt that French officers had interfered with their customary entitlements. The event entered popular memory through British colonial propaganda, which portrayed it as an Indigenous atrocity and French failure, and through the 1826 novel The Last of the Mohicans, which fixed a dramatic but inaccurate image in many readers' minds. A responsible account must separate documented events from these retellings.

Section 57.8

Borderland Warfare, Captivity, and the Survival of Communities

Most of the war's violence was not fought in sieges or pitched battles but in raids, ambushes, and counterraids against farms, villages, and small forts. From the autumn of 1755 onward, war parties of Lenape, Shawnee, Mingo, and other peoples attacked settlements in Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia, often in coordination with French operations from Fort Duquesne. Colonial settlers abandoned farms and crowded into forts and towns; colonial governments debated defense and in some cases declared war on particular Indigenous nations and offered bounties for scalps. Pennsylvania declared war on the Lenape and Shawnee in 1756, and Massachusetts offered scalp bounties in 1755. These policies targeted Indigenous people generally and could endanger communities that had remained peaceful, such as the Moravian converts, who found themselves suspected by settlers and officials.

Indigenous communities suffered attacks of their own. In September 1756 a Pennsylvania force under John Armstrong destroyed the Lenape town of Kittanning on the Allegheny, a base for raids led by leaders including Shingas and Captain Jacobs, and recovered some captives. The raid reduced the threat from that town, but it also drove Indigenous families from their homes and intensified resentment. Elsewhere, forces from New England and New York destroyed villages and crops in the Wabanaki and Haudenosaunee peripheries, while French and Indigenous raiders did the same to colonial settlements. The destruction of corn stores and housing was among the most damaging consequences of the war, because it threatened winter survival and forced reliance on kin, allies, or scarce gifts.

Captivity was a central feature of this warfare, and it served different purposes in different societies. For many Indigenous communities, taking captives was linked to mourning practices, in which the loss of a family member could be offset by adopting a captive to replace the dead person. Adopted captives became members of kin groups and, in many cases, lived out their lives in their new communities. Others were held for ransom, traded to the French or British, or sold into servitude. Colonial captives experienced a wide range of outcomes: some were killed, some died of hardship or disease, some were redeemed or exchanged, and some, especially children and young women, chose or were pressured to remain with their adoptive families. Mary Jemison, taken from Pennsylvania in 1758 and adopted by Seneca women, lived the rest of her life among the Seneca; her narrative, recorded in the 1820s by a local writer, is a later retrospective source and must be used with caution. Indigenous people were captives too, taken by colonial raids or sold into slavery in the colonies and the Caribbean, though their experiences are far less documented.

Prisoner exchange and return became a diplomatic issue. Treaties and councils during the war, notably the Easton conferences of 1757 and 1758, regularly raised the question of captives held by Indigenous nations and by colonial governments. British officials demanded the return of colonial captives, while Indigenous leaders sometimes argued that adopted individuals had become members of their communities and should not be forced to leave. Such disputes continued after 1763 and are examined further in Chapter 58.

Food security reveals why civilian experience cannot be separated from military strategy. Farming communities depended on the seasonal coordination of household work: preparing fields, tending maize and beans, harvesting, drying stores, and carrying provisions to kin who had joined war parties or fled violence. An attack during the growing season could prevent cultivation even when a village survived; an autumn raid could destroy the stores on which families depended through winter. Moving to another town placed additional demands on hosts whose own crops were limited. In colonial settlements, garrisons also consumed grain and livestock while refugee families crowded into towns and improvised defenses. The destruction of food sources was not merely collateral damage: some commanders understood it as a way to deprive opponents of the capacity to return, rebuild, or sustain resistance. Historians must distinguish these effects without assuming that every attack pursued precisely the same purpose.

A captive’s fate likewise depended on decisions made far from the battle in which that person had been taken. Some families adopted an outsider into a specific place within their kinship network and expected the new relative to live according to that community’s obligations. Others exchanged prisoners to recover relatives, negotiated ransoms, or retained captives under conditions of coercion. Colonial officials often classified all these situations under a single word, “captivity,” while redemption campaigns could be experienced differently by an adopted person and the family in which that person lived. Accounts written after return to a colonial community have particular value for personal experience but may reproduce expectations demanded by readers and religious sponsors. The evidence is stronger when such narratives can be compared with council minutes, lists of exchanged prisoners, community traditions, and descriptions of the circumstances of adoption. The chapter cannot treat Indigenous practices as uniform across the continent, nor romanticize captivity as free from violence.

Historical Vocabulary
Captivity, Prisoner Exchange, and Frontier Warfare

Captivity refers to the taking and holding of people in war. In Indigenous practice, it served varied purposes, including adoption to replace lost kin, ransom, labor, and political leverage. It was not identical to chattel slavery, although some captives were sold into servitude, and the line between categories shifted in practice.

Prisoner exchange was the negotiated return of captives, usually at councils or through intermediaries. It involved competing expectations: European officials treated captives as property or subjects to be recovered, while some Indigenous communities regarded adoptees as kin.

Frontier warfare is a historians' term for raiding and counterraiding in contested settlement zones. The word "frontier" carries the assumption of a settler perspective; it was a homeland for Indigenous peoples, and this chapter uses the term cautiously.

Potential misunderstanding: accounts of captivity written by colonial survivors emphasize fear and suffering, and they were often published to influence opinion. They are valuable evidence but do not describe the full range of captives' experiences, nor the perspective of those who adopted them.

Everyday Life and Social History

War, Household Survival, Displacement, and Supplies

Indigenous households organized their year around farming, hunting, fishing, and gathering, with women in many nations responsible for cultivating corn, beans, and squash and men for hunting and trade. War disrupted both parts of this cycle. When men left on campaigns that lasted weeks or months, hunting and the defense of towns suffered. When fields were burned, as in the raids described above, communities faced hunger and relied on stored food, relatives, and allies. Households that took in refugees shared limited stocks.

Supplies were a related concern. Powder, lead, and trade cloth reached many communities as gifts or purchases, and when French or British supply lines were interrupted, local economies were affected. Some communities relocated to places closer to French posts or mission towns, where food and protection were available, and others moved away from the fighting altogether. Mission communities on the St. Lawrence received refugees, as did Indigenous towns in the Ohio Country and the Great Lakes.

The war also affected religious and family life. Moravian and Catholic mission communities were caught between suspicion from neighbors and demands from officials. Captives adopted into families changed the composition of households. Disease, often carried by armies and returning war parties, added to losses. Because Indigenous sources for these experiences are limited, historians use archaeology, mission records, and later oral traditions alongside the dominant colonial documents to reconstruct them.

Section 57.9

Haudenosaunee Diplomacy and the Difficulties of Neutrality

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy occupied a position unlike that of any other Indigenous polity in the war. Its homeland lay between New France and the English colonies, its member nations had treaty relationships with both, and its diplomats had for half a century used the policy established in the 1701 agreements to avoid being drawn into European conflicts. The Covenant Chain, discussed in Chapter 56, linked the Confederacy with the English colonies, particularly New York, but it was a relationship of mutual obligation rather than subordination. The Confederacy's council fire at Onondaga was the center of its confederal diplomacy, where decisions required consensus among representatives of the nations. In wartime, consensus was difficult to reach, and the Confederacy's members made different choices.

The Mohawk, the easternmost nation, lived closest to the English settlements and had the closest ties to William Johnson, who had lived among them as a trader and landowner and had family connections with Mohawk families. Many Mohawk men served with Johnson's forces in 1755 and afterward, though as the Lake George campaign showed, they did so reluctantly when faced with fighting Kahnawake relatives. The Oneida and Tuscarora, whose territories lay to the west of the Mohawk, tended also to favor the British, though with caution, and Oneida men served with British forces on several occasions. The Onondaga, who kept the council fire, strove to maintain neutrality, and they received messages and gifts from both sides. The Cayuga followed a generally neutral line. The Seneca, the westernmost and most populous nation, were divided: many in the eastern Seneca towns supported neutrality or the British, while western Seneca communities along the Genesee and in the Ohio Country had close ties with the French and with the Ohio nations, and some Seneca warriors fought with French forces.

The loss of Oswego in 1756 weakened the British position among the Haudenosaunee, and for the next two years Johnson worked to maintain their friendship through councils, gifts, and recognition of their importance. His appointment as superintendent gave him authority and resources, and he organized major conferences at his home at Fort Johnson and elsewhere. Haudenosaunee leaders were aware that the British needed them. They bargained over gifts, forts, and land, and some sought assurances that British forces would not occupy sites on Haudenosaunee territory. A few, such as the Mohawk leader Little Abraham, were openly critical of British policy and of Johnson's reliance on land-hungry colonial interests.

The Confederacy's participation increased as British prospects improved. In 1758 Johnson assembled several hundred Haudenosaunee warriors for the campaigns of that year, and in 1759 a larger force, approaching a thousand by most estimates, joined the expedition against Fort Niagara. At Niagara, as Section 57.16 describes, Haudenosaunee diplomats on the British side communicated with Indigenous warriors who had come to relieve the French, including Seneca from the western towns, and urged them to withdraw. This episode shows the Confederacy acting as a diplomatic force in its own right, and it also illustrates the ways its members' differing alignments could be used to prevent bloodshed between kin.

The war exposed the limits of confederal unanimity. The Confederacy had no mechanism to compel its member nations or towns to follow a common policy, and the leadership of each nation had to contend with the views of its own communities. After the war, Haudenosaunee leaders emphasized that they had remained a political entity, but the experience of divergent wartime choices contributed to the later strains that Chapter 58 and Chapter 59 will examine, notably in the western Seneca's role in the resistance of 1763 and the Confederacy's division in the American Revolution.

Historical Debate

Can the Haudenosaunee Confederacy as a Whole Be Called Neutral?

The question: Historians and the Haudenosaunee themselves have often described the Confederacy as pursuing a policy of neutrality in the wars between the empires. But in 1754–1763 members of the Six Nations fought on both sides and some not at all. Does the term "neutrality" meaningfully describe what the Confederacy did?

Interpretation A — Neutrality was a real policy of the Confederacy: Some scholars emphasize that the Onondaga council consistently proclaimed neutrality, that it was a principle of Haudenosaunee diplomacy since 1701, and that the Confederacy as an institution refused to declare war on either empire. The fact that individuals and towns acted otherwise, in this view, reflects the voluntary nature of participation rather than the abandonment of the policy.
Interpretation B — Neutrality was a label that concealed fragmentation: Other historians argue that in practice, the Confederacy's members pursued their own interests, with Mohawk and Oneida leaning toward Britain and western Seneca toward France, so that "neutrality" described a diplomatic claim more than a coordinated strategy. On this reading, the Confederacy functioned less as a unified actor than as a forum for negotiation among nations with different positions.
Interpretation C — Divergence was itself a strategy: A third view suggests that members' differing alignments gave the Confederacy flexibility, allowing it to maintain contacts with both sides and to adjust as circumstances changed, so that division and neutrality worked together as a form of risk management.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on council records transcribed by colonial secretaries, Johnson's correspondence, French reports, and the records of individual campaigns. All of these were produced by outsiders, and Haudenosaunee oral traditions about the war are less well documented in the published record.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and interpretation more than the basic events. Historians agree that different Haudenosaunee communities acted differently; they differ on whether "neutrality" best characterizes the whole.

Why it matters: If the Confederacy is treated as a unified neutral power, its later divisions may appear as a break from earlier practice. If it is treated as a federation with diverse interests, those divisions appear as a continuation of existing patterns.

Section 57.10

The Great Lakes and St. Lawrence: Indigenous Allies of New France

New France depended on Indigenous allies more than any other European colony in North America, and the Seven Years' War made that dependence especially clear. The French population of Canada was small relative to that of the British colonies, and French military strategy relied on being able to concentrate Indigenous and Canadian fighters quickly, strike at exposed British positions, and retire. The system worked only if allies chose to come, and their choices rested on a long history of relationships with French traders, missionaries, and officials in the Great Lakes region.

Among the most important allies were the Odawa, Ojibwe, and Potawatomi, the Anishinaabe peoples of the upper Great Lakes, along with the Wendat (Wyandot) around Detroit and Sandusky, the Miami, and peoples further west such as the Menominee, Sauk, Meskwaki, Ho-Chunk, and Illinois. These nations conducted their own diplomacy and had their own aims. Many supplied warriors to French campaigns in the east as part of the exchange relationships that provided them with goods, access to French posts, and a counterweight to rival nations. Charles Langlade, a man of Odawa and French ancestry who led warriors from the Great Lakes in several major operations, exemplified the ways in which kinship and trade created bridges between communities. French officers, in turn, depended on such intermediaries and on interpreters.

The mission communities of the St. Lawrence were a second pillar of French military power. Kahnawake, Kanesatake (the Lake of Two Mountains), Akwesasne (St. Regis, established in the 1750s), Oswegatchie (La Présentation), Lorette, Odanak, and Wôlinak had been founded by Catholic missionaries and settled by people from many Indigenous nations, including Mohawk, Algonquin, Nipissing, Abenaki, and Wendat. These and related mission communities are frequently discussed through the diplomatic designation “Seven Nations of Canada,” although historical lists of the constituent settlements vary according to period and source. The designation should not be treated as an unchanging roster or a centralized state: councils sometimes acted jointly, while individual communities retained their own interests and diplomatic relationships. They lived within a day or two of French settlements and provided warriors, scouts, and intelligence. Yet they were not merely French instruments. Their councils decided policy, they maintained contacts with Haudenosaunee kin south of the border, and they weighed the risks of the war to their own communities. Their position, as both allies of New France and people with ties to the other side, shaped the negotiations of 1760.

Relations between Indigenous allies and French officials were managed through a system of gifts and councils that was costly and politically delicate. Governor-General Vaudreuil, born in Canada and experienced in Indigenous diplomacy, favored the use of allied raiding parties, while Montcalm and other officers from France were more skeptical of irregular tactics and of the cost and unpredictability of Indigenous allies. The disagreements affected operations and were noticed by Indigenous leaders. They also shaped how French officers responded to incidents such as that at Fort William Henry.

Supply was a recurring problem. As the British naval blockade tightened and the war's costs rose, French stocks of trade goods declined and prices increased. By 1758 and 1759, French officials had difficulty maintaining the flow of gifts that allies expected. In addition, the defeats of 1758, particularly the loss of Fort Frontenac, which was a major supply depot for the western posts, constrained French ability to equip allies at a distance. Fewer western warriors came east as the war continued, and some nations began to explore their options with the British. The reasons western nations sent warriors to eastern campaigns varied: some sought to fulfill alliance obligations, others to defend trade routes and homeland security indirectly, and some judged the campaigns too distant from immediate community needs. Their participation was a matter of alliance and trade, which meant that it could change when those considerations did.

There was also a geographic distinction within the alliance system itself. Great Lakes diplomats commonly assessed the war through the security of travel corridors between Michilimackinac, Detroit, the Niagara portage, and the eastern forts. St. Lawrence mission towns, by contrast, were near enough to French centers of supply and military command to experience the threat of British advance directly. Their councils combined Catholic religious institutions with their own kinship arrangements and diplomatic procedures. Connections across the border complicated a simple division between French and British ‘Indians’: some Haudenosaunee households maintained relatives and correspondents on both sides of the imperial contest. Communication across those lines could enable intelligence gathering, the recovery of captives, or negotiations for neutrality. It could also draw suspicion from colonial authorities. These relations help explain why the mission communities became participants in deliberate diplomatic negotiations in 1760 rather than passive communities awaiting the fall of Montreal.

When French supplies diminished, the consequences were political as well as material. Presents, repairs to firearms, access to powder, and assistance to visitors were components of an ongoing reciprocal relationship. A French officer who could no longer fulfill expectations faced questions about the value of his promises; an Indigenous council could decide that risking warriors in a distant siege was no longer justified. Yet withdrawal from a particular campaign must not automatically be read as a conversion to the British cause. A community might reject French requests while simultaneously opposing British settlement or insisting that both empires keep soldiers from its homeland. The distinction matters most at the war’s end: negotiations with Britain were shaped by expectations formed during the French alliance, including security of land, freedom of movement, and the ceremonial recognition of political relationships. British military superiority did not eliminate the need to bargain over those conditions.

Section 57.11

Wabanaki, Mi'kmaq, Acadia, and the Atlantic Northeast

In the Atlantic Northeast, the war was an extension of conflicts that had begun long before 1754. The Wabanaki, including the Penobscot, Passamaquoddy, Maliseet, and Abenaki, and the Mi'kmaq, whose homeland included present-day Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Prince Edward Island, had fought and negotiated with New England and Acadia for generations. Their territories bordered the French colony of Acadia, which Britain had formally acquired in 1713, and they had treaties with Massachusetts and, later, Nova Scotia that dealt with peace, trade, and settlement. These agreements, understood differently by the parties, formed the diplomatic background to the war.

Tensions rose after 1749, when Britain founded Halifax as a military and settlement base in Nova Scotia. The Mi'kmaq regarded the new settlement as a threat to their lands, and fighting broke out. Colonial officials offered bounties for the scalps of Mi'kmaq, and a treaty negotiated in 1752 between the Governor of Nova Scotia and a Mi'kmaq leader from the Shubenacadie region did not bring a lasting peace, since many Mi'kmaq communities did not consider themselves bound by it. The French, from their bases at Louisbourg on Île Royale and in the Isthmus of Chignecto, supported Mi'kmaq and Acadian resistance, and French missionaries, notably the priest Jean-Louis Le Loutre, helped mobilize it. These Mi'kmaq decisions were their own: they sought to protect their access to hunting and fishing grounds, to retain French trade, and to prevent the establishment of British settlements on their territory.

The British capture of Fort Beauséjour in June 1755 led directly to the deportation of the Acadians. Governor Charles Lawrence and the Nova Scotia council, with New England support, ordered the removal of the Acadian population, beginning that year. Thousands of Acadians were forcibly transported, and many died of disease and hardship. The deportation had direct consequences for Indigenous peoples. The Mi'kmaq and Maliseet had economic, religious, and in some cases kinship relationships with Acadian families, and some Acadians sought refuge in Mi'kmaq communities. Mi'kmaq and Maliseet warriors raided British settlements, and Indigenous leaders played roles in sheltering and moving refugees. The removal of the Acadians also opened lands for British settlement, intensifying the pressure on Indigenous territory.

In New England, colonial governments adopted aggressive measures against the Wabanaki. In the fall of 1755, Massachusetts declared war on the Penobscot, who had been trying to avoid the conflict, and offered bounties for scalps, an action that drew in people who might otherwise have remained neutral. Wabanaki communities responded in different ways: some retreated to the Canadian mission villages, such as Odanak and Wôlinak, which received refugees and served as bases for raids; others negotiated; others fought. The Abenaki of Odanak, as Section 57.17 describes, became targets of a British ranger raid in 1759.

The British siege and capture of Louisbourg in 1758 was a major turning point. The fortress had been the center of French power in the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the place where the French distributed annual presents to Mi'kmaq allies. Its loss ended that system, severing a crucial source of goods, and left Mi'kmaq communities without a European protector in the region. In 1758 British forces also raided French and Indigenous settlements along the Gulf of St. Lawrence coast. Under these circumstances, Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Passamaquoddy leaders opened negotiations with British officials in Halifax in 1760 and 1761, which resulted in peace and friendship treaties that, as Section 57.19 notes, confirmed trade and relationships while not, in the Indigenous understanding, ceding sovereignty over their lands.

The experience of these communities underscores that the war in the Northeast was not simply a prelude to a British victory at Quebec. It was a struggle in which Indigenous nations tried to maintain control over their territories and resources against settlement pressure that had begun well before the imperial war and continued after it. The Mi'kmaq and Wabanaki negotiated peace on their own terms when the strategic situation changed, and those treaties remain legally significant in Canada today.

Section 57.12

Cherokee Diplomacy and the Anglo-Cherokee War, 1759–1761

The Cherokee, who lived in the southern Appalachians across several regional town groupings, commonly described as Lower, Middle, Valley, and Overhill towns, were not parties to the early fighting in the north, but their diplomacy was closely linked to the wider war. Each of their towns governed itself, and Cherokee politics reflected competing views about relations with Britain, France, and neighboring nations. They traded with Carolina, and since the 1730s, they had treaty relationships with Britain. They also had contacts with the French in Louisiana and with Creek and Chickasaw neighbors, and Cherokee leaders considered how best to retain access to trade while preserving their autonomy.

In the mid-1750s the Cherokee asked for British forts to protect their towns, and in 1756 South Carolina built Fort Loudoun among the Overhill towns, partly to meet promises of protection for Cherokee families. In 1757 and 1758 Cherokee warriors went north to serve with Virginia and with British forces, in part because they had been promised supplies and gifts. Many Cherokee believed the British had agreed to provide for them and to protect their families while they were away. Instead, they found that supplies were short, that their horses were sometimes taken or lost, and that colonial officials treated them as subordinate. The relationship was strained even before the campaigns of 1758 were over.

The immediate breakdown followed. In 1758, as Cherokee warriors returned from Virginia, some were attacked and killed by Virginia frontier settlers, who either mistook them for enemy Indians or took advantage of a colonial bounty on scalps. The victims were allies who had been serving with British forces. The killings violated Cherokee expectations of reciprocity and, under Cherokee custom, created an obligation of revenge for the relatives of the dead. Cherokee war parties struck settlers in the backcountry of the Carolinas and Virginia in 1758 and 1759. Cherokee leaders who wanted peace, notably Attakullakulla, tried to restrain these retaliations, while others, such as Oconostota, took a harder line. The Cherokee did not act as a single body.

In 1759 South Carolina's governor, William Henry Lyttelton, responded by raising a militia and demanding that the Cherokee surrender those responsible. A delegation of Cherokee leaders traveled to Charleston to negotiate, and Lyttelton detained them as hostages, confining them at Fort Prince George. The detention of respected leaders was perceived as a gross violation of diplomatic norms. After Lyttelton returned to Charleston, Cherokee forces besieged Fort Prince George, and in February 1760, after a British officer was killed in an incident outside the fort, the garrison killed the hostages. The killings made peace much harder, and Cherokee forces attacked settlements along the frontier and besieged Fort Loudoun.

Fort Loudoun's garrison, cut off and short of food, surrendered in August 1760 under terms allowing them to march out with arms. Cherokee warriors attacked the column the next day, killing a number of soldiers and taking others captive. Cherokee accounts and colonial accounts differ on the reasons, and historians generally connect the attack to the killing of the hostages and to the failure of the British to fulfil earlier promises. Attakullakulla, who had earlier been friendly to the British, took action to ransom some captives, including the officer John Stuart, who would later become British Superintendent for the Southern District.

Britain responded with expeditions in 1760 and 1761. Colonel Archibald Montgomery led a force that destroyed Lower Towns in the spring of 1760, but he was checked in an engagement near Echoee and withdrew. In 1761 Colonel James Grant led a larger force that, after another engagement near Echoee, destroyed fifteen Middle Towns, burning houses and crops and driving many people into the mountains. The devastation, and the resulting shortages of food and supplies, led Cherokee leaders to seek peace. In September 1761 Cherokee representatives negotiated preliminary peace terms at Charleston, involving Attakullakulla and other leaders; subsequent negotiation and ratification, including a December stage, completed the settlement. In 1762 a delegation under Ostenaco traveled to London. The Cherokee had not been conquered, but they had suffered heavily and their ability to play the colonies against one another had been reduced.

The regional geography of Cherokee country affected the war. The Lower Towns stood closer to South Carolina’s approach routes; the Middle and Valley Towns occupied mountain landscapes where movement of large armies and supplies was difficult; the Overhill towns had their own connections with Virginia and the interior. Town councils did not automatically adopt one policy, and influential speakers could favor accommodation without commanding every warrior. The burning of homes and food stores consequently had uneven immediate effects, while journeys between regional groups were essential to wartime consultation. Understanding this geography also explains the importance of Fort Loudoun: it was not simply a British outpost, but a place where allied expectations, imperial logistics, and Cherokee control over access intersected. The eventual Charleston peace was negotiated after severe losses, yet its stages demonstrate that Cherokee delegates continued to contest terms and exercise diplomatic judgment rather than merely accept a battlefield verdict.

Section 57.13

Indigenous Military Strategy, Logistics, and Wartime Decision-Making

European officers often described Indigenous warfare as irregular, sudden, and unpredictable, and many later writers adopted this vocabulary. A more careful reading of the evidence shows that Indigenous forces operated within established military institutions that differed from European ones in structure and purpose, but that were not less organized. Understanding these institutions helps explain why Indigenous participation was so influential and why it was so difficult for European commanders to control.

Indigenous military action generally began with a decision in a council or among a group of kin and associates. Leaders who proposed a campaign needed to persuade others to join, and success depended on their reputation, their record, and the plausibility of their plan. In many societies, women, particularly clan matrons, could influence decisions by supporting or discouraging war, and by controlling the food and supplies that sustained war parties. Once a party had formed, its members generally followed a leader chosen for the expedition, but the leader's authority rested on consent rather than formal command. A warrior who judged that omens were unfavorable, that the party had suffered excessive losses, or that the objective had been achieved, could withdraw without being treated as a deserter. European officers who interpreted such departures as unreliability misunderstood the principles on which Indigenous forces were organized.

Mobility and intelligence were central strengths. Indigenous fighters knew the country, traveled lightly by foot and canoe, and gathered information about enemy movements from scouts, from allied communities, and from captives. Such knowledge allowed them to choose the time and place of an engagement, as at the Monongahela, and to avoid battles that they judged unfavorable. Their preference for ambush and surprise was not a lack of discipline but a strategy designed to limit casualties, which mattered greatly in small societies where each death was a serious loss to a family and community. It was a rational response to a situation in which sustained losses could imperil a village's survival. The same concern shaped decisions to retire after taking captives or plunder, which European commanders often considered premature.

Logistics were not absent from Indigenous warfare, but they operated differently. Indigenous parties carried parched corn, dried meat, and other portable provisions, and supplemented them by hunting, by drawing on stores in allied villages, and, during larger campaigns, by receiving food and goods from French magazines. Canoe transportation allowed movement over long distances with small supply burdens. Firearms, powder, and lead increasingly depended on trade or gifts, and Indigenous communities were aware that alliance with a supplier gave them access to these goods, while rupture could cut them off. Such dependence gave European suppliers leverage, but it did not eliminate Indigenous bargaining power, because the suppliers also depended on the warriors' participation.

Indigenous peoples also used fortifications and siege techniques. Cherokee, Haudenosaunee, and other communities had palisaded towns, and some had experience of siege warfare against rival nations and Europeans. During the Seven Years' War, Indigenous participants at sieges such as Fort William Henry and Fort Loudoun played roles in surrounding forts, cutting supply lines, and applying pressure. They also adapted to European weapons and tactics while retaining their own priorities. Describing Indigenous warfare as only "forest fighting" misses this range of experience.

European forces, in turn, adapted. The Canadian militia and the French colonial marines, who had grown up in the region, absorbed many Indigenous practices, and British and provincial forces developed ranger units, such as those of Robert Rogers, that adopted light equipment, scouting, and ambush. The British regular army, however, retained a doctrine of massed fire and disciplined formations, and after Braddock's defeat it made efforts to incorporate light infantry. These changes were not simple imitations; they reflected the military problems that the Indigenous way of war had posed. They also reflected the fact that some British commanders eventually concluded that Indigenous allies could be useful when properly supported, as Forbes would attempt in 1758.

The central point for this chapter is that Indigenous participation was voluntary, negotiated, and strategically motivated. European commanders could attract Indigenous support through gifts, respect, and credible plans, and could lose it through insult, neglect, or defeat. Leaders who understood this, including Johnson and Vaudreuil, gained advantages. Those who did not, such as Braddock, paid for their ignorance. This capacity to decide participation, rather than any inherent warrior tradition, is what made Indigenous nations important to the war's outcome.

Section 57.14

The British Strategic Recovery of 1758

By the end of 1757, British prospects in North America looked poor. The campaigns of 1755 through 1757 had produced few lasting gains, Oswego and Fort William Henry were lost, and the frontier was vulnerable to raids. But the British political and military system was beginning to change. In 1757 William Pitt assumed leadership of the war effort in the British government, and he pursued a policy of concentrating resources on North America and the naval war while subsidizing allies in Europe. Pitt's government increased the number of regular troops sent across the Atlantic, strengthened the navy's ability to blockade French ports, and offered to reimburse colonial governments for the costs of raising provincial forces. These measures made it easier for colonies to recruit and equip soldiers and reduced the resistance of colonial assemblies to funding the war.

The result was a much larger and better coordinated force in 1758. The British planned three principal operations: an assault on Louisbourg, an advance against Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) on Lake Champlain, and an expedition against Fort Duquesne. The first achieved its goal: a fleet under Admiral Edward Boscawen and an army under Jeffery Amherst, with James Wolfe as a brigade commander, besieged Louisbourg and forced its surrender in late July 1758. The loss of the fortress deprived France of its main naval base in North America, disrupted communications with Canada by sea, and, as Section 57.11 noted, ended a major source of presents for the Mi'kmaq. The second operation failed: General James Abercromby's army, much larger than that of its opponents, was repulsed by Montcalm's defenders at Carillon in July with heavy losses. The French victory, however, did not change the strategic trend.

The most consequential British success of that summer was small in scale. In August 1758, Lieutenant Colonel John Bradstreet led a force of provincial troops across Lake Ontario in boats and captured Fort Frontenac, a French post at the lake's eastern end. Fort Frontenac was a depot for supplies and trade goods destined for Fort Niagara, for the Ohio posts, and for the western Great Lakes. Its capture, along with the destruction of French vessels and stores, disrupted these supply lines. French garrisons in the interior became more dependent on shrinking stocks, and the gifts on which Indigenous alliances rested became harder to provide. The loss at Frontenac affected the diplomatic position of France with its western allies more than the larger victory at Carillon helped it.

British recovery was also a matter of diplomacy. Officials in London, Johnson in the north, and Forbes in Pennsylvania recognized that military success would depend partly on the position of Indigenous nations. Johnson increased the volume of gifts and held councils to enlist Haudenosaunee help, and Forbes, who initially had little respect for Indigenous methods, came to value Indigenous intelligence and diplomacy and encouraged Pennsylvania and its allies to reach an agreement with the Lenape and their neighbors. The Cherokee and Catawba, who sent warriors to join Forbes's force, were a different part of this effort, though, as Section 57.12 showed, relations with the Cherokee soon unraveled.

It would be a mistake to attribute British recovery solely to material superiority. British resources did grow, and by 1758 Britain was able to field armies larger than anything France could match in North America. But these resources were available in 1755 as well, and their use in that year had produced failure. What changed was the combination of better financing, naval power that strangled French supply, improved coordination, and a more sophisticated approach to Indigenous diplomacy. At the same time, France, blockaded and distracted by European demands, could not deliver the goods that its Indigenous allies expected. The balance of incentives began to shift, and Indigenous leaders took notice.

For Indigenous nations, the expansion of British capabilities changed the options available. When France had appeared likely to win or at least hold its positions, alliance with France offered security and goods. As French supply shrank and British armies advanced, the value of remaining with France declined, and the benefits of negotiating with Britain grew. This realignment was not automatic, and it depended on what the British would promise. The Treaty of Easton, discussed next, was the test case.

Section 57.15

The Treaty of Easton and the Ohio Diplomatic Realignment, 1758

The Treaty of Easton of October 1758 was one of the most consequential Indigenous diplomatic episodes of the war. It was not a single document signed in one sitting but the outcome of a long series of meetings in the town of Easton, Pennsylvania, involving Pennsylvania and New Jersey officials, delegates from the Haudenosaunee nations, Lenape leaders including Teedyuscung, and representatives of other peoples. It followed earlier conferences at Easton in 1756 and 1757 in which Teedyuscung had pressed Pennsylvania on accusations of fraud in land purchases, especially the notorious Walking Purchase of 1737. These earlier meetings had begun to build a framework for negotiation, though they had also exposed disagreements between Teedyuscung and the Haudenosaunee, who objected to his claim to speak for a large Lenape alliance.

By 1758, Pennsylvania officials and General John Forbes had concluded that peace with the Ohio nations was necessary for the success of the campaign against Fort Duquesne. They understood that many Lenape and Shawnee, having fought since 1755, were open to peace if their grievances were addressed. Forbes and Pennsylvania authorities therefore sent the Moravian missionary Christian Frederick Post to the Ohio towns in the summer of 1758, accompanied at times by the Lenape leader Pisquetomen. Post traveled to Indigenous towns near French positions, at personal risk, to deliver messages and wampum. His journal records conversations in which Ohio leaders expressed their conditions: they wanted the British to confirm that they would not settle on Ohio lands, to withdraw their forces after the French were expelled, and to deal with them as independent peoples. Post's journal is a valuable source, but it was written by a missionary for British readers, and it filters Indigenous speech through his own understanding.

At Easton in October, the conference involved large delegations and long discussions. Representatives of the Haudenosaunee, Lenape, Shawnee, Mohican, and several other peoples met with Governor William Denny of Pennsylvania and with colonial commissioners. The secretary Charles Thomson, who assisted Teedyuscung, helped record the proceedings. Among the central issues were land: the Haudenosaunee and the Lenape both raised grievances about purchases, and Pennsylvania agreed to return to the Haudenosaunee the land west of the Allegheny mountains that had been obtained at the Albany conference of 1754. This was a significant concession, since it meant that the Penn family and the colony relinquished a large claim and agreed not to settle those lands. The conference also dealt with exchanges of captives, trade, and the establishment of peace. Delegations carried messages from the Easton conference to the Ohio towns, where the Lenape and Shawnee were urged to cease fighting and to remain neutral during the British advance.

The assurances concerning western land mattered because the Ohio nations' principal concern was that a British victory would lead to settlement. The promise that settlement would not cross the mountains, made by Pennsylvania and reinforced by Forbes's message, gave them a reason to reduce or withdraw their support for the French. It did not guarantee that they would join the British, and most did not. Rather, many returned home or declined to defend Fort Duquesne. The French, who had counted on Indigenous support, found that their allies were leaving, and, with supplies short after the loss of Frontenac, the garrison was in a weak position.

The relationship between promise and later practice is the most important historical lesson of the treaty. The assurances of 1758 were made through several different channels—Pennsylvania proprietary negotiations, treaty speeches, and messages associated with Forbes’s military expedition—rather than one uniform, legally identical promise by every British authority. Their subsequent application proved deeply disputed and inadequate to restrain imperial fortification and settler encroachment. After the French abandoned Fort Duquesne, the British built Fort Pitt, a large and permanent fortification at the forks, which Ohio leaders saw as a violation of the promise that British forces would withdraw. Settlers from Pennsylvania, Virginia, and Maryland crossed the mountains in growing numbers in the early 1760s, and efforts by British officers to remove them, including a proclamation issued by Colonel Henry Bouquet in 1761, were largely ineffective. The failure to honor the Easton understandings contributed to the resentments that exploded in 1763, as Chapter 58 will examine.

The Easton minutes also expose a problem of representation. Leaders speaking in the Susquehanna valley, the Haudenosaunee delegates attending the conference, and the Ohio Country councils reached by messengers did not constitute a single authority. A promise could be persuasive to one group while another questioned whether its land claims had been adequately addressed. The presence of governors and proprietors did not remove the distinction between a public declaration of peace and a specific conveyance or reservation of land. Students reading the proceedings should identify which speaker made each undertaking, which territories were described, and whose consent was actually obtained. Christian Frederick Post’s report of Ohio conversations provides evidence that messages were heard and debated, but his account is not a stenographic record of each council’s deliberations. The importance of Easton lies in precisely this intersection of local decision-making, printed colonial diplomacy, and an approaching military campaign.

Section 57.16

Fort Duquesne, Fort Niagara, and the Loss of French Strategic Positions, 1758–1759

While the Easton conference was under way, General Forbes's army advanced slowly across Pennsylvania, cutting a road through the mountains. Forbes, ill throughout the campaign, was determined to avoid a repetition of Braddock's disaster and moved cautiously, building supply posts as he went. A detachment under Major James Grant, sent forward to reconnoiter the French position, was defeated near Fort Duquesne in September 1758 by French and Indigenous forces, a reminder that Indigenous allies remained capable of effective action. But the strength of the defenders was declining. Many Indigenous warriors who had been at the fort earlier in the year were leaving, in part because of the diplomatic initiatives described above, and because French supplies were short.

When Forbes's army approached the forks in November, the French commander at Fort Duquesne, François-Marie Le Marchand de Lignery, had few Indigenous allies remaining and limited provisions. On November 24 the French blew up the fort and withdrew, and the British arrived the next day. Forbes ordered the construction of a new fortification, which he named Pittsburgh in honor of Pitt. Historians generally agree that the French withdrawal resulted from several factors, among them the loss of Fort Frontenac, the campaign's momentum, and the departure of Indigenous allies, and they differ about the relative weight of each. What the sequence shows is that Indigenous decisions about participation, influenced by the diplomacy of 1758, were an important component of the collapse.

The British position at the forks led to further councils. In 1759 British officers, including Brigadier General John Stanwix, met with Ohio leaders at Pittsburgh, repeating assurances that the British had come to trade rather than to settle. Ohio Indigenous leaders watched the large fort rising and compared it with what they had been told. The British were establishing a presence that could not be easily reversed, and the Ohio nations began to consider what that presence meant.

In the north, the British campaign of 1759 aimed at Fort Niagara, which controlled the portage around Niagara Falls and the passage between Lake Ontario and the upper lakes. A force under Brigadier General John Prideaux, with Johnson commanding the Indigenous contingent, besieged the fort in July 1759. Prideaux was killed in the trenches, and Johnson took command. A French relief force, composed of French soldiers and Indigenous warriors from the Ohio and the western Great Lakes, advanced toward the fort. On July 24, at the engagement known as La Belle-Famille, British light infantry and regulars defeated the relief column. According to British accounts, Haudenosaunee on Johnson's side had sent messages to the Indigenous warriors in the relief force, including Seneca from the western towns, reminding them of kinship and neutrality and urging them not to fight. Many Indigenous warriors withdrew before the battle, leaving the French relief force weakened. The next day the fort's commander, Captain Pierre Pouchot, surrendered.

The fall of Niagara had large consequences. It severed the main route by which French supplies and officers reached the western posts, isolating Detroit, Michilimackinac, and the Illinois country. It also demonstrated the effect of Indigenous diplomacy on the outcome of a battle: the withdrawal of western Indigenous allies in response to Haudenosaunee mediation contributed to the French defeat. And it presented the western nations with a new strategic situation, in which British forces controlled the water routes to their homelands and French goods could no longer be relied upon.

By the end of 1759, then, the French had lost both the Ohio forks and the Niagara portage, and British forces were poised for the final campaign against Canada. In neither case had European arms alone produced the result. The French position in the Ohio Country eroded as Indigenous allies reconsidered their commitments, and at Niagara the diplomatic interaction among Indigenous kin affected the military outcome. These were not isolated events but reflected the pattern that the Indigenous decision-making described in this chapter produced across the war.

Section 57.17

Quebec, 1759, and the Declining Position of New France

The British campaign against Quebec in 1759 is among the most famous episodes of the war, and it is usually told as a story of two generals, James Wolfe and the marquis de Montcalm. Yet its setting, the St. Lawrence valley, was also the homeland of Indigenous communities, and the campaign cannot be fully understood without them. Quebec was the political center of New France and a gateway to the interior, but it also lay close to the mission towns of Lorette, Odanak, Wôlinak, and Kahnawake, whose people had been participants in the colony's defense and whose futures depended on its outcome.

Wolfe's army, carried by a large fleet under Admiral Charles Saunders, arrived in the St. Lawrence in June 1759 and spent the summer in an inconclusive siege. The French, commanded by Montcalm with Governor Vaudreuil in overall authority, held a strong position on the heights and along the Beauport shore. Indigenous allies from the Canadian mission communities, together with Canadian militia, harassed British positions, raided foraging parties, and gathered intelligence. Their contribution was significant, though not decisive, and by most accounts their numbers were smaller than in earlier campaigns, because many western allies had not come east and because supply constraints limited the force the French could maintain. British forces responded to such harassment with destruction of farms and villages along the river, which affected Canadian civilians and increased the war's toll on local communities.

On September 13, 1759, Wolfe's army climbed to the Plains of Abraham, the open ground above the town, and in a brief battle defeated Montcalm's army. Wolfe was killed in the fighting, and Montcalm died of wounds the next day. Quebec surrendered on September 18. The battle's outcome reflected the effectiveness of British regular infantry and the French decision to meet the British in open order rather than wait for reinforcements; Indigenous participation in the action itself appears to have been limited. The loss of Quebec was a heavy blow, depriving New France of its capital, its principal port, and much of its administrative apparatus.

The fall of Quebec did not end the war or resolve the status of Indigenous communities. The French army, under the chevalier de Lévis, retreated to Montreal and retained substantial forces, and in April 1760 it defeated the British garrison under James Murray at Sainte-Foy outside Quebec. Only the arrival of British ships in the spring prevented the recapture of the town. In addition, the French still held Montreal and the western posts, and Indigenous nations had not been defeated or compelled to accept British authority. The war would be decided in the following summer, and the Indigenous communities of the St. Lawrence were now directly exposed to British military pressure.

That pressure was already felt in the autumn of 1759. In October, a ranger force under Major Robert Rogers, dispatched from Lake Champlain, struck the Abenaki village of Odanak (St. Francis) on the St. Francis River. Odanak had received refugees from New England and had been a base for raids on frontier settlements. Rogers's party burned the village and killed a number of its inhabitants, though accounts of the casualties vary widely: Rogers claimed hundreds of dead, while modern scholarship generally places the toll far lower, probably in the dozens. The raid devastated the community, destroyed its stores, and, for British observers, represented retaliation for earlier attacks. For the Abenaki, it was a profound blow, and it demonstrated that British forces could reach the mission communities. The Seven Nations were thus pushed to consider how best to protect their towns.

New France's institutions were vulnerable in ways that went beyond the military situation. The colony's economy depended on supply from France, which the British blockade had constricted. Canadian farmers had endured years of militia service and requisitioning, food shortages, and inflated paper currency. The colonial administration, never large, was strained. When Indigenous leaders assessed the colony's prospects, they saw a government that could not protect its own capital and could no longer supply its allies. The decision of many Indigenous communities to seek an accommodation with Britain in 1760 reflected these assessments.

Section 57.18

Montreal, 1760: Indigenous Neutrality and Negotiated Transition

In 1760 Britain planned a converging campaign against Montreal. Murray would advance up the St. Lawrence from Quebec, Colonel William Haviland would move north from Lake Champlain, and General Jeffery Amherst, the commander-in-chief, would lead the main force from Oswego, across Lake Ontario, and down the river. Amherst's army included several hundred Haudenosaunee warriors under Johnson. The French had a smaller army, short of supplies, and no prospect of reinforcement. The campaign was in effect a race to see whether the three British columns could converge before French resistance could be organized, and the Indigenous communities of the St. Lawrence stood in the path of the advance.

Johnson's diplomacy was central. As Amherst's army moved down the river in August, it captured Fort Lévis, the French post on an island near present-day Ogdensburg, and approached the mission town of Oswegatchie (La Présentation). There Johnson met with delegations from the Seven Nations of Canada, who had traveled to hear British terms. The surviving records, preserved in Johnson's papers and in later treaty compilations, indicate that the delegates were prepared to withdraw from the French cause on conditions. They sought assurances that their towns, lands, and religion would be protected, that they would not be punished for having served the French, and that their trade would continue. Johnson was authorized to offer such assurances in return for their neutrality, and the exchange of wampum belts and speeches affirmed the understanding. Historians note that the agreement did not simply record a surrender: the Seven Nations were negotiating a transition on their own terms, using their ability to withdraw from the French as leverage.

A parallel agreement was made with the Wendat of Lorette. On September 5, 1760, General Murray, whose army had advanced up the river from Quebec, issued a written document concerning the Lorette Wendat that explicitly describes their chief as coming to “submit” to the British Crown and make peace, while promising British protection, freedom from molestation on returning to Lorette, exercise of religion and customs, and liberty to trade. The recorded British language of submission is evidence of a colonial claim to hierarchy; it does not by itself settle what Wendat negotiators understood about continued political authority. The document, which Lorette's descendants preserved, was later examined by Canadian courts, and in 1990 the Supreme Court of Canada, in the case R. v. Sioui, held that it constituted a treaty protected by Canadian law. That later judicial interpretation is itself part of the document's history, and it should be distinguished from the circumstances of 1760, when the participants understood themselves to be arranging a practical peace.

The French capitulation at Montreal followed on September 8. Governor Vaudreuil, facing an army of overwhelming strength and without the means to resist, surrendered the colony. The capitulation articles included a provision concerning Indigenous allies of France. In general terms, it asked that they be maintained in the lands they inhabited if they chose to remain, that they not be molested for having served France, and that they enjoy religious liberty and keep their missionaries. Amherst accepted the protection of Indigenous allies but refused certain religious provisions. The French article was a European agreement, made between commanders, and Indigenous communities were not signatories to it. Its significance for them lay in its confirmation of the assurances that had been given in the local councils.

After the capitulation, Johnson held further councils with the Seven Nations, including a gathering at Kahnawake, at which the deputies renewed their commitments and sought confirmation of what had been promised. No complete written minutes of the September 14–15 meetings at Kahnawake are known to survive. Instead, the nature of the negotiations is reconstructed from related colonial correspondence, later treaty references, and Indigenous oral historical traditions. Those sources describe the negotiation and renewal of understandings concerning movement, lands, trade, protection, and treatment after the end of the French alliance. Wampum exchanges formed part of the diplomatic process, but absent meeting minutes it would be improper to present a reconstructed exchange as a verbatim record of what each deputy said. The communities understood the agreements as establishing a relationship of alliance with the Crown, based on the Covenant Chain's principles, under which their lands and customs would be respected. Later disputes over these understandings, which continue in Canadian law, show that the British and the Indigenous parties did not necessarily interpret them in the same way.

The events of 1760 illustrate the central argument of this chapter. The end of French power in Canada was not a conquest of Indigenous nations. It was a negotiated transition in which Indigenous communities used diplomacy, neutrality, and the threat of continued resistance to secure protections, and in which Indigenous understandings of peace, alliance, and continuing autonomy did not necessarily coincide with British assertions of submission and protection. The British Crown's later conduct, and the pressures of settlement and trade, would test those understandings.

Three distinct documentary situations must therefore be kept separate. Johnson’s late-August discussions at Swegatchy involved representatives seeking terms while British forces approached Montreal; later records and Indigenous memory help establish what was carried forward. Murray’s September 5 instrument relating to the Wendat at Lorette survives in a specific written form and can be read clause by clause. The mid-September Kahnawake meetings took place after the French capitulation, but their full written proceedings are not extant. Treating these as one text would create a false impression of certainty and erase differences among the communities and the British negotiators with whom they dealt. The most defensible account combines surviving government communications with later recollection and the continued diplomatic significance of the understandings. The comparison also allows students to ask how a colonial officer’s term “protection” or “submission” could coexist with an Indigenous expectation of reciprocal alliance and continuing control of local affairs.

Section 57.19

Regional Peace Settlements and the Treaty of Paris, 1763

The fall of Montreal did not end all fighting or settle every Indigenous relationship with Britain. The conclusion of the war involved a series of regional arrangements, and only afterward the European treaty. In the south, the Anglo-Cherokee conflict ended with preliminary peace terms negotiated at Charleston in September 1761 and subsequently ratified in December, following the destruction of the Middle Towns. The treaty restored trade and set arrangements for contact, including a boundary line between Cherokee and colonial settlement. Cherokee leaders such as Attakullakulla, who had favored peace, were strengthened, though the devastation of the war left many Cherokee communities short of food, dependent on trade, and wary of colonial intentions. Fort Loudoun was not rebuilt.

In the Atlantic Northeast, Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Passamaquoddy leaders concluded a series of treaties with Nova Scotia's government in 1760 and 1761. These agreements, often called peace and friendship treaties, confirmed peace, regulated trade through designated trading houses, and provided for the submission of disputes to British authority. Their language and the understanding of Indigenous participants have been the subject of extensive later interpretation. The Mi'kmaq tradition holds that the treaties established a relationship between sovereign nations and did not cede land, and Canadian courts have given weight to the treaties' recognition of Indigenous rights, including trade and harvesting, in several modern decisions. The 1760–61 treaties illustrate that the Atlantic Indigenous nations, like the Seven Nations, framed the end of the war as a negotiated peace.

In the west, the surrender of Montreal had left the French posts in the Ohio and Great Lakes regions without support. British forces under Rogers took possession of Detroit in November 1760, and further posts were occupied in the following year. Indigenous leaders in the Great Lakes region, long accustomed to French gifts and trade, now dealt with British officers who had different practices. In 1761 the commander-in-chief, Amherst, ordered restrictions on gifts and on the sale of powder and lead, policies that Indigenous nations experienced as a violation of the relationship of reciprocity that diplomacy had established. These matters, together with the pressure of settlement beyond the mountains, form part of the background of the resistance examined in Chapter 58, and this chapter returns to them only to indicate the new conditions.

The European settlement came in the Treaty of Paris, signed on February 10, 1763, by Britain, France, and Spain, following preliminary articles agreed in November 1762. France ceded to Britain its claims in Canada and in the lands east of the Mississippi River, with the exception of New Orleans, and Spain ceded Florida to Britain. In a separate agreement, France had transferred to Spain its claim to Louisiana west of the Mississippi. The treaty included provisions for the free exercise of Catholic worship in Canada, as far as British law permitted, and fixed the Mississippi as a boundary between British and Spanish claims. It also dealt with fishing rights, the return of captured territory, and other matters among the European powers.

Indigenous nations were not parties to the treaty and were not consulted in its drafting. Their relationship to the territories involved was not addressed in the provisions on cession. The treaty transferred claims that France had asserted against other empires, but France had not controlled most of the territory it was ceding, which in large part remained under Indigenous governance and occupation. Britain, which received the claims, acquired a legal basis for asserting sovereignty against other European powers, not the effective capacity to govern Indigenous nations. Indigenous leaders, many of whom had understood France as a partner rather than a sovereign over their lands, learned of the treaty's terms through British officers and traders, often without clear explanation. The gap between imperial claim and Indigenous understanding was one of the most important legacies of the war.

The stages of the Cherokee peace illustrate a related distinction between military defeat and political settlement. In September 1761, Cherokee representatives and South Carolina authorities reached preliminary terms after destructive campaigns; further negotiations adjusted particular demands, and ratification followed in December. That sequence matters because the terms had to be communicated among Cherokee towns and considered by people with different experiences of British conduct. The resulting peace did not reverse the destruction of crops, loss of life, or disputes over boundaries, and it did not end the need for negotiations over captive returns and access to trade. Nor did the peace agreements of Atlantic Northeast communities simply mirror the Cherokee experience. Every regional settlement was shaped by its own history of landholding, maritime or interior trade, and relationships with colonial officials. A continental conclusion must preserve these distinctions rather than flatten all Indigenous peacemaking into one British victory ceremony.

Section 57.20

Indigenous Sovereignty after the Seven Years' War: The Continent in 1763

In 1763, large parts of the continent east of the Mississippi remained under Indigenous control. The Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Shawnee, Lenape, Wendat, Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Miami, Mi'kmaq, Wabanaki, and many other nations governed their own territories, conducted their own diplomacy, and could mobilize armed forces. British forts and posts, though numerous after the war, were small garrisons scattered across a vast region, and British settlements remained concentrated east of the Appalachians and along the St. Lawrence. The British Crown claimed sovereignty over much more territory than it could govern, and it had made agreements with Indigenous nations that acknowledged their separate status.

The war had nevertheless changed the conditions of Indigenous politics in profound ways. The removal of France as a major imperial power in eastern North America eliminated the rivalry on which many Indigenous diplomats had relied. For generations, nations had obtained gifts, trade, and respect by negotiating with two empires, and by threatening to shift allegiance. After 1763, Britain was the only European power in much of the region, with Spain to the west of the Mississippi. The ability to play empires against each other, long a source of Indigenous leverage, was reduced.

Trade and military security were affected as well. Britain's postwar government, burdened by debt, sought to reduce expenditures, including the cost of gifts, and its commanders were disinclined to sustain the diplomatic practices that had characterized the French alliance system. The reduction of presents and the regulation of powder and lead sales, as noted, struck at the foundations of Indigenous diplomatic practice and at the material needs of communities that depended on trade goods. At the same time, British traders offered goods at lower prices than the French in many areas, and some Indigenous communities benefited from trade. The picture was mixed, but the expectation of reciprocity, which Indigenous nations had built into the alliance system, was under strain.

Land was the most explosive issue. Settlers moved west after the war, and colonial governments and land companies sought to acquire Indigenous land through purchase, fraud, or occupation. The assurances given at Easton in 1758 and in councils at Pittsburgh in 1759, and the agreements of 1760, collided with this pressure. British imperial officials, aware of the danger, began to consider a policy that would regulate settlement and purchases, an effort that culminated in the Royal Proclamation of 1763, whose origins and consequences belong to Chapter 58. That chapter will also examine the multinational movement that took up arms in 1763 in response to the new conditions, including the role of nations that had been allies of France, and the religious revitalization movements that accompanied it.

The war's human costs for Indigenous peoples were significant. Communities lost members to fighting, disease, captivity, and displacement. Towns such as Kittanning, Odanak, and many Cherokee settlements were destroyed. Agricultural production was disrupted, and families had to relocate. Yet Indigenous communities also showed resilience: they rebuilt towns, adapted their diplomacy, and used the treaties and councils of the war years to secure recognition of their rights. The experiences of the Seven Nations, the Wabanaki and Mi'kmaq, the Cherokee, and the Ohio nations were different, and no single narrative fits them all.

The war therefore left a mixed legacy. British victory transformed the geopolitical environment and created a new imperial order. But it did not extinguish Indigenous sovereignty, and it left unresolved questions about land, trade, and authority that Indigenous nations and the British Crown would contest in the years that followed. Understanding this unfinished business is essential to understanding the resistance of 1763 and, more broadly, the rest of the eighteenth century.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Seven Years' War in North America was not simply a contest between Britain and France. It was also a series of interconnected Indigenous struggles over territory, trade, political autonomy, alliances, and security, in which Indigenous nations made independent decisions about participation, neutrality, negotiation, and withdrawal. Those decisions often shaped military outcomes, and the war's conclusion transformed the political environment without extinguishing Indigenous sovereignty.

The war began in a contested landscape. In the Ohio Country, France, Britain, Virginia land interests, the Haudenosaunee, and resident Lenape, Shawnee, Mingo, Wyandot, and Miami communities held overlapping and incompatible claims. French fort construction and British trade and land speculation pressed on communities that sought to preserve their own autonomy. The events of 1754 at Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity illustrate the role of Indigenous actors: Tanaghrisson was a political leader pursuing Haudenosaunee and Ohio interests, and his decisions, along with those of other Indigenous participants, helped determine how and when skirmishing became war. The sources for these events are conflicting and partisan, and historians cannot resolve every question.

The early campaigns of 1755 to 1757 showed the military effectiveness of Indigenous forces and the limits of European control over them. At the Monongahela, French and Indigenous fighters destroyed Braddock's column, and the victory opened the Pennsylvania and Virginia backcountry to war. In the north, the campaigns around Lake George and Oswego revealed divisions among the Haudenosaunee and the role of Indigenous allies in scouting, transportation, and intelligence. At Fort William Henry, a very large Indigenous contingent participated in a French victory, and the violence that followed the surrender remains difficult to reconstruct, with conflicting evidence about numbers, motives, and responsibility.

War was a social and economic process as well as a military one. Raids, counterraids, and the destruction of towns and crops created displacement, hunger, and loss. Captivity served varied purposes in Indigenous societies, including adoption and ransom, and it became a diplomatic issue in councils. Households and communities coped with disrupted farming and hunting, interrupted supply, disease, and the arrival of refugees. These experiences are less fully documented than battles but are essential to understanding what the war meant for the communities it affected.

Indigenous nations pursued different strategies. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy officially sought neutrality, while Mohawk, Oneida, and some other communities supported Britain, and western Seneca communities had ties to the French. The Seven Nations of Canada and the Great Lakes allies of New France supported French operations in ways shaped by gifts, trade, kinship, and local security, and they could reconsider as French supply failed. The Wabanaki and Mi'kmaq defended territories against settlement pressure that predated the imperial war, and they endured the consequences of the Acadian deportation and the fall of Louisbourg. The Cherokee began as British allies and became their enemies as a result of unfulfilled expectations, settler violence, and the seizure of hostages, and they suffered devastating campaigns before negotiating peace.

The strategic transformation of 1758 and 1759 resulted from several interacting causes. British financing, naval power, and coordinated campaigns increased British capabilities and disrupted French supply, particularly after the capture of Fort Frontenac. At the same time, diplomatic realignment among Indigenous nations played a significant role. The Treaty of Easton in 1758, built on assurances about western land, helped lead many Ohio nations to withdraw or reduce their support for the French, contributing to the abandonment of Fort Duquesne. At Niagara in 1759, Haudenosaunee mediation contributed to the withdrawal of French-allied warriors. These episodes show that British success was not solely a product of European resources.

The final campaigns of 1759 and 1760 demonstrated that the end of French power did not mean the conquest of Indigenous nations. After the fall of Quebec, the war continued, and in 1760 the Seven Nations of Canada and the Wendat of Lorette negotiated protections concerning land, religion, movement, and trade as British armies approached Montreal. Similar peace and friendship arrangements were made with Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Passamaquoddy leaders in 1760 and 1761, and a peace was concluded with the Cherokee in 1761. These agreements reflected Indigenous understandings of alliance and autonomy and have continued to be debated and interpreted.

The Treaty of Paris of 1763 transferred European claims among empires without Indigenous participation. It gave Britain a legal basis against rival powers, not effective control over Indigenous nations or territories. The distinction between imperial claim and Indigenous sovereignty is central to understanding the situation of 1763. Large parts of the continent remained under Indigenous governance, and the nations that governed them retained armed forces, councils, and diplomatic networks.

The war nevertheless changed Indigenous options. The removal of France reduced the ability to bargain between two empires, British policies on gifts and supply struck at established diplomatic practice, and pressure from settlers and speculators threatened the territorial assurances given during the war. These tensions form the immediate background to the resistance movements of 1763 examined in Chapter 58.

Key Terms

Seven Years' War
The global conflict between Britain, France, and their allies, 1756–1763, which in North America began with hostilities in 1754 and involved extensive Indigenous participation.
"French and Indian War"
The traditional name in the United States for the North American war, reflecting the viewpoint of the British colonies and obscuring the variety of Indigenous positions.
Ohio Country
The region of the upper Ohio River and its tributaries, home to Lenape, Shawnee, Mingo, Wyandot, and Miami communities, and contested among empires and colonies.
Tanaghrisson
Haudenosaunee-affiliated leader in the Ohio Country whose political aims and actions in 1754 influenced the outbreak of war.
Jumonville Glen
The site of the May 28, 1754 skirmish in which Joseph Coulon de Jumonville was killed, remembered for its conflicting accounts.
Fort Necessity
A stockade built by Washington's force in 1754, where it surrendered to French and Indigenous forces.
Neutrality
A diplomatic position of non-participation, which required active maintenance and was often hard to sustain in wartime.
Covenant Chain
The alliance system linking the Haudenosaunee and the English colonies, strained by wartime pressures.
Haudenosaunee Confederacy
The league of Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora nations, whose members adopted differing wartime positions.
Seven Nations of Canada
A diplomatic grouping of Indigenous mission communities on the St. Lawrence, allied with New France and later negotiating with Britain.
Middle ground
A historians' term for the accommodations between Algonquian-speaking peoples and the French in the Great Lakes region, built on mutual dependence and negotiation.
War council
A deliberative meeting in which leaders and community members weighed participation in a conflict; decisions guided but did not compel individuals.
Mourning war
A practice in some Indigenous societies of taking captives or seeking revenge to replace and honor the dead, shaping motives for raiding.
Captivity
The taking and holding of people in war, serving purposes including adoption, ransom, labor, and diplomatic leverage.
Adoption
The incorporation of a captive into a family and community, often replacing a lost member.
Prisoner exchange
The negotiated return of captives, which involved differing views among Indigenous and European parties on the status of adoptees.
Gift diplomacy
The practice of exchanging presents to create and maintain alliances; reductions in gifts were read as breaches of reciprocity.
Wampum
Shell beads woven into belts and strings used to authenticate, memorialize, and transmit diplomatic messages and agreements.
Superintendent of Indian Affairs
A Crown official, such as William Johnson in the north, responsible for coordinating British dealings with Indigenous nations.
Capitulation
A negotiated surrender under agreed terms, as at Fort Necessity, Fort William Henry, and Montreal.
Bloody Morning Scout
The ambush of September 1755 near Lake George in which the Mohawk leader Hendrick was killed.
Acadian deportation
The forced removal of the Acadian population from Nova Scotia beginning in 1755, with consequences for neighboring Indigenous communities.
Treaty of Easton
The 1758 series of conferences in Pennsylvania in which Indigenous and colonial delegates negotiated peace and land assurances affecting the Ohio Country.
Albany Purchase of 1754
A land transaction in which Pennsylvania acquired land from Haudenosaunee representatives, part of which was returned at Easton.
Anglo-Cherokee War
The conflict of 1759–1761 between the Cherokee and British colonies, with its own causes though linked to the wider war.
La Belle-Famille
The July 1759 engagement near Fort Niagara in which a French relief force was defeated after many Indigenous allies withdrew.
Murray agreement
The September 1760 document issued to the Lorette Wendat, later held by the Supreme Court of Canada to be a treaty.
Peace and friendship treaties
Agreements of 1760–1761 between Nova Scotia and Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Passamaquoddy leaders, understood by many Indigenous nations as relationships between sovereign peoples.
Treaty of Paris (1763)
The European treaty ending the wider war, transferring imperial claims without Indigenous participation.
Sovereignty
The political authority of Indigenous nations to govern themselves and their territories, distinct from European imperial claims.

Major People and Places

Identification & Significance

Tanaghrisson (d. 1754)
Haudenosaunee-affiliated leader whose actions at Jumonville Glen contributed to the outbreak of war.
Scarouady
Oneida-born leader living among the Ohio nations who advised the British during the Braddock expedition.
Shingas and Pisquetomen
Lenape leaders whose wartime and diplomatic choices reflected shifts among Ohio communities.
Teedyuscung
Lenape leader who used the Easton conferences to press land claims and negotiate peace.
Hendrick (Theyanoguin, d. 1755)
Mohawk leader and pro-British diplomat killed near Lake George.
Sir William Johnson
British superintendent of northern Indian affairs who directed much of the diplomacy with the Haudenosaunee and Seven Nations.
Charles Langlade
Odawa-French officer who led Great Lakes warriors in French campaigns.
Attakullakulla, Oconostota, and Ostenaco
Cherokee leaders with differing responses to British conduct in the Anglo-Cherokee War.
Christian Frederick Post
Moravian missionary whose 1758 mission to the Ohio towns supported the Easton diplomacy.
John Forbes
British general whose 1758 campaign reached the forks of the Ohio.
Louis-Joseph de Montcalm and Pierre de Rigaud de Vaudreuil
French military commander and governor-general whose differing approaches affected relations with Indigenous allies.
Jeffery Amherst
British commander-in-chief who led the 1760 advance on Montreal and later restricted gifts and supplies.
The Ohio forks (Fort Duquesne, Fort Pitt)
A strategic site where French and later British fortifications stood, central to Ohio diplomacy.
Fort Niagara
French post controlling the Niagara portage and the route to the upper Great Lakes, captured in 1759.
Kahnawake and Odanak
Mission communities of the St. Lawrence, one a site of 1760 councils and the other destroyed in a ranger raid in 1759.
Louisbourg
French fortress on Île Royale, the site of gift distribution to Mi'kmaq allies, captured in 1758.
Fort Loudoun and Fort Prince George
British posts in Cherokee country that figured in the siege, hostage killings, and surrender of 1760.
Treaty of Paris (1763)
European settlement transferring imperial claims without Indigenous consent.

Constructing a Source Dossier on an Indigenous Diplomatic Moment in the Seven Years' War

This project asks you to build a source dossier on one diplomatic or military episode discussed in the chapter, comparing documents created by different participants and assessing the perspectives they represent.

  3. Locate scholarship. Find at least two scholarly works that interpret the episode, and compare how they use the sources.

Chapter Reflection

The Seven Years' War is often remembered as the conflict that decided which European empire would dominate eastern North America. This chapter has shown that this description, while accurate for the imperial claims recorded in 1763, conceals much of what happened. The war was fought in homelands, decided in councils, and sustained by Indigenous participation that could be given or withdrawn. Seeing the war this way changes how its key events appear: Braddock's defeat, the fall of Fort Duquesne, and the surrender of Montreal become moments in which Indigenous political judgment can be traced.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs throughout the book: the difference between imperial claims and effective control. Treaties among European powers could reassign claims across a continent, but they could not by themselves change who governed particular lands. That difference helps explain why 1763 was a moment of danger rather than settlement, and why Indigenous nations would continue to contest British authority in the years that followed.

The war's consequences extended far beyond 1763. The negotiated agreements of 1760 and 1761 continue to be cited in Canadian courts, and the broken assurances of 1758 shaped Ohio Indigenous attitudes toward British power. The reduction of Indigenous leverage after the removal of France also influenced the choices available to nations during the American Revolution, the subject of Chapter 59, and the political order described in Chapter 60. Understanding how Indigenous nations experienced the Seven Years' War provides a basis for understanding why they acted as they did in each of those later struggles.

Finally, the chapter shows why Indigenous history needs its own sources, questions, and chronologies. The same events look different when the reader asks why a Shawnee council changed course, what a Mi'kmaq leader expected of a treaty, or how a Cherokee community understood a hostage's death. Historians cannot always answer such questions, but asking them is what distinguishes this textbook's approach from a campaign history with Indigenous participants added. The next chapter takes up the resistance of 1763 and shows what Indigenous nations did when the postwar order failed to match their expectations.
