Gaming and the New Tribal Economy

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In December 1979, the Seminole Tribe of Florida opened a bingo hall on its Hollywood reservation, offering prizes larger than Florida law allowed anyone else to offer. County officials called it illegal. The tribe called it an exercise of governmental authority over its own land. Within a generation, that dispute would help produce an industry reporting $46.2 billion in gross gaming revenue in a single federal fiscal year.

That figure, announced by the National Indian Gaming Commission (NIGC) in July 2026 for fiscal year 2025, is easy to misread. It is not profit, it is not tribal government income, and it is not wealth distributed to Indigenous households. It is the amount wagered on tribal gaming operations minus the prizes paid out, before wages, debt payments, regulatory costs, and every other expense of running a business. The number measures the size of an industry. It says nothing by itself about who benefits from that industry, how much of its revenue reaches public budgets, or whether it has strengthened or merely commercialized Indigenous self-government. Answering those questions is the work of this chapter.

Chapter 121 traced the broader history of Indigenous economic development: enterprise, finance, and the long effort to build productive economies under federal administration. This chapter examines one industry that became a particularly consequential instrument of that effort. Its argument is that tribal gaming was not simply a new form of entertainment. It grew from Indigenous nations asserting governmental authority over their own lands, defending that authority in court, and building commercial institutions capable of financing government. It then entered a federal statutory framework, the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, that both protected and constrained what tribes had won.

The chapter follows that transformation in five divisions. The first examines origins: Indigenous games and wagering before colonization, the economic conditions of reservations in the twentieth century, and the two foundational pieces of litigation, the Seminole bingo cases and California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians (1987). The second examines the federal architecture built in 1988: the statute, the three classes of gaming, the NIGC, and the rules governing which lands may host gaming. The third turns to compacts between tribes and states, the Supreme Court's decision in Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida (1996), and the spread of destination resorts such as Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun. The fourth considers money and its consequences: revenue accounting, public finance, per capita payments, and the social controversies surrounding the industry. The fifth examines diversification, Canadian arrangements, sports betting and digital gaming, and the question of legacy.

Two cautions run through the narrative. First, tribal gaming is geographically uneven. The NIGC's fiscal year 2025 report counted 545 gaming establishments operated by 246 tribes in 29 states, but the federally recognized tribes of the United States number well over five hundred, and many of them operate no casino. Others operate small facilities whose revenues cover little more than their own costs. A relatively small number of large properties produce a majority of the industry's revenue. Second, the history of gaming cannot be told only as a success story or only as a cautionary tale. Documented gains in employment and public investment coexist with documented concerns about problem gambling, dependence on a single industry, and competition from new technologies. A responsible account holds both.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. Court opinions and congressional statutes establish the legal framework. NIGC revenue reports, audited financial statements, and tribal budgets document the economics. Academic studies, including long-term research on the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians and analyses by Census Bureau economists, offer evidence about household and community outcomes. Compacts, tribal gaming ordinances, and press accounts reveal the politics of negotiation. Each source type has limits, which this chapter notes as it goes. Tribal voices appear in this evidence mainly through governments and their leaders, so students should ask whose perspective a given source represents and whose is missing.

The chapter also stays within its assigned boundaries. Termination (Chapter 104), self-determination policy (Chapter 114), Alaska Native land claims (Chapter 115), Canadian constitutional politics (Chapter 117), tribal courts and sovereignty doctrine (Chapter 118), land recovery (Chapter 119), and the economic history introduced in Chapter 121 are treated elsewhere. Here they appear only as far as needed to explain gaming. Cultural programs supported by gaming revenue connect to Chapters 123, 124, and 126, and the institutional capacity of tribal governments is the subject of Chapter 139.

This chapter is a history of commercial and governmental institutions. It is not a guide to gambling, and it does not evaluate gambling as an activity. Its question is how Indigenous nations used a particular legal and commercial opening to build fiscal capacity, what that cost them, and what it left unresolved as of November 2026.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the rise of tribal gaming is best understood as an assertion of governmental authority and a search for public revenue rather than as the arrival of a new entertainment industry.
  2. Distinguish traditional Indigenous games and wagering from modern commercial gaming without implying a direct line of descent between them.
  3. Analyze the legal reasoning in Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Butterworth (1981) and California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians (1987), including the distinction between criminal/prohibitory and civil/regulatory state laws under Public Law 280.
  4. Describe the structure of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 and the responsibilities it assigns to tribes, states, and federal institutions.
  5. Differentiate Class I, Class II, and Class III gaming and explain why classification, not a machine's appearance, determines the legal pathway.
  6. Evaluate how federal recognition, trust land, and statutory exceptions interact to determine whether gaming may lawfully occur on a given parcel.
  7. Explain the purpose and limits of tribal-state compacts, including revenue sharing, and the effect of Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida (1996) on bargaining power.
  8. Distinguish gross gaming revenue, net revenue, public revenue, and per capita payments, and interpret NIGC data accurately.
  9. Assess evidence for the social and economic effects of tribal gaming, separating documented findings from political allegation and universal claims from case-specific results.
  10. Compare the American system under IGRA with Canadian provincial arrangements such as the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority.
  11. Analyze how sports betting, internet gaming, and prediction markets have created new jurisdictional disputes between 2018 and 2026.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether gaming has strengthened Indigenous sovereignty, using historical evidence rather than commercial success as the measure.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Indigenous governments in the 1970s and 1980s turn to gaming, and what does that choice reveal about the limits of other development options?
  2. In what sense did tribal gaming predate the federal statute that regulates it, and in what sense did that statute create new legal conditions?
  3. Why did the Supreme Court's distinction between prohibition and regulation matter so much for tribal economic development?
  4. Did the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act expand tribal sovereignty, restrict it, or do both, and how would a historian decide?
  5. How did the sovereign immunity of states reshape the bargaining relationship between tribes and states after 1996?
  6. Why has tribal gaming been so geographically uneven, and what does that unevenness imply for claims about Indigenous economic recovery?
  7. What distinguishes using gaming as a revenue source from using it as investment capital, and why does the distinction matter for the long term?
  8. How should historians weigh evidence of improved employment and income against evidence of social harms and economic dependence?
  9. How do Canadian provincial arrangements differ from American tribal-state compacting, and what do those differences reveal about sovereignty?
  10. What is at stake, historically and legally, in disputes over online sports betting and prediction markets?

Historical Context

By the 1970s, most Indigenous governments in the United States faced a structural fiscal problem. Their lands were largely held in trust and could not easily be mortgaged. Reservation populations were often small and dispersed, with limited local markets and few banks willing to lend. Federal funding came through annual appropriations and program-specific grants, which tied governments to federal priorities and left them without discretionary revenue. Unlike states and municipalities, most tribes could not rely on property or sales taxes: on lands with low incomes, small tax bases, and complicated jurisdictional rules, taxation raised little. The result was governments with legal authority but few independent means of exercising it.

The policy environment was shifting. The termination era of the 1950s and early 1960s, treated in Chapter 104, had sought to end federal recognition and push Indigenous people toward assimilation and relocation. Its failure and the political reaction against it helped produce the policy of self-determination, discussed in Chapter 114, which sought to transfer program administration to tribal governments. Self-determination expanded governance responsibilities, but it did not by itself supply a revenue base. Tribal leaders therefore sought enterprises that governments themselves could own, rather than depending only on private individuals, outside lessees, or federal contracts.

Law supplied a second part of the context. In 1953 Congress enacted Public Law 280, which transferred criminal jurisdiction over many reservation lands, and some civil jurisdiction, to certain states, including California and, later, Florida. For decades the question of what Public Law 280 permitted states to do on reservations was unsettled. Where a state outlawed an activity outright, state officials could argue that PL 280 let them enforce that prohibition against Indigenous operators. Where a state merely regulated an activity, the argument was weaker. That distinction between prohibiting conduct and regulating it became the legal hinge on which early tribal gaming turned.

Finally, American gambling law was changing. By the 1970s and 1980s, many states had legalized lotteries and charitable bingo and were expanding other forms of gaming to raise public revenue. Nevada and, after 1976, Atlantic City, New Jersey, remained the only places with commercial casinos. Tribes thus approached gaming in a setting in which states themselves increasingly relied on it, a fact that later weakened state arguments that gaming was inherently contrary to public policy.

Students should keep in mind that gaming did not arise in a vacuum or from a single decision. It grew from the intersection of fiscal need, self-determination policy, a distinctive jurisdictional doctrine, and a shifting national attitude toward legal wagering. The next sections trace how these elements combined.

Division I · Section 122.1

Indigenous Games, Cultural Traditions, and the Distinction between Ceremony and Commercial Gaming

Games of skill, chance, and athletic competition were widespread among Indigenous peoples of North America long before European colonization. Archaeologists and ethnographers have documented dice games played with marked plum stones, bones, or shell; hand or stick games in which teams guess the location of hidden objects; hoop-and-pole games; and team sports played with sticks and balls, including the stickball traditions of the Southeast and the lacrosse of the Great Lakes and Northeast. At Cahokia, discussed in Chapter 22, the chunkey game, in which players threw spears at a rolling stone disk, was important enough to be associated with a specialized playing field and with elite display. Among the Haudenosaunee, treated in Chapter 26, a bowl-and-peach-pit dice game formed part of seasonal ceremonial calendars. Such games were not interchangeable. Each belonged to particular communities, seasons, and meanings.

Their functions were correspondingly varied. Some games were recreation. Some were ceremonial, performed to heal, to honor, or to maintain relations with other beings. Some were diplomatic or intercommunity contests that organized rivalry and exchange between neighboring peoples. Many involved wagering of goods, which could redistribute property among families and settle reputations. In some communities, play carried rules of fairness, hospitality, and obligation that made it social institution as much as pastime. Historians should be careful, however, not to describe these activities with modern gambling vocabulary. Words such as "casino," "odds," or "house" import commercial assumptions that did not shape these practices.

That caution matters for a second reason. Contemporary advocates sometimes point to Indigenous games to argue that gaming is a traditional Indigenous practice, and opponents sometimes reply that casinos have nothing to do with tradition. The historical record supports neither simple claim. Indigenous games are real and old, and some tribal governments have drawn on them in explaining their relationship to gaming. But modern casinos are commercial enterprises regulated under twentieth-century law, financed through modern capital markets, and open to the general public. They did not descend from a single Indigenous gaming tradition, and the legal right to operate them rests on governmental authority and statutory framework, not on cultural continuity alone.

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act itself recognizes this distinction. Its Class I category covers social games played for prizes of minimal value and traditional forms of Indigenous gaming engaged in as part of tribal ceremonies or celebrations, and it leaves regulation of those games to tribal governments alone (§122.6). Commercial bingo, slot machines, and banked card games are placed in different categories with different oversight. The statute thereby separates ceremony from commerce, even though some people living in these communities encounter both.

The evidence for earlier games is uneven. Material remains such as gaming pieces and playing fields survive archaeologically. Ethnographic descriptions, recorded largely by outside observers in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, describe games in detail but filter them through the observers' assumptions, and they often describe communities after generations of colonial change. Oral tradition and community knowledge, discussed in Chapter 1, provide further understanding where communities choose to share it. Students should treat these sources as complementary and note that some game knowledge is held by communities with restrictions on its public discussion.

Division I · Section 122.2

Reservation Economies, Federal Policy, and the Search for Independent Revenue

The reservation economy of the mid-twentieth century was shaped by decades of federal policy. Allotment, treated in Chapter 86, had fragmented landholdings, and many allotments passed out of Indigenous ownership. Trust status, intended as protection, limited the ability to use land as collateral. Federal administration of leases and funds gave officials extensive control over Indigenous resources. Jobs on reservations were scarce, often limited to federal and tribal programs, small trading posts, agriculture, and seasonal work. Many families depended on wage labor elsewhere, which Chapter 105 examines through the relocation program and the growth of urban Indigenous communities.

For tribal governments, the problem had a distinctive fiscal form. Governing a community requires revenue for police, courts, roads, water, housing, and administration. Most tribal governments received those funds through federal programs that were restricted by purpose, renewed annually, and vulnerable to shifts in federal priorities. They could not borrow readily because creditors hesitated to lend against trust land and because sovereign immunity and jurisdictional uncertainty complicated enforcement of loan terms. Their ability to tax was legally recognized but practically limited by small and low-income populations. A government that cannot raise its own revenue is dependent on those who can, and that dependence has historically carried political costs.

Earlier development efforts had produced mixed results. Federal programs encouraged tribes to attract manufacturing, tourism, and extractive industries. Some succeeded, including tribally owned timber, fisheries, and recreation enterprises, but many failed for reasons that had little to do with tribal ability: remote locations, thin labor markets, scarce capital, and the absence of control over land and resources. Chapter 121 discusses these enterprises. What gaming offered was unusual. Its principal inputs, a customer base and legal authority to operate, did not require large natural resource endowments, and in places with access to population centers it could produce revenue quickly enough to matter at the scale of a government's budget.

Self-determination policy created further incentives. As Chapter 114 explains, tribal governments took over the administration of programs previously run by federal agencies. That expansion of responsibilities increased the need for discretionary revenue. Federal funds could administer a program, but tribes also needed flexible money to match grants, fill gaps, and make long-term investments. Gaming, which a government could own and whose proceeds it could direct, appealed to leaders seeking to turn legal authority into fiscal capacity.

Students should note a historiographical point. It is tempting to treat gaming as an inevitable outcome of these conditions. It was not. Tribes chose it among alternatives, many nations chose differently, and the legal route to gaming remained uncertain until courts resolved it. The pressures described above explain why gaming was attractive. They do not explain by themselves why it became legally possible, which is the subject of the next two sections.

Division I · Section 122.3

The Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Rise of High-Stakes Tribal Bingo

The Seminole Tribe of Florida organized under a federally approved constitution in 1957, after a history that included the Seminole Wars discussed in Chapter 67. By the 1970s the tribe was governing reservations in southern Florida with limited resources. Tribal leaders, including Chairman James Billie, searched for enterprises that could generate revenue. They already operated a smoke shop selling tobacco without state tax, a business that tested the boundary between tribal and state authority. In December 1979 the tribe opened a bingo hall in Hollywood, Florida, with prizes substantially above the low caps Florida law imposed on bingo and with open attendance by non-members.

Florida officials responded. Broward County's sheriff, Robert Butterworth, sought to enforce state limits on bingo prizes and operations against the tribe, arguing that exceeding them was criminal under state law and that Public Law 280 allowed the state to apply its criminal law on the reservation. The tribe sued, contending that Florida had no authority to enforce its bingo regulations against a tribal government on tribal land. The dispute was therefore not a debate about whether gambling was good or bad. It was a dispute about who held governing authority over an activity on tribal territory.

In Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Butterworth (1981), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit sided with the tribe. The court reasoned that Florida did not prohibit bingo; it permitted bingo for charitable and other purposes and regulated its conduct through limits on stakes and operation. A state law that regulates a permitted activity is, in this analysis, civil and regulatory, and Public Law 280 did not give states power to enforce civil regulatory laws on reservations. Florida's bingo limits therefore could not be applied to the tribe. The Supreme Court declined to review the decision in 1982, leaving it in force.

The consequences extended well beyond Florida. Tribes in other states saw that a nation could operate bingo with large prizes if the state allowed bingo at all. Tribal bingo enterprises multiplied during the 1980s, attracting customers from regional populations and generating revenue for tribal governments. The expansion occurred through political initiative and commercial experimentation. No comprehensive federal gaming policy existed. Tribes acted on their own assessments of authority, and states responded with enforcement efforts, litigation, and in some cases accommodation.

The Seminole example also demonstrates something about method. The court did not announce a general right to gamble on reservations. It addressed a specific question about the limits of state power under a specific statute. That narrowness was characteristic of the early cases and became a recurring theme: courts resolved jurisdictional questions, and the consequences for the industry followed from those answers. Students should also recognize that the outcome depended on Florida's own gambling laws. A state that had flatly prohibited bingo would have presented a different case, a point that became central in Cabazon.

Division I · Section 122.4

California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, 1987

The Cabazon and Morongo Bands of Mission Indians are small nations in Riverside County, California, a Public Law 280 state. In the early 1980s both operated bingo, and the Cabazon Band also operated a card club offering poker and other card games. These enterprises generated revenue and employment on reservations with limited alternative opportunities. Riverside County sought to apply its ordinances regulating bingo and card playing, and California sought to apply its own limits, which restricted bingo prizes and required that bingo be operated by charitable organizations staffed by volunteers. The bands brought suit to prevent enforcement.

The case reached the Supreme Court, which decided it on February 25, 1987, in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, 480 U.S. 202. By a vote of six to three, the Court held that California and Riverside County could not enforce those laws against the bands. Writing for the majority, Justice Byron White focused on a question that had been developing in lower courts: when does a state law fall within the criminal jurisdiction Public Law 280 transferred to states, and when is it a civil regulatory law that PL 280 did not extend to reservations?

The Court's answer was a test of purpose. If a state law generally prohibits certain conduct, it is criminal and prohibitory, and PL 280 permits enforcement. If a state law generally permits the conduct but regulates it, it is civil and regulatory, and PL 280 does not apply. The Court examined California's actual policy. California ran a state lottery, allowed horse racing with pari-mutuel wagering, permitted card rooms in some localities, and authorized bingo for charities. The state did not treat gambling as contrary to public policy overall; it regulated it. The bingo and card laws were therefore regulatory, and the state could not enforce them on reservations through PL 280.

The Court also considered whether other federal law, or federal and tribal interests, barred state regulation. It found that federal policy encouraged tribal economic development and self-sufficiency and that the bands' enterprises supplied funds for government services and employment. California's asserted interest in preventing infiltration by organized crime was not persuasive on the record before the Court, which showed no demonstrated problem and a federal and tribal regulatory capacity. Where federal and tribal interests were strong and the state's interest was not sufficient to override them, state regulation was preempted.

The ruling mattered, but students must avoid overstating it. Cabazon did not authorize every form of gambling on every reservation. Its outcome depended on California's own policies; in a state that criminally prohibited a form of gaming, the analysis could differ. It also did not address federal regulation, because at that time no comprehensive federal statute existed. Its practical effect was to confirm that in many states tribes could offer the same games states permitted others to offer, without the state's regulatory limits. That effect alarmed state governments and established gaming interests, and it created pressure on Congress to legislate, which it did in 1988.

Historical Vocabulary
Criminal/Prohibitory and Civil/Regulatory Laws

Definition. In the framework used in Cabazon, a state law is criminal/prohibitory if its general intent is to forbid conduct as contrary to public policy, and civil/regulatory if the state generally permits the conduct but subjects it to rules and conditions.

Historical context. The distinction arose from the interpretation of Public Law 280 (1953), which transferred criminal jurisdiction over reservation lands to certain states and a more limited civil jurisdiction. Courts needed a way to decide whether a given state law was a criminal law enforceable under PL 280.

Why it matters. The test turns on a state's actual treatment of an activity. A state that allows lotteries, racetracks, and charitable gaming is hard pressed to argue that gaming in general is prohibited.

Potential misunderstanding. The label a state attaches to a law does not control. A violation may be punished by criminal penalties without the law being "prohibitory" in the Cabazon sense. Students should also avoid thinking this test governs all gaming today; after 1988 the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act supplies the primary statutory framework, though the test remains relevant to how IGRA's Class III requirement has been interpreted.

Primary Source Window

California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, 480 U.S. 202 (1987)

Creator: Majority opinion by Justice Byron White; dissent by Justice John Paul Stevens, joined by Chief Justice William Rehnquist and Justice Antonin Scalia · Date: February 25, 1987 · Type: Supreme Court judicial opinion

Context: The opinion resolved a dispute between California and Riverside County on one side and the Cabazon and Morongo Bands on the other over bingo and card-playing operations on reservations in a Public Law 280 state. It was issued with the benefit of lower-court rulings, including the Seminole case, and of extensive briefing by tribes, states, and the United States.

Brief paraphrase of the reasoning (not a quotation): The majority separated state laws that prohibit conduct from state laws that regulate conduct the state otherwise allows. Because California permitted and promoted many forms of gambling, its bingo and card-game limits were regulatory. Division II · Section 122.5

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988

Congress had already been considering bills on Indian gaming before Cabazon, but the decision sharpened the debate. States feared losing the ability to regulate gaming within their borders. Established gaming interests, including Nevada and Atlantic City casinos, were concerned about new competition. Tribes wanted protection for the enterprises they had built and for the authority courts had recognized. Federal officials worried about the possibility of fraud and criminal infiltration, although the documentary record for the latter concern in tribal gaming was thin. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, signed by President Ronald Reagan on October 17, 1988, was a compromise among these positions.

The statute states its purposes in its opening provisions. It aims to provide a statutory basis for tribal gaming as a means of promoting tribal economic development, self-sufficiency, and strong tribal governments; to protect gaming from organized crime and other corrupting influences; and to establish standards and a regulatory body so that tribes are the primary beneficiaries and gaming is conducted fairly. The statute also requires that tribes be the primary beneficiaries of their gaming operations and that net revenues be used for specified public purposes (§122.14).

Its structure reflects the compromise. IGRA sorted gaming into three classes, with different regulatory arrangements. For the games courts had already allowed tribes to operate, bingo and similar activities, it left regulation primarily to tribes, with federal oversight. For casino-style games, it required tribes and states to negotiate compacts, giving states a role they had lost under Cabazon. It created the National Indian Gaming Commission to oversee the system. It also restricted gaming to "Indian lands" and, for lands acquired after the statute's enactment, imposed additional conditions (§122.8).

Indigenous leaders disagreed about the bargain. Some regarded federal legislation as necessary to protect gaming from continued state challenge and to give lenders and investors the certainty large projects require. Others viewed the compact requirement as a concession that placed a new restriction on tribal sovereignty, since it gave states a negotiating role in an activity that Cabazon had treated as beyond their regulatory power. A third view held that Congress had acted within its recognized plenary authority and that the practical question was how to use the statute rather than whether to accept it. These differences reflect more than strategy; they reflect different understandings of what the relationship between tribes, states, and the United States ought to be, subjects later examined in Chapter 118.

The statute thus did not create tribal gaming. By 1988 it already existed on many reservations. What the statute did was give it a defined structure, extend federal oversight, and add state participation for the most lucrative forms. Whether this enhanced or limited sovereignty is a matter of ongoing debate, taken up in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Division II · Section 122.6

Class I, Class II, and Class III Gaming

IGRA divides gaming into three classes, and the class of an activity determines who regulates it and what must be done before it can lawfully operate. Students should understand that these are legal categories. They are not categories of business size or prominence.

ClassificationGeneral descriptionRegulatory framework
Class ITraditional ceremonial gaming and qualifying social games for prizes of minimal valueExclusive tribal jurisdiction under IGRA
Class IIBingo and certain related games, and certain nonbanking card gamesTribal regulation subject to federal statutory and regulatory oversight
Class IIIAll forms outside Classes I and II, including conventional slot machines and banking card gamesTribal and federal requirements, ordinarily including an effective tribal-state compact

Class I connects to the discussion in §122.1: it protects ceremonial and minor social gaming from outside regulation. Class II covers bingo, including bingo played with electronic aids, and nonbanking card games, meaning games in which players compete against each other rather than the house, if state law allows them in some form. The statute explicitly excludes banking card games and electronic or electromechanical facsimiles of games of chance and slot machines from Class II. Class III is the residual category and includes most casino games, such as slot machines, roulette, craps, and blackjack, and also, in modern interpretation, sports wagering.

Class III gaming is subject to three interconnected statutory conditions. It must be authorized by a tribal ordinance approved by the NIGC chair; it must take place in a state that permits the relevant form of gaming for some purpose by some person; and it must ordinarily operate under a tribal–state compact in effect under IGRA. A compact is the usual route, not the only mechanism contemplated by Congress. Section 2710(d)(7)(B)(vii) also authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to prescribe procedures for Class III gaming when the law's specified court-and-mediation sequence reaches that stage and a state declines the mediator's proposal. After Seminole Tribe v. Florida (1996), the availability of this sequence was complicated by states' sovereign immunity and by challenges to Interior's alternative regulations, particularly Texas v. United States (2007); §122.10 traces those limits. The statutory permission condition recalls Cabazon, but state permission, the tribal ordinance, compact negotiation, and possible federal procedures are distinct legal questions. A state does not possess an unconditional veto in every possible statutory setting, nor does IGRA authorize casino gaming without requirements.

The boundary between Class II and Class III has produced decades of controversy because technology blurred it. Bingo is a Class II game, but bingo can be played on electronic systems whose terminals resemble slot machines. Tribes in states that refused to negotiate Class III compacts, or where compacts were costly, sometimes operated electronic Class II systems, and states, competitors, and federal officials contested whether particular systems were permissible aids to bingo or prohibited electronic facsimiles. Federal courts and the NIGC addressed these disputes over many years, and the Commission developed technical standards for Class II systems. The key legal point is that appearance does not decide classification. A machine that looks like a slot machine may be lawful Class II equipment if it meets the statutory definition, and a machine that looks modest may be Class III if it does not.

Students should therefore not read IGRA as granting an unrestricted right to build casinos. It created several different legal pathways, each with conditions, and tribes in different circumstances have used them differently. Some nations operate only Class II gaming. Others, in states that agree to compacts, operate large Class III facilities. Still others, as §122.12 explains, operate no gaming at all.

Division II · Section 122.7

The National Indian Gaming Commission and Tribal Regulatory Institutions

IGRA created the National Indian Gaming Commission (NIGC) as an independent federal regulatory body within the Department of the Interior. When fully constituted, the Commission consists of a chair appointed by the President with Senate confirmation and two associate commissioners appointed by the Secretary of the Interior. The agency began operating in the early 1990s and developed the regulations, audit requirements, and enforcement practices through which federal oversight works. The NIGC chair, rather than the Secretary of the Interior, has the designated federal approval authority for tribal gaming ordinances and management contracts, within the applicable provisions of IGRA. The Commission and its staff perform oversight, compliance, investigative, regulatory, and enforcement functions, including reviews of independent audits, minimum internal control standards, and sanctions. The Secretary of the Interior separately reviews Class III tribal–state compacts and has other statutory responsibilities, including specified land decisions and possible Secretarial Procedures. These are related, but distinct, federal functions.

The NIGC does not run casinos, and it does not act alone. The regulatory system has several layers. Tribal governments are primary regulators of gaming on their lands. Most establish tribal gaming commissions, separate from the enterprises they oversee, that license employees and vendors, inspect facilities, review surveillance and accounting, and enforce tribal gaming ordinances. The NIGC oversees the system and acts where tribal regulation fails or federal law requires. State agencies take on regulatory roles in Class III gaming according to the terms of compacts. The Department of the Interior approves compacts and acquires land in trust. Federal law enforcement agencies pursue criminal conduct.

A central principle is the separation between management and regulation. Many tribes have contracted with outside companies to develop or manage casinos, especially in the early years when they lacked experience and capital. IGRA requires NIGC approval of management contracts, limits their duration and fees, and requires that tribes retain proprietary interest and ultimate responsibility. A tribe that owns a casino is in effect both the owner and the regulator, and the system tries to protect integrity by assigning those roles to distinct governmental bodies, with licensing independent of operations. Whether this separation works well depends on the quality of tribal institutions, which varies, as it does for regulators in any industry.

Corruption prevention was a reason for the statute, and the record has included real enforcement actions, including cases of embezzlement, improper management contract practices, and financial misreporting. The record does not support the older popular claim that Indigenous gaming was dominated by organized crime. In Cabazon itself, the Court noted that the state's showing of organized crime involvement was inadequate, and later federal reviews found problems of the sort that occur in any cash-handling industry. Historians should treat enforcement cases as evidence of how the regulatory system operates, not as proof of a distinctive pattern of Indigenous misconduct.

The NIGC also became the principal archive of industry data. Tribes must submit audited financial statements, and the Commission compiles annual gross gaming revenue reports. These reports are essential evidence for this chapter, but they have limits. They describe revenue from operations on Indian lands, aggregate data by region, and lag actual operations; the fiscal year 2025 report states that it may include revenue earned up to sixteen months before publication. They do not show profits, tribal government distributions, or how revenue was spent.

Division II · Section 122.8

Indian Lands, Federal Recognition, and Eligibility for Gaming Development

IGRA authorizes tribal gaming only on "Indian lands," a term defined in the statute to include all lands within the limits of an Indian reservation and lands held in trust by the United States for a tribe or individual, or held by a tribe or individual subject to federal restriction on alienation, over which the tribe exercises governmental power. Ordinary fee-simple property owned by a tribe off a reservation, without trust or restricted status, does not qualify. A tribal government's casino on such property would not be authorized under IGRA, even if the tribe owned the land outright.

For lands acquired in trust after October 17, 1988, IGRA generally prohibits gaming unless an exception applies. The exceptions include lands within or contiguous to a tribe's reservation as it existed on that date; lands in Oklahoma within the tribe's former reservation or contiguous to it; lands taken into trust as part of the initial reservation of a tribe acknowledged by the federal government after 1988; lands taken into trust as part of a settlement of a land claim; and lands restored to a tribe whose federal recognition was terminated and later restored. A separate procedure permits the Secretary of the Interior, after consultation, to determine that gaming on newly acquired lands would be in the best interest of the tribe and not detrimental to the surrounding community, but only if the governor of the state concurs. Federal regulations, including those at 25 C.F.R. Part 292, implement these provisions.

Federal recognition matters because only federally recognized tribes may use IGRA. Recognition and eligibility are not the same, however, and the difference is often misunderstood. A recognized tribe with no qualifying land cannot game. A recognized tribe with qualifying land must still conform to the class and compact requirements. And land acquisition by itself does not confer gaming rights. The Mohegan Tribe's quick progress after its 1994 acknowledgment (§122.11) depended on its having an existing land base and on the specific legal posture of Connecticut gaming. Other tribes, notably those restored after termination, such as nations discussed in Chapter 104, have relied on the restored-lands exception, and still others have faced years of administrative and judicial review.

Off-reservation gaming has been among the most contested areas. Tribes seeking to develop sites near larger markets, sometimes far from their historic reservations, have argued that history of removal, termination, or landlessness justifies such acquisition. Opponents, including some other tribes whose markets would be affected, local governments, and competitors, have argued that it circumvents the purposes of the 1988 limits. Governors' concurrence requirements give states significant influence. These disputes interact with the land recovery history in Chapter 119, which this chapter does not repeat.

Territorial jurisdiction also affects eligibility in unusual cases. In Texas, the Ysleta del Sur Pueblo's gaming is governed by a restoration statute that subjects the tribe to Texas gaming law, and in 2022 the Supreme Court held that the statute allows Texas to enforce its restrictions, which has limited the Pueblo's gaming. The case is a reminder that IGRA is not the only statute that governs Indigenous gaming and that individual tribes' legal histories differ. Related questions of state authority and sovereign immunity were addressed in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014), in which the Court held that tribal sovereign immunity barred a state's suit over a casino the tribe opened on land outside its reservation. The case did not decide that such a casino was lawful under IGRA. It decided that a particular remedy was unavailable.

Division III · Section 122.9

Tribal-State Gaming Compacts and Intergovernmental Negotiation

A compact is an agreement between a tribal government and a state government setting the terms on which Class III gaming may be conducted on Indian lands. IGRA obliges a state to negotiate in good faith; the Secretary of the Interior reviews agreements under the statute; and a compact takes effect when the required notice of approval is published in the Federal Register. A compact has contractual and intergovernmental features and can be compared by analogy to a treaty insofar as two governments negotiate obligations. It is not, however, a historical federal Indian treaty or the same legal category as a treaty between an Indigenous nation and the United States. It is a specific statutory instrument governed by IGRA.

Historical Vocabulary
Tribal-State Compact and Class III Gaming

Definition. A tribal-state compact is a negotiated agreement between a tribe and a state that governs Class III gaming on the tribe's Indian lands. Class III gaming is the residual category of IGRA, covering casino-style games and similar activities.

Historical context. Congress chose compacts in 1988 as a compromise after Cabazon had removed state regulatory authority over certain gaming. Compacts gave states a voice without giving them unilateral control.

Why it matters. The compact is the chief instrument through which games, regulatory roles, payments, and dispute procedures are set. It is also where political leverage between tribes and states is exercised.

Potential misunderstanding. A compact is not a state license, and it does not place a tribe under state law generally. It is a limited agreement among governments, operating within federal oversight.

Compacts typically address the games permitted, the number or type of machines, the allocation of regulatory responsibilities between tribe and state, standards of operation, licensing, law enforcement, dispute resolution, and in many cases payments from the tribe to the state or to other public purposes. The term of a compact and the procedures for renewal are also negotiated, and these have produced significant conflicts. For example, disputes arose in Oklahoma in the 2020s over whether model compacts had renewed automatically, and tribal governments largely prevailed in the proceedings that followed.

Payments are the most controversial element. IGRA bars states from imposing taxes on tribal gaming and gives a state no authority to demand a payment as a condition of negotiating. Yet many compacts include revenue-sharing provisions. The Department of the Interior and federal courts have generally permitted such provisions when the state offers something of substantial economic value in exchange, such as exclusive rights to operate certain games in a region or a market advantage a tribe would not otherwise enjoy. In that case, the payment is understood as a bargained price rather than a tax. In Rincon Band of Luiseno Mission Indians v. Schwarzenegger (2010), the Ninth Circuit held that California could not insist on revenue sharing without giving a meaningful concession. Where the state provides exclusivity, payment may be lawful; where it provides nothing, a demand for payment looks more like a tax.

Bargaining positions differ. A state has valuable assets: the authority to permit gaming forms beyond what the state otherwise allows, the potential to approve or block land acquisitions through gubernatorial concurrence, and, as explained in §122.10, constitutional protections from federal lawsuits. A tribe has assets as well: land, a market, a legal claim to good-faith negotiation, and sometimes the political support of voters. Compacts are therefore negotiated not simply by lawyers but by governors, legislatures, tribal councils, and voters in states that require ballot measures. California is an example: voters amended the state constitution in 2000 through Proposition 1A to allow compacts for Class III gaming, an outcome reached after an earlier initiative, Proposition 5 (1998), was struck down by the state supreme court.

Compact negotiation has become one of the main arenas of contemporary Indigenous intergovernmental politics. Compacts are periodically renegotiated, and each renewal is an occasion to reconsider exclusivity, payment levels, new games, and regulatory authority. Competing commercial interests lobby legislatures. Tribes sometimes disagree with each other, because one tribe's market exclusivity or location can be another's competition. The compact thus ties tribal economies to state political processes in ways that many tribal leaders regard as a continuing constraint, even as they recognize the revenue it makes possible.

Division III · Section 122.10

Seminole Tribe v. Florida, 1996, and the Limits of Compact Enforcement

IGRA's compact system rested on an enforcement mechanism. If a state refused to negotiate or failed to negotiate in good faith, the tribe could sue the state in federal court. If the court found a failure, it could order the parties to conclude a compact within sixty days, and if they did not, a mediator would select between the parties' proposals, with the Secretary of the Interior prescribing procedures if the state still refused. The mechanism was meant to prevent states from blocking tribal gaming through delay.

The Seminole Tribe of Florida used it. After Florida declined to negotiate on certain Class III games, the tribe sued in federal court. Florida moved to dismiss, arguing that the Eleventh Amendment, which reflects the sovereign immunity of states from certain lawsuits, barred the suit. The case reached the Supreme Court, which decided it on March 27, 1996, in Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44.

By a five-to-four vote, the Court held that Congress cannot use its power under the Indian Commerce Clause to abrogate a state's sovereign immunity and authorize a lawsuit against a nonconsenting state in federal court. Chief Justice William Rehnquist's majority opinion overruled an earlier decision, Pennsylvania v. Union Gas (1989), that had allowed such abrogation under the Interstate Commerce Clause. The Court also held that the alternative of suing a state official under the doctrine of Ex parte Young was unavailable here, because IGRA's detailed remedial scheme was designed for a different kind of suit and suggested that Congress had not intended to permit the broader remedy. Justice David Souter, joined by Justices Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Stephen Breyer, dissented at length, and Justice John Paul Stevens wrote separately.

Students must be precise about what the decision did and did not do. It did not eliminate the statutory duty of states to negotiate in good faith, and it did not make tribal gaming unlawful. It held that the particular federal-court remedy IGRA created could not constitutionally be enforced against a state that had not consented to suit. The tribe's statutory right continued to exist, but the primary mechanism for enforcing it did not.

The consequence was a shift in bargaining power. Before 1996, a state refusing to negotiate faced a potential judicial order and secretarial procedures. After 1996, states could refuse, and tribes had fewer options. Interior responded in 1999 by issuing regulations establishing procedures through which the Secretary could prescribe terms for Class III gaming when a state invoked immunity, and the validity of those procedures was later challenged in court; a federal appeals court limited them in Texas v. United States (2007). Many states continued to negotiate, because they valued the revenue, because voters and legislatures supported compacts, or because the alternative was prolonged conflict. Others used their strengthened position to demand greater payments or to withhold agreement. Tribes with strong political relationships and large markets could bargain effectively; those with weaker leverage faced difficult choices.

Division III · Section 122.11

Foxwoods, Mohegan Sun, and the Emergence of Destination Casino Resorts

The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation of southeastern Connecticut had endured centuries of dispossession, discussed in Chapter 48, and by the 1970s its reservation population was very small. The tribe achieved federal recognition by act of Congress in 1983 as part of a settlement of a land claim. In the mid-1980s its leaders began exploring economic options and opened a high-stakes bingo hall in 1986. A legal dispute with Connecticut followed, and in 1990 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit held that because Connecticut allowed charity "Las Vegas nights," it permitted casino-style gaming for some purposes and so could not forbid the tribe from negotiating for it under IGRA. After negotiation and federal intervention, the tribe opened the Foxwoods casino in February 1992, and in subsequent agreements the state received a share of slot machine revenue in exchange for exclusivity on certain games.

Foxwoods grew into a resort with hotels, entertainment venues, and conference facilities. It benefited from an unusual geographic position: a location between the large metropolitan populations of New York and Boston, with no other casinos for many miles in the early 1990s. Financing came from outside lenders willing to invest in a new type of project, and the tribe's development of the enterprise became a model for later projects. It employed thousands of people, many of them not tribal citizens, and generated revenue for the tribe's governmental and community programs. The tribe also built a museum and research center devoted to its history.

The Mohegan Tribe of Connecticut, a separate nation with a long history in the region, gained federal acknowledgment through the administrative process in 1994. It partnered with an outside gaming company, Sun International, to develop and manage a casino, and Mohegan Sun opened in October 1996 near Uncasville. Its design and management reflected a different business strategy, and it competed directly with Foxwoods. Over time the tribe assumed greater control of operations. The two Connecticut casinos together became a major regional industry, and the state's revenue-sharing arrangements made gaming a significant source of public funds.

These enterprises shaped national perceptions. News coverage of Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun created an image of Indigenous wealth that was often exaggerated and generalized. Public debate about gaming frequently treated the two Connecticut resorts as typical. They were not. Their advantages included proximity to large markets, early entry, available capital, and legal circumstances, particularly Connecticut's existing gaming laws and a state willing to compact. Most Indigenous nations lacked such conditions. A tribe in a remote area with a small surrounding population could not replicate them, and a tribe without recognized land could not use IGRA at all.

The Connecticut examples also show the political and economic costs of success. The casinos drew objections from neighboring towns concerned with traffic and infrastructure, from competing businesses, and from critics who worried about problem gambling. They became targets in debates over recognition: some opponents argued that gaming wealth encouraged groups to seek federal acknowledgment for financial reasons, a claim that recognition historians have examined and that is easy to assert but difficult to generalize, since most recognition petitions predated gaming interest and most petitioning groups were not seeking casinos. Students should evaluate such claims with care. Chapter 144 addresses questions of citizenship and identity.

Division III · Section 122.12

The Expansion of Tribal Gaming Across the United States

During the 1990s and 2000s, tribal gaming spread across much of the United States, but it did so in very different forms. In Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Michigan, tribes operated casinos after compacts negotiated around 1990. Minnesota's Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community developed a major facility near the Minneapolis–St. Paul metropolitan area. In Washington State, compacts in the 1990s led to a network of casinos across urban and rural sites. In Arizona, a statewide ballot measure in 2002 established the framework for expanded gaming compacts. In New Mexico, Pueblo nations negotiated compacts after a contentious early period. In Oklahoma, where many nations have trust and restricted lands, tribal gaming expanded first through bingo and then, after a 2004 state ballot measure authorizing a model compact, into a larger Class III industry that reaches the Oklahoma City and Tulsa markets.

California became the largest regional market. After voters approved Proposition 1A in 2000, tribes negotiated compacts with the governor and the legislature and built casinos ranging from small rural properties to large resorts near major cities. The NIGC's fiscal year 2025 report credits the Sacramento region, which covers California and northern Nevada, with $12.6 billion of the national total, the largest of its eight regions, produced by 88 operations. The Washington, D.C. region, which covers states including New York, Connecticut, North Carolina, Florida, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana, reported $11.2 billion, with growth of about ten percent over the previous year. The St. Paul region, which includes Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, Iowa, Indiana, and Nebraska, reported about $5.3 billion.

These numbers demonstrate concentration. The NIGC's FY 2025 Gross Gaming Revenue Report, page 7, states that approximately 8.6 percent of reporting gaming operations generated more than $250 million each and accounted for approximately 55.8 percent of national gross gaming revenue (rounded by the agency to 9 percent and 56 percent). At the other end, about 54.3 percent of establishments reported less than $25 million and received only 4.8 percent of total gross gaming revenue. These are shares of reported operations and GGR, not shares of all federally recognized tribes, households, or net income. The NIGC's Rapid City region, which includes much of the Dakotas, Montana, and Wyoming, was the only region to decline in fiscal year 2025, reporting about $440 million. A small rural casino may employ community members and contribute to local public services while receiving a fraction of a large metropolitan resort's receipts.

Geography explains much of this pattern. Casinos near large metropolitan populations, highways, airports, or tourism corridors can draw many customers. State policy matters equally: a state that limits the number of machines, requires revenue sharing, or authorizes competing commercial casinos shapes the market. Existing infrastructure, labor supply, and access to financing also matter. A small remote reservation may have willing management and a good plan but no market.

Many Indigenous nations did not establish casinos at all. Some lacked suitable or eligible land, some confronted state or federal legal barriers, and some concluded that projected receipts would not justify the costs. Others debated whether gaming accorded with cultural, religious, social, and economic priorities. The Navajo Nation offers an instructive example of contested community choices. Navajo voters rejected proposals in referendums in 1994 and 1997. The Navajo Nation Council nevertheless adopted a reservation-wide gaming ordinance in October 2001; in 2004 it considered additional casino authorization, and a contemporary Indigenous news account reported that a proposed further referendum was removed from the ballot after council action. Those episodes were not a sequence of successful gaming referendums. The Nation's first casino, Fire Rock Navajo Casino, opened on November 19, 2008. This history illustrates the distinction between direct popular decisions, legislation by a sovereign nation's council, and subsequent commercial development; it does not erase disagreement within Navajo communities. Alaska's distinctive legal situation, examined in Chapter 115, also limits casino-style operations. Students should treat gaming as a strategy adopted by some nations, not as a universal Indigenous preference or a uniform solution.

Division IV · Section 122.13

The Economics of Tribal Gaming: Revenue, Profit, Employment, and Investment

Reading the economics of tribal gaming requires a clear vocabulary. The total amount wagered, often called the handle, is not revenue. Gross gaming revenue is, roughly, the amount wagered minus the prizes paid to players; it is what the establishment retains before expenses. From that figure an operation pays wages, utilities, marketing, regulatory costs, taxes where applicable, management fees, payments under compacts, and interest and principal on debt. What remains is net revenue, from which the tribal government may make distributions for public purposes. Each stage is smaller than the last, and by a margin that varies widely between facilities.

Historical Vocabulary
Gross Gaming Revenue

Definition. Gross gaming revenue (GGR) is the amount wagered less the amount paid out as winnings, before operating costs and other expenses. The NIGC reports GGR annually, drawn from tribes' audited financial statements.

Historical context. GGR became the standard national measure because federal law requires audited statements, giving regulators a consistent basis for comparison across tribes. It is the nearest equivalent to an industry's sales figure.

Why it matters. It measures the scale of the industry and trends over time, and it permits regional comparison.

Potential misunderstanding. GGR is not profit, not tribal government income, not the amount distributed to citizens, and not the same as the economic impact of casinos on a region. The $46.2 billion reported for fiscal year 2025 is a gross figure for the whole industry.

The costs of operating are substantial. Casinos and resorts require large capital investments in buildings, gaming equipment, hotels, parking, and utilities. Many were financed by borrowing, often with the help of outside investors or management partners, and debt service can absorb a significant share of revenue, especially in early years. Competition requires reinvestment: properties must update equipment and expand amenities to hold customers. Labor is a large expense. Regulation, security, and surveillance add costs. A facility with high gross revenue can therefore generate modest net income, or even losses, in difficult years.

The NIGC's reports allow students to trace the industry's growth. The Commission reported gross gaming revenue above forty billion dollars in 2022, and $43.9 billion for fiscal year 2024. On July 21, 2026, it announced $46.2 billion for fiscal year 2025, an increase of 5.3 percent, the highest total recorded in the report series, from 545 gaming establishments operated by 246 tribes in 29 states. Seven of the eight NIGC regions increased. These numbers are presented here as dated evidence from the NIGC's report, which notes that fiscal-year data can include revenue earned many months before publication.

Employment effects are a major part of the economic story. Casinos employ many people, including tribal citizens and non-citizens, in jobs ranging from dealers and servers to engineers and executives. Research by economists, including Census Bureau studies discussed in §122.16, has found that casino openings are associated with employment and wage gains on reservations. At the same time, casino jobs are often service-sector jobs, and quality varies. The distinction between jobs created and long-term economic development is important. A casino can reduce unemployment while leaving a tribal economy dependent on one industry and one location.

The industry also faces market limits. Gaming revenue depends on consumers' discretionary spending, regional economic conditions, competition from other casinos and online gaming, and public health events. In the spring of 2020, pandemic closures eliminated revenue at many properties for months, an event that exposed how heavily some governments relied on gaming income. Recovery was rapid in many markets, but the episode reinforced arguments for diversification examined in §122.17.

Division IV · Section 122.14

Gaming Revenue, Tribal Public Finance, and Community Investment

IGRA does not allow a tribe to spend net gaming revenue however it chooses. The statute authorizes five uses: to fund tribal government operations or programs; to provide for the general welfare of the tribe and its members; to promote tribal economic development; to donate to charitable organizations; and to help fund the operations of local government agencies. Per capita distributions to members are permitted only under a federally approved plan (§122.15). These limits reflect the statute's premise that gaming is a governmental activity serving public purposes.

For tribal governments, this made gaming something unfamiliar: a source of revenue they controlled. Governments could plan multiyear budgets, match federal grants, and fund programs that federal appropriations did not. Revenue has gone to health clinics and hospitals, housing construction and rehabilitation, roads, water and sewer systems, police and fire services, courts, elder care, early childhood programs, schools and scholarships, and tribal administration. In many nations, gaming revenue funds a large fraction of governmental operations. The effects are visible in infrastructure, in the professionalization of tribal government, and in the ability to hire staff, lawyers, and planners.

Cultural programs have also benefited. Tribal governments have used gaming revenue for language programs, museums, cultural centers, ceremonial support, and archives. Chapter 123 examines language revitalization, Chapter 124 educational institutions and tribal colleges, and Chapter 125 repatriation, and gaming-supported funding appears in each. Here the point is institutional: revenue that a government controls can be directed to priorities defined by that community, rather than by external funders.

Some nations contribute to surrounding communities as well. Compacts may direct a share of revenue to state or local governments, and tribes may voluntarily support local schools, fire departments, or charities. These payments serve several purposes: they fulfill obligations, they respond to local concerns about the burden casinos impose, and they build political support.

Students should distinguish this collective public investment from direct payments to individuals. Many nations spend most of their net gaming revenue on governmental services and reinvestment and make no per capita payments. The decision reflects each community's priorities and the state of its finances. The historical significance of gaming for sovereignty lies largely in this public finance function: it allows governments to exercise authority through institutions they fund, a theme developed in Chapter 139.

Evidence for these uses comes primarily from tribal budgets, annual reports, and NIGC aggregate data, but the available information is uneven. Not every tribe publishes detailed financial information, and published reports reflect the government's own presentation. Independent studies, such as those discussed in the next sections, supplement tribal reporting but cover a small number of nations.

Division IV · Section 122.15

Per Capita Payments, Tribal Citizenship, and the Distribution of Gaming Benefits

A per capita payment is a distribution of tribal gaming revenue directly to individual tribal members. Under IGRA, a tribe may make such payments only if it has a revenue allocation plan approved by the Secretary of the Interior, if the plan provides for the funding of governmental and community purposes first, and if it protects the interests of minors and legally incompetent persons, typically by placing their shares in trust. Payments are subject to federal income tax, and tribes must notify members of that obligation. The arrangement is optional. Per capita payments are not an entitlement under IGRA, and many gaming tribes do not make them.

Where they exist, per capita payments vary widely. A nation with a small citizenry and a large casino may distribute substantial sums; a nation with a large population and a small casino distributes little or nothing. The distinction between nations matters for public debate, in which a few highly publicized high-payment communities have shaped popular assumptions about gaming. The reality is that most tribal citizens in the United States do not receive large payments from gaming, and many receive none.

The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians offers a documented case, not a national template. Harrah's Cherokee Casino opened in 1997 in western North Carolina; tribal distributions to enrolled citizens occurred as gaming income became available and under the Nation's particular revenue policy. The Great Smoky Mountains Study had begun in 1993, before the casino opened, with longitudinal research on children in rural western North Carolina, including an American Indian subsample associated with the Eastern Band. That timing gave researchers information about children and their households before the casino-related transfer of income, permitting comparisons as economic circumstances changed. Researchers did not randomly assign casinos or income to tribes; their analysis exploited a historically occurring income change and compared groups in a defined regional sample.

Published analyses of this study, by Costello, Randall Akee, and colleagues, reported that children in families who moved out of poverty because of the income supplement showed reductions in certain behavioral and emotional symptoms, and later studies reported gains in educational attainment and some changes in personality traits associated with adult outcomes. These findings are notable because they are longitudinal and have a credible comparison. Students should also read them carefully. They concern one nation, in one region, with a particular distribution policy and a particular community context. The results show that, in this case, an unconditional income supplement was associated with benefits for children. They do not show that every per capita program yields the same results, nor do they measure outcomes for adults, or for nations without distributions.

Documentary evidence: National Academies overview and studies on the Great Smoky Mountains natural experiment

Distribution also raises questions of citizenship. Because payments depend on enrollment, disputes over who is a citizen of a nation can acquire financial significance. Some nations have disenrolled members, and in some cases public commentary has attributed disenrollment to a desire to increase the shares of remaining members. Evidence on such motives varies by case, and they are often contested within the nation. Historians should not assume that every enrollment dispute is a gaming dispute. Many arise from questions of lineage records, residency, political conflict, or tribal law that long predate gaming. Chapter 144 examines enrollment, descent, and blood quantum, and this chapter notes only that gaming has intensified attention to citizenship criteria in some communities.

Division IV · Section 122.16

Gaming, Social Welfare, Political Opposition, and Economic Inequality

Tribal gaming has attracted criticism and support on several grounds, and a careful account must separate documented findings from allegation. The strongest broadly comparative evidence concerns employment and income. Economist Laurel Wheeler's 2023 Census Bureau working paper, using confidential microdata and an identification strategy for local openings, reported improvements in employment and wages associated with tribal gaming, especially for American Indians. A 2025 Census working paper by Randall Akee, Maggie Jones, and Emilia Simeonova examined casino-related local labor-market outcomes using linked data and reported positive effects in relevant employment sectors and geographic units. These are research findings within defined designs, not proof that casinos everywhere or for every citizen caused identical results. A November 2025 Census Bureau article, citing 2024 population poverty figures, reported a 19.6 percent American Indian poverty rate against a 12.1 percent national rate. Those statistics use broad U.S. population categories and are not poverty rates for citizens of gaming tribes, nor directly comparable measures of individual casino benefit. The continued disparity illustrates why gains in some places cannot be generalized to all Indigenous communities.

Researchers have also studied adverse effects. Problem gambling is a recognized public health concern, and studies of gambling generally, including work commissioned by national bodies in the late 1990s, have found that a minority of gamblers experience serious harm, with consequences for families, finances, and mental health. Evidence specific to tribal casinos is mixed and differs by location and method. Some studies of communities near casinos have reported increases in bankruptcies or crime in some periods, and others have found little or no effect; economists continue to debate the results. Students should note that these are general findings about casino gambling and not unique to Indigenous operations. They also should recognize that tribal governments themselves have funded treatment and prevention programs and employ responsible gaming practices, though the effectiveness of those programs is not fully documented.

Local government concerns are common. Casinos increase traffic, demand for police, fire, and emergency services, and pressure on water and roads. Compacts may include payments to local governments, but local officials have sometimes argued that the payments are insufficient. Competing businesses, including racetracks, card rooms, and commercial casinos, have opposed tribal expansion on economic grounds, sometimes allied with other interests. Some opposition has rested on concerns about land taken into trust, which removes property from local tax rolls and local regulatory authority. Some has rested on arguments about fairness, and some on moral objection to gambling. Some Indigenous people have likewise opposed casinos on cultural or religious grounds, and some nations have debated the issue internally.

Political controversy has also included claims about corruption and organized crime, discussed in §122.7, and claims about the influence of gaming money on elections. Tribal governments, like other organizations, make political contributions and lobby, and their activities have drawn scrutiny. A well-known federal investigation of lobbyist Jack Abramoff in the 2000s found that he and a partner had defrauded several tribes of large sums, an example in which tribes were victims, not perpetrators. Students should read reports of such episodes critically, distinguishing proven wrongdoing from partisan accusation.

Economic inequality runs through the history. Gaming has benefited some nations enormously and others little. Within nations, benefits may reach members unevenly, depending on distribution policy, employment, and residence. Some nations with large revenue have invested heavily in services and in diversification; others have struggled. The Census Bureau authors emphasized the limits of a single-industry strategy, and the unevenness documented in NIGC data suggests that the industry has not erased differences in Indigenous economic circumstances. Economic gains and social concerns can coexist, and an honest history says so.

Everyday Life and Social History

Casino Employment, Families, and Community Services

For many families, the most immediate effect of gaming is employment. A casino may be the largest employer within a reservation or within the surrounding county. Jobs may bring regular wages, health insurance, and training in skills from accounting to surveillance, and some employees move into supervisory or managerial positions. For people previously commuting long distances or relying on seasonal work, local employment can change household routines, childcare arrangements, and the ability to remain in the community. Some tribes give preference to tribal citizens in hiring, a policy permitted under federal law for tribal governments, and many casinos also employ large numbers of non-Native workers from nearby towns.

Casino work is also demanding. Shift schedules, nights and weekends, and customer-service pressure shape family life. Wages vary by job, and some positions rely on tips. Employees may hold the same community and professional ties as their customers, a source of both support and awkwardness in small communities. Unionization has occurred at some tribal casinos and been contested at others, raising questions about the application of federal labor law to tribal governments that this chapter does not resolve.

Government services funded by gaming also reach households directly: clinics, elder meals, housing assistance, youth programs, and scholarships. Where per capita payments exist, they become part of household budgets, with effects documented in research like the Cherokee study. Social harms reach households too: gambling problems may strain families, and community members have debated how to address them. Evidence from these experiences comes from tribal reports, interviews, academic research, and journalism; it is unevenly distributed across nations, and voices of workers and families are less well documented than those of leaders and executives.

Division V · Section 122.17

Gaming Revenue as Capital for Economic Diversification

The central analytical distinction of this division is between gaming as a revenue source and gaming as a source of investment capital. A revenue source supports current governmental spending. Investment capital is money used to build assets that produce income in the future. A government that spends all of its gaming income on present services depends on gaming continuing; a government that invests part of it in other enterprises can, in principle, reduce that dependence and generate revenue across generations. Many leaders have framed this as a priority, and the pandemic closures of 2020 and the emergence of new competitors intensified the argument for it.

Diversification has taken many forms. Some nations have invested in hospitality and real estate beyond their casinos, including hotels, golf courses, and commercial property in nearby cities. Some have built manufacturing, construction, and agricultural enterprises. Some have purchased or founded banks and financial institutions, discussed further in Chapter 121. Some have used federal contracting programs available to Indigenous-owned firms to build companies that provide services to federal agencies. Some have invested in technology, energy, and health care. Others have bought businesses outside their traditional regions to spread risk. Chapter 121 introduced the enterprise histories of the Cherokee Nation, whose holdings through Cherokee Nation Businesses include entertainment, manufacturing, and government services, and of the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska, whose economic development corporation, Ho-Chunk, Inc., built holdings in real estate, construction, and contracting. In each case, gaming revenue contributed to the capital base from which other enterprises grew, though neither history can be reduced to gaming alone.

The Seminole Tribe of Florida's acquisition of Hard Rock International provides the most prominent example of Indigenous commercial expansion into international markets. In 2007 the tribe completed its purchase of the Hard Rock brand and its portfolio of cafés, hotels, and casinos, for roughly $965 million. The Seminole Tribe, whose total population is a few thousand, thereby took ownership of a company with operations across many countries. Through Hard Rock, the tribe developed new properties and licensed its brand, and it later built the guitar-shaped Hard Rock hotel at its Hollywood, Florida casino. The acquisition showed how a gaming government could use capital and borrowing capacity to enter global hospitality and entertainment markets. It also showed the risks: the purchase involved considerable debt, and the enterprise depended on tourism and consumer demand.

Documentary evidence: Seminole Hard Rock acquisition, completed 2007

Diversification has constraints. Many nations lack the capital to invest at scale. Investment requires management expertise and governance structures that insulate business decisions from short-term political pressures, a challenge for governments with frequent elections and many competing demands. Tribal governments must decide how much of their revenue to reinvest and how much to spend on present needs, a decision that involves tradeoffs between current services and future security. Some nations have created separate corporations with independent boards to manage investments, while others keep enterprises under direct council control. The right approach depends on the nation's institutions, and the historical record includes both successes and failures.

A further point concerns inheritance. Because tribal governments are perpetual institutions, their time horizon differs from that of most private firms. Some leaders have described diversification in terms of obligations to future generations, and some nations have created permanent funds or trusts to hold part of their revenue. These choices connect economic strategy to sovereignty: a nation with durable assets across multiple industries is less vulnerable to changes in federal, state, or market conditions than one dependent on a single revenue stream.

Division V · Section 122.18

First Nations Gaming and Provincial Revenue-Sharing Agreements in Canada

Canada's system differs fundamentally from the American one. There is no federal statute resembling IGRA, and Canadian courts have not recognized a body of jurisdictional doctrine parallel to Cabazon. Gambling is governed by the federal Criminal Code, which prohibits gaming and betting in general, with exceptions. Section 207 of the Code allows a provincial government to conduct and manage lotteries and other gaming schemes in its province and to license charitable and religious organizations to conduct certain schemes. Gaming is therefore a provincial matter, and First Nations gaming has developed through agreements with provinces, not through independent tribal authority recognized in federal law. The Canadian constitutional framework for Aboriginal and treaty rights, examined in Chapter 117, has not been interpreted to give First Nations a gaming right of the kind American tribes asserted.

Provinces have authorized First Nations gaming through varied agreements. In Ontario, Casino Rama opened in 1996 on land connected to the Mnjikaning (Rama) First Nation under an arrangement involving provincial gaming management and broader First Nations sharing. The allocation of proceeds and the host community's claim to a distinctive entitlement became subjects of legal and political conflict; the Ontario courts considered disputes involving the host and other First Nations. It would therefore be misleading to depict Casino Rama's distribution as an uncontested formula accepted from the outset. Manitoba and Alberta developed different licensing, ownership, and revenue arrangements, demonstrating that Canadian First Nations gaming has not followed one provincial model.

Saskatchewan provides an especially instructive institutional case. The Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority (SIGA), a First Nations–owned, nonprofit corporation created in the mid-1990s, operates casinos under agreements between the provincial government and the Federation of Sovereign Indigenous Nations (FSIN). A 2002 Gaming Framework Agreement sets the terms for First Nations casino operations, has been periodically amended, and is distinct from the American tribal–state compact system. Under SIGA's published governance framework, net profits from its casino operations are distributed 50 percent to Saskatchewan First Nations through the First Nations Trust, 25 percent to community development corporations, and 25 percent to Saskatchewan's General Revenue Fund. These percentages apply to distributed net casino profits, not to gross gaming receipts, customer wagers, or total operating revenue. The corporations fund projects in communities around casino locations, while the trust supports priorities selected through First Nations institutions; provincial allocation and slot-machine ceilings remain matters of negotiation.

Online gaming follows a separate distribution rule. SIGA launched Saskatchewan's PlayNow online platform in November 2022 under the updated provincial framework. Under SIGA's reported terms, online-gaming net profits are divided 50 percent to First Nations through the First Nations Trust and 50 percent to the provincial General Revenue Fund; the 25 percent community-development allocation for casinos does not apply to that online stream. On August 31, 2026, the Saskatchewan government and FSIN announced a further amendment continuing SIGA's exclusivity as the province's licensed online gaming provider until September 2029, allowing a pilot gaming centre with up to 49 slot machines on Flying Dust First Nation, and increasing SIGA's provincial slot-machine allocation to 2,619. These measures were announced as agreed by the province and chiefs in assembly; they illustrate how growth depends on continuing intergovernmental consent.

Comparison with the United States clarifies the stakes. Under IGRA, a tribe has statutory authority to operate gaming on its lands, subject to compacts. In Saskatchewan, a First Nations–owned authority operates under a province's decision to authorize, with revenue shared with the province and exclusivity set by agreement. American tribes retain larger shares of revenue but face state negotiation and federal oversight. Canadian First Nations in the Saskatchewan model gain exclusivity and institutional coordination across 74 First Nations but share a significant portion of revenue with the province and depend on provincial consent to expand. Each model reflects its own constitutional and political history, and neither is simply better. Students should also resist assuming that agreements are uniform across Canada.

Mexico requires brief and careful comparison. Gambling in Mexico is governed by federal law and permits, and no framework like IGRA or the Saskatchewan agreement recognizes Indigenous governments as gaming regulators. Indigenous communities in Mexico have developed other community economic institutions, including communal land enterprises, forestry cooperatives, and tourism ventures, and Mexico's 2024 constitutional reform, noted in Chapter 1, recognizes Indigenous peoples and communities as subjects of public law. This chapter does not claim an equivalent tribal gaming system for Mexico where none has been established.

Division V · Section 122.19

Sports Betting, Online Gaming, and Contemporary Jurisdictional Challenges

On May 14, 2018, the Supreme Court decided Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Association, which invalidated the federal Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act, a 1992 statute that had barred most states from authorizing sports betting. The Court held that the law improperly commandeered state legislatures. After Murphy, states could legalize sports wagering, and many did, including mobile and online wagering through commercial operators. The decision created new opportunities for tribes in states that authorized sports betting and new competition, since commercial online platforms could reach customers outside casinos.

Tribes responded in different ways. Many added in-person sportsbooks at casinos under compact amendments. Some negotiated for mobile wagering. The most closely watched case was Florida. In 2021 the Seminole Tribe and Florida negotiated a new compact that, among other terms, authorized the tribe to offer sports betting throughout the state through servers on tribal land, with bets placed by users anywhere in Florida "deemed" to occur at the server location. State estimates projected multi-billion-dollar payments to Florida over the following years. The Secretary of the Interior allowed the compact to take effect, and competitors challenged it.

In November 2021, a federal district court in West Flagler Associates v. Haaland vacated the Secretary's decision, reasoning that IGRA authorizes gaming only on Indian lands and that the compact attempted to authorize betting off those lands. In June 2023, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit reversed. It reasoned that IGRA regulates gaming on Indian lands, and that the compact's provision about where bets occur allocated jurisdiction between the state and the tribe rather than independently authorizing off-reservation gaming; whether a wager placed off tribal lands is lawful depends on state law, which Florida had authorized for the tribe. The court denied rehearing in September 2023, the Supreme Court declined to stay the ruling in November 2023, the Seminole Tribe launched its statewide mobile sports betting program on December 7, 2023, and the Supreme Court denied review on June 17, 2024.

Documentary evidence: West Flagler Associates v. Haaland (D.C. Cir. 2023)

The Department of the Interior's compact regulations, finalized in February 2024 and codified at 25 C.F.R. Part 293, addressed related questions. The Department's rule explained how it would review compact provisions, including those allocating jurisdiction over wagers. The Florida litigation and the regulations together indicate that, as of late 2026, federal authority approves what happens on Indian lands, while state law determines whether wagering transactions that originate elsewhere within a state may be offered; students should consult the regulation text for its exact scope. The legal distinction is between federal authorization of gaming on Indian lands and state authorization of wagering outside those lands.

Documentary evidence: 25 C.F.R. Part 293, 2024 revised compact regulations · Official February 21, 2024 rule

A newer controversy concerns prediction markets: federally regulated derivatives-market platforms that list contracts on event outcomes, including sports. The dispute engages two bodies of law whose functions can overlap without being identical. Tribal governments contend that sports-related event contracts entered by persons on their Indian lands constitute unauthorized Class III gaming contrary to IGRA and tribal ordinances. Platform operators contend that the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's federal-market jurisdiction govern the contracts and can displace conflicting state or tribal regulation. Whether a contract is a federally regulated derivative does not itself answer every separate question under IGRA; conversely, an IGRA-based ruling about activity on Indian lands does not determine all state-law disputes across the country.

In Ho-Chunk Nation v. Kalshi on May 11, 2026, a federal district judge in Wisconsin considered the Ho-Chunk Nation's request to stop sports-event contracts accessible on its land. The judge concluded that the Nation showed a likelihood of success on its IGRA theory but denied preliminary injunctive relief because immediate irreparable harm had not been sufficiently demonstrated. The case was not decided on final merits. On September 11, 2026, the same court certified a controlling legal question for interlocutory appellate review. Separately, in KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad, the Ninth Circuit on August 28, 2026 rejected the platform's bid for a preliminary injunction against Nevada's state gaming regulators. That proceeding concerned CEA preemption of state regulation; it did not itself adjudicate a tribe's territorial jurisdiction.

A distinct tribal-land dispute reached the Ninth Circuit in Blue Lake Rancheria v. Kalshi. On September 16, 2026, the court held that Blue Lake Rancheria and Chicken Ranch Rancheria of Me-Wuk Indians were likely to succeed on their IGRA claim regarding specified sports-event contracts entered from their Indian lands. It reversed the denial of preliminary relief in part and remanded for further proceedings. It did not issue a final merits judgment or automatically grant every form of relief sought. On remand, on November 4, 2026, U.S. District Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley entered a preliminary injunction ordering Kalshi Inc. and KalshiEX not to offer sports-event contracts on the plaintiff tribes' Indian lands, with boundary maps to be supplied and a compliance report due within 21 days. The order is geographically limited, provisional, and directed to the Kalshi defendants named in it, rather than a nationwide prohibition of prediction markets or a final adjudication. The litigation also involved changing party participation, including other tribes at different stages; the appellate holding specifically addressed the two named tribal appellants.

As of the chapter's November 22, 2026 evidence cutoff, the broader relationship among the CEA, IGRA, tribal ordinances, and state gambling law remained actively contested. Preliminary injunctions and likelihood-of-success findings are not final nationwide rules; holdings from one circuit do not automatically resolve every distinct statute, territory, or factual arrangement. Students consulting this chapter in later years must check court dockets for changes. What is historically significant here is not a betting strategy but an institutional encounter: the territorial law of sovereign Indigenous governments is being tested by technology built to operate across jurisdictional boundaries, just as earlier gaming innovations tested the balance between tribal authority and other governments.

The Historical Legacy and Future of the Tribal Gaming Economy

The progression traced in this chapter can be stated briefly. In the 1970s, tribal governments searched for independent revenue. In the 1980s, the Seminole and Cabazon cases established that states could not simply apply their regulatory laws to tribal gaming. In 1988, Congress built a federal framework around that outcome. In the 1990s, compacts and destination resorts expanded the industry and exposed the limits of tribal leverage after Seminole Tribe v. Florida. In the 2000s, revenue funded public investment and, for some nations, diversification. From the 2010s to 2026, new competition from sports betting, online platforms, and prediction markets has produced renewed jurisdictional disputes.

Historical Debate

Has Tribal Gaming Strengthened Indigenous Sovereignty?

The question: Does the history of gaming show growing Indigenous sovereignty, or has the industry's dependence on federal law, state agreements, and markets limited it? Commercial success and sovereignty are not the same thing, and historians disagree about how to weigh them.

Interpretation A — Economic self-determination: Gaming generated independent revenue, employment, and public investment. Governments with their own funds can set priorities, build institutions, and negotiate from greater strength. Revenue has financed health, housing, education, and cultural programs, and the industry's growth reflects Indigenous initiative in asserting jurisdiction and building enterprises.
Interpretation B — Regulatory and structural limitations: Gaming remains dependent on federal legislation, state negotiations, market access, and geographic advantage. IGRA gave states a role they had lost, Seminole Tribe weakened tribal leverage, and new technologies challenge exclusivity. The benefits are unevenly distributed, and tribes with small markets gain little. On this view the framework channels sovereignty into forms Congress and states can accept.
Interpretation C — Diversification and community development: Gaming is most valuable when its earnings support long-term investment and collective prosperity. Sovereignty depends less on casino revenue than on whether governments convert it into durable institutions and diversified assets; success is measured by what is built from the revenue.

Evidence: Court opinions, IGRA's text, compacts, NIGC revenue data, tribal budgets, and research on employment and household outcomes all inform the interpretations. They often support different readings because they measure different things: law, revenue, institutions, or welfare.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than evidence: what counts as sovereignty, how to weigh revenue against constraint, and which time horizon to use.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether gaming is seen as a model, a compromise, or a partial solution, and which policies, such as compact reform or support for diversification, seem most important.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Benefits from Tribal Gaming?

Tribal leaders: Gaming provides government revenue that funds services and institutions, and compact or federal restrictions are seen as constraints on an inherent right of self-government. Some emphasize protecting exclusivity; others stress diversification.
Tribal citizens: Experiences vary widely. Some receive per capita payments, scholarships, or services; others see little direct benefit, have concerns about governance or enrollment, or object to gambling on cultural grounds.
Casino employees: Jobs provide wages and benefits, often in communities with few alternatives, but they involve service-sector conditions and variable pay. Many employees are not tribal citizens.
Gaming regulators: Tribal commissions and the NIGC emphasize integrity, internal controls, and compliance, and see strong regulation as protecting both the public and tribal governments' credibility.
State governments: Many value compact revenue and regulatory participation; some resist expansion or off-reservation development. State officials differ by party, region, and the structure of their own gaming markets.
Neighboring municipalities and competing businesses: Local governments weigh jobs and payments against infrastructure costs and the loss of property from local tax rolls. Competitors, including commercial casinos, argue about fair competition and market access.
Public-health advocates: They emphasize problem gambling and prevention, and they raise concerns that apply to gambling in general rather than to tribal operations alone.

Distinguishing disagreements: Some disputes concern economic benefit (who gains and loses). Others concern sovereignty and jurisdiction (who has authority to decide). The two overlap but are not identical, and students should ask which kind of disagreement is at issue.

Cause and Effect

From Tribal Bingo to a Multibillion-Dollar Industry

Long-term causes

Reservation economies shaped by allotment, trust restrictions, and federal administration; thin revenue bases; the self-determination policy of the 1960s and 1970s; and the unsettled jurisdictional reach of Public Law 280.

Short-term causes and trigger

Indigenous political initiative in opening high-stakes bingo; state attempts at enforcement; state legalization of lotteries and charitable gaming; and court cases applying the prohibitory/regulatory distinction.

Event or transformation

The Cabazon decision and the 1988 Indian Gaming Regulatory Act created a legal pathway, followed by compacts, financing, and resort development in the 1990s.

Immediate effects

Rapid growth in employment and revenue for some nations; new state roles through compacts; and new federal regulation through the NIGC.

Long-term consequences

Greater public finance capacity, diversification for some nations, uneven distribution of benefits, dependence on a single industry, and renewed jurisdictional conflict over sports betting and digital gaming.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Fiscal need explains motivation but not legality; law explains opportunity but not commercial success; location and market explain why results differ. Contingency mattered: state laws, court composition, and political choices might have produced different results.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Tribal Economic Authority Before and After IGRA

What Changed

  • Some tribal governments gained substantial independent revenue where before they had little.
  • Regulatory institutions, including tribal commissions and the NIGC, became part of tribal governance.
  • Compacts created regular negotiation between tribes and states, and tribal governments became significant participants in state politics.
  • Some nations built diversified enterprise portfolios and international businesses.

What Stayed the Same

  • Federal oversight and approval remained central to tribal economic activity.
  • State authority, including the power to withhold compacts and to concur in land acquisitions, continued to shape outcomes.
  • Economic inequality among and within Indigenous nations persisted, and poverty rates remained higher than national averages.
  • The claim to self-government that motivated early gaming continued as the basis for later disputes.

Pattern and experience: Change was uneven and came in stages, driven by litigation and legislation rather than gradual drift. Nations with large markets experienced the most dramatic change; nations without gaming experienced greater continuity of earlier constraints. Recognizing both prevents two errors: treating gaming as a universal transformation or as a trivial change.

Evaluating the legacy requires weighing evidence rather than choosing a slogan. Gaming has strengthened the fiscal capacity and institutions of many Indigenous governments, as employment studies, public investment, and enterprise histories indicate. It has not erased inequality, and its benefits remain concentrated in a relatively small number of large operations. It depends on consumer demand, on regulatory arrangements set by governments other than tribes, and on legal conditions still being contested. Some of its effects, particularly social harms, remain incompletely documented.

Three comparisons clarify what “success” requires. The Seminole acquisition of Hard Rock shows how a government with substantial operating cash flow can mobilize capital, assume commercial risk, and build businesses beyond reservation gaming. The Eastern Band of Cherokee experience shows that distributions to households can matter independently of casino jobs or national industry receipts, but only under a particular policy and social setting. The contrast between major market-adjacent casinos and small rural operations demonstrates why gross industry growth is not evidence of equal fiscal autonomy among nations. The legal right to operate, the size and stability of the market, the share of operating receipts ultimately available for public investment, and the institutional choices governing spending are separate variables. Their interaction determines whether gaming strengthens a nation’s governing capacity over time.

The Canadian comparison adds another dimension. Saskatchewan’s First Nations gaming arrangements distribute net profits under a provincial framework that allocates shares to First Nations, community development corporations, and the province itself. American Indian gaming generally rests on a federal statute that places tribal governments closer to the legal center of operations, yet still subjects Class III gaming to compact bargaining, federal regulation, and contested judicial boundaries. Neither framework removes economic dependence: tourism demand can weaken, online competitors can change consumer behavior, and public-service budgets can become vulnerable when a large share of them rests on a single enterprise. Prudent investment, effective oversight, transparent public finance, and enterprises outside gaming can reduce some risks, though they cannot abolish market uncertainty. The enduring historical question is therefore not only how much revenue an industry produced, but how Indigenous governments used a conditional commercial opportunity to exercise authority, support households, protect future generations, and maintain choices when the external legal and market environment changed.

The chapter's principal historical proposition follows from this record. The enduring significance of tribal gaming lies not simply in the number of casinos established or the revenue they generate, but in the capacity of Indigenous nations to transform commercial opportunities into durable governmental institutions, community prosperity, and economic self-determination. Where that capacity has been realized, it has rested on tribal governance, legal assertion, and long-term investment. Where it has not, the cause has often been market, legal, or geographic limits rather than the absence of effort. The chapters that follow, on language revitalization (Chapter 123) and educational institutions (Chapter 124), show some of what gaming-supported governments have chosen to build.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that tribal gaming was not merely the arrival of a new entertainment industry. It grew from Indigenous governments seeking independent revenue, asserting jurisdiction over their lands, and building commercial institutions to finance self-government. Its legal foundations were established through litigation before Congress legislated, and its later history has been shaped by the tension between tribal initiative and the federal and state structures built around it.

The starting conditions were fiscal. Reservation economies shaped by allotment, trust restrictions, and federal administration left tribal governments with limited capital and thin tax bases. Self-determination policy expanded their responsibilities without guaranteeing revenue. Indigenous games and wagering had a long history, but modern commercial gaming did not descend from a single tradition, and its legal basis rests on governmental authority and statute rather than cultural continuity alone.

Early tribal gaming emerged through political initiative. The Seminole Tribe's high-stakes bingo in 1979 and the Butterworth decision of 1981 applied the distinction between criminal prohibition and civil regulation under Public Law 280. The Supreme Court confirmed that distinction in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians (1987), recognizing both the limits of state power and the significance of tribal revenue, while not authorizing every form of gambling on every reservation.

Congress responded with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, a compromise that gave tribal gaming a statutory basis and federal oversight. It created three classes of gaming, the National Indian Gaming Commission, a requirement for compacts governing Class III gaming, and rules limiting gaming to Indian lands, with restrictions on lands acquired after 1988. Indigenous leaders differed on whether the statute protected or constrained sovereignty, a disagreement that historians still debate.

The 1990s brought compacts, large-scale development, and constitutional limits. Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida (1996) held that Congress could not subject nonconsenting states to the particular federal lawsuit IGRA authorized, weakening tribal bargaining leverage while leaving the statutory duty to negotiate in place. Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun showed what destination resorts could achieve in favorable markets, but their advantages were not widely available. Gaming expanded across many states, concentrated in a limited number of large facilities.

The economic evidence demands precise reading. The NIGC reported $46.2 billion in gross gaming revenue for fiscal year 2025, from 545 establishments operated by 246 tribes in 29 states, a record and an increase of 5.3 percent. This is gross revenue, not profit or distributions. A relatively small number of properties generate a majority of it, and regional results differ. Net revenue supports government operations, general welfare, economic development, charity, and local government, and some nations make per capita payments under approved plans while many do not. Research on employment, wages, and, in the Eastern Band of Cherokee case, child outcomes shows documented benefits, while concerns about problem gambling, local costs, and dependence on a single industry remain.

Diversification marks an important phase. Some nations have used gaming income as investment capital in other sectors, with the Seminole Tribe's 2007 acquisition of Hard Rock International the most prominent example. Canada's arrangements differ: gaming is provincial, and First Nations operate through agreements such as Saskatchewan's Gaming Framework Agreement, amended on August 31, 2026 to extend SIGA's online exclusivity to September 2029. Mexico has no comparable tribal gaming framework.

Since 2018, sports betting, online gaming, and prediction markets have produced new disputes. The West Flagler litigation tested whether a compact could support statewide mobile wagering, and the Supreme Court's June 2024 denial of review left the D.C. Circuit's ruling in place. Prediction market litigation, including decisions in 2026, remains unsettled as of November 2026. These disputes show that the framework built in 1988 is still being tested.

Taken together, the history shows that gaming strengthened the fiscal capacity and institutions of many Indigenous governments while leaving federal and state constraints, geographic inequality, and market dependence in place. Its significance rests on what nations built from it. The enduring significance of tribal gaming lies in the capacity of Indigenous nations to transform commercial opportunities into durable governmental institutions, community prosperity, and economic self-determination.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Public Law 280
A 1953 federal statute transferring criminal jurisdiction, and some civil jurisdiction, over reservation lands to certain states.
Criminal/prohibitory law
A state law whose general purpose is to forbid conduct as contrary to public policy; enforceable on reservations in PL 280 states.
Civil/regulatory law
A state law that permits conduct subject to conditions; not enforceable against tribes through PL 280 under Cabazon.
Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA)
The 1988 federal statute that established the framework for tribal gaming, including gaming classes, compacts, and the NIGC.
Class I gaming
Ceremonial gaming and social games for minimal prizes, regulated exclusively by tribes.
Class II gaming
Bingo, related games, and certain nonbanking card games, regulated by tribes with federal oversight.
Class III gaming
All other gaming, including slot machines and banking card games; ordinarily requires a tribal-state compact.
Tribal-state compact
A negotiated agreement between a tribe and a state governing Class III gaming, reviewed by the Secretary of the Interior.
National Indian Gaming Commission
The federal regulatory body created by IGRA to oversee tribal gaming and publish industry data.
Tribal gaming commission
A tribal governmental regulator, distinct from the enterprise, that licenses and inspects gaming operations.
Management contract
An agreement under which an outside company manages a gaming operation, subject to NIGC approval and statutory limits.
Indian lands
Lands within reservations, and trust or restricted lands over which a tribe exercises governmental power; the only lands on which IGRA permits gaming.
Restored lands exception
An IGRA exception allowing gaming on lands taken into trust for a tribe whose recognition was terminated and later restored.
Sovereign immunity (state)
The constitutional principle limiting suits against nonconsenting states in federal court, central to Seminole Tribe v. Florida.
Revenue sharing
Payments from a tribe to a state or other bodies under a compact, lawful where tied to a meaningful state concession.
Gross gaming revenue
Amounts wagered minus prizes paid, before expenses; not profit.
Net gaming revenue
Revenue remaining after operating costs and other expenses, available for statutorily authorized uses.
Revenue allocation plan
A federally approved plan governing per capita payments of gaming revenue to tribal members.
Per capita payment
A direct distribution of gaming revenue to individual tribal citizens; optional, taxable, and not universal.
Destination resort
A large casino complex with hotels, entertainment, and conference facilities that draws customers from a wide region.
Diversification
Investment of revenue in businesses beyond the original industry to reduce dependence and build long-term assets.
Gaming Framework Agreement
The Saskatchewan agreement between the province and the FSIN setting the terms for First Nations gaming.
Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority (SIGA)
A First Nations–owned corporation operating casinos and the province's online platform.
Section 207, Criminal Code (Canada)
The provision permitting provincial governments to conduct and manage gaming and to license certain charitable gaming.
Murphy v. NCAA (2018)
The Supreme Court decision invalidating the federal ban on most state-authorized sports betting.
Deemed-location provision
A compact term treating wagers placed within a state as occurring at the tribal server; at issue in West Flagler.
Prediction market
A federally regulated exchange offering contracts on the outcomes of events; the subject of tribal litigation under IGRA.
Natural experiment
A research setting in which an unplanned event creates comparable groups, as in the Eastern Band of Cherokee income study.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a legal assertion became an institution. Indigenous governments that claimed authority over their own lands defended it in court, built enterprises, and used the revenue to fund public life. The story is a distinctive episode in the larger history of Indigenous self-determination traced across Volume X: sovereignty not only declared but financed.

It also reveals the limits of that process. Federal statutes, state negotiations, constitutional doctrine, and market conditions all shaped what was possible. The unevenness of results, from large urban resorts to small rural facilities and from nations with casinos to nations without, warns against treating gaming as a universal solution. The record of employment gains and public investment is real, and so are the concerns about social harm and dependence.

Later developments in this volume build on these foundations. Language revitalization (Chapter 123), tribal colleges and educational sovereignty (Chapter 124), and the institutional capacity of contemporary governments (Chapter 139) all draw on resources that gaming helped create for some nations. Disputes over online wagering, treaty-based exclusivity, and technology connect to the broader questions of jurisdiction examined in Chapter 118 and Indigenous knowledge and data sovereignty in Chapter 145.

Students should leave with a method as well as a narrative: read revenue figures precisely, distinguish a legal pathway from a guarantee, ask whose evidence is available, and judge sovereignty by what governments build and control rather than by the size of an industry. Those habits will serve the rest of this textbook.
