Chapter Opening

In 1663 a Bible was printed in Massachusetts in an Indigenous language of the Atlantic coast, the first complete Bible printed in the Americas. More than three centuries later, no Wampanoag community had living first-language speakers of that language, and members of those communities decided to bring it back anyway.

The book printed in 1663, usually called the Eliot Bible after the English missionary John Eliot, was produced in the Massachusett language with the labor of Indigenous collaborators. The language is known to its descendants as Wôpanâak, and it is closely related to the speech of the Wampanoag peoples of southeastern New England. For historians of language, the Bible is one document among thousands. Wampanoag writers also used the Roman alphabet to produce deeds, petitions, wills, and church records in their own language across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. When the last generations of fluent speakers died, that written record remained. In 1993, Wampanoag women and men began using it to reconstruct the language, and in the 2010s children were again being taught in Wôpanâak. This chapter returns to that case, but the case is also a way into a larger question: what does it take to make an Indigenous language live again, and who has the authority to decide?

Chapter 123 studies the effort to preserve, reclaim, and revitalize Indigenous languages across North America. It is placed in Part XXI, "Economic and Cultural Resurgence," because language revitalization belongs to the same era of Indigenous self-determination as the economic development discussed in Chapters 121 and 122 and the educational and artistic movements of Chapters 124 and 126. The geographic frame follows the textbook's continental scope. The chapter treats the United States, Canada, Mexico, the Arctic regions of Alaska, northern Canada, and Greenland, and the many Indigenous communities whose languages cross the borders among them.

The chapter's central argument is that language revitalization is not simply the preservation of endangered vocabulary. A dictionary can record words, and an archive can hold a recording, but neither makes a language live. A language lives when people speak it to one another, when children hear it from adults who value it, and when institutions such as families, ceremonies, councils, schools, broadcasters, and governments create reasons to use it. Revitalization is therefore the restoration of relationships among generations, communities, knowledge systems, and institutions. This is why the movement is inseparable from questions of sovereignty: communities that control their own schools, budgets, archives, and public life are able to make their languages useful in daily affairs.

The chapter also takes a historical rather than a technical approach. Chapters 87 and 88 examined boarding and residential schools and their effects on Indigenous children. Chapter 123 does not repeat that institutional history, but it does explain the linguistic consequences of those policies and of other pressures such as relocation, wage labor, and mass media, and then traces the recovery. The story begins with disruption of a particular kind, the breaking of the chain by which parents and grandparents spoke to children, and it moves through the decades of organized response beginning in the 1960s and 1970s, the legal and institutional frameworks of the 1990s and after, and the digital tools of the twenty-first century.

The evidence for this history is varied and uneven. Historians draw on census and survey data, which count speakers in ways that differ among countries and that often miss proficiency; on legislation and government reports; on the records of schools and language programs; on archival manuscripts, recordings, grammars, and dictionaries; on the published testimony of survivors and elders; and on the accounts of language activists themselves. Each of these sources has limits. Statistics, for example, can show that a language has fewer speakers, but they cannot show whether a child who counts as a speaker uses the language to joke, argue, pray, or manage a household. Where numbers are discussed in this chapter they are given as approximate, and the sources of disagreement are noted.

Finally, the chapter avoids two opposite errors. One treats Indigenous languages as relics doomed to disappear, a narrative that ignores the many communities where languages are spoken daily and the substantial growth of learner populations. The other treats revitalization as a success story already won, a narrative that ignores the aging of first-language speakers, the shortage of teachers, uncertain funding, and the fact that many languages remain in grave danger. The history in this chapter is one of loss, resistance, invention, disagreement, and uneven achievement, and it is still unfolding.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the major language families of Indigenous North America and explain why language boundaries seldom correspond to modern political borders.
  2. Explain how Indigenous languages carry kinship systems, place knowledge, ceremonial practice, and political concepts, without treating the languages as culturally interchangeable.
  3. Analyze the several causes of language shift, including schooling policy, relocation, economic pressure, and mass media, and distinguish them from the institutional history of Chapters 87 and 88.
  4. Trace the movement from family and ceremonial survival of languages to organized community-based revitalization from the 1960s onward.
  5. Evaluate the roles of elders, fluent speakers, archives, and outside scholars in language documentation and transmission, including questions of consent and control.
  6. Explain how the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project reconstructed a language without living first-language speakers, and assess what this case shows about community authority.
  7. Compare language nests, immersion schools, and mentor–apprentice programs as methods of building new speakers, identifying the strengths and limits of each.
  8. Distinguish classroom acquisition from sustained community-wide language use in homes, councils, ceremonies, and workplaces.
  9. Compare the legal frameworks for Indigenous languages in the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland, and explain the difference between statutory recognition and actual resources.
  10. Assess the opportunities and risks of broadcasting, publishing, and digital technology, including questions of data sovereignty and unauthorized use.
  11. Interpret measures of language vitality critically, recognizing what speaker counts can and cannot show.
  12. Connect language revitalization to Indigenous sovereignty, community institutions, and cultural continuity across future generations.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Indigenous languages lose speakers so rapidly in the twentieth century when they had persisted through earlier centuries of colonization?
  2. What does it mean to say that language revitalization restores relationships and not just vocabulary?
  3. How did communities with no living first-language speakers reconstruct and relearn their languages, and what kinds of evidence made that possible?
  4. Who should hold authority over spelling, teaching materials, recordings, and digital data: elders, program staff, tribal governments, universities, or national agencies?
  5. Why have language nests, immersion schools, and mentor–apprentice programs each emerged as distinct strategies, and what problem was each meant to solve?
  6. How do the legal frameworks of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland differ, and why does legal recognition not by itself produce speakers?
  7. In what ways have broadcasting and digital technologies expanded the places where Indigenous languages are used, and what new dangers do they introduce?
  8. How should historians and communities measure success in language revitalization, given the limits of census and survey data?
  9. In what sense is language revitalization an exercise of political sovereignty, and in what sense does it depend on factors outside the control of Indigenous governments?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter need four pieces of background from earlier in the textbook. First, Indigenous North America was linguistically extraordinary. Chapter 1 introduced the major language families, and Volume III described the peoples and homelands where those languages were spoken. Many societies were multilingual, and language was woven into trade, diplomacy, and kinship long before sustained European colonization.

Second, colonization introduced European languages as languages of power: Spanish in New Spain and later Mexico, French and English in Canada, English in the United States, and Danish in Greenland. Missionaries often learned and printed Indigenous languages in order to convert people, and Indigenous people adopted writing for their own purposes, including the Cherokee syllabary of the 1820s discussed in Chapter 64. Colonial regimes were therefore not uniformly hostile to Indigenous-language writing. The decisive shift came with the assimilation era treated in Chapters 85 through 88, when governments built schooling and administrative systems aimed at replacing Indigenous languages with national ones.

Third, the twentieth century brought changes that undermined the use of Indigenous languages even in communities that had resisted schooling policy: relocation and urbanization (Chapters 104 and 105), wage labor, military service (Chapters 101 and 102), radio and television, and the growing dominance of national languages in public life. Language loss was the cumulative result of many pressures and not of any single policy.

Fourth, Indigenous political movements of the 1960s and 1970s, described in Chapters 108 through 114 and in the Canadian and Mexican parallels, created the conditions for language revitalization. Demands for community control of education, for self-determination, and for recognition of Indigenous rights included demands to restore the languages that were the medium of Indigenous knowledge. When Part XXI of this textbook speaks of "cultural resurgence," language is one of its central threads.

Students should also be aware of a limit on terminology. This chapter uses "language" and "dialect" in their ordinary scholarly senses, but the boundary between them is partly political, and communities may name their languages differently from outside scholars. Where possible the chapter uses names used by the communities themselves, such as Kanien'kéha for Mohawk, Diné bizaad for Navajo, Wôpanâak for the Massachusett language of the Wampanoag, Kalaallisut for Greenlandic, and Inuktut as a collective term for Inuit languages in Canada.

Indigenous Languages: Disruption and Survival

This division introduces the languages themselves, the knowledge they carry, the pressures that broke the chain of transmission, and the ways families and communities kept languages alive before the modern movement began.

Section 123.1

The Linguistic Diversity of Indigenous North America

Indigenous North America contains some of the greatest linguistic diversity found anywhere in the world. Estimates of how many languages were spoken at the time of sustained European contact vary with how languages and dialects are counted, but north of Mexico they are commonly placed in the low hundreds, and in Mexico today the national language institute catalogs sixty-eight Indigenous linguistic groups with several hundred recognized variants. These languages belong to dozens of unrelated families and to a number of language isolates. In the north, Eskimo–Aleut languages, a family that includes Inuit languages, Yupik languages, and Unangam Tunuu (Aleut), extend from Alaska across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland. Algonquian languages, including Cree, Ojibwe, Blackfoot, Mi'kmaw, and the Wôpanâak language of southern New England, stretch from the Atlantic to the Rocky Mountains. Athabaskan languages are spoken across the western subarctic and, through the migrations noted in Chapter 30, by the Diné and Apache peoples of the Southwest. Iroquoian languages include Mohawk and Cherokee, Siouan languages include Lakota and Dakota, Muskogean languages are spoken across the Southeast, and the Salishan, Wakashan, and many other families are concentrated along the Pacific coast. In Mexico, Uto-Aztecan languages such as Nahuatl, Mayan languages such as Yucatec Maya and K'iche', and Oto-Manguean languages such as Mixtec and Zapotec account for a large share of living speakers.

The relationship among these languages is often misunderstood. A language family is a group of languages descended from a common ancestor; it describes historical relationship, not present mutual intelligibility. Two languages in the same family may be as different from each other as English is from Russian. The distinction between a language and a dialect is also not purely linguistic. Where neighboring communities speak varieties that shade into one another along a chain, as with the Inuit varieties spoken from Alaska to Greenland or the dialects of Cree, deciding where one language ends and another begins depends partly on community identity and political circumstance. Communities often name their language and its varieties in ways that reflect their own history of relationship, and this has consequences for revitalization, since decisions about which variety to teach are decisions about which community's speech counts.

Multilingualism was common in Indigenous societies. On the Northwest Coast, in California, on the Plains, and in the Southwest, people routinely spoke several languages as a consequence of trade, marriage, diplomacy, and travel. Plains peoples with mutually unintelligible languages communicated through a widely understood sign language, and trade languages such as Chinook Jargon in the Pacific Northwest and Mobilian Jargon in the lower Mississippi Valley served commercial and diplomatic needs. This history matters because it contradicts a common assumption that language loss is simply the replacement of one language by another. Many Indigenous speakers were already skilled in multiple languages, and a number of communities treat multilingualism as a normal condition.

Language boundaries seldom correspond to modern political borders. Inuit communities share closely related language varieties across the borders of Alaska, Canada, and Greenland. Anishinaabemowin is spoken on both sides of the Canada–United States boundary in the Great Lakes region. The Mohawk community of Akwesasne straddles the international border and the boundary between Ontario, Quebec, and New York. The O'odham language is spoken in Arizona and Sonora, Kickapoo in Mexico and the United States, and Kumeyaay in California and Baja California. Continental history therefore requires attention to national frameworks as well as to speech communities that cross them, a theme returned to in Chapters 141 and 142.

Historical Vocabulary
Endangered, Dormant, and Reclaimed Languages

Definition. An endangered language is one whose use is declining and whose transmission to children is weakening or has ended. A dormant or sleeping language is one with no living first-language speakers, though documentation and community memory remain. Revitalization refers broadly to efforts to increase use of a threatened language, while reclamation usually describes work on languages that have gone dormant.

Historical context. Many communities reject the word "extinct" or "dead" because those words imply that a language cannot return and that the community has no continuing relationship with it. Terms like "sleeping language" and "reclamation" were developed in part to express this view, and they reflect the self-understanding of communities as active owners of their language rather than passive victims of its loss.

Potential misunderstanding. The categories are not sharp. A language with a handful of elderly speakers may be called critically endangered, while the same language may be taught to a growing number of second-language learners. The terms are tools for describing a spectrum of situations, and communities may choose different words for the same language.

Section 123.2

Language, Identity, and Indigenous Knowledge

Indigenous languages are not interchangeable codes for the same ideas. Each carries a particular body of knowledge built up over generations in a particular place. Kinship terminology, for example, may specify relationships that English collapses into a few words, and in doing so it encodes obligations about marriage, respect, and mutual support. Place names often record history, ecology, and law: a name may describe a feature of the landscape, remember an event, or instruct a traveler. The anthropologist Keith Basso, working with Western Apache communities, documented how place names and the stories attached to them serve as a system of moral teaching, and his work is often cited to show how a language and a landscape can be bound together. Names for plants, animals, seasons, and weather reflect close observation and are tied to practices of hunting, fishing, farming, and gathering that Chapter 133 examines as Indigenous environmental knowledge.

Languages also carry ceremonial and political knowledge. Prayers, songs, and narratives may be performed only in the language to which they belong, and some ceremonial vocabulary is not shared with outsiders. Among the Haudenosaunee, the opening address known in Mohawk as the Ohén:ton Karihwatéhkwen, often rendered as the Thanksgiving Address, is spoken in each nation's language and begins gatherings by greeting the natural world in sequence. Concepts of governance, such as those found in the Great Law of the Haudenosaunee or in the council traditions of other nations, are expressed in terms that translations render imperfectly. Oral histories, discussed in Chapter 1 as a legitimate form of historical evidence, are transmitted most fully in the language in which they were composed, and their meaning can be altered when they are translated.

It is important to be careful about how these claims are made. Scholars of language have long cautioned against the strong claim that the structure of a language determines how its speakers think. The more defensible and historically useful claim is that languages are embedded in specific practices, relationships, and institutions, and that when a language ceases to be spoken, particular forms of knowledge and particular ways of performing obligations become harder to transmit. Communities themselves often describe the connection in terms of responsibility: to ancestors who spoke the language, to children who will inherit it, and to the land and beings to which the language speaks.

For the same reason, this chapter avoids treating Indigenous languages as culturally interchangeable. A Mohawk longhouse ceremony, a Diné blessing way, a Yup'ik subsistence calendar, and a Nahuatl agricultural ritual each belong to a particular language community, and revitalization movements correspondingly differ in aims. Some communities prioritize restoring ceremonial language, because ceremonies cannot be held without it. Others emphasize everyday conversation, because they see daily use as the only way to keep the language alive. Others emphasize political uses, such as conducting council business in the language. These choices are not technical details; they are expressions of what a community believes its language is for.

Language is also tied to identity, though not in a simple way. Chapters 1 and 144 emphasize that Indigenous identity is a matter of political and community belonging and not simply a matter of ancestry or linguistic ability. Many citizens of Indigenous nations do not speak their ancestral language fluently, often because of the policies examined in the next section, and treating fluency as a test of authenticity would punish people for the consequences of colonization. At the same time, many language activists describe speaking as a form of participation in the community that its members can choose and that strengthens collective continuity. Revitalization movements generally try to hold both ideas together: that language matters deeply and that no one should be excluded from belonging because they were deprived of it.

Section 123.3

Colonial Assimilation and Language Suppression

Indigenous languages survived several centuries of colonization, and the steepest declines came in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, when states acquired the administrative capacity and the political will to reshape the language of children. In the United States, the federal government's boarding school system, described in Chapters 87 and 88, was accompanied by directives requiring English as the language of instruction. In the 1880s, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, J. D. C. Atkins, issued orders that schools serving Indigenous students, including those run by missionary organizations that received federal support, must teach in English only. These orders reflected a broader belief among policymakers that language was an obstacle to what they called civilization. Surviving testimony from former students and the findings of later inquiries describe punishments for speaking Indigenous languages, including physical punishment and humiliation, though practices varied by school, region, and decade.

In Canada, the residential school system, expanded after the Indian Act of 1876 and the compulsory attendance provisions of the early twentieth century, followed a similar logic under both church and federal administration. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, whose final report appeared in 2015, documented the prohibition of Indigenous languages in many residential schools and concluded that the system formed part of a policy of cultural genocide. The Commission's Calls to Action included specific demands concerning language, discussed in Section 123.14. Chapter 127 examines the reckoning with this history in more detail.

In Mexico, the principal tool was not a boarding system but a national project of education and citizenship. Liberal nineteenth-century governments and, after the Revolution of 1910, the federal education ministry promoted Spanish literacy as the foundation of national unity. Rural and Indigenous schools used a policy often called castellanización, the teaching of Spanish, and in many cases Indigenous children were instructed by teachers who did not speak their language. Mid-century indigenismo, the official policy of integrating Indigenous communities into national life, sometimes used bilingual promoters but treated Indigenous languages primarily as a bridge to Spanish. In Greenland, Danish colonial administration after the eighteenth century created a bilingual situation in which Greenlandic remained the daily language of most people while Danish held the positions of administrative and, increasingly, educational authority. Mid-twentieth-century modernization programs, including the reforms of the 1950s and 1960s, expanded Danish-language instruction and administration and favored Danish as the language of advancement.

Schooling was not the only force. Discrimination in public institutions such as courts, hospitals, and government offices meant that speaking an Indigenous language could be a disadvantage in dealing with the state. Relocation and urbanization, treated in Chapters 104 and 105, moved families to cities where English was the language of work and Indigenous-language communities were small and scattered. Wage labor, military service, and later radio and television reinforced the dominance of national languages. Many parents who had themselves been punished for speaking their languages chose, out of protectiveness or because they believed it would help their children, not to teach them. Language loss thus became partly a decision by parents made under pressure, not a simple imposition from above, and understanding that history is essential to treating it with respect.

Not all language contact with colonial institutions was suppressive. Missionaries learned, printed, and taught in Indigenous languages in many regions, and some missions maintained written languages that became part of later revitalization. Moravian missionaries in Greenland and Labrador, for example, produced Inuit-language materials. These contributions were made for conversion and often involved decisions about vocabulary and spelling that communities later questioned. The point is not that every colonial institution opposed Indigenous languages but that the dominant tendency of state policy, from the late nineteenth century to the mid-twentieth, was toward replacement.

Section 123.4

Linguistic Survival and the Beginnings of Organized Revitalization

Language survival in the twentieth century depended first on families. Grandparents spoke to grandchildren at home, parents used the language with each other and with neighbors, and children who attended English-language schools often returned to communities where the Indigenous language was the language of daily life. In some communities, especially those that were relatively isolated, that retained strong ceremonial institutions, or that controlled access to their ceremonies, languages stayed vital far longer than in others. Several Pueblo communities in the Southwest, for example, kept their languages closely tied to ceremonial life that was deliberately protected from outside observation. In the Arctic, in much of northern Mexico and southern Mexico, and in parts of the Northwest Coast and the subarctic, large numbers of people continued to use Indigenous languages in everyday life well into the twentieth century.

Communities also built their own institutions of literacy. The Cherokee syllabary, created by Sequoyah and widely adopted in the 1820s, supported the bilingual Cherokee Phoenix (1828), and the Cherokee continued to use the syllabary in religious, family, and community writing for generations. The Navajo Nation's publications, Inuit-language periodicals, and the Greenlandic newspaper Atuagagdliutit, first printed in 1861, are other examples of Indigenous-language print culture that preceded the modern movement. During the Indian New Deal of the 1930s, discussed in Chapter 97, some federal educators produced bilingual primers and encouraged the use of Indigenous languages in schools. Although these efforts were limited and short-lived, they showed that federal policy could change.

The modern movement for organized revitalization emerged from the political climate of the 1960s and 1970s. In 1966, the Rough Rock Demonstration School on the Navajo Nation was established as a community-controlled school that included Diné language and culture in its program, and it became a model for later community-run schools. In 1972, the National Indian Brotherhood of Canada published Indian Control of Indian Education, a policy paper that demanded parental and community authority over schooling and emphasized language and culture. The same year, the Alaska Native Language Center was established at the University of Alaska Fairbanks to document and support the state's Indigenous languages, and the U.S. Bilingual Education Act of 1968 and the Indian Education Act of 1972 offered some federal funding for programs that served Indigenous students. These measures were uneven and often designed to ease transition to English, but they gave communities a legal and financial foothold.

Communities seized the opportunity. Kahnawake Survival School was established as a community-controlled secondary school in Kahnawà:ke in 1978, and Mohawk parents founded Akwesasne Freedom School in 1979. These founding dates are not identical to the subsequent development of immersion education: Akwesasne adopted a total Mohawk-immersion curriculum in 1985, while Kahnawà:ke also developed distinct elementary-language programs. Section 123.10 examines that progression. In Greenland, the Home Rule Act that took effect in 1979 gave Greenlandic authorities responsibility for education and made Greenlandic the principal language of the territory. In northern Canada, the Inuit Broadcasting Corporation, established in 1981, began producing Inuktitut programming, a development examined in Section 123.17. In the early 1990s, as the movement gained force, linguist Michael Krauss published an influential warning about the pace of language loss worldwide, which drew international attention to the endangerment of Indigenous languages in North America.

What distinguished this early movement was its organizational form. Revitalization was no longer simply the persistence of a language in homes, nor was it the work of outside collectors of vocabulary. It was a deliberate project directed by Indigenous communities and institutions with an explicit goal: to produce new speakers. That goal required teachers, materials, funding, and political authority, and it is the subject of the next three divisions. It also required confronting an uncomfortable fact: by the time most programs began, many of the fluent speakers were elderly, and the programs had to be built in a race against time.

Division II · Sections 123.5–123.8

Reclaiming Endangered Languages

This division examines how communities turned fragmentary knowledge into usable language: through elders, archives, dictionaries, reconstruction, and the contested question of how a language should be written and standardized.

Section 123.5

Elders, Fluent Speakers, and Intergenerational Transmission

Every revitalization program begins with the same fact: the people who learned the language as children, in a community where it was the ordinary medium of life, are the principal living repositories of its sounds, grammar, idioms, humor, and ceremonial use. These first-language speakers are most often elders. In many communities they are now a small and shrinking group. The Cherokee Nation's 2019 Durbin Feeling Language Preservation legislation, for example, estimated approximately two thousand first-language Cherokee speakers at that time, many of them elders, and comparable situations exist for many languages in the United States and Canada. By contrast, some languages, including Diné bizaad, Cree, Inuktitut, Nahuatl, and Yucatec Maya, retain large speaker populations, though even for these the proportion of children learning them as first languages has declined in many places.

Intergenerational transmission, the passing of a language from parents to children in the home, is widely regarded by linguists and by community leaders as the foundation of long-term language survival, because a language that is not learned by children in the course of ordinary life requires continuous institutional support to survive. The sociolinguist Joshua Fishman, in his 1991 study of language shift, made this point central to a framework for assessing how endangered a language is and what steps might reverse its decline. Fishman's model has been criticized and adapted, but its emphasis on the family and community as the place where languages are reproduced has influenced many Indigenous programs.

Cause and Effect

The Collapse of Intergenerational Transmission

Long-term causes

State policies of assimilation, including English-only schooling, the removal of children from families, and the treatment of Indigenous languages as obstacles to progress, combined with the economic marginalization of Indigenous communities.

Short-term causes

Relocation to cities, wage labor and military service that moved people away from language communities, intermarriage across language groups, and the arrival of radio and television in the home.

Immediate context

Parents who had themselves been punished for speaking the language, or who believed English would improve their children's opportunities, spoke English to their children, often while continuing to use the Indigenous language with other adults.

Immediate effects

A generation of children who understood the language but did not speak it, or who knew only fragments, creating a gap between grandparents and grandchildren.

Long-term consequences

A shrinking, aging body of first-language speakers; loss of specialized ceremonial and environmental vocabulary; and the need for deliberate programs to rebuild transmission.

Historical complexity

The outcome varied widely. It differed with the strength of ceremonial institutions, geographic isolation, population size, local political power, and the choices of individual families. No single factor, including schooling, explains the pattern.

Why the pattern matters: If language loss resulted only from direct prohibition, ending prohibition would suffice. Because it also resulted from economic, demographic, and social changes, revitalization must create new reasons and new places for the language to be used.

The relationship between elders and revitalization programs is also a relationship of authority. In most Indigenous communities, language competence is closely connected to cultural knowledge and to moral standing, and elders are consulted not only as sources of vocabulary but as authorities on what is appropriate to say, to whom, and in what setting. Language programs that bring elders into classrooms, language nests, and recording projects therefore also have to respect protocols concerning payment, acknowledgment, and what may be shared. Some communities have established elder councils or language committees to approve materials, and programs that disregard these structures have sometimes encountered resistance.

The scarcity of speakers also creates emotional strain. Elders who endured punishment for speaking their language as children may find teaching it painful, and some are reluctant to be recorded or to correct learners they fear will make mistakes. Younger participants, for their part, may feel shame at their inability to speak and anxiety about speaking imperfectly in front of elders. Effective programs have recognized these dynamics and developed practices that make learning supportive and that give elders roles in which they feel respected rather than burdened. Many programs also have to confront the loss of elders as a recurring grief: when a fluent speaker dies, the community loses a unique voice and a body of knowledge that is difficult to replace.

In some places, speakers who were never fluent but retained partial knowledge, sometimes called semi-speakers or "rememberers," have played a significant role. They may understand a language but speak it hesitantly, or know songs, prayers, and phrases. Their knowledge is valuable and, in programs that value it, they act as bridges between fluent elders and young learners. This reflects a broader point of the chapter: language revitalization works with the full range of linguistic knowledge in a community, not only with the idealized fluent speaker.

Section 123.6

Language Documentation, Dictionaries, and Archives

Language documentation, the recording and description of a language through texts, recordings, grammars, and dictionaries, has a long and mixed history in North America. Missionaries compiled vocabularies and translated scripture from the seventeenth century onward. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, anthropologists and linguists, many working through the Smithsonian's Bureau of American Ethnology and later through university departments, collected texts and word lists, often under the belief that Indigenous cultures were vanishing and needed to be recorded before disappearing. The tradition associated with Franz Boas and his students, including Edward Sapir, produced extensive grammars and text collections. Much of this work depended on Indigenous collaborators, among them the Yankton Dakota scholar Ella Deloria, the Omaha ethnologist Francis La Flesche, and the Tuscarora scholar J. N. B. Hewitt, who were themselves authorities on their languages and contributors of the knowledge on which outsiders drew.

This documentation has proved enormously valuable, and in some cases it is the only record of a language. It also raises questions. Materials were often collected without the informed consent or control of the community, were deposited in distant institutions, and were sometimes published under the name of the collector rather than the speaker. Some contain material, such as sacred narratives, that the community considers restricted. Communities have therefore approached archival work with both gratitude and caution, a relationship echoed in the repatriation debates concerning objects and remains treated in Chapter 125.

Beginning in the late twentieth century, Indigenous communities and a new generation of linguists redefined documentation as collaborative work under community direction. The Alaska Native Language Center, founded in 1972, built an archive of manuscripts and recordings that Alaska Native communities and learners continue to consult. California language activists organized the Advocates for Indigenous California Language Survival and developed Master–Apprentice work in 1992; the first California Breath of Life archival language-restoration workshop followed in 1996 through collaboration with the University of California, Berkeley. A later national Breath of Life institute drew learners to collections in Washington, D.C., including those at the Smithsonian. These were related but distinct developments. These workshops showed that learners could use field notes, wax cylinder recordings, and manuscripts to begin rebuilding languages that had fallen silent. In British Columbia, the First Peoples' Cultural Council supported documentation and created FirstVoices, an online archive launched in the early 2000s that allows communities to control what is public.

Dictionaries occupy a special place in this history. Early dictionaries were produced by outsiders for outsiders, often as tools for translation or conversion. Modern community dictionaries serve teachers and learners, and many are built with fluent speakers as co-authors. The Cherokee–English Dictionary compiled by Durbin Feeling, a fluent speaker and scholar, and published by the Cherokee Nation in the 1970s, is a notable example of a dictionary created by a Cherokee speaker for Cherokee use. In Navajo, grammatical and colloquial dictionaries produced in the twentieth century by Robert Young and William Morgan, who worked with Diné collaborators, became standard references. Increasingly, dictionaries are digital and audio-enabled, a development discussed in Section 123.18.

The changing relationship between outside scholars and Indigenous-controlled documentation has two dimensions. The first is control: who owns recordings, who decides what is made public, and how communities can restrict sensitive material. The Mukurtu content management system, developed in collaboration with Indigenous communities, and the Canadian principles of Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession, known by the acronym OCAP and developed by First Nations information governance organizations, are examples of frameworks designed to give communities authority over their cultural information. The second is training: as more Indigenous people become linguists, archivists, and teachers, they move from being the subjects of documentation to its directors. This shift is central to the idea of language sovereignty examined in Section 123.20.

Documentary source: Myaamia Center, history of California and National Breath of Life.

Section 123.7

Reclaiming Languages Without Living First-Language Speakers

For languages with no remaining first-language speakers, revitalization takes a different form. There are no elders from whom to learn, and the work must begin with documents. The Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project is the most prominent North American example. The Massachusett language, known to the Wampanoag as Wôpanâak, was spoken across southeastern New England at the time English colonists arrived. As noted in the opening, the seventeenth century produced a large written record in the language, including the Eliot Bible and, in the following century, a substantial body of documents written by Wampanoag authors for their own communities, such as deeds, petitions, wills, and church records. By the nineteenth century, as a consequence of colonial pressure, epidemic disease, dispossession, and cultural suppression, the language had ceased to be spoken as a community language, and for much of the twentieth century it was dormant. Chapters 48 and 54 provide the background on Wampanoag history and on the religious missions in which much of the written record was produced.

The project began in 1993 under the leadership of jessie little doe baird, a member of the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe, together with other Wampanoag community members. It serves several Wampanoag communities in Massachusetts and Rhode Island, including the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah). Baird later studied linguistics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where she worked with linguists who helped develop methods for analyzing the historical record, and the project drew on comparison with related Algonquian languages that remain spoken, such as Cree, Ojibwe, and Mi'kmaw, to fill gaps in the documents. In 2010, Baird received a MacArthur Fellowship, which brought national attention to the effort, and a documentary film, We Still Live Here, told the story to a broad audience.

The method is a form of linguistic reconstruction combined with community teaching. Researchers analyzed the written texts to determine grammar and vocabulary, supplemented the record with comparative evidence, and produced teaching materials and a dictionary. Teaching began with classes for community members and gradually expanded into summer programs and, by the mid-2010s, an immersion school for children in which Wôpanâak was the language of instruction. Children who grew up in the program, along with their parents and teachers, form a new speech community. Some have been described as the first people in several generations to use the language as a first language, a claim that rests on community and program reports and is best treated as a description of the movement's aims and achievements.

The case teaches several lessons. It shows that a written record can support reconstruction, but only when communities have access to the documents and the training to analyze them. It also shows the importance of community ownership: the project is directed by the Wampanoag communities themselves, who decide what to teach and how to use the language. And it shows how revitalization can function as an act of political and cultural assertion. The Wampanoag are a people whose history includes some of the earliest and most sustained encounters with English colonists, as discussed in Chapter 48, and the reclamation of their language is understood by many participants as a reassertion of continuity across centuries of disruption.

Wôpanâak is not the only case. The Myaamia (Miami) of Oklahoma and Indiana have rebuilt their language, which was dormant by the mid-twentieth century, through a partnership between the Miami Tribe of Oklahoma and Miami University in Ohio, and one of the project's leaders, Daryl Baldwin, learned the language from archival materials and taught it to his children. In California, many participants in Breath of Life workshops have used archives to relearn languages that no longer have living speakers. These cases also raise the question of authority: when no first-language speaker can say whether a form is correct, who decides? Communities have answered in different ways, often by establishing language committees, consulting the historical record and related languages, and accepting that a reclaimed language may differ from the language of the past in ways that reflect the circumstances of its relearning.

Documentary sources: Pilgrim Hall Museum, 1700 Wampanoag-language land deed · National Archives, Wôpanâak historical lexicon project.

Section 123.8

Writing Systems, Standardization, and Linguistic Authority

Revitalization requires decisions about how a language is written. Indigenous languages have been written in several kinds of systems. Writing systems include the Cherokee syllabary, devised by Sequoyah around 1821, in which characters represent syllables; and Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, developed for Cree in the nineteenth century and later adapted for Inuktitut and other languages. Despite their conventional name, Canadian Aboriginal syllabics work differently from the Cherokee syllabary: the orientation of a basic sign typically indicates its vowel, while other signs can represent syllable-final consonants. Roman-alphabet orthographies were developed for many languages by missionaries, linguists, and communities, sometimes more than one for a single language. Navajo, for example, has a widely used orthography that emerged from 1930s collaborative work by Robert W. Young, Diné linguist William Morgan, and associates, particularly in 1937, and Greenlandic underwent a major spelling reform in 1973 that created a more consistent system. In Mexico, many languages have been written in orthographies promoted by governmental and scholarly bodies, often with disagreements among communities.

Orthography matters because spelling is a form of teaching technology and of identity. A system that is easy for English-speaking learners may fail to represent sounds accurately, and a system that is linguistically precise may be hard for beginners. A syllabary may carry strong associations of community ownership, while a Roman orthography may be better suited to digital tools. Communities also differ over whether to use a single system for all dialects. Among the Inuit of Canada, regional preferences for syllabics or Roman letters long coexisted, and in 2019 Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, the national Inuit organization, formally adopted the unified Roman orthography Inuktut Qaliujaaqpait, intended to promote communication across regions while leaving existing systems in use. The proposal was received with a mixture of support and concern, reflecting the importance of syllabics to many communities.

Standardization affects dialect. Many languages are spoken in several varieties, and a school or dictionary typically must choose which to teach. The Cherokee language, for example, has varieties associated with different communities, notably the speech of the Cherokee Nation in Oklahoma and that of the Eastern Band in North Carolina, and programs in each place have chosen to emphasize their own speech while recognizing the others. In the Arctic, the Inuit dialect continuum raises similar choices. Standardization can support teaching and publication by providing a common reference, but it can also privilege one variety, marginalize others, and displace the local forms that elders speak.

Documentary sources: University of New Mexico, Young–Morgan orthography chronology · Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, 2019 adoption of Inuktut Qaliujaaqpait.

Historical Debate

Who Decides What Counts as Correct Language?

The question: When a language is revitalized, who should determine its spelling, its standard forms, and the acceptable variation among speakers: fluent elders, program leaders, tribal governments, trained linguists, or outside organizations?

Interpretation A — Unified standards make revitalization practical: Advocates argue that a single orthography and a shared reference grammar make it possible to produce textbooks, train teachers, build digital tools, and communicate across communities. For languages with few speakers and limited resources, they argue, duplicating materials for every variety is unaffordable, and agreement on standards reduces confusion among learners.
Interpretation B — Local authority and variation must be preserved: Others argue that standardization imposed from outside, or even from within by a program, can erase local speech, override the authority of elders, and conflict with community protocols. They point out that language varieties often carry the identity of particular places and families, and that materials created by outside organizations may not respect community ownership. Disputes in the 2010s and 2020s involving an outside nonprofit that produced teaching materials for Lakota communities, for example, raised concerns about orthographic choices and control of materials.

Evidence: Both views draw on the experience of specific programs, on the history of orthography development, and on linguistic research about variation. The disagreements concern terminology, the balance of authority, and the practical effects of different strategies and not the basic facts of language use.

Points of disagreement: The debate involves interpretation more than chronology: whether unified standards strengthen or weaken the link between a language and its community, and whether outsiders with technical skills can be partners without becoming authorities.

Why it matters: Decisions about standardization determine who can teach, which materials are used, and whose speech is treated as correct. They also reveal whether a language is treated as a community's collective property.

Division III · Sections 123.9–123.12

Revitalization Through Teaching and Daily Life

This division examines the institutional methods by which communities build new generations of speakers, and the difference between learning a language in a program and living in it.

Section 123.9

Language Nests and Early Childhood Immersion

The language nest is a program in which very young children spend much of the day in an environment where only the Indigenous language is spoken, cared for by fluent elders and other speakers. The model originated in New Zealand, where Māori communities founded the first kōhanga reo in 1982, and it influenced Hawaiʻi, where ʻAha Pūnana Leo was founded in 1983 and opened its first Hawaiian-language immersion preschool on Kauaʻi in 1984. Indigenous communities in North America observed these programs and adapted them to their own circumstances. The underlying idea draws on the observation that young children acquire languages most easily through sustained, meaningful exposure, and that a nest recreates the conditions of a home in which the language is the language of care.

Historical Vocabulary
Language Nest and Immersion

Definition. A language nest is an early childhood program in which the Indigenous language is the only language of interaction. Immersion more broadly refers to education in which the language is used as the medium of instruction and daily communication, rather than taught as a separate subject.

Historical context. The terms entered Indigenous North American discussion in the 1980s and 1990s through contact with Māori and Hawaiian programs. Communities adopted and adapted them, and many programs use their own names and combine elements of different models.

Why it matters. Immersion differs from conventional bilingual or second-language instruction, in which the Indigenous language may be taught for a short period each day. Communities adopted immersion because short periods of instruction had rarely produced fluent speakers.

Potential misunderstanding. Immersion does not mean that the national language is excluded from a child's life or that children cannot succeed in it. Most programs aim for bilingualism, with the Indigenous language as the language of the program.

In North America, early immersion efforts came from several communities at nearly the same time. The Piegan Institute, founded in 1987 by Blackfeet language activists in Browning, Montana, launched immersion instruction in the Blackfeet language, and its school, Cuts Wood, began in the mid-1990s. In British Columbia, Secwépemc communities in the interior established immersion programs in the late 1980s and 1990s, including a school associated with the Adams Lake community. Cherokee communities developed language nests and immersion preschools in the late twentieth century. These programs shared a pattern: they were started by small groups of committed community members, often with minimal funding, relying on volunteers and on elders who agreed to participate.

A language nest depends on fluent speakers who are available, willing, and able to spend long hours with children, a requirement that illustrates the tension discussed in Section 123.5. Many nests have relied on a small number of elders, and when those elders become ill or die, programs can falter. Some have developed ways of pairing elders with younger learners who gradually become more fluent and assume teaching roles. Funding is a further challenge. Nests are labor-intensive and require facilities, wages, and materials, and their financing has often combined tribal or First Nation revenue, grants, and, in the United States, federal programs discussed in Section 123.13.

The central problem of early immersion is continuity. A child who spends the preschool years in a language nest and then enters an English-language elementary school may stop using the language, because the daily environment shifts, peers speak English, and there are few places to use the language. Research and program experience suggest that a few years of immersion can produce strong comprehension and good pronunciation, but that sustained use requires continued schooling and, equally, a family and community environment that rewards the language. This is the reason that many nests were soon followed by immersion elementary schools, and that programs increasingly include parents in the language-learning process, so that the home becomes a place of language use.

Language nests also have political meaning. They embody a principle that communities, not outside institutions, are responsible for the education of their youngest members, and they have often been established as acts of local sovereignty. The experience of nest programs in North America, as in New Zealand and Hawaiʻi, supports the view that children's language acquisition is a collective community project and not just a private family matter.

Documentary sources: ʻAha Pūnana Leo, history of the 1983 organization and 1984 preschool · Piegan Institute, official history.

Section 123.10

Indigenous Language Immersion Schools

Immersion schools extend the nest model into the school years. This section concentrates on how they developed and what they have done for language acquisition. The broader institutional history of Indigenous education, including tribal colleges and community control of curriculum, belongs to Chapter 124.

Mohawk communities became early leaders in community-controlled education and immersion, though the founding of a school and its adoption of full immersion were often different events. In Kahnawà:ke, near Montreal, Kahnawake Survival School opened in 1978 as a community-controlled secondary school in the context of political resistance to provincial language policy; separate Mohawk-language early-years and elementary initiatives developed in subsequent years. At Akwesasne, Mohawk parents founded Akwesasne Freedom School in 1979 to assert cultural and educational control. Its administrators adopted a total Mohawk-immersion curriculum in 1985, making Kanien'kéha the teaching language while sustaining Haudenosaunee history and values. Because these schools arose from community decisions to withdraw from or reshape conventional schooling, they show how language revitalization could be a political act. Chapter 26 describes the Haudenosaunee political traditions in which these schools are rooted.

Cherokee communities developed immersion schooling in the same period. The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina pursued language planning and teaching in the 1990s; its first New Kituwah Cherokee immersion classroom opened on April 19, 2004, beginning with seven children and later developing into an academy. In Oklahoma the Cherokee Nation began a language-preservation school program in Tahlequah in 2001, subsequently expanding its immersion-school operation. These programs began with young children and added grades over time, aiming to produce cohorts of young speakers. These programs use the syllabary in instruction and have produced a generation of children who can speak and read the language, though their numbers remain small relative to the need. In 2019 the Cherokee Nation adopted major language legislation providing multi-year funding, which illustrates the connection, treated more fully in Section 123.12, between tribal government revenue and language programs.

Diné (Navajo) communities approached the problem from a different starting point. Diné bizaad is the most widely spoken Indigenous language in the United States, and for much of the twentieth century children entered school speaking it. Rough Rock Demonstration School (1966) and later bilingual programs such as that at Rock Point used Navajo in instruction and literacy. Over the late twentieth century, however, the proportion of children entering school as fluent speakers declined markedly, as English became the language of many homes. The Navajo Nation and local schools responded with immersion programs and with efforts to certify teachers fluent in the language. The Diné case shows that a large speaker population does not guarantee transmission, and that immersion may be needed even where the language remains widely spoken.

Immersion schools encounter predictable problems. They need teachers who are both fluent and certified, a combination that is rare, and many have worked to create teacher-training pathways. They must meet state or provincial standards, including standardized testing in English, which can pull attention away from the language of instruction. They must create curricula in the Indigenous language for subjects such as mathematics and science, requiring the development of new vocabulary. And they must decide what to do after the early grades, since few immersion programs extend through high school. Evidence on outcomes suggests that immersion can produce children with strong abilities in the language and good performance in English and other subjects, but program quality and continuity vary, and many graduates need continued opportunities to use the language outside school.

The history of immersion schooling also illustrates the movement's dependence on local conditions. Some programs began as private initiatives funded by families, others as tribal departments, and others as part of public school systems. Their relationship to the state differs by country: in Canada, First Nations schools on reserves operate under distinct funding arrangements, while Inuit and Cree communities in northern Quebec gained control of school boards through the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975. These differences mean that the same model has different political and financial implications in different places.

Section 123.11

Adult Learners and Mentor–Apprentice Programs

Children's programs build future speakers, but a language cannot wait twenty years. In many communities the generation that could pass the language on, the parents and young adults, did not learn it. Adult language learning has therefore become a central strategy. Adults can learn intensively, can commit to long-term study, and can become the teachers and parents who bring the language into classrooms and homes.

The best-known adult model is the Master–Apprentice program, devised in California in 1992 by linguist Leanne Hinton and Indigenous Californians working with the Advocates for Indigenous California Language Survival. The model pairs a fluent speaker, the mentor or master, with a committed learner, the apprentice, who agree to spend many hours each week together, typically around ten or more, speaking only the language while engaging in everyday activities such as cooking, gardening, and traveling. The program rejects the classroom, in which learners study grammar apart from use, and instead recreates, for adults, some of the conditions of childhood acquisition. Hinton and others wrote practical guides for communities, and the model spread across North America and to other regions of the world.

British Columbia's First Peoples' Cultural Council offers one of the best-documented applications. The province is home to a large number of distinct First Nations languages, and the Council, working with communities, established a mentor–apprentice program in the late 2000s, providing funding for pairs to spend extended time together and offering training and support. The Council's periodic reports on the status of First Nations languages in British Columbia have used program data to document the number of speakers, the age of fluent speakers, and the results of language programs, creating a substantial body of evidence about what has and has not worked. Similar adult programs exist in the Cherokee Nation, where an apprentice program trains adult learners who then become teachers, and in many other communities.

Adult learning faces serious obstacles. Apprentices usually have jobs and families, and spending many hours each week on language study requires financial support, flexibility, or sacrifice. Many programs provide stipends for this reason. Adult learners often find it difficult to attain full fluency, especially in languages with complex grammar that is very different from English, and some experience frustration and burnout. The shortage of mentors, who are elders in declining numbers, limits how many pairs can be formed. Some programs have responded by training apprentices to become mentors, in a chain that extends the reach of the limited number of fluent speakers.

The adult movement has changed the meaning of fluency in revitalizing communities. Because many of the new speakers learned the language as adults, their speech may differ from that of elders in pronunciation, vocabulary, and idiom. Programs and communities have had to decide how to regard this speech. Many have embraced it, regarding new speakers as essential to the language's future, while continuing to give elders a special role as sources of cultural knowledge. The adult learner thus occupies a position between the archive and the elder: shaped by documentation and formal teaching, but also by close personal relationships with fluent speakers.

Section 123.12

Restoring Indigenous Languages to Everyday Life

A student who learns a language in a classroom has not yet joined a speech community. The difference between acquiring a language in a program and living in it is one of the most important lessons of the revitalization movement. Many programs have produced children who speak well in school but switch to English at home, on the playground, and with friends. Sustained language use requires what linguists call domains of use: the settings, such as home, ceremony, workplace, council chamber, and recreation, in which a language is the normal medium of communication.

Home is the most important domain, and it is also the hardest to change. Parents who did not grow up speaking the language must learn it while raising children, and many programs now emphasize family language plans, parent classes, and resources for use at home such as books, recordings, and games. Some communities have designated "language homes" or have encouraged families to make particular times, such as meals, occasions for speaking the language. The development of a household in which a language is spoken from the start of a child's life, even by parents who learned it as adults, is one of the clearest indicators of success, though it is difficult to measure.

Community institutions are the second domain. Ceremonies, as discussed in Section 123.2, often require the language and are a strong reason for learners to continue. Some tribal and First Nation governments have begun to conduct council meetings, opening prayers, or portions of public business in the Indigenous language, and have created language policies for government offices. Signs, place names, tribal websites, and government forms in the language make it visible and signal its legitimacy. Some communities have hired language coordinators within their governments and have required government employees to learn the language. These steps connect language to the exercise of self-government and to the institutions described in Chapter 139.

Economic and public life form a third domain. Language use in businesses and workplaces gives people practical reasons to speak and creates jobs for speakers as teachers, translators, broadcasters, and administrators. Tribal revenue, including revenue generated by enterprises discussed in Chapters 121 and 122, has paid for language departments, immersion schools, and dictionaries in a number of nations, though the amounts and priorities vary widely and many communities have no such revenue. This chapter does not revisit the gaming industry, but the connection shows that economic resurgence and cultural resurgence are mutually reinforcing: communities with greater financial resources have more capacity to support their languages.

Everyday Life and Social History

Language at Home and in the Community

Language revitalization is often described in terms of schools and statutes, but its success depends on small daily acts: a parent who greets a child in the language each morning, a grandparent who tells a story at supper, a young person who answers an elder in the language rather than in English, a council member who offers an opening prayer. Such acts are hard for historians to document because they leave few records, but oral histories, community surveys, and program reports suggest that they matter more than any single policy.

The experience differs by place. In remote northern communities where Inuktut or Yup'ik is the language of the store, church, and household, children may hear the language everywhere, and the challenge is to keep it in schools, offices, and digital media. In cities, where many Indigenous people now live, as described in Chapters 105 and 140, learners may live far from elders and from one another, and revitalization may take the form of weekend classes, evening gatherings, and online groups. For migrants from Mexico and Central America who speak Mixtec, Zapotec, K'iche', or other Indigenous languages in the United States, language use may be shaped by work, immigration status, and the need for interpreters in courts and clinics.

Gender, age, and class also matter. In some communities women have been prominent as teachers and program leaders, in part because they have had greater responsibility for children, while elders of both genders serve as speakers and cultural authorities. Young people may regard the language as a mark of belonging, as a burden, or as both. Chapter 147 returns to youth leadership in contemporary Indigenous communities.

The difference between classroom acquisition and community-wide use shapes what program leaders now say about success. A program that produces fluent children but leaves them without places to use the language has not achieved restoration in the fuller sense. Communities have therefore moved toward comprehensive approaches that combine schools, adult learning, family support, government policy, and media, recognizing that revitalization is a project of the whole community rather than of a single institution.

Division IV · Sections 123.13–123.16

Language Rights and Continental Revitalization Movements

This division compares the legal and political frameworks of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and the Arctic, and shows how each shaped, and was shaped by, Indigenous language activism.

Section 123.13

The United States: Tribal Sovereignty and Language Policy

United States policy toward Indigenous languages reversed over the twentieth century, from suppression to formal support. The turning point came in the era of self-determination described in Chapter 114. The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 gave tribes greater control over programs, including education, and made it possible for tribal governments and communities to design language programs. Activists, educators, and tribal leaders then pressed for a statement of national policy. In 1990 Congress enacted the Native American Languages Act, a statement of federal policy that rejected the earlier assumption that Indigenous languages should be replaced.

Historical Vocabulary
Linguistic Rights and Language Sovereignty

Definition. Linguistic rights are legal or political claims that people may use their language in education, public institutions, and everyday life without penalty. Language sovereignty describes the authority of an Indigenous nation or community to make decisions about its own language, including how it is taught, recorded, and used.

Historical context. The idea of linguistic rights developed in international and national law in the twentieth century. Indigenous activists adapted it to claim not only individual rights but collective authority, connecting language to the inherent sovereignty discussed in Chapter 1.

Why it matters. The two ideas differ. A government may recognize a right to use a language without giving an Indigenous nation authority over it, and the difference determines who controls funding, curriculum, and archives.

Potential misunderstanding. Legal recognition is not the same as language vitality. A language may be official in law and still lack speakers, teachers, and resources.

The Native American Languages Act of 1990 declared that it was the policy of the United States to "preserve, protect, and promote" the right of Native Americans to use, practice, and develop their languages, and it acknowledged that the status of Native languages was unique and the federal government bore a responsibility to act with Native Americans to secure their survival. It also stated that Native American languages should be permitted in schools, and it directed federal agencies to review and, where necessary, revise policies that interfered with such use. The Act as first passed contained no substantial funding, and its immediate effects were largely declaratory. In 1992 Congress passed a further statute establishing grant programs administered through the Administration for Native Americans, and in 2006 it enacted the Esther Martinez Native American Languages Preservation Act, named for a Tewa storyteller and language advocate from Ohkay Owingeh, which authorized funding for immersion programs, language nests, and survival schools. Appropriations have never matched the scale of need, and funding has been uncertain from year to year.

Tribal governments have also acted as language policymakers. Several have declared their languages official tribal languages, adopted language preservation laws, or created departments of language. The Cherokee Nation's 2019 legislation, and Navajo Nation measures on instruction in Diné bizaad, are examples. In Alaska, the state legislature in 2014 designated twenty Alaska Native languages as official languages of the state, a symbolic step that did not create new service obligations but acknowledged the languages' standing. These measures show tribal and state governments using their different powers to support languages, and they connect to the jurisdictional questions of Chapter 118.

Implementation challenges persist. Federal education policy, especially standardized testing requirements introduced in the early 2000s, created pressure on schools to emphasize English, and teacher certification rules have complicated the hiring of fluent speakers who lack formal credentials. Federal programs often fund short-term projects rather than the long-term staffing and infrastructure that language revitalization requires. English-only political campaigns of the 1980s and 1990s, which sought to make English the official language of states and of the nation, created a political climate that Indigenous advocates had to counter. The Native American Languages Act was passed in part in response to these currents.

Section 123.14

Canada: First Nations, Métis, and Inuit Language Revitalization

Canada's constitutional order recognizes Indigenous peoples as three groups, First Nations, Inuit, and Métis, whose languages and circumstances differ. The linguistic landscape is correspondingly varied: Cree, Ojibwe, Dene languages, and Inuktut are among the larger languages, while many languages on the Pacific coast and in the interior of British Columbia have very small speaker populations. Census data have generally shown declining numbers of people reporting an Indigenous mother tongue, alongside growth in the number of people who speak an Indigenous language as a second language, a pattern consistent with the rise of adult and school-based learning.

Community initiatives preceded federal law. First Nations schools in the 1970s and later, inspired by Indian Control of Indian Education, introduced language instruction. The James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975 gave the Cree and Inuit of northern Quebec school boards with authority over education, in which Cree and Inuktitut are used. In the territories, language law developed early. The Northwest Territories adopted its first Official Languages Act in 1984, but major amendments in 1990 extended formal recognition to northern Indigenous languages; its present list includes eleven official languages, nine of them Indigenous. The Yukon passed a Languages Act in 1988, and Nunavut, created in 1999 as discussed in Chapter 116, adopted the Inuit Language Protection Act and a new Official Languages Act in 2008, making Inuktut a language of government and education.

At the national level, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 2015 report included Calls to Action on language, which called for recognition of Indigenous language rights, for an Indigenous Languages Act, for the creation of a language commissioner, and for post-secondary programs in Indigenous languages. Parliament responded with the Indigenous Languages Act, which received Royal Assent in June 2019. The Act recognizes that Indigenous language rights are among the rights protected by section 35 of the Constitution Act of 1982, commits the federal government to support language reclamation, and establishes the Office of the Commissioner of Indigenous Languages and directors for First Nations, Inuit, and Métis languages. The Act was developed with Indigenous organizations and accompanied by funding commitments of several hundred million dollars over five years, with more later. The Commissioner's office began operating in 2021. Chapter 117 examines the broader constitutional context.

The Act's distinction between statutory recognition and the resources needed for revitalization is a central issue. Indigenous leaders and language advocates welcomed the legislation but have pressed the question of whether funding is sufficient, stable, and allocated according to community priorities, as opposed to project-by-project competition. Others have noted the importance of ensuring that funds reach small communities with few administrative staff. The Métis language, Michif, a mixed language combining French noun elements with Cree verb structures and one of the most distinctive Indigenous languages of the continent, remains critically endangered, and the Métis Nation has developed its own language strategies. The Inuit, whose Inuktut remains widely spoken in Nunavut and Nunavik, have emphasized protecting the strength of an already vital language and extending it into education and the workplace.

The Canadian experience also shows the link between language and the residential school reckoning. Many survivors and descendants describe language recovery as a form of healing, and some programs are explicitly connected to survivors' initiatives. This connection, examined in Chapter 127, helps explain why language rights became a national issue after 2015.

Section 123.15

Mexico: Indigenous Languages, Recognition, and Revitalization

Mexico has the largest number of Indigenous language speakers in North America. Mexico's 2020 Population and Housing Census counted 7,364,645 people aged three or older who spoke an Indigenous language, or 6.1 percent of the population in that age group. The National Institute of Indigenous Languages (INALI) classifies the country's linguistic diversity into eleven families, sixty-eight linguistic groups, and 364 linguistic variants; those catalog categories do not imply that every variant is mutually unintelligible with every other. The largest language by speakers is Nahuatl, followed by Yucatec Maya and other Mayan, Oto-Manguean, and Totonac languages. The size of the speaker population should not obscure the endangerment of many smaller languages, nor the inequality that accompanies Indigenous language use: Indigenous-language speakers are, on average, poorer and have less access to education, health care, and the justice system than other Mexicans.

The history of policy parallels that of other countries but follows a distinct logic. The post-revolutionary state promoted Spanish literacy through rural schools, and the National Indigenist Institute, established in 1948, trained bilingual promoters to bring Indigenous communities into national life. In 1978 the federal government created a directorate for Indigenous education that expanded bilingual and bicultural schooling, a major change from earlier policy even though schooling often remained oriented toward Spanish. The Zapatista uprising of 1994 in Chiapas, led by Mayan peoples, brought Indigenous rights to the center of national politics, and the San Andrés Accords of 1996 included commitments on language and culture. A constitutional reform in 2001 recognized Mexico as a pluricultural nation with Indigenous roots and addressed Indigenous rights, though its implementation was criticized by many Indigenous organizations as inadequate.

The General Law of Linguistic Rights of Indigenous Peoples, enacted in 2003, is the central statute. It recognizes Indigenous languages as national languages with the same validity as Spanish in the territories where they are spoken, affirms the right of Indigenous people to use their languages in public and private life, and requires that public services, including access to justice, be available in Indigenous languages. It mandated the creation of INALI, which was established institutionally in 2004 and has catalogued languages, promoted programs, and supported interpreters and translators. Over time Mexican institutions have produced standardized grammars, bilingual textbooks, interpreter certification, and a network of Indigenous radio stations, the first of which began in the late 1970s under the national indigenist institution. Chapter 141 places these developments in the context of Indigenous peoples of northern Mexico and the borderlands.

The gap between the law and practice remains wide. Public services in Indigenous languages are often unavailable, interpreters are scarce, and many courts and health facilities lack personnel who can communicate with Indigenous patients and defendants. Schools frequently lack trained bilingual teachers or materials. Discrimination against Indigenous speakers persists in many contexts, and migration to cities and to the United States removes speakers from communities where languages are used. Yet Indigenous communities, organizations, and writers are active in revitalization, including programs to teach Nahuatl and Maya languages, publication in Indigenous languages, and the work of Indigenous linguists and teachers. The Mexican case illustrates that a large speaker population and a favorable statute can coexist with persistent inequality and endangerment, and that revitalization in the Mexican context is entangled with questions of poverty, land, and citizenship.

Section 123.16

The Arctic and Greenland: Language, Government, and Continuity

The Arctic offers the sharpest contrasts in the vitality of Indigenous languages. Greenlandic, known as Kalaallisut in its principal variety, is the first language of the large majority of Greenland's population, which is predominantly Inuit, and it has long been used in schools, newspapers, and broadcasting. Two other varieties, Tunumiit in East Greenland and Inuktun in the far northwest, are more endangered. Under the Home Rule Act that took effect in 1979, Greenlandic became the principal language of the territory, and under the Self-Government Act of 2009, which Chapter 148 discusses in the context of changing sovereignty, Greenlandic was made the sole official language, while Danish remains widely used in some higher education, in certain professions, and in relations with Denmark. The long history of Danish administration left a legacy of bilingualism, and Greenland's language politics concern the balance between Greenlandic, Danish, and, increasingly, English.

In northern Canada, Inuktut, the collective term for Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun, remains the language of daily life in many communities of Nunavut and Nunavik, though its use among young people has declined in some places as English and French gain ground in schooling and media. Nunavut's laws of 2008 commit the territorial government to support Inuktut in education and public life, and the government has faced the problem of finding enough qualified Inuktut-speaking teachers and public servants. Chapter 116 describes the political movement that created Nunavut, in which language was a central concern. The Inuit organization Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami and regional bodies have pressed for investment in Inuktut education.

In Alaska the situation is more varied and more precarious. Central Alaskan Yup'ik remains spoken by children in some villages, while Iñupiaq, Alutiiq (Sugpiaq), St. Lawrence Island Yupik, and Unangam Tunuu have far fewer speakers, many of them elderly. The Alaska Native Language Center and Native-controlled organizations have documented languages and supported programs, such as Alutiiq language efforts on Kodiak Island. Chapters 99 and 115 provide the context of Alaska Native political and land arrangements, including the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act and the corporations created under it, which have in some cases supported language and culture programs.

Comparison shows how political context and demography shape language vitality. Greenland has a relatively large Inuit majority and political authority over its public institutions, which gives Greenlandic a secure institutional base. Nunavut has an Inuit majority and territorial government, but Inuktut competes with English in schooling and employment. Alaska Native peoples are minorities in a state with an English-speaking majority and fewer institutional supports. These differences are a reminder that revitalization depends on political power as well as on teaching methods.

Division V · Sections 123.17–123.20

Digital Revitalization and the Future of Indigenous Languages

This division studies how media and technology extend language into modern life, how success is measured, and what conditions would allow Indigenous languages to flourish across generations.

Section 123.17

Indigenous Broadcasting, Publishing, and Public Communication

A language that appears only in the classroom is treated by many of its learners as a school subject. A language that appears on the radio, on television, in newspapers, and in children's books is part of public life. Indigenous communities have used broadcasting and publishing for this purpose, and they have done so in ways that connect directly to language continuity. This chapter treats these activities as instruments of linguistic continuity; Chapter 126 examines Indigenous art, literature, film, and media more broadly.

Radio was an early and effective medium. In Alaska, public radio stations such as KYUK in Bethel, which began broadcasting in the early 1970s, carried Yup'ik-language programming to a region where the language was widely spoken. In the Navajo Nation, KTNN, established in the 1980s, broadcasts extensively in Diné bizaad. In Mexico, the national indigenist institution began a network of Indigenous cultural radio stations in the late 1970s, with broadcasts in many languages, and community radio has since become an important medium for Indigenous languages. In Greenland, Kalaallit Nunaata Radioa has broadcast in Greenlandic since the mid-twentieth century. Radio suits oral cultures and spreads quickly through dispersed communities, and it provides work for speakers.

Television followed. In Canada, the Inuit Broadcasting Corporation, founded in 1981, produced Inuktitut-language programs for northern audiences, and in 1999 the Aboriginal Peoples Television Network began national broadcasting, carrying programming in a number of Indigenous languages along with English and French. Television has been a mixed influence on languages, since commercial media in national languages helped to displace Indigenous languages in the household. Indigenous-language television therefore represents an effort to reclaim the medium that had contributed to language shift.

Print and publishing have a long history, as noted in Section 123.4. The Cherokee Phoenix and the Greenlandic Atuagagdliutit are nineteenth-century examples, and twentieth-century publications include community newspapers, tribal newsletters, and bilingual periodicals. In recent decades communities have produced children's books, readers, and story collections in Indigenous languages, often with illustrations and audio, which provide materials for immersion schools and for families. Small Indigenous presses and tribal departments publish these works, and they supply content that classroom programs need. Some publications translate well-known stories into Indigenous languages, while others record traditional narratives told by elders, serving both language and cultural transmission.

More recent forms include podcasts, video channels, and social media. Language activists have used social media to create online communities of learners, to share daily phrases, and to connect dispersed speakers. These media allow Indigenous languages to be used for contemporary topics, such as sports, humor, and politics, rather than only for traditional ones, an important step in showing that the language is a medium of modern life. They also enable people in cities, or in other countries, to participate in a language community. Indigenous media organizations face the same challenges as language programs: limited funding, a shortage of speakers who can produce content, and competition with large-scale commercial media.

Section 123.18

Digital Technology and Language Revitalization

Digital technology has changed the tools available to language programs. The first requirement was the ability to type and display Indigenous writing systems. The Unicode standard incorporated the Cherokee syllabary and Canadian Aboriginal syllabics in the late 1990s, and subsequent developments by technology companies made keyboards and fonts available on computers and phones. Cherokee-language support appeared on Apple mobile devices in 2010, a milestone widely noted in Cherokee communities, since it meant that young people could text in the syllabary. For many languages with special characters, including those of the Pacific Northwest and the Arctic, keyboard and font development remains a significant task.

Digital dictionaries and archives are a second application. The FirstVoices platform, created in British Columbia in the early 2000s, allows communities to build online dictionaries, phrase collections, and archives with audio, video, and images, and to control access. Other communities have built their own searchable dictionaries and talking dictionaries. Digitization of older audio recordings and manuscripts, often held in university and museum collections, has made archival materials more accessible to communities, linking to the work described in Section 123.6. Language-learning applications, online courses, and videoconferencing have made it possible for dispersed learners, including urban Indigenous people and diaspora communities, to study with teachers who may live far away.

The newest developments include speech technology and artificial intelligence. Researchers and communities have experimented with speech recognition, text-to-speech, and machine translation for Indigenous languages. These tools could, in principle, support learners, help transcribe archival recordings, and make digital services available in Indigenous languages. But they depend on data, such as recordings and texts, and Indigenous languages typically have small data sets. They also raise serious questions of control. Recordings of elders may include sensitive cultural material, some of it restricted by community protocol. If recordings are used to train commercial systems without the consent of the community, the community may lose control of its language and gain none of the benefits. Systems built on limited data may also produce inaccurate or misleading output, which can mislead learners and misrepresent a language.

For these reasons, Indigenous data governance has become a prominent concern. The principles of Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession (OCAP), developed by First Nations organizations in Canada, and the CARE principles for Indigenous data governance, which emphasize collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics, offer frameworks for decisions about language data. Systems such as Mukurtu enable communities to apply their own protocols to digital collections. Chapter 145 examines these questions across fields including genetic research, archaeology, and artificial intelligence. Applied to language, the principle is that communities should decide who may record, store, use, and commercialize their languages, and that technology should serve revitalization on the community's terms.

Technology also has limits. A phone application cannot replace the relationships among generations that the movement seeks to restore, and a language that exists only on screens is not a living speech community. The most successful uses of technology have supported, rather than substituted for, human relationships: for example, by providing materials for classroom teachers, by helping parents who are learning alongside their children, by recording elders so that their speech remains available, and by connecting learners to mentors. Language programs tend to adopt technology selectively, according to whether it serves community goals.

Section 123.19

Measuring Language Revitalization: Progress and Limitations

Assessing whether revitalization is succeeding is difficult, and different measures can yield conflicting answers. The most common source is census or survey data on the number of people who speak an Indigenous language, but census questions differ among countries and over time. Canadian censuses distinguish between mother tongue, the first language learned, and language spoken at home, and they allow respondents to report knowledge of a language. The United States census collects data on languages spoken at home through a survey that does not capture proficiency or the many people who are learning languages as adults. Mexico's census asks about language use among people of age three and older and relies on self-reporting. Each source has limits, and comparisons across borders must be made with care.

International frameworks offer another approach. The UNESCO Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger classifies languages from vulnerable to extinct according to factors such as the extent of intergenerational transmission, absolute number of speakers, proportion of speakers within the population, and use in different domains. Sociolinguists have developed scales, such as Fishman's graded intergenerational disruption model, to assess where a language stands and which interventions are appropriate. British Columbia's First Peoples' Cultural Council has issued periodic assessments of the languages of the province, using the number of fluent speakers, semi-speakers, and learners, and these reports have repeatedly shown that the number of fluent speakers is small and in many languages falling, even as the number of learners rises.

These assessments show a complex picture. On the one hand, there is real progress. New immersion schools exist where none did. Thousands of children and adults are learning Indigenous languages. Some languages that were dormant are being spoken again, and national governments have enacted laws and funded programs. On the other hand, many languages have lost their last fluent speakers during the revitalization era, and for many others, the speakers are elderly and few. Learner numbers do not necessarily translate into fluent speakers, since many learners study for limited periods.

Several factors limit success. Teacher shortages are persistent. Funding is often short-term and competitive, making it hard to plan programs over the decades that language recovery requires. Urban migration disperses language communities. Schools face pressure to emphasize national languages and standardized testing. Families may feel that the language has little economic value. And the fact that revitalization takes place over generations means that the results of programs begun in the 1990s will be fully apparent only decades later. Measuring "success" is also a conceptual problem: is it the number of speakers, the proportion of children who speak the language, the number of domains in which it is used, or the community's own sense that its language is strong?

Multiple Perspectives

What Counts as Success in Language Revitalization?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns documented in program reports and community discussions. They are not quotations from named individuals.

From the perspective of a fluent elder speaker: Success is measured by whether children can speak with their grandparents and take part in ceremony in the language, and by whether the language is used with proper respect, accurate forms, and understanding of what may be said and to whom. An elder may worry that learner speech departs from older forms.
From the perspective of an adult learner or new speaker: Success is the ability to hold conversations, to raise children in the language, and to be accepted as a speaker. A new speaker may stress that learning as an adult is a legitimate path and that perfection should not be the condition of participation.
From the perspective of a tribal or First Nation government and program administrator: Success must be documented in ways that satisfy funders and councils: numbers of enrolled students, teachers trained, materials produced, and trends in proficiency. An administrator may be concerned with sustaining funding and building permanent institutions.
From the perspective of an academic linguist or a government statistician: Success is assessed with comparable indicators such as intergenerational transmission and the number of speakers by age. This approach allows comparison across communities but may not reflect each community's own values.

These perspectives did not have equal institutional power, since funders and governments set many of the terms of evaluation. They nonetheless show that a complete assessment of revitalization requires both quantitative indicators and community-defined goals. Many programs now collect data themselves and define their own standards, an expression of the language sovereignty discussed in the next section.

Section 123.20

Language Sovereignty and the Future of Indigenous North America

The history traced in this chapter supports a central conclusion: language revitalization is a form of political and cultural self-determination. Decisions about what language children will be raised and educated in, how a language will be recorded and who will own the record, what will be taught and by whom, and what role the language will have in government are decisions about collective identity and authority. When Indigenous nations make them, they exercise the sovereignty described in Chapter 1; when governments or outside institutions make them, the effect may be to reproduce the dependence of earlier eras. This is why the movement has been closely connected to the Indigenous political movements of Part XIX and to the institutions of self-government discussed in Chapters 114 and 139.

The regional comparison shows both common patterns and differences. Everywhere, community initiatives came first, and legal frameworks followed. The United States moved from suppression to a policy of support but with limited funding, relying on tribal governments and competitive grants. Canada, shaped by the residential school reckoning, developed a statutory framework with a national commissioner and an explicit link to reconciliation, though communities press for sustained resources. Mexico recognized Indigenous languages as national languages and built an institute, but faces deep inequalities in the delivery of services. Greenland and Nunavut, where Indigenous peoples form majorities and have significant political authority, show how institutional power can secure a language's place in public life. Chapter 143 describes the international dimension, including the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, which recognizes rights relating to Indigenous languages, and the United Nations' proclamation of 2022 to 2032 as an International Decade of Indigenous Languages.

What conditions allow languages to remain or become widely used across generations? Historical experience suggests several. First, family transmission, since children who hear the language from parents and grandparents are the most reliable foundation. Second, community institutions that use the language, including schools, ceremonies, governments, and media, so that the language has authority and reasons for use. Third, a sufficient number of speakers and teachers, which requires training and support for people who can work in the language. Fourth, economic and social opportunities for speakers, since people are more likely to use a language that offers employment and recognition. Fifth, stable funding and legal recognition, which provide security. And sixth, community control, so that revitalization reflects local values and protocols.

None of these conditions guarantees success, and each is vulnerable. Fluent speakers are aging, and for many languages the window for working with them is closing. Political support can change with governments, and funding can be cut. Pressures toward national languages, global media, and migration remain strong. Yet the history also suggests that outcomes are not predetermined. Communities with few speakers and little money have created programs that produced new speakers, and communities that had no first-language speakers have begun to restore languages. Whether a language endures depends on decisions made by families, communities, and governments, and not on an inevitable course of decline.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Language and Nationhood, from Precolonial Multilingualism to Revitalization

What Changed

  • The number of fluent first-language speakers declined sharply for many languages during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, with the steepest losses often following the expansion of national schooling and urbanization
  • Languages gained new written forms, institutions, and media, including syllabaries, orthographies, dictionaries, radio, television, and digital tools
  • The sources of authority shifted: from outside collectors and state schools toward community-controlled programs, tribal and First Nation governments, and Indigenous linguists
  • Legal status changed from suppression and neglect to formal recognition in the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland

What Stayed the Same

  • Languages continued to carry kinship, place knowledge, ceremony, and political concepts, and communities continued to regard them as inheritances held in trust
  • Families and elders remained the foundation of transmission, even when schools and governments took on large roles
  • Multilingualism persisted as a normal condition of many Indigenous communities
  • Communities continued to claim the authority to speak, teach, and decide for themselves

Pattern of change. Change was uneven and gradual in some places and abrupt in others, and it was contested at every step. Those who experienced the most direct disruption were the children and parents of the boarding and residential school generations and the families moved to cities, while communities that were remote or protected ceremonial life experienced greater continuity. Why it matters: Understanding both loss and continuity prevents treating language loss as inevitable and treating revitalization as a simple return to a fixed past.

Language revitalization also changes the meaning of continuity. A language that is learned by a new generation is not identical to the language of earlier generations, since it is shaped by new circumstances, new words, and new speakers. Many communities accept this, regarding living languages as inherently changing. The aim is not to freeze a language in the form recorded in an archive but to restore its place as a language that its people can use to talk about their present and future. In that sense, language revitalization expresses the argument of this textbook, that Indigenous nations are continuing political and cultural communities and not remnants of an earlier world.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 123 has argued that language revitalization is the restoration of relationships among generations, communities, knowledge systems, and institutions, and not merely the preservation of vocabulary. The chapter began with the diversity of Indigenous North American languages, which belong to dozens of unrelated families, are often spoken across modern borders, and have long been used in multilingual societies.

It then explained that languages carry kinship, place knowledge, ceremonial practice, and political concepts specific to each community, so that they cannot be treated as interchangeable. Because communities differ in what they believe their languages are for, revitalization goals differ as well.

The chapter traced the disruption of transmission to assimilation policy, including English-only instruction in the United States, residential schooling in Canada, Spanish-language national education in Mexico, and Danish-language administration in Greenland, and also to relocation, wage labor, and mass media. Because many parents who had been punished for speaking their languages chose not to teach them, loss was partly the result of decisions made under pressure. The institutional histories of schooling belong to Chapters 87 and 88.

Languages nonetheless survived through families, ceremony, and community literacy. From the 1960s and 1970s, community-controlled schools, language centers, and Indigenous organizations began the modern movement, linked to the wider political currents of self-determination.

Reclaiming endangered languages required elders, documentation, and new methods. Elders and fluent speakers are the principal living authorities, but their numbers are declining, and programs must respect protocols of authority and consent. Archives, manuscripts, and dictionaries, many produced by outside scholars with Indigenous collaborators, became resources for communities that now direct documentation themselves. The Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project showed that a language with no first-language speakers could be rebuilt from written records, comparison with related languages, and sustained community effort.

Decisions about writing systems and standardization showed that revitalization involves questions of authority. Unified orthographies and standards can support teaching and technology, but they can also override local varieties and the authority of elders, and communities have resolved these tensions in different ways.

Language nests, immersion schools, and mentor–apprentice programs developed as complementary strategies for building speakers among children and adults. Each addresses a different gap, and each faces the challenges of teacher shortages, funding, and the need to sustain language use beyond programs. The chapter emphasized the distinction between classroom acquisition and community-wide use, and the importance of homes, ceremonies, governments, and workplaces as domains of use.

The legal frameworks of the four jurisdictions differ. The United States offers a policy of support under the Native American Languages Act and later funding statutes, with tribal governments as language policymakers. Canada has the Indigenous Languages Act of 2019, shaped by the residential school reckoning. Mexico recognizes Indigenous languages as national languages under the 2003 law and has INALI, but faces persistent inequality. Greenland and Nunavut have built language into self-government. In every case, legal recognition has not by itself produced speakers.

Broadcasting, publishing, and digital technology have extended Indigenous languages into modern communication, while raising issues of consent, data sovereignty, and accuracy. Measuring revitalization is difficult, because census data, vitality scales, and program records capture different things, and communities may define success differently from governments and scholars.

The chapter concluded that language revitalization is an exercise of sovereignty and an intergenerational project whose success depends on family transmission, institutions that use the language, trained speakers and teachers, economic opportunity, stable funding, and community control. These conditions are vulnerable, and many languages remain in grave danger, but outcomes are the result of human decisions and not of an inevitable decline.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Language shift
The gradual replacement of a community's language by another as the language of daily use, usually through changes in what children learn.
Endangered language
A language whose use is declining and whose transmission to new generations is weakening or has stopped.
Dormant (sleeping) language
A language with no living first-language speakers but with documentation and community connections that allow its return.
Language revitalization
Organized efforts to increase the number of speakers and the uses of a threatened language.
Language reclamation
Revitalization of a language that has gone dormant, usually based on documents, recordings, and related languages.
First-language speaker
A person who learned a language as a child in the home or community; often called a fluent or native speaker.
Intergenerational transmission
The passing of a language from parents and elders to children in ordinary family and community life.
Language nest
An early childhood program in which only the Indigenous language is used, adapted from Māori and Hawaiian models.
Immersion
Education or care in which the Indigenous language is the medium of communication and instruction rather than a separate subject.
Master–Apprentice program
A model, developed in California in 1992, pairing a fluent speaker with a learner who spends many hours a week using the language in daily activities.
Orthography
A conventional system for writing a language, including its letters or symbols and spelling rules.
Syllabary
A writing system with characters for syllables, exemplified by Cherokee; Canadian Aboriginal syllabics use systematic orientation of signs to indicate vowels and also represent final consonants.
Standardization
The selection of agreed forms for spelling, grammar, and vocabulary, which may favor one variety over others.
Language documentation
The recording and description of a language through texts, audio and video, grammars, and dictionaries.
Breath of Life workshop
Archival reclamation workshops first held in California in 1996, building on organizing that began in 1992, helping communities use historical records in language recovery.
Domain of use
A setting, such as home, ceremony, school, council, or workplace, in which a language is the normal medium of communication.
Linguistic rights
Legal or political claims to use one's language in public institutions, education, and daily life.
Language sovereignty
The authority of an Indigenous nation or community to govern decisions about its own language.
Native American Languages Act (1990)
U.S. federal law declaring a policy of preserving, protecting, and promoting Native American language use; later supplemented by funding statutes.
Indigenous Languages Act (2019)
Canadian law supporting Indigenous language reclamation, creating a Commissioner of Indigenous Languages and addressing First Nations, Inuit, and Métis languages.
General Law of Linguistic Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2003)
Mexican law recognizing Indigenous languages as national languages and creating INALI.
Kalaallisut
The principal variety of Greenlandic and the official language of Greenland under the 2009 Self-Government Act.
Inuktut
A collective term used in Canada for Inuit languages, including Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun.
Michif
A language of the Métis that combines French noun phrases with Cree verb structures; critically endangered.
OCAP
Principles of Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession, developed by First Nations organizations in Canada to govern community data and information.
Data sovereignty
The right of Indigenous communities to control the collection, ownership, and use of data about their people, languages, and cultures.
Language vitality
The strength of a language as measured by speakers, transmission to children, and domains of use.
Castellanización
The Mexican policy of teaching Spanish to Indigenous children, often to the exclusion of their own languages.

Chapter Reflection

Language revitalization matters because it shows Indigenous history as an unfinished story. The disruption described in earlier volumes did not simply end in conquest; it left a task that each generation has had to take up. Reclaiming a language means choosing, against considerable pressure, to rebuild the connections that let knowledge pass from one person to another.

The chapter reveals larger processes that run through Volume X. Self-determination was not only a legal status but a daily practice, carried out in preschools, council chambers, recording sessions, and kitchens. Economic resurgence, examined in Chapters 121 and 122, provided resources for some communities, while legal change, examined in Chapters 114 through 118, created room for community authority. Language is where these developments meet cultural life.

The movement also shaped what followed. Chapter 124 takes up Indigenous education and tribal colleges, where language programs are an important part of community-controlled schooling, and Chapter 126 considers the art, literature, and media in which Indigenous languages are used creatively. Chapters 143 through 147 return to questions of international rights, data sovereignty, and youth leadership that language work has already raised.

What survives from earlier centuries is not only the languages but the idea that they belong to their communities. That idea explains why elders, children, scholars, and governments have often negotiated over language, and why the answers they give will help determine whether Indigenous languages are spoken widely in the generations to come. As this textbook has argued throughout, Indigenous nations are continuing communities, and their languages are among the clearest evidence of that continuity.
