Chapter Opening

A court can declare that a nation holds a right to water, but a right is not a pipe, a canal, a fish ladder, or a faucet. This chapter follows more than a century of Indigenous efforts to turn recognition into water that actually flows to fields, rivers, lakes, and kitchens.

In the early twentieth century, a dispute over a modest northern Plains river reached the United States Supreme Court. Upstream from the Fort Belknap Reservation in Montana, non-Indigenous settlers had built dams and ditches on the Milk River and drawn off water that the reservation's Gros Ventre (Aaniiih) and Assiniboine (Nakoda) residents were being encouraged to use for farming. The Court's answer in 1908, in Winters v. United States, became one of the most consequential water decisions in North American Indigenous history. Yet its consequences unfolded slowly, and in some places have not yet fully unfolded. A principle announced in 1908 required decades of litigation, negotiation, appropriation by Congress, and construction before water could be delivered to many of the communities that held it.

That gap between a right and its exercise is the central subject of this chapter. Indigenous nations did not first acquire an interest in water through American law. Pueblo communities along the Rio Grande, the ancestors of the Akimel O'odham on the Gila River, Columbia River salmon peoples, the Paiute of the Truckee River and Pyramid Lake, the Yaqui of the Río Yaqui, and the Diné of the arid Colorado Plateau all organized settlement, subsistence, ceremony, and governance around specific waters long before any state or province existed. When the United States and its western states built systems of water allocation in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, those systems largely treated water as a commodity to be claimed by use, and they were designed with little or no Indigenous participation.

The legal history that followed is therefore not a simple story of recognition. It includes Winters and its doctrine of reserved rights; Arizona v. California and the quantification of rights by measuring irrigable land; the McCarran Amendment and the long, expensive general stream adjudications it made possible; and, from the late twentieth century, a growing practice of negotiated settlements approved by Congress. It also includes treaty-based claims to keep water in rivers for fish, struggles over dams on the Columbia and Klamath, the restoration of flows to Pyramid Lake, the application of reserved rights to groundwater, a 2023 Supreme Court decision declining to impose a particular federal duty on behalf of the Navajo Nation, and regulatory authority under the Clean Water Act.

The chapter also asks students to look beyond the courtroom. A household in the Navajo Nation may hold, through its government, substantial legal claims to water and still haul water by truck. A First Nation in Canada may sit beside a reservoir serving a distant city and still live under a drinking-water advisory. A Yaqui community in Sonora may hold a historic claim to a river that has been dammed, diverted, and allocated to others. These are not only questions of law. They concern engineering, public health, money, ecology, and political authority, and they connect to this textbook's recurring argument that Indigenous nations are continuing political communities rather than remnants of an earlier North America.

Historians reconstruct this subject from an unusually varied body of evidence: treaties and executive orders; Supreme Court and lower-court opinions; special-master reports and decrees; congressional settlement statutes; Bureau of Reclamation and Bureau of Indian Affairs records; tribal council resolutions and ordinances; environmental reviews; hydrological and fisheries data; oral histories; and the testimony of tribal members, irrigators, engineers, and public officials. Each of these sources reflects particular interests. Court opinions explain legal reasoning but rarely describe daily life; agency records document projects but often reflect federal priorities; tribal sources record community experience and governmental strategy but may not be as widely archived. The chapter uses them together, and it tries to distinguish carefully among a legal recognition, a quantified entitlement, a funded settlement, a built facility, and delivered water.

The chapter's geographic center is the American West, but its scope is continental. Later sections compare the American framework of federally reserved rights with Canada's drinking-water and regulatory problems on reserves and with Mexico's recent efforts to return irrigation infrastructure to the Yaqui people. The chapter extends through November 2026, and it treats very recent developments, including the 2026 federal decisions on Colorado River operations, with particular care about what has been decided and what has not.

Chapter 119 examined Indigenous claims to ancestral lands and sacred landscapes. This chapter examines the waters on which those lands, and the communities living on them, depend. Its argument is that Indigenous water sovereignty has never rested on a single legal doctrine. It has required the combined ability to secure a right, quantify it, defend it against competing users, build the means to deliver it, regulate its quality, and govern its use.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Indigenous relationships with rivers, lakes, springs, and aquifers before the creation of western state water-rights systems, and distinguish stewardship concepts from the legal idea of water as an appropriable resource.
  2. Explain the doctrines of prior appropriation and riparian rights and how federal reclamation and state water law marginalized Indigenous nations.
  3. Analyze Winters v. United States (1908) and the doctrine of reserved water rights, including the importance of priority dates and the principle that rights may exist before water is put to use.
  4. Distinguish recognition, quantification, and implementation of a water right, using Arizona v. California (1963) and later settlements.
  5. Assess the significance of the Colorado River Compact of 1922, including its exclusion of Indigenous nations and its preservation of federal obligations to tribes.
  6. Explain the McCarran Amendment and Arizona v. San Carlos Apache Tribe (1983), and why general stream adjudications became long and costly.
  7. Compare litigation and negotiated settlements as strategies for securing Indigenous water, including benefits and criticisms of each.
  8. Evaluate treaty-based instream and ecological water claims in the Klamath, Columbia, and Truckee systems, and distinguish them from consumptive irrigation rights.
  9. Interpret the Ninth Circuit's 2017 Agua Caliente decision and the Supreme Court's 2023 Arizona v. Navajo Nation decision, identifying what each did and did not decide.
  10. Describe the growth of tribal water-quality regulation under the Clean Water Act and distinguish eligibility from approved programs.
  11. Compare Indigenous water governance in the United States, Canada, and Mexico, and explain why water at the tap can lag behind legal rights.
  12. Interpret the 2026 Colorado River operating decisions and Canada's proposed First Nations Clean Water Act with precision about their legal status.

Historical Context

The American West is defined as much by aridity as by any political boundary. Much of the interior receives limited precipitation, and many communities have depended on snowmelt, rivers fed from distant mountains, springs, and groundwater. Indigenous societies adapted to these conditions in different ways: irrigated farming in the Southwest and parts of the Great Basin, floodwater farming, intensive salmon fisheries on the Columbia, Klamath, and coastal rivers, lake-based fisheries in the Great Basin, and mobile use of springs and seasonal water on the Colorado Plateau. These were governed systems with norms about access, labor, timing, and obligation, even where they left few written records.

The United States claimed sovereignty over much of this region in the nineteenth century through treaty, conquest, and annexation, including the transfers discussed in Chapters 69 through 72. It then created Indian reservations by treaty, statute, agreement, and executive order, often on lands that settlers considered marginal. Reservations were intended, in the language of federal policy, to support Indigenous people while encouraging farming and settled life. Those purposes depended on water, but federal officials and Congress seldom addressed the question directly. Meanwhile, non-Indigenous miners, farmers, ranchers, and towns developed their own claims to water, and states and territories built legal systems for recording and protecting them.

Two features of that environment shaped the later conflicts. First, reservations were frequently created before nearby non-Indigenous users had diverted large volumes of water. Second, the dominant western water doctrine rewarded the earliest use of water, and Indigenous farming, often interrupted by federal policy and by lack of capital, did not always generate early recorded use. The tension between a reservation's creation date and a settler's actual diversion became the heart of Indian water law.

This chapter builds on earlier discussions of treaty rights (Chapter 106), the fish wars (Chapter 113), tribal courts and sovereignty doctrine (Chapter 118), and land recovery (Chapter 119). It leaves detailed treatment of fisheries management to Chapter 135, extractive industries to Chapter 136, and Arctic and climate questions to Chapter 138. It uses the term Indian water rights where law and sources use it, while preferring specific national names where possible.

Section 120.1 · Division I — Indigenous Water Relationships and the Foundations of Western Water Law

Indigenous Peoples, Waterways, and the Governance of Ancestral Waters

Long before any western state wrote a water code, Indigenous communities across North America organized their lives around specific rivers, lakes, springs, and aquifers. Water supplied drinking water and food, but it also structured settlement, labor, trade, ceremony, and political relationships. Because evidence for these systems comes from archaeology, oral tradition, ethnography, and later documents, historians must be cautious about generalizing, and they should avoid treating "Indigenous water philosophy" as a single belief system. What can be said is that many nations developed durable, place-specific rules about access and obligation.

In the Southwest, Pueblo farming communities along the Rio Grande and its tributaries built and maintained irrigation works and organized labor, ceremony, and community decision-making around the timing of water. The Hohokam of the Sonoran Desert constructed extensive canal systems in the Salt and Gila valleys, which archaeologists have studied through canal remains and settlement patterns; their relationship to the later O'odham peoples, including the Akimel O'odham of the Gila River, is discussed in Chapter 17 and involves both descendant-community understandings and scholarly debate. On the Columbia and its tributaries, salmon fisheries at places such as Celilo Falls supported regional trade and seasonal gatherings and were governed by customary rules about fishing sites and first-salmon ceremonies. Diné relationships with springs, seeps, and seasonal waters across the Colorado Plateau tied pastoral movement, ceremonies, and family land-use to scarce and dispersed sources of water.

These examples differ greatly, and they should not be flattened into a single model. Irrigation farming required collective maintenance of ditches; fisheries required cooperation among villages and attention to migration timing; desert pastoralism depended on knowledge of many small sources. What they shared was that water was embedded in social obligation. The legal system that later developed in the American West, by contrast, increasingly treated water as a quantity that could be claimed, measured, transferred, and sold. Indigenous concepts of stewardship, which this textbook discusses more fully in Chapter 133, are not identical to legal categories such as "beneficial use," and students should avoid assuming that the two vocabularies translate neatly.

The central point for this chapter is chronological. Indigenous water management existed before the creation of western state water-rights systems. When courts later recognized Indian water rights, they were not conferring an entirely new relationship with water, though the particular legal form of a "right" was a product of American law.

Section 120.2

Settler Expansion, Irrigation, and the Rise of Western Water Law

American settlement in the arid West created a water problem that eastern law did not anticipate. The eastern tradition of riparian rights gave owners of land bordering a stream a right to reasonable use of its water, which suited regions with abundant rainfall. In the West, miners and farmers needed to move water away from streams to diggings and fields. During and after the California Gold Rush, mining camps developed customs that rewarded the first person to put water to use, and courts and legislatures turned these customs into the doctrine of prior appropriation, often summarized as "first in time, first in right." Many western states adopted it in constitutions or statutes, sometimes alongside riparian principles.

Historical Vocabulary
Prior Appropriation

Definition. A system in which water rights are established under applicable law for recognized beneficial uses, with earlier priorities generally served before later ones during shortage. Historically appropriation commonly required diversion; some states also recognize instream uses without physical diversion.

Historical context. The doctrine grew out of nineteenth-century mining and irrigation practice in the arid West and was later formalized by state law, which usually required a permit and a record of use.

Why it matters. The system measured rights by use and by date. Because Indigenous nations were frequently excluded from the political bodies and institutions that recorded claims, and because their reservations were often not irrigated early, the doctrine seemed to favor later non-Indigenous users.

Potential misunderstanding. Prior appropriation does not mean that the oldest human use of a river counts as the oldest right. It counts the date of an appropriation recognized under state law.

Federal policy then accelerated development. The Reclamation Act of 1902 created a federal program to build dams, canals, and irrigation works, financed by repayment from water users, and the Bureau of Reclamation became one of the most powerful agencies shaping the region. Projects such as the Newlands Project in Nevada, discussed in Section 120.12, delivered water to farms while reducing flows to downstream Indigenous communities. State governments established offices to record and prioritize claims, and irrigation districts organized local landholders. Indigenous nations were seldom represented in these institutions, in part because they were not treated as citizens of the states for many years and in part because federal officials supervised their lands as wards.

Federal Indian policy compounded the problem. Allotment, discussed in Chapter 86, and leasing arrangements shifted some irrigable reservation lands to non-Indigenous owners, while federal irrigation projects on reservations were sometimes designed to serve non-Indigenous settlers as much as Indigenous farmers. When later non-Indigenous appropriators claimed the water that reservations needed, the conflict forced a question that neither state law nor federal statute had clearly answered: did a reservation established before a settler's diversion carry its own claim to water, and if so, from what date?

Section 120.3

Winters v. United States, 1908, and the Doctrine of Reserved Water Rights

The Fort Belknap Reservation in north-central Montana was established through an agreement in 1888 with the Gros Ventre and Assiniboine peoples, who ceded much of their larger earlier reservation and retained a smaller area along the Milk River. The surrounding land was semi-arid. Federal officials and the agreement's language anticipated that the residents would become farmers and stock raisers, a goal that required irrigation. Within a few years, settlers upstream, including Henry Winters and others, built dams and ditches that diverted the Milk River's waters, and the United States brought suit on behalf of the reservation's residents.

In Winters v. United States (1908), the Supreme Court held that the agreement creating the reservation had impliedly reserved enough water to fulfill the reservation's purposes. The Court reasoned that the lands were arid and would be nearly useless for the intended agricultural purposes without water, and that the Indians had not intended to give up the water on which the reservation's usefulness depended. It also rejected the argument that the later admission of Montana as a state, and the state's water law, defeated the earlier federal reservation. Because the reservation was created in 1888, its water right carried a priority date of 1888, earlier than the claims of the upstream defendants.

Historical Vocabulary
Reserved Water Right (the Winters Doctrine)

Definition. A federal-law right to the amount of water necessary to fulfill the purposes of a federal reservation, implied when the reservation is created.

Historical context. The doctrine arose from Winters and was later applied to other reservations, and, in some contexts, to federal lands that were not Indian reservations.

Why it matters. It meant that a tribe's right did not depend on having already diverted or used the water and did not need to comply with the state's appropriation system. The priority date is generally the date of the reservation's creation, though treaty-based rights may reach earlier.

Potential misunderstanding. A reserved right is not the same as a delivered supply. The doctrine says that a right exists, but it does not itself build ditches, fund pumps, or comprehensively quantify a particular reservation’s entitlement.

The doctrine's importance lies in a combination of features. First, the right arises from the creation of the reservation rather than from use. Consequently, a tribe could hold an unused right, with an early priority, that cut ahead of later farms and towns. Second, because the right is a creature of federal law, it is not extinguished by non-use under state law. Third, the priority date matters because, in a river with limited water, earlier rights are served first. Together these features gave tribes a powerful legal claim.

The actual litigation was more concrete than a declaration of abstract principle. The lower court restrained interference with the reservation’s use of 5,000 inches of Milk River water, a flow measured in the historical miner’s-inch convention; the Supreme Court affirmed the decree. That protection should not be confused with an adjudication of every future reservation need. The federal reserved-rights doctrine generally attaches to water available and unappropriated at the reservation date, to the extent necessary for the reservation’s purposes. It can defeat later appropriations, not simply erase every earlier valid right. See the opinion in Winters.

Yet Winters left much unsettled. It did not comprehensively quantify the reservation’s full present and future entitlement, how reserved rights would be administered alongside state-law rights, or who would pay for the works needed to deliver water. For decades, federal agencies promoted irrigation projects that served non-Indigenous users while the Indian rights recognized in Winters remained undeveloped. Meanwhile, nonuse allowed other users to build economies on water that the doctrine implied belonged first to reservations. The long tension between early recognition and delayed implementation is the thread that runs through the rest of the chapter.

Primary Source Window

Winters v. United States, 207 U.S. 564 (1908)

Creator: The United States Supreme Court · Date: 1908 · Type: Judicial opinion

Context: The United States sued on behalf of the Indians of the Fort Belknap Reservation to stop upstream appropriators from diverting Milk River water. The case turned on the meaning of an 1888 agreement and on whether Montana's later statehood or state water law defeated the reservation's claim.

Paraphrase of the holding (not a quotation): The Court concluded that the agreement reserved water sufficient for the reservation's purposes, that the right dated from the reservation's creation, and that later appropriators under state law could not defeat it. The opinion treated the reservation of land and the reservation of water as inseparable because the land's intended agricultural use required water.

Analysis: The decision is significant for its method as much as its result. The Court did not find an express water clause in the agreement. It inferred the right from purpose and circumstance, an approach that later courts extended to other reservations. Students should read the opinion's reasoning about the dry climate and the agreement's purposes, and the way it treats the Indians' interests.

Limitations: A judicial opinion states a legal conclusion, not the daily experience of Gros Ventre and Assiniboine families, and it does not comprehensively quantify all reservation needs. The opinion's language about the Indians also reflects the assumptions of its time about Indigenous peoples and the aim of agricultural assimilation, which students should read critically.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the Court and the federal government litigating as trustee. The reservation's residents were not the named parties, and the opinion does not present their own account.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about how the reservation's water right arose?
  2. Who created it, and in what circumstances?
  3. What purposes may have shaped the Court's reasoning, including assumptions about farming and assimilation?
  4. What can historians learn from it about the early twentieth-century relationship between federal law and state water systems?
  5. What can they not establish from the opinion alone, such as the total future entitlement or the practical delivery of water?

Students should consult the official text in the United States Reports (207 U.S. 564), available in the full judicial opinion.

Section 120.4

Arizona v. California and the Quantification of Tribal Water Rights, 1963

The Colorado River became the main arena in which reserved rights acquired numbers. After decades of conflict among California, Arizona, and Nevada over the Lower Basin's share of the river, Arizona sued California in the Supreme Court, and the United States intervened to assert claims for federal facilities and Indian reservations. A special master, Simon Rifkind, took evidence and made recommendations, and the Court's opinion in Arizona v. California (1963) was followed by a decree in 1964.

The Court held that the United States had reserved water for five Indian reservations along the river: the Colorado River Indian Reservation, the Fort Mojave, Chemehuevi, and Cocopah reservations, and the Yuma (Quechan) reservation. It adopted the special master's approach of measuring the water reserved for those reservations by the amount needed to irrigate all practicably irrigable acreage within them. The March 9, 1964 decree established separate annual diversion ceilings: Chemehuevi, 11,340; Cocopah, 2,744; Yuma (Quechan), 51,616; Colorado River, 717,148; and Fort Mojave, 122,648 acre-feet. Their sum was 905,496 acre-feet. These original ceilings were subject to the decree’s alternative limit based on the amount required to satisfy irrigation consumptive use and related uses, whichever was less. Priority dates were tied to the relevant land reservations, sometimes with multiple dates for different parcels. An acre-foot is the volume of water that would cover one acre to a depth of one foot.

The decree counted mainstream consumptive use on reservations within the apportionment for the relevant state. That accounting rule did not convert federal reserved tribal rights into state-created appropriations. Nor is a diversion ceiling identical to the volume permanently consumed: part of water diverted for use may return to the river and remain available for others. The distinction is especially important when comparing the original 1964 decree with the 1984 supplemental decree or with modern shortage accounting. A tribal right’s legal basis, priority, permissible quantity, and actual annual use must be examined separately.

The decision showed why recognition alone was insufficient. Without a quantity, a reserved right could not be compared against the claims of states and districts, and without a priority date, it could not be placed in the order of service. By supplying both for the five reservations, the Court made reserved rights concrete and made them part of the Colorado River's allocation system. Later proceedings addressed additional claims and disputed boundaries. The Court rejected certain attempts to reopen earlier determinations but issued supplemental decrees adjusting some quantities, including in 1984. The 1964 figures are therefore a historical baseline, not a statement of current entitlements.

Students should be careful about what the case did not establish. The practicably irrigable acreage standard was the method used to quantify water for those reservations in that case, and later courts have not applied it as an automatic universal rule. Some courts have used other measures, and critics have argued that an irrigation-based standard is poorly suited to reservations whose purposes include fisheries, homeland needs, or non-agricultural uses. Still, Arizona v. California remained a landmark because it joined the doctrine of Winters to a method of measurement and to the practical politics of a major river.

Section 120.5 · Division II — Tribal Water Claims, Adjudication, and Negotiated Settlements

The Colorado River Compact of 1922 and the Exclusion of Indigenous Nations

The Colorado River Compact, negotiated in 1922 by representatives of seven basin states with a federal representative presiding, divided the river between an Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and a Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada). It allocated quantities of water to each basin, and it became the first component of what is often called the Law of the River, a body that now includes statutes, court decisions, treaties, contracts, and operating rules, among them the Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928, the 1944 treaty with Mexico, and later basin legislation.

Indigenous nations were not parties. The compact's negotiators were state commissioners, and the federal government, which held trust responsibilities to the tribes, did not give them a seat. Yet the compact did not extinguish tribal rights. Article VII provided that nothing in the compact affected the obligations of the United States to Indian tribes. Students should read that provision carefully: it preserved existing federal obligations, but it did not itself define the tribes' rights, nor did it specify from which state's apportionment the water for tribes would come.

The omission mattered because negotiators allocated water without a clear accounting of the claims that tribes could later assert. Reclamation records and later studies showed that the river's average flow was lower than the figures some negotiators had assumed, which increased the strain on any system in which large reserved rights remained unquantified. In Arizona v. California, the Court treated water for the Lower Basin reservations as a charge against the apportionment of the state in which each reservation lies, a point of continuing importance to state negotiators.

The political consequence was a basin-wide system built without direct Indigenous participation. Over the following century, tribes entered the system through litigation, settlements, and eventually negotiation over operating rules, as described in Section 120.19. Their claims became important in disputes over water already allocated, diverted, or promised to others, precisely because the compact had not erased them.

Section 120.6

State Water Adjudication, the McCarran Amendment, and Tribal Legal Claims

To sort out competing claims on a river system, western states conduct general stream adjudications, proceedings in which a court identifies and ranks all water rights on a stream or basin. Because reserved rights are federal, one question was whether state courts could hear them. The McCarran Amendment of 1952 provided that the United States consents to be joined as a defendant in comprehensive state adjudications of water rights where it owns or is in the process of acquiring rights. In Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States (1976), the Supreme Court held that the amendment extended to water rights the United States holds in trust for Indians.

In Arizona v. San Carlos Apache Tribe (1983), the Court confirmed that state courts have concurrent jurisdiction to adjudicate Indian reserved rights in qualifying adjudications and that federal courts should generally defer in such cases. Students should distinguish procedure from substance. The decision allowed state courts to determine competing claims, but those courts were obliged to apply federal law to the content of reserved rights. State law did not become the measure of what a reservation's right was.

The consequences were practical and heavy. Tribes that preferred federal courts, which they viewed as more familiar with federal Indian law and less exposed to local political pressure, found themselves litigating in state forums. General stream adjudications could become extraordinarily lengthy, in some Western states lasting decades, involving thousands of claimants, extensive hydrological and historical evidence, and expensive expert testimony. Tribes needed lawyers, engineers, historians, and hydrologists, and many tribal governments had limited budgets. These burdens help explain why negotiated alternatives, discussed next, became attractive.

Section 120.7

From Litigation to Negotiated Indian Water Rights Settlements

Beginning in the late 1970s, Congress began approving agreements that resolved tribal water claims by negotiation rather than by judicial decree. Early examples included the Ak-Chin settlement of 1978, and the number grew over succeeding decades into dozens of settlements, including agreements involving the Crow Tribe, the Blackfeet Nation, the White Mountain Apache Tribe, Taos Pueblo, the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, and the Navajo Nation in specified basins. Federal policy developed criteria and a working group within the Department of the Interior to guide negotiations, and in 2021 the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act created a substantial fund to help meet federal settlement obligations.

A typical settlement involves several parties: the tribe, the federal government, one or more states, and often irrigation districts, municipal suppliers, and other water users. The agreement may quantify the tribe's water, identify sources, authorize storage and delivery facilities, provide funds for construction and operation, permit leasing or marketing of water under specified conditions, and resolve competing claims. In return, the tribe commonly waives certain claims against other users and the United States, and agrees to limits on particular uses. Settlements requiring federal appropriations, new statutory authority, or congressional ratification must secure those approvals; other agreements may be concluded under existing authority. Courts often incorporate settlement terms into a decree.

The strengths of negotiation are clear. A settlement may deliver a defined quantity of water, with money to build the facilities needed to use it, years earlier than a court could. It can reduce legal uncertainty for non-Indigenous water users, which can build political support. It also allows creative arrangements, such as exchanging a tribe's senior but undeveloped right for a firmer supply from a project.

The criticisms are also real. Some tribal leaders and legal scholars argue that settlements can require Indigenous nations to compromise strong legal claims because of the cost and delay of litigation and the political leverage of competing users. Others note that Congress has not always provided the full funding, and that a settlement's wet water may depend on infrastructure that takes decades to build. The measure of a settlement is therefore not only its legal text but whether the promised water has actually reached the people who need it, a question taken up in the case of the Gila River Indian Community.

Settlement architecture also reflects the difference between paper entitlements and dependable supplies. A decree establishing a senior entitlement can leave the owner responsible for finding a point of diversion, building delivery infrastructure and obtaining operating funds. By negotiating these elements together, tribal governments seek a workable institutional arrangement rather than simply a favorable judgment. The resulting agreements differ substantially: a water budget in one settlement is not a measure of all water available to every other nation.

Section 120.8

The Gila River Indian Community and the Arizona Water Settlements Act of 2004

The Gila River Indian Community, home of the Akimel O'odham (River People) and Pee Posh (Maricopa), occupies a reservation south of present-day Phoenix. Nineteenth-century accounts describe O'odham farming along the Gila, and the community supplied grain to travelers and the United States Army in the mid-nineteenth century. As non-Indigenous settlers established farms upstream and diverted the river, flows reaching the reservation dwindled. By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the community faced serious hardship, and the Gila's lower channel was often dry. A 1935 decree in a federal case recognized the Community's rights in relation to upstream users, but legal recognition did not restore dependable irrigation, and the federal government's own projects were not enough to make good the loss.

The Central Arizona Project, authorized in 1968 and constructed over the following decades, eventually carried Colorado River water to central and southern Arizona through a canal system that reached Tucson in the early 1990s. It created a new, expensive water source, and it also became bound up with tribal claims, because Congress and negotiators used CAP water as a means of settling Indian water rights in Arizona. The Gila River Indian Community pressed its claims for decades and participated in negotiations among Arizona water users, the state, the federal government, and other tribes.

The Arizona Water Settlements Act of 2004 resolved a cluster of Arizona claims, including the Gila River Indian Community's. The Community's settlement provided an annual water budget of 653,500 acre-feet from the Central Arizona Project, Gila River, Salt River, and groundwater, along with funding for infrastructure and agreements on how the water could be used and marketed. The settlement also required the Community to waive certain claims, and its implementation depended on federal appropriations and on construction of facilities to carry the water onto Community lands.

The Gila River settlement illustrates the chapter's central distinction. As a legal act, it was a major achievement: it recognized and quantified rights and ended litigation. As an engineering and institutional undertaking, it was equally large, requiring canals, delivery systems, management capacity, and long-term financing. The Community has also participated actively in Colorado River conservation arrangements, including drought agreements in 2019, in which it agreed to leave some water in Lake Mead. The Gila River case therefore demonstrates both the achievement of an entitlement and the ongoing work of making that entitlement usable.

Section 120.9 · Division III — Treaty Fisheries, River Systems, and the Struggle over Instream Water

Treaty-Protected Fisheries and the Right to Maintain River Flows

Not every Indigenous water claim concerns diversion. Many treaties secured rights to fish, hunt, and gather, and those rights lose their value if the streams, lakes, and wetlands that sustain the resources dry up or warm beyond what fish can survive. The legal question is whether a treaty right to fish carries with it a right to enough water to keep the fishery alive. This chapter treats that question as one of water allocation and instream flow, leaving the history of treaty-fishing protests to Chapter 113 and fisheries management to Chapter 135.

Historical Vocabulary
Instream Flow, Consumptive Use, and Firming

Definitions. Instream flow is water that remains in a river, lake, or wetland to support fish, habitat, or other values. Consumptive use is the part of a withdrawal that does not return to the relevant water system for reuse, for example through evaporation, transpiration, incorporation into crops or transfer out of the basin. A withdrawal may include substantial return flow. Firming is a legal or physical commitment that makes a water supply more reliable during shortages.

Why it matters. A right to leave water in a stream is different from a right to divert it, and courts have treated the two differently. Firming determines who receives water when supplies decline.

Potential misunderstanding. A right that appears large on paper may be less valuable if it lacks a senior priority or a firming commitment in a drought.

The Klamath Tribes (the Klamath, Modoc, and Yahooskin Band of Snake peoples) signed a treaty in 1864 that reserved their homeland in the upper Klamath Basin in south-central Oregon and secured hunting, fishing, trapping, and gathering rights. The federal government terminated the tribes' federal status in the 1950s, and the termination took effect in 1961, before the tribes were restored in 1986. In Kimball v. Callahan (1974), the Ninth Circuit had held that termination did not extinguish the tribes' treaty hunting and fishing rights on former reservation lands. In United States v. Adair, the Ninth Circuit (November 15, 1983, modified January 24, 1984; Supreme Court review denied June 18, 1984) recognized that the tribes' water rights included the amount needed to protect the resources on which those rights depended, with a priority reaching back to "time immemorial" rather than to the date of the treaty, because the treaty confirmed an existing way of life.

In Adair, the agricultural reserved right and the nonconsumptive treaty-resource right had different historical foundations and priority dates. The former dated to the 1864 reservation; the latter protected pre-existing resource uses confirmed by treaty. The court recognized a right to prevent other appropriators from depleting water below the level needed to sustain the treaty-protected resources, not an unlimited entitlement to the entire river. Establishing the levels necessary for particular species and habitats required evidence and subsequent adjudication. The case shows why measuring all Indigenous water claims exclusively by acreage irrigable for farming would miss an important part of their legal and historical content.

Section 120.10

The Columbia River Basin: Dams, Hydroelectric Power, and Indigenous Water Interests

In the twentieth century, the Columbia River and its largest tributary, the Snake, were transformed into one of the most heavily engineered river systems in North America. Bonneville Dam was completed in 1938 and Grand Coulee in the early 1940s, followed by many additional dams built for hydropower, flood control, navigation, and irrigation. The Grand Coulee Dam blocked salmon migration to a large part of the upper Columbia, affecting peoples such as the Colville, Spokane, and others. Celilo Falls, a major fishing and trading place for Columbia Plateau peoples, was inundated in 1957 by the reservoir behind The Dalles Dam. The four lower Snake River dams were built between the early 1960s and mid-1970s.

The treaties of 1855 with the Yakama, the Nez Perce, the Umatilla, and the Warm Springs peoples reserved rights to fish at their usual and accustomed places, and tribal governments argued that the federal government's operation of dams was a matter of treaty responsibility. The federal government, through the Bureau of Reclamation, the Army Corps of Engineers, and the Bonneville Power Administration, balanced power generation, flood control, irrigation, and fisheries obligations, and decisions about reservoir levels, spill, and flow timing determined whether migrating fish survived. The 1964 Columbia River Treaty between the United States and Canada, which coordinated flood control and power, was negotiated without tribal or First Nations participation, and tribes and First Nations pressed for inclusion when negotiations to modernize it began in 2018.

For this chapter, the emphasis is on control of flows and river operations. Tribes formed the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission in 1977 to coordinate technical and legal work. Federal legislation in 1980 required regional planning to take fish and wildlife into account. In December 2023, the federal government, four lower Columbia treaty tribes, and the states of Oregon and Washington announced the Resilient Columbia Basin Agreement, which paused litigation while committing to restoration, tribal clean-energy projects, and study of replacing the services of the lower Snake River dams. On June 12, 2025, a presidential memorandum directed federal agencies to withdraw from the December 2023 memorandum of understanding and associated commitments; disputes returned to litigation. The dams remain owned and operated by federal agencies, and removing them would require congressional action. The Columbia case shows that an Indigenous interest in water may take the form of authority over operations, not an allocation of a fixed quantity.

Section 120.11

The Klamath Basin: Irrigation, Drought, Tribal Rights, and Dam Removal

The Klamath Basin spans the Oregon–California border. In the early twentieth century, the federal Klamath Project drained and irrigated lands around Upper Klamath Lake and Tule Lake, creating a farming economy, while private hydroelectric dams were built along the river. Together, these developments reduced habitat for salmon and for the endangered C'waam (Lost River sucker) and Koptu (shortnose sucker), fishes of cultural importance to the Klamath Tribes. Downstream, the Yurok and Karuk peoples and the Hoopa Valley Tribe depended on salmon of the Klamath for food and ceremony.

In 2001, a severe drought and requirements under the Endangered Species Act led federal managers to withhold project irrigation water, provoking intense protest from farmers. In 2002, deliveries were restored, and in September of that year tens of thousands of adult salmon died in the lower river in one of the largest fish kills recorded on the West Coast; estimates of the mortality vary, and investigations linked it to low, warm flows and disease conditions. The episode became a defining event for tribes, farmers, and agencies. Tribes issued calls on upper-basin water in later years, drawing on rights recognized after Adair, and the Oregon adjudication process confirmed tribal claims in the upper basin, though litigation and administration have continued.

Negotiations in the 2000s produced the Klamath Basin agreements, including agreements concerning dam removal that were amended in 2016, and a nonprofit, the Klamath River Renewal Corporation, took responsibility for removing four dams: Copco 1, Copco 2, J.C. Boyle, and Iron Gate. Copco No. 2 was removed in 2023; reservoir drawdown for the remaining three began in January 2024, and the project announced completion of dam removal on October 2, 2024, among the largest dam-removal projects in United States history. Reports from monitoring programs documented salmon returning to habitat above the former dams within the first months, and scientific monitoring continues through 2026. Students should be careful not to equate dam removal with the resolution of the basin's water-rights disputes: irrigation allocation, drought operations, endangered species protections, and upper-basin tribal rights remain subjects of continuing negotiation and administration.

Section 120.12

The Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe, the Truckee River, and the Recovery of Water Flows

Pyramid Lake in northwestern Nevada is the terminal lake of the Truckee River, which flows from Lake Tahoe. For the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe (Kuyuidokado, "cui-ui eaters"), the lake and river have long supplied fish, including the cui-ui, a lakesucker found nowhere else, and the Lahontan cutthroat trout. The reservation was set aside under Interior Department directives in 1859 and confirmed by President Ulysses S. Grant’s executive order of March 23, 1874.

The federal Newlands Reclamation Project, one of the earliest projects under the Reclamation Act, built Derby Dam in 1905 to divert Truckee River water to farmland in the Carson Sink area. With less water reaching the lake, its level fell dramatically over the following decades, on the order of eighty feet by the 1960s, and the river's delta eroded, blocking fish from spawning runs. The lake's native cutthroat population disappeared, and the cui-ui was listed as endangered in 1967.

The Pyramid Lake Paiute pursued litigation and political action for generations. A 1944 federal decree, the Orr Ditch Decree, recognized a tribal right for irrigation on the reservation but did not secure water for the lake's fishery. In its November 8, 1972 opinion and February 20, 1973 supplemental opinion and order in Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe v. Morton, a federal court held that the Secretary of the Interior had to consider the tribe's interests in operating the project, and in Nevada v. United States (1983), the Supreme Court held that the earlier decree barred the tribe from relitigating certain additional water claims, a significant setback. The tribe then helped advance negotiation, and Congress passed the Truckee–Carson–Pyramid Lake Water Rights Settlement Act in 1990, followed by the Truckee River Operating Agreement, signed in 2008 and implemented in December 2015 and coordinates reservoir operations in part to provide flows for fish and water quality.

The case is instructive because the tribe sought restoration of a functioning aquatic environment, not only an allocation for human consumption or irrigation. It also shows that legal outcomes can turn on procedural questions such as preclusion, and not only on the substance of rights. Recovery of the lake's fisheries has continued over time and remains a matter of management and monitoring discussed further in Chapter 135.

Section 120.13 · Division IV — Groundwater, Water Quality, and the Right to Safe Water

Groundwater and the Agua Caliente Decision of 2017

Groundwater is water stored in the pores and fractures of rock and sediment beneath the surface. An aquifer is a layer that holds and transmits enough water to supply wells and springs. Groundwater can be recharged by rainfall, rivers, and other sources, but pumping faster than recharge lowers water tables, and overextraction can dry wells and springs and cause land to subside. In arid Indigenous territories, groundwater has long mattered for springs, wells, and ceremonial sites, and it has become more important as surface supplies are overallocated.

Reserved rights doctrine was developed largely for surface water. In Cappaert v. United States (1976), the Supreme Court protected reserved water in the Devils Hole pool, within a national monument, against depletion by nearby groundwater pumping. The Court did not decide that all groundwater beneath a federal reservation was reserved. The question for tribes was whether the doctrine itself could encompass groundwater as a reserved resource. The Agua Caliente Band of Cahuilla Indians, whose reservation includes much of Palm Springs, California, sued the Coachella Valley Water District and the Desert Water Agency in a case over groundwater beneath the reservation. In 2017, the Ninth Circuit held that the federal reserved-rights doctrine can extend to groundwater and that the tribe's rights were not displaced by state groundwater law. The Supreme Court declined review on November 27, 2017.

Students should read the decision precisely. The Ninth Circuit recognized the principle that reserved rights can include groundwater, but the decision did not itself quantify the tribe's entire groundwater entitlement or settle all questions about water quality and use, which required further proceedings. Its significance lies in applying the Winters doctrine to an increasingly important category of water in a region where aquifers support farms, cities, golf courses, and reservations alike. Other courts and states are not bound to follow the Ninth Circuit outside its jurisdiction, and the law of tribal groundwater rights continues to develop.

Section 120.14

Arizona v. Navajo Nation, 2023, and the Limits of Federal Treaty Obligations

The Navajo Nation (Diné) reservation spans parts of Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. The Treaty of 1868, which ended the Bosque Redondo captivity described in Chapter 71, set aside a reservation and allowed the Diné to return to their homeland. The Nation's rights to water in the Colorado River system remain not fully quantified in several basins, and large parts of the reservation depend on scarce local sources. In the Lower Basin, the Nation's claims involve the Little Colorado River and tributaries, and general stream adjudications in Arizona have continued for decades.

In litigation that reached the Supreme Court as Arizona v. Navajo Nation, the Navajo Nation asked courts to require the United States to assess its water needs and develop a plan to secure water for the reservation from the Lower Colorado River. In June 2023, the Court, by a vote of five to four in an opinion by Justice Kavanaugh, held that the 1868 treaty reserved necessary water for the reservation but did not impose the specific affirmative duty the Nation sought, namely a duty on the United States to take steps to secure water for the Nation. Four justices dissented, in an opinion by Justice Gorsuch.

The majority’s reasoning turned on the distinction between a substantive treaty right and a specific enforceable trust obligation. It required identification of a treaty, statute, or regulation imposing the particular affirmative obligation alleged. In the majority’s view, the reservation of water did not itself require the United States to undertake the planning or measures requested. Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson, disputed both that characterization of the claim and the interpretation of the treaty relationship. He understood the Nation to be asking that its trustee identify rights already held for it and address interference with them. The two opinions thus differed not simply about sympathy for water scarcity, but about the nature of the claim, the relevant obligations, and the role of courts in enforcing them. Neither opinion fixed a quantified Navajo entitlement.

Section 120.15

Tribal Water Quality Standards and the Clean Water Act

Water sovereignty includes authority to regulate water quality, not only to hold or divert it. The federal Clean Water Act of 1972 set national goals for protecting the nation's waters, and amendments in 1987 authorized the Environmental Protection Agency to treat eligible Indian tribes in a manner similar to states for specified programs. A tribe that qualifies may, among other things, set water-quality standards for waters within its reservation, designate uses such as fishing, drinking supply, and cultural uses, and have its standards considered in federal permitting for upstream discharges. Courts upheld this approach in cases involving the Isleta Pueblo's standards on the Rio Grande and the Sokaogon Chippewa Community's standards in Wisconsin, in decisions in the 1990s and early 2000s that confirmed tribal authority to set standards that could be more protective than those of upstream states.

Eligibility must be distinguished from approval. EPA determines whether a tribe is eligible for treatment in a manner similar to a state for a particular program, and a tribe must then develop and submit standards that EPA reviews. In the EPA chart consulted in November 2026, the agency listed 85 tribes as eligible to administer water-quality-standards programs, with approved standards for 54 of those tribes. EPA separately identified federally promulgated standards for the Colville Reservation, excluded from the count of tribal standards approvals. These categories should not be conflated. Developing standards requires monitoring data, staff, legal and scientific expertise, and funding, and many tribal environmental departments have built that capacity over decades.

The institutional significance is considerable. Tribal environmental offices now monitor streams, inspect facilities, develop water-quality codes, and participate in federal permitting reviews. This regulatory work shows that Indigenous water sovereignty involves institutions and technical capability, not only ownership of a right or access to a supply. It also connects to the conflicts over mining and energy discussed in Chapter 136, where water pollution has often been central.

Section 120.16

Water at the Tap: Infrastructure, Public Health, and the Gap between Rights and Access

The guiding question of this section is: What does a legally recognized water right accomplish when a community lacks the means to deliver safe water to its households? The answer begins with a distinction among four things that are often conflated: a legal allocation, a source of water, a treatment and delivery system, and a household connection that people can afford and use safely.

Navajo Nation communities illustrate the gap. The reservation is large, sparsely populated in many areas, and crossed by rough roads, so that extending pipelines to scattered homes is costly. Many households have long relied on hauling water in barrels or tanks from a distant source, sometimes from unregulated livestock wells that may contain contaminants, and the legacy of uranium mining discussed in Chapter 136 has raised additional concerns about some sources. A University of Arizona expert account published June 23, 2023, reported an estimate of roughly 30 percent of households on the reservation lacking running water. This is a dated estimate, not a measured November 2026 household rate; running water, complete indoor plumbing and reliably safe drinking water are distinct measures. A widely cited 2019 report found that Native American households were far more likely than white households to lack complete indoor plumbing. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the difficulty of hand-washing without running water was frequently cited as one contributing factor, along with crowded housing and limited health care, in the severe impact on the Navajo Nation.

Several institutions work on the problem. The Navajo Tribal Utility Authority, a tribal enterprise, extends water and other utility service. The Indian Health Service has built and improved sanitation facilities since Congress authorized the work in 1959. Reclamation, through rural water projects, has constructed regional systems such as the Navajo-Gallup Water Supply Project in New Mexico, authorized as part of a 2009 settlement. Nonprofit organizations also help install household cisterns and connections. Yet responsibility is divided among federal agencies, tribal governments, and sometimes states and utilities, and funding has often been insufficient or inconsistent.

Everyday Life and Social History

Water, Households, Agriculture, and Community Continuity

For a family hauling water, the abstract legal distinctions of this chapter become daily labor: driving to a fill station, storing water in barrels, rationing it among cooking, drinking, washing, and livestock, and planning trips around weather and road conditions. The labor falls unevenly, often on women, elders, and children, and it shapes education, employment, and health. Families with piped service plan differently, and the difference is one of the clearest everyday measures of the gap between rights and access.

Water also sustains farming and foods. Pueblo and Hopi communities have cultivated traditional crops using ditches or dry-farming techniques, and loss of water threatens not only income but seed lines and knowledge. Salmon, cui-ui, and suckers anchor ceremonies and food systems for river and lake peoples, and when dams or diversions harm these fish, the injury is cultural and nutritional as well as economic. In Gila River, the return of reliable water has allowed the Community to expand agriculture on its own lands. The intergenerational effect is that children who grow up near a restored river or a functioning irrigation ditch inherit a different set of possibilities from those who grow up near a dry channel.

Source note. Evidence for everyday life comes from oral histories, tribal and nonprofit surveys, health and utility reports, and ethnographic work. Estimates vary, and students should note who produced each source and for what purpose.

The difference between a legally recognized allocation and affordable household service is therefore both technical and political. A tribe may need not only water but money for treatment plants, trained operators, and long-term maintenance, and it may face the practical question of whether a household can pay for service. The experience of reservations shows that rights, infrastructure, and operating capacity must advance together.

Section 120.17 · Division V — Continental Water Governance and Contemporary Struggles

First Nations, Drinking Water, and Water Governance in Canada

Canada's legal framework differs from the American doctrine of federally reserved rights. Under the Constitution Act, 1867, the federal Parliament has authority over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians," while provinces have authority over many matters concerning water and local services off reserves. On reserves, the federal government has historically funded water and wastewater infrastructure, while First Nations operate systems, and for many years no comprehensive regulatory regime governed drinking water on reserves in the way that provincial law governs it elsewhere. Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, protects Aboriginal and treaty rights, a framework discussed in Chapter 117, but Canadian courts have not developed a doctrine of reserved water rights equivalent to Winters.

Shoal Lake 40 First Nation, an Anishinaabe community in Treaty 3 territory on the Manitoba–Ontario border, provides a stark case. In the early twentieth century, the City of Winnipeg built an aqueduct drawing drinking water from Shoal Lake, completed in 1919. Construction of the intake and a diversion channel separated the community's reserve from the mainland, leaving it on an artificial island. Winnipeg residents received water from the lake, while the First Nation lacked a reliable all-season road and, for years, safe drinking water; a boil-water advisory remained in effect from 1997 until the community's new treatment plant allowed it to be lifted in September 2021. An all-season road, called Freedom Road, opened in 2019 after sustained advocacy and with funding from several governments.

Across Canada, long-term drinking water advisories, defined as advisories lasting at least a year, became a national political issue. The federal government committed in 2015 to end them within five years and did not meet the 2021 target. The 2013 Safe Drinking Water for First Nations Act was repealed in 2022 after criticism that it lacked adequate funding and First Nations participation. A class action settlement approved in 2021, valued at roughly eight billion dollars, provided compensation and required infrastructure commitments. In Parliament's 2026 reporting, long-term advisories remained in effect in dozens of communities despite substantial progress since 2015; the Minister of Indigenous Services stated on October 9, 2026 that 38 remained in 36 communities, and that government count should be read as an official figure rather than the only possible measure.

Bill C-37, the proposed First Nations Clean Water Act, was introduced in the House of Commons on June 16, 2026, together with a federal funding commitment announced at $4.6 billion over five years. It incorporates much of an earlier bill, C-61, which died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025. As of the date of this chapter's latest revision, Bill C-37 had passed second reading and was referred to a House of Commons committee on October 9, 2026; it remained proposed legislation, not enacted law, and First Nations organizations, including the Assembly of First Nations, have called for it to be strengthened through engagement with First Nations. Students should keep the distinction between a bill, a funding announcement, and delivered water in view.

Section 120.18

The Yaqui River, Indigenous Water Rights, and Territorial Governance in Mexico

The Yaqui (Yoeme) people live in eight pueblos along the lower Río Yaqui in Sonora, Mexico, where the river supported agriculture and community life. Yaqui resistance to colonial and later national encroachment, including the wars of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and the deportations of many Yaqui people, belongs to the political history treated in Chapter 141. What matters here is that the river's waters became a focus of conflict as farmland in the valley was developed for commercial agriculture, and that control of the water became closely tied to control of Yaqui territory.

In 1937, President Lázaro Cárdenas issued a decree restoring territory to the Yaqui, and a later decree is commonly described as allocating a substantial share of the river's flow to them, though the exact legal effect has been disputed. Meanwhile, the federal government built major dams on the river and its tributaries, including Angostura, Álvaro Obregón (Oviáchic), and El Novillo, and irrigation districts developed for non-Indigenous growers. Mexico's national water system vests ownership of waters in the nation and grants concessions to users, so Yaqui claims have often been pursued through administrative, constitutional, and judicial channels rather than through a doctrine like Winters. Conflict deepened after the construction of the Independence Aqueduct in the early 2010s, which carries water to the city of Hermosillo; Mexican courts ordered consultation with Yaqui communities, and protests, legal action, and violence against some Yaqui defenders of territory and water followed.

Mexico's Yaqui Justice Plan, begun in the early 2020s, was designed to address historical grievances, and on December 3, 2023 the federal government transferred the hydroagricultural infrastructure of Irrigation District 018 to the Yaqui people. Under the 2021 decree establishing the district, the Comisión Jiaki del Agua, constituted through the eight Yaqui communities’ own authorities or representatives, was assigned responsibility for irrigation-water administration. The transfer assigned the commission operation, conservation and maintenance of the works, as well as administration of water within the district. This is an important development, but students should distinguish it from unrestricted ownership of the entire river system: the transfer concerns irrigation infrastructure and management responsibilities within a national water framework that continues to govern the river.

The district demonstrates the importance of distinguishing community authority from national water ownership and from actual delivery capacity. An Indigenous-administered organization responsible for canal operations and maintenance can determine how irrigation service is organized within its defined jurisdiction, while upstream allocation, reservoir releases and the national concessions system still affect the water reaching it. The establishment of the district in 2021 and the subsequent transfer of infrastructure in 2023 were separate legal and administrative events. Whether the arrangements fulfill historical Yaqui claims must also be assessed through the operation of the works, the reliable availability of water, and the decisions of the communities themselves. The founding decree and Conagua transfer announcement document these distinct institutional steps.

The Yaqui case helps establish a continental comparison. Indigenous water sovereignty has been pursued through reserved rights and settlements in the United States, through treaty, constitutional, and regulatory debates in Canada, and through territorial restitution and administrative transfer in Mexico. These frameworks are not equivalent, yet in each, Indigenous nations have had to contend with state ownership or control of water and with large infrastructure built for others.

Section 120.19

The Colorado River Crisis, Tribal Water Rights, and Basin Governance through 2026

By the early twenty-first century, the Colorado River's reservoirs had been drawn down by prolonged drought, warming temperatures, and demands that exceeded dependable supply. Lake Powell and Lake Mead fell to historically low levels, and continued drought and reduced runoff placed reservoir operations under further pressure in 2026. Federal and state officials adopted interim guidelines in 2007 and drought contingency plans in 2019, and those arrangements were due to expire at the end of 2026.

Tribal participation grew over the same period. Roughly thirty federally recognized tribes lie within the Colorado River Basin, and their claims to the river are commonly estimated to total a substantial share of its flow, though many remain unquantified. The Colorado River Basin Ten Tribes Partnership, formed in the 1990s, brought together tribes with major rights to coordinate their positions, and other tribes, including those without settled rights, pressed for participation and for recognition that their claims are legal rights rather than surplus. Tribes sought consultation on a government-to-government basis, and several, including the Gila River Indian Community, took an active role in conservation and operating discussions.

The seven basin states were unable to reach agreement on successor guidelines. Reclamation published its final environmental impact statement on July 31, 2026, and on August 21, 2026, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum signed a Record of Decision adopting a ten-year decision framework for coordinated operations of Lake Powell and Lake Mead covering 2027 through 2036, and issued operating guidelines for 2027 and 2028 only. Federal officials described the framework as leaving room for continued negotiation. Reclamation described provisions for conserved water and tribal supply firming in its announcement. Such mechanisms are governed by the specific eligibility and operating terms of the guidelines and existing settlement obligations; they should not be read as universal guarantees of water delivery to all basin nations.

The international dimension also continued to evolve. On September 2, 2026, representatives of the United States and Mexico signed Minute 334 of the International Boundary and Water Commission, providing cooperative measures through December 31, 2028. The measures address drought-related reductions, salinity and possible additional supplies. The minute complements the federal reservoir-operating framework but is not itself a settlement of the water rights of Indigenous nations on either side of the border. See Reclamation’s September 9 statement.

The 2026 decisions should be understood for what they are: operating rules for reservoir management for specified years, not a comprehensive resolution of tribal rights or of the allocation disputes among states. Tribal governments had raised legal, infrastructure, and water-access questions during the federal review, and those questions were not resolved by the record of decision. Negotiations involving seven states, the federal government, tribes, and Mexico continue, and Indigenous nations insist that their rights cannot be treated as surplus claims. The Colorado River case brings the chapter's central pattern into the present: exclusion from the original design of the system, gradual entry through law and negotiation, and continued struggle for a decision-making role equal to the stakes.

Historical Debate

Do Reserved Water Rights Provide Effective Indigenous Sovereignty?

The question: Does the reserved-rights framework, as it developed after Winters, give Indigenous nations meaningful authority over the waters on which their communities depend, or does it provide a legal entitlement that remains hard to use?

Interpretation A — Reserved rights as an important legal protection: Because reserved rights arise from federal law, carry early priority, and do not depend on use, they protected Indigenous claims against the appropriation system and became the foundation for later settlements and negotiations. On this view, the doctrine gave tribes bargaining power that otherwise would not have existed.
Interpretation B — Recognition as incomplete without infrastructure and enforcement: Others emphasize that rights have often remained undeveloped for generations, that irrigable-acreage measures can favor agriculture over other purposes, that courts have limited enforceable federal duties, as in 2023, and that many households still lack service. On this view, legal recognition without funding and delivery leaves sovereignty partial.
Interpretation C — Negotiated basin management as a means of reconciling claims: A third approach argues that comprehensive negotiation among tribes, states, and federal agencies can deliver water and facilities more reliably than litigation, while acknowledging that settlements may require compromise and that basin-wide arrangements may still treat tribes as junior participants.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same record: Winters, Arizona v. California, settlement statutes, funding histories, household-service data, and tribal statements. They differ in how they weigh legal doctrine against practical outcomes.

Points of disagreement: Scholars and participants differ over interpretation and weighting rather than over the basic facts: whether settlements represent success or compromise, how much weight to give the delay between recognition and delivery, and whether sovereignty should be measured by legal status or by practical control.

Why it matters: How one answers shapes whether the priority should be litigation, negotiation, infrastructure funding, regulatory authority, or participation in basin governance.

Student task: Distinguish a legal entitlement from the institutional and economic ability to use it, then identify one case in this chapter that best supports each interpretation and explain what additional evidence could test it.

Section 120.20

The Historical Legacy of Indigenous Water Rights and Sovereignty

The chapter's cases follow a pattern. Winters recognized a category of reserved rights tied to reservations. Arizona v. California showed how a court could quantify such rights, though not as a universal formula. The McCarran Amendment and the 1983 decisions placed many claims in state-court adjudications, and settlements offered a negotiated alternative. Treaty-based claims such as those of the Klamath Tribes extended the idea of a water right to the maintenance of rivers and lakes, and cases on groundwater, water quality, and household service widened the field further. In 2023, Arizona v. Navajo Nation demonstrated how a recognized right may still not generate an enforceable federal duty to deliver water.

Cause and Effect

From the Winters Doctrine to Modern Water Settlements

Long-term causes

Creation of reservations in arid lands; western settlement and the doctrine of prior appropriation; federal reclamation; exclusion of Indigenous nations from state water institutions.

Short-term causes and trigger

Upstream diversions on the Milk River in the early twentieth century and the United States' decision to sue on behalf of the reservation's residents.

Immediate effects

Winters recognized reserved rights, but quantity, administration, and delivery remained unsettled, and many rights stayed undeveloped for decades.

Long-term consequences

Quantification in Arizona v. California, lengthy adjudications, negotiated settlements, struggles over infrastructure and ecological flows, and regulatory authority.

Historical complexity: The path was not inevitable. Different court decisions, congressional choices, and federal funding decisions could have produced different outcomes, and tribal advocacy, drought, endangered-species law, and political coalitions shaped each case. No single cause explains the shift from litigation toward settlement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Water Authority, 1908–2026

What Changed

  • Tribes moved from exclusion from allocation decisions to participation in adjudications, settlements, regulatory programs, and basin negotiations
  • The doctrine expanded from surface water for irrigation to groundwater, instream flows, and water quality
  • Settlement statutes and funds provided money and facilities for some nations
  • Dam removal on the Klamath showed that river restoration could be achieved through coalition and negotiation

What Stayed the Same

  • Delay between recognition and delivery persisted, and many communities still lack dependable service
  • Indigenous nations continued to defend ancestral waters against competing demands from agriculture, cities, power, and industry
  • Basin-wide arrangements were still largely negotiated among states and federal agencies, with tribes pressing for equal standing

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual, uneven, and contested. Nations with senior rights and strong legal capacity experienced the most direct gains, while communities with unquantified rights or limited infrastructure experienced greater continuity of hardship.

Comparison across the continent reveals distinct legal routes to related objectives. In the United States, the reservation-based federal right and its priority may constrain later water users, yet a tribe may still require settlement funds and infrastructure to put that right to work. Canadian First Nations may assert Aboriginal and treaty rights and seek safe-water governance and infrastructure through constitutional, legislative and negotiated mechanisms that do not reproduce the United States’ priority-date doctrine. In Mexico, the Yaqui district illustrates the transfer of specific administrative authority and infrastructure within a national system of water governance. These differences matter when assessing sovereignty: a right of allocation, an obligation of consultation, an operating mandate and a household service connection each address a different problem.

The history also makes visible a recurring temporal imbalance. Settlers and public agencies were often able to build diversions and distribution networks first, while Indigenous nations had to litigate or negotiate for decades to obtain comparable facilities. Later recognition could not instantly undo the resulting distribution of capital, ecological damage, or administrative capacity. Tribal advocacy changed legal priorities and institutional arrangements, but meaningful assessment of those changes requires evidence of flows, water quality, affordable connections, and decision-making responsibilities over time, rather than treating an enacted statute or judicial victory as the endpoint.

The competing goals of irrigation, fisheries protection, household water access, groundwater security, hydroelectric generation, ecological restoration, and conservation are not always compatible. Indigenous nations have sometimes allied with other users and sometimes confronted them, and they have themselves held different priorities. Nations have gained greater authority, through legal recognition, regulatory programs, and negotiated agreements, without in every case achieving practical control over the resources needed for self-government. The principal historical lesson is that water sovereignty depends not merely on the formal recognition of water rights, but on the ability of Indigenous nations to secure, protect, distribute, and govern the waters upon which their communities and territories depend.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Indigenous efforts to secure legal and practical authority over water from the early twentieth century to November 2026. It began with the recognition that Indigenous nations governed and relied on rivers, lakes, springs, and aquifers long before western water law existed, and that the later legal systems of prior appropriation and federal reclamation treated water as a resource claimed by use and largely excluded Indigenous nations from decision-making.

The foundation of the modern legal struggle was Winters v. United States (1908), which held that the creation of the Fort Belknap Reservation impliedly reserved the water needed for its purposes, with a priority date tied to the reservation's establishment and a right that did not depend on prior use. The doctrine gave tribes a powerful claim. The injunction protected a specified flow, but comprehensive quantification, administration, and the means of delivery remained open.

Quantification came in specific cases. In Arizona v. California (1963–1964), the Supreme Court recognized and quantified rights for five Colorado River reservations, using practicably irrigable acreage as the measure in that case. The chapter emphasized that this was not a universal rule, and that the Colorado River Compact of 1922, negotiated without tribes, preserved federal obligations to Indian tribes rather than extinguishing their claims.

Enforcement shifted toward state forums and negotiated agreements. The McCarran Amendment and Arizona v. San Carlos Apache Tribe allowed comprehensive state adjudications to include reserved rights, producing long, costly proceedings. Congress increasingly approved settlements that quantified rights, authorized infrastructure, and resolved claims, as in the Gila River Indian Community's settlement under the Arizona Water Settlements Act of 2004. Such settlements delivered benefits but required compromises, and they demonstrated the difference between an entitlement and the facilities needed to use it.

Treaty-based claims added an ecological dimension. In the Klamath, Columbia, and Truckee systems, Indigenous nations sought water left in rivers and lakes to sustain fisheries and habitat. United States v. Adair recognized an instream treaty right with a time-immemorial priority, the Klamath dams were removed in 2024, and Pyramid Lake Paiute efforts produced a settlement and operating agreement that coordinated flows. The Columbia case showed that control of river operations can matter as much as allocation, and that agreements may be reversed by later policy, as with the Resilient Columbia Basin Agreement.

Groundwater, water quality, and household access extended the field. The Ninth Circuit's 2017 Agua Caliente decision applied reserved rights to groundwater without quantifying the whole entitlement. Arizona v. Navajo Nation (2023) distinguished a reserved right from a judicially enforceable federal duty to secure water. Clean Water Act authority enabled tribes to build regulatory institutions, and Navajo Nation communities illustrated how legal claims can coexist with water hauling and limited service.

The continental comparison showed different frameworks. Canada has addressed First Nations drinking water through federal funding, regulation, litigation, and proposed legislation, with Bill C-37 introduced in June 2026 and not yet enacted. Mexico has pursued Yaqui territorial and water claims through national water law and restitution, including the 2023 transfer of Irrigation District 018 infrastructure. In the Colorado River Basin, the 2026 federal decisions provided operating rules for specified years while leaving tribal rights and basin-wide allocation questions unresolved.

The chapter's argument is that Indigenous water sovereignty rests on the ability to secure, protect, distribute, and govern water, not only on legal recognition. Indigenous nations have gained greater authority over water than at the start of the twentieth century, yet that authority has frequently remained incomplete without money, infrastructure, ecological conditions, and a seat at the table where allocation decisions are made. These themes carry forward to the discussion of economic development in Chapter 121, fisheries stewardship in Chapter 135, and climate change in Chapter 138.

Key Terms

Reserved water right
A federal-law right to the water needed to fulfill the purposes of a federal reservation, implied at its creation.
Winters doctrine
The principle derived from the 1908 Supreme Court decision that reservations carry implied water rights with a priority date tied to their establishment.
Prior appropriation
A western water-law system under which rights are established for legally recognized beneficial uses, with earlier priorities generally served first in shortage; physical diversion is not universally required.
Riparian right
A right of landowners bordering a water body to make reasonable use of its water, common in wetter regions.
Priority date
The date that determines a right's place in the order of service during shortage.
Acre-foot
The volume of water needed to cover one acre to a depth of one foot.
Practicably irrigable acreage
A measure used in Arizona v. California and other cases that quantifies reserved water by the land on a reservation that could feasibly be irrigated.
Quantification
The legal or negotiated determination of the amount of water a right entitles its holder to use.
General stream adjudication
A comprehensive court proceeding that determines and ranks all water rights on a stream system.
McCarran Amendment
A 1952 federal statute consenting to joinder of the United States in qualifying comprehensive state water adjudications.
Settlement
A negotiated agreement, usually approved by Congress, resolving tribal water claims in exchange for defined water and resources and waivers of certain claims.
Firming
A commitment that makes a water supply more reliable during shortage.
Instream flow
Water left in a river, lake, or wetland to support fish, habitat, and related values.
Consumptive use
The portion of water withdrawn that is not returned to the relevant water system for reuse; distinct from total withdrawal.
Time immemorial
A priority date for treaty-confirmed rights reflecting use that predates the treaty.
Groundwater
Water stored beneath the surface in soil and rock.
Aquifer
A geologic formation that holds and transmits usable quantities of groundwater.
Law of the River
The collective body of compacts, statutes, decrees, treaties, and operating rules governing the Colorado River.
Treatment in a manner similar to a state
A Clean Water Act status allowing eligible tribes to administer specified programs, including water-quality standards.

In Canadian federal reporting, a drinking-water advisory lasting at least one year.
Trust responsibility
The federal government's legal and moral obligations toward Indian nations, whose enforceable scope depends on treaties, statutes, and case law.
Water sovereignty
The ability of an Indigenous nation to secure, protect, distribute, and govern the waters on which its community depends.
Irrigation district
A local entity organized under state law to deliver irrigation water to landholders, often holding long-standing appropriative rights.

A legal doctrine barring relitigation of claims already decided, which affected the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe in 1983.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because water makes visible the difference between recognition and power. A century of litigation, negotiation, and construction has established that Indigenous nations hold important rights to water, yet the pattern of delay shows that rights alone do not ensure water in a river, a canal, or a household. Understanding that gap helps explain why Indigenous governments have combined legal strategy with administration, engineering, and coalition-building.

The chapter also reveals how Indigenous water struggles connect different regions and periods. The Winters doctrine shaped later cases on the Colorado, Klamath, and Truckee. The treaty tradition linked fisheries on the Pacific coast with Great Basin lakes. The Canadian and Mexican cases showed that Indigenous nations confronted state control of water under quite different legal systems, and that the aim of authority over water has been continental in scope.

The developments discussed here shaped later questions in this textbook. Economic development, treated in Chapter 121, often depends on water delivery. Fisheries and wildlife stewardship in Chapter 135 depends on the flows secured here. Climate change in Chapter 138 intensifies scarcity and raises the stakes of allocation. Continuities that survive beyond the chapter include the insistence that Indigenous nations be participants in decisions rather than objects of consultation.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger argument that Indigenous nations are continuing political communities. Water sovereignty is not a symbolic matter: it concerns whether communities can farm, fish, drink, and govern themselves on their own terms. The history of water rights shows both real gains and persistent limits, and it asks students to keep both in view.
