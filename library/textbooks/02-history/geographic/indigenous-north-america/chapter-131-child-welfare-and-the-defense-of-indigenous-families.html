Child Welfare and the Defense of Indigenous Families

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In 2005, a five-year-old Cree boy named Jordan River Anderson died in a Winnipeg hospital after spending more than two years there, unable to move to a family home because governments could not agree on which of them should pay for his care. His story became a symbol for a larger question: who decides how Indigenous children are cared for, who pays for it, and what happens when jurisdictions argue while a child waits?

Jordan River Anderson was born in 1999 to a family from Norway House Cree Nation in Manitoba. He had a serious medical condition and spent much of his short life in hospital. According to the accounts that led to the policy named in his memory, medical staff concluded that he could live in a family setting, but the federal and provincial governments disputed responsibility for the cost of home care. In December 2007 the House of Commons of Canada unanimously adopted a motion supporting what became known as Jordan's Principle: the government that first receives a request for a service for a First Nations child should pay for it and settle any dispute over jurisdiction afterward. The principle is a modern one, but the problem behind it is old. For well over a century, decisions about where Indigenous children would live, who would raise them, and what knowledge they would inherit were made in contests among families, Indigenous nations, churches, charities, and governments that held very unequal power.

This chapter examines that contest as it developed through adoption, foster care, and the administration of child welfare in the United States and Canada, from the colonial regulation of Indigenous households through the legal reforms and court decisions of the early twenty-first century. Its central argument is that child welfare cannot be understood only as a series of individual custody decisions. The removal of Indigenous children also concerned jurisdiction, kinship, cultural continuity, and the authority of Indigenous nations to protect and raise the next generation. When governments and agencies separated large numbers of children from their families, they affected not only those households but the capacity of communities to reproduce their languages, relationships, and political life. When Indigenous parents, grandparents, tribal governments, First Nations organizations, lawyers, and social workers organized against removal, they turned what had been treated as a private or administrative matter into an assertion of collective rights and self-government.

That argument requires care. Abuse and neglect of children are real, and Indigenous communities have themselves insisted on protecting children from harm. This chapter therefore does not treat every separation as unjustified, nor does it treat child protection as inherently suspect. Its question is how institutional standards, funding arrangements, racial and class assumptions, and competing political authorities shaped decisions about which children were removed, why, by whom, and with what consequences. Historians and tribunals have found that poverty, inadequate housing, and unequal access to services were often treated as evidence of neglect, and that governments sometimes funded removal more readily than they funded help for families who could have kept their children safe at home.

The chapter gives substantial and balanced attention to the United States and Canada while preserving the differences between their legal systems. In the United States, the central statute is the Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978, rooted in Congress's authority over Indian affairs and in the political relationship between the federal government and tribal nations, and upheld against a major constitutional challenge in Haaland v. Brackeen in 2023. In Canada, the central framework is the Act respecting First Nations, Inuit and Métis children, youth and families, enacted in 2019, which affirms an Indigenous right of self-government in child and family services and was held constitutionally valid by the Supreme Court of Canada in 2024. These are not interchangeable models. They rest on different constitutional foundations, assign authority differently among federal, state or provincial, and Indigenous governments, and operate in different institutional settings.

The evidence for this history is varied and uneven. Statutes, congressional and parliamentary records, court decisions, and the findings of the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal provide detailed documentation of law and policy. Agency reports, adoption files, and statistical series show patterns of placement, though these data have well-known limitations: the categories used to count Indigenous children changed over time, records were often incomplete, and adoption files were sometimes sealed from the people they concerned. Testimony, memoirs, community histories, and oral accounts from adoptees, birth parents, grandparents, and social workers show how removal was experienced and resisted, and they challenge the tendency of official records to describe families only through the observations of outsiders.

The chapter's boundaries matter. The residential and boarding school systems that also separated children from their families are the subject of Chapters 127 and 128, and this chapter refers to them only where they help explain the origins of child welfare intervention. Chapter 129 treats violence against Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people, and Chapter 130 addresses criminal justice and jurisdictional conflicts; both intersect with child welfare but are not retold here. Chapter 132 examines public memory, monuments, and decolonization. The focus of this chapter is adoption, foster care, child welfare legislation, family separation, and Indigenous authority over family services.

The narrative moves from Indigenous understandings of childhood and kinship, through colonial intervention and the expansion of modern child welfare systems, to the adoption and foster care practices of the mid-twentieth century and the movements that resisted them. It then follows the divergent American and Canadian legal paths, and concludes with the institutions through which Indigenous governments now exercise, and sometimes struggle to exercise, authority over the care and protection of children. The story is not one of simple victory. It is one of persistent resistance, partial reform, unresolved disparities, and a continuing effort to turn legal recognition into operational control.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the place of children, extended kinship, and customary adoption in Indigenous social systems, and why family relationships carried political and cultural significance beyond the household.
  2. Trace how colonial governments, missionaries, and administrators extended authority over Indigenous parents and children, and how this authority connected to assimilation policy.
  3. Describe how modern child welfare institutions, including juvenile courts, public welfare agencies, adoption procedures, and foster care, acquired power over Indigenous families.
  4. Distinguish documented threats to children's safety from conditions such as poverty and inadequate housing that were often treated as neglect.
  5. Analyze the Indian Adoption Project, the Sixties Scoop, and related practices, distinguishing documented policies from broader trends.
  6. Assess the varied experiences of children, birth parents, adoptive families, and relatives, without assuming that every placement produced identical outcomes.
  7. Explain the origins, principal protections, implementation, and constitutional challenges of the Indian Child Welfare Act, including what Haaland v. Brackeen decided and left undecided.
  8. Explain the roles of funding inequality, Jordan's Principle, and the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal in the reform of First Nations child and family services.
  9. Compare the constitutional foundations, jurisdictional structures, protections, and implementation mechanisms of ICWA and Canada's 2019 legislation.
  10. Evaluate the difficulty of converting legal recognition of Indigenous jurisdiction into functioning institutions, using concrete examples.
  11. Interpret competing arguments about how the best interests of Indigenous children should be determined and who holds authority to determine them.
  12. Connect the defense of Indigenous families to the broader history of Indigenous sovereignty and self-determination.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is child welfare in Indigenous history a question of jurisdiction and collective survival as well as individual custody?
  2. How did colonial assimilation policy and the expansion of public child welfare agencies connect to one another, and where did they differ?
  3. How can historians distinguish genuine child protection from discriminatory or unnecessary separation, and what evidence is needed to do so?
  4. What does it mean that the same disparities in poverty, housing, and services could be treated as evidence of parental failure rather than of government underfunding?
  5. Why did the United States and Canada develop different legal responses to the removal of Indigenous children, and what were the consequences of those differences?
  6. How did Indigenous parents, tribal governments, First Nations organizations, and lawyers turn local family crises into national political movements?
  7. What do court decisions such as Haaland v. Brackeen and the 2024 Canadian reference decide, and what do they leave unresolved?
  8. Why does legislative recognition of Indigenous authority not automatically produce functioning child and family services?
  9. How should historians weigh the testimony of adoptees, birth families, and adoptive families when their accounts differ?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter should bring three earlier lines of history with them. The first is the long history of Indigenous nations as political communities with their own laws, kinship systems, and ways of raising and educating children, introduced in Chapter 1 and developed throughout Volumes I through V. The second is the history of colonization and settler-state policy examined in Volumes VI through IX: removal, treaty-making, reservation and reserve systems, allotment, the Indian Act, and the assimilation policies that sought to transform Indigenous societies by reshaping their children. The third is the history of Indigenous political resurgence in the second half of the twentieth century, treated in Chapters 104 through 118, in which termination, relocation, Red Power, treaty-rights activism, and the era of self-determination changed what Indigenous governments could demand and how they could act.

Child welfare also belongs to the history of modern states. Beginning in the late nineteenth century and expanding sharply in the twentieth, governments in the United States and Canada built institutions to supervise childhood: juvenile courts, school attendance systems, public assistance, adoption law, foster care, and professional social work. These institutions were not designed with Indigenous nations in mind, and they were often built on assumptions about the nuclear household, parental fitness, and appropriate living conditions drawn from the experiences of non-Indigenous, middle-class families. When these institutions reached Indigenous communities, they did so within already unequal relationships shaped by treaty violation, land loss, and federal or provincial neglect of basic services. The result was that the same systems that protected many children also became a channel through which Indigenous families were disproportionately investigated and separated.

Finally, students should recognize that the legal frameworks for Indigenous peoples differ across the two countries at the center of this chapter. In the United States, federally recognized tribes are domestic political entities with inherent sovereignty, subject to the plenary authority of Congress, and tribal courts and governments exercise defined powers over their members and territories. In Canada, the Constitution Act, 1867 assigns Parliament authority over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians," while provinces and territories administer most social services, and section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis peoples have distinct legal and political circumstances. These differences, which Chapters 117 and 118 introduce, explain why the same underlying problem produced different statutes, and why the term "Indigenous child welfare" can obscure as much as it reveals unless the jurisdiction is specified.

Division I — Indigenous Families and the Origins of Child Removal Section 131.1

Indigenous Childhood, Kinship, and Community Responsibility

Indigenous societies across North America organized childhood through relationships that extended well beyond the parents and children who make up the nuclear household familiar from many European and Euro-American models. Because Indigenous nations differed enormously in language, economy, and political form, as Chapter 1 emphasized, no single description applies to all of them. Yet many shared features that matter for this chapter: children were raised within webs of extended family, clan, and community; grandparents, aunts, uncles, and older siblings often held significant caregiving and teaching roles; and a child's relationships to relatives and to a nation carried obligations and identities that were social, political, and spiritual at once. Matrilineal clan systems, such as those of the Haudenosaunee and many Pueblo and southeastern nations, tied children to a clan through the mother's line. Lakota communities organized life around the tiospaye, an extended family group, and Diné kinship, expressed in the concept of k'é, defined proper relationships among relatives. Inuit communities likewise placed children within extended family networks, and Anishinaabe peoples understood belonging through doodem (clan) as well as through family and community.

These systems often included well-developed practices of customary adoption and fostering. Children could be raised by relatives or by other community members for many reasons: the death or illness of parents, a family's desire for a child, or the strengthening of ties between families. Among Inuit, customary adoption was widespread and often arranged within kin networks; among many other peoples, adoption into a family or a nation could follow warfare, diplomacy, or ceremony, as Chapter 53 discusses in connection with captivity and kinship incorporation. Such adoption was typically understood not as the severing of a child's ties to birth relatives but as an extension of relationship, and the adopted child usually remained within a community that knew the child's origins. Historians must be cautious about generalizing: customary practices varied across nations and changed over time, some were shaped by hierarchy and by gender, and written descriptions of them were often produced by outsiders who interpreted them through their own categories. Indigenous oral tradition, community histories, and, increasingly, Indigenous-authored scholarship are therefore essential evidence.

Understanding this background is necessary because the central conflict in this chapter is not simply between child safety and child endangerment. It is between different definitions of what a family is and who has authority over children. A government agency that regarded a grandmother's household as insufficient because it lacked certain amenities, or that regarded the sharing of child-rearing among relatives as neglect, was applying one conception of family while overriding another. At the same time, Indigenous communities were never untouched by harm: poverty, violence, addiction, and the consequences of earlier removals affected children in many places, and Indigenous governments and families have themselves sought ways to protect children from danger. The historical question is who has the authority and resources to do so, and whose standards govern.

Historical Vocabulary
Kinship Care

Definition: Care of a child by relatives or by members of the child's extended family or community, as opposed to placement with unrelated caregivers. In child welfare law, the term describes formal placements with relatives; in Indigenous contexts, it also describes ongoing customary relationships in which extended family members share child-rearing.

Historical context: Customary kinship caregiving long preceded modern child welfare systems. Child welfare agencies in the twentieth century frequently overlooked or devalued it, and many licensing standards made it difficult for relatives to qualify as foster or adoptive caregivers. Modern statutes and policies, including the placement preferences of the Indian Child Welfare Act and the priorities in Canada's 2019 legislation, give kinship placements formal priority.

Why it matters: Kinship care shows that the dispute over Indigenous child welfare was partly a dispute over which family forms the law would recognize.

Potential misunderstanding: Formal "kinship care" in a state licensing system and customary kinship caregiving are not identical. The former is a legal category with eligibility rules; the latter is a social system with its own obligations.

Section 131.2

Colonial Intervention in Indigenous Family Life

From the earliest period of sustained colonization, missionaries and officials viewed Indigenous child-rearing as a field for intervention. Spanish, French, and English colonial missions sought to educate and convert children, and later settler-state governments built assimilation policy around the premise that changing the way children were raised would change Indigenous societies within a generation. In the United States, this logic informed treaty provisions for education, federal support for mission schools beginning with the Civilization Fund Act of 1819, and the off-reservation boarding schools of the late nineteenth century. In Canada, it shaped the Indian Act, the partnership between the federal government and churches in residential schooling, and amendments in the early twentieth century that made attendance compulsory for many First Nations children. Chapters 87 and 88 examine these schools; Chapters 127 and 128 examine contemporary investigation and reckoning. This chapter emphasizes a related point: such institutions were one expression of a broader claim that governments and churches had the authority to supervise Indigenous households.

That broader claim operated through several mechanisms. Indian agents, in both countries, regulated reserve and reservation life, including marriage, residence, and school attendance, and in some places could compel parents to send children away. Guardianship, whether formal or informal, allowed officials to speak for Indigenous children in matters of property and schooling. Rationing, pass systems, and, in the United States, the withholding of benefits could be used as leverage over families. Missions and charities operated orphanages and children's homes, some of which accepted Indigenous children whom officials, or sometimes desperate relatives, placed there during illness, famine, or economic crisis. The motives of those involved varied. Some missionaries and officials genuinely believed they were rescuing children; some parents used schools or institutions strategically, to secure food or education for children; others resisted removal by hiding children, refusing to send them, or negotiating terms. The historical record, much of it produced by officials, must be read critically to recover these differences.

By the mid-twentieth century, large-scale institutional schooling was beginning to decline in some places, but the underlying assumption that Indigenous family life required outside supervision persisted. In the United States, Bureau of Indian Affairs boarding schools were still being used in the 1960s as placements for children whose families were deemed unable to care for them, a role later criticized in congressional hearings. In Canada, as the federal government began to transfer responsibility for services to the provinces and to reduce its direct role in running residential schools, provincial child welfare agencies increasingly took over the role of intervening in Indigenous family life. The transition from one set of institutions to another, rather than a clean break, is a central theme of the next two sections. Historians continue to debate how closely to link the schools to the later child welfare systems: some emphasize continuity of purpose and personnel, others emphasize the distinct legal, professional, and administrative character of child welfare, while acknowledging that the effects on families compounded one another.

Section 131.3

The Expansion of Modern Child Welfare Administration

The institutions that came to dominate child welfare in the twentieth century developed from reform movements of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Reformers established juvenile courts, beginning in the 1890s and spreading rapidly, on the theory that the state could act as a protective parent for children in difficulty. Societies for the protection of children, later public agencies, investigated allegations of cruelty and neglect. In the United States, the Social Security Act of 1935 created federal programs of aid to dependent children and child welfare services, and in the following decades federal funding increasingly shaped the practices of state agencies. In Canada, provinces developed children's aid societies and public welfare departments under their constitutional responsibility for social services. Adoption law, which in the nineteenth century had been fragmentary, was increasingly standardized, and the professionalization of social work introduced standardized assessments of homes, parents, and children.

For most of this period, these systems did not reach Indigenous families in a consistent way. In the United States, the federal government's responsibility for Indian affairs, and the reluctance of states to extend services to reservations, left a patchwork of responsibilities. Reservations were often treated as federal responsibilities, and states hesitated to provide services or to bear their costs. Several developments of the 1950s changed this. Federal termination policy, which Chapter 104 examines, sought to reduce the federal role in Indian affairs, and Public Law 280 of 1953 extended state criminal and certain civil jurisdiction over Indian country in some states. The combination of federal withdrawal, state expansion, and the broader growth of child welfare administration meant that state social workers and state courts increasingly made decisions about Indigenous children. In Canada, the 1951 amendments to the Indian Act, which made provincial laws of general application applicable to Status Indians unless they conflicted with treaties or the Act, provided a legal pathway for provincial child welfare agencies to operate in First Nations communities, though federal and provincial governments disputed who should pay.

The consequences depended on the standards these institutions applied. Social workers and judges had broad discretion in deciding whether a child was neglected, whether a home was fit, and whether a placement served the child's welfare. These decisions rested on professional training and cultural assumptions that were rarely examined. A home without indoor plumbing or with many occupants, a parent who left a child with relatives, or a family that depended on community sharing rather than wages might be judged inadequate. Child welfare law also relied on the idea that placement with unrelated families in other communities could be better for a child than remaining with relatives, a view reinforced by the then-prevailing emphasis on adoption as a path to stable family life. These institutional and professional frameworks, rather than any single policy, explain why Indigenous children came into the care of public agencies in rapidly increasing numbers during the 1950s through the 1970s.

Section 131.4

Poverty, Neglect, and Institutional Discrimination

The relationship between poverty and child removal is one of the most important and contested themes in this history. Many Indigenous families in the mid-twentieth century lived in conditions shaped by dispossession and government policy: reservations and reserves with limited economic opportunity, housing shortages, inadequate water and sanitation, remote locations far from health and social services, and legacies of earlier family separation. Child welfare statutes in both countries defined neglect in terms that could encompass the effects of such conditions, and social workers, many of whom were not Indigenous and had little knowledge of the communities, often interpreted them as evidence of parental failure. A family lacking adequate housing could, in some jurisdictions, be found to be neglecting its children, even though the housing shortage was a matter of public policy rather than parental choice.

Historians and, later, tribunals and courts have emphasized the need to distinguish actual threats to children's safety from conditions that might have been addressed by assistance. Serious physical abuse, abandonment, and severe neglect did occur, and removal was in some cases necessary and protective. But numerous studies, hearings, and judicial findings have concluded that in many other cases children were removed because of poverty, because of culturally unfamiliar child-rearing practices, or because of the judgments of outsiders about family structure, drinking, or housing, when support services, if available, could have allowed families to remain together. The line between the two is not always visible in the records, which were produced by the agencies making the decisions, and historians must be careful not to infer either that all removals were unjustified or that agency records reliably describe the reasons for them. The adequacy of the evidence differs across places and decades, and some of the best sources, such as the congressional testimony of the 1970s in the United States and the proceedings of the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal decades later, were produced through adversarial processes with their own purposes.

Institutional structure also mattered. Funding formulas often provided money for taking children into care but little for prevention or for supporting families at home. In the United States, federal child welfare financing historically reimbursed foster care placements more readily than preventive services, and tribes were for decades largely excluded from direct access to those funds. In Canada, as discussed in Section 131.14, federal funding for First Nations child and family services was calculated in ways that, according to the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal, created incentives to remove children. Judges, social workers, and agency supervisors worked within these constraints, exercising discretion in ways that reflected both professional norms and available resources. Understanding the role of such structures does not remove individual responsibility from decision-makers, but it explains why patterns of overrepresentation persisted across many jurisdictions, regardless of the intentions of individual workers.

Cause and Effect

Poverty, Discriminatory Funding, Institutional Decisions, and Unnecessary Family Separation

Long-term causes

Dispossession of land and resources, the dismantling of Indigenous economies, treaty and trust obligations unevenly fulfilled, the legacy of schooling and earlier removals, and the long-standing assumption that Indigenous households required outside supervision.

Short-term causes

Expansion of provincial and state child welfare systems in the 1950s and 1960s, jurisdictional disputes over responsibility for services on reservations and reserves, funding structures that rewarded placement over prevention, and the rapid professionalization of social work without Indigenous input.

Immediate trigger or context

An individual investigation by a caseworker or agency, often prompted by reports of poverty, overcrowding, parental drinking, or absence of caregivers, assessed against standards drawn from non-Indigenous households.

Event or transformation

Removal of the child and placement in foster care or adoption, frequently with non-Indigenous families and sometimes in distant communities, often without notice to the child's tribe or nation.

Immediate effects

Separation from parents, siblings, relatives, language, and community; in many cases multiple placements; loss of contact with the child's nation.

Long-term consequences

Disruption of community continuity, intergenerational effects on families, overrepresentation of Indigenous children in care, and eventual legal and political movements to reform the systems.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains why a particular child was removed. Poverty, discretion, funding, and cultural assumptions interacted, and in some cases genuine safety concerns were present alongside them. The historical task is to explain patterns without erasing the individual circumstances behind them.

Division II — Adoption, Foster Care, and Indigenous Family Separation Section 131.5

The American Indian Adoption Project and Mid-Twentieth-Century United States

The Indian Adoption Project, which ran from 1958 to 1967, was an organized effort to place Native children for adoption with non-Native families. It was administered by the Child Welfare League of America, a national organization of child welfare agencies, with support from the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the federal Children's Bureau. The project drew on a broader postwar emphasis on adoption as a solution for children thought to lack stable homes, and on the assumption of many welfare professionals that assimilation into mainstream society offered Native children better prospects. Records and later scholarship indicate that the project placed on the order of several hundred children, commonly cited as about 395, in adoptive families, most of them in the eastern United States, from tribes in several western states. The number is modest compared with the broader flow of Native children into non-Native foster and adoptive homes, and historians treat the project as one visible, documented element of a much larger pattern rather than as its sole cause.

That larger pattern developed through the activities of state and county welfare agencies, private adoption agencies, religious organizations, and federal officials. In the 1950s and 1960s, many Native children entered state foster care and adoption systems through court orders, agency decisions, or voluntary relinquishment, often with little participation by their tribes. Some religious programs also placed Native children with non-Native families, including a program of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints that, beginning in the 1950s, placed thousands of Native children with Latter-day Saint families for the school year. Participants' later accounts of such programs vary: some describe opportunities and supportive families, while others describe separation from family and community and loss of language and identity. Historians caution against treating these accounts as either uniformly positive or uniformly negative, while recognizing that the programs were designed and operated without the participation of the children's nations in decisions about their upbringing.

Documented policies must be distinguished from broader trends. The Indian Adoption Project, state agency policies, and individual programs are documented in reports, correspondence, and later research. The overall scale of Native child placement in the 1960s and 1970s is known mainly through surveys and testimony rather than a single comprehensive count. A series of surveys by the Association on American Indian Affairs, published in 1969 and 1974, together with congressional hearings in the mid-1970s, indicated that substantial proportions of Native children had been separated from their families, with estimates often cited at 25 to 35 percent of children in some contexts, and that the great majority of those children had been placed in non-Native homes or institutions. These figures come from advocacy research and testimony and varied by state and tribe; they should be read as indicating a serious and widespread pattern, with the caution that precise national totals depend on the definitions and records used.

Section 131.6

The Sixties Scoop and Canadian Child-Removal Policies

In Canada, the expansion of provincial child welfare systems into First Nations communities after the 1951 amendments to the Indian Act produced a rapid rise in the number of Indigenous children in care. Figures compiled in the 1960s and later cited by researchers indicate that in British Columbia, for example, Indigenous children, who made up a very small share of children in care in the early 1950s, accounted for roughly a third of them by the mid-1960s. A federal government study of the contemporary situation of Indians in Canada in the mid-1960s, the Hawthorn report, drew attention to the disproportionate number of Indigenous children in child welfare. Federal and provincial governments negotiated cost-sharing agreements, such as the 1965 agreement between Canada and Ontario, under which provinces extended services to reserves. Provincial social workers apprehended children, courts placed them in foster homes and, in many cases, put them up for adoption, and agencies frequently placed Indigenous children with non-Indigenous families in other communities, other provinces, and the United States.

The practice later became known as the Sixties Scoop, a phrase popularized by Patrick Johnston in his 1983 study of Native children and the child welfare system, which quoted a British Columbia social worker's description of the removal of Indigenous children. The term has been widely adopted, though historians note that removals did not begin or end in the 1960s; they continued into the 1970s, 1980s, and later. Some Indigenous writers use terms such as the "Millennium Scoop" to emphasize continuity with present-day disparities. Placement programs varied by province. Saskatchewan's Adopt Indian and Métis project, in operation in the late 1960s, publicly sought non-Indigenous adoptive families for Indigenous children. In Manitoba, the inquiry led by Judge Edwin Kimelman, whose 1985 report was titled No Quiet Place, documented the placement of large numbers of Indigenous children outside the province, including in the United States, and characterized the practice as cultural genocide. Provincial responsibilities and federal responsibilities were also contested: provinces administered child welfare, while Ottawa retained constitutional authority over Indians and funded services on reserves, and disputes over who should pay and who should supervise affected how children were treated.

The categories of children affected differed. Status Indian children living on reserves, Inuit children in northern communities, Métis children, and non-Status First Nations children fell under different legal and administrative arrangements, which later shaped who could seek redress. In 2017, the Ontario Superior Court in Brown v. Canada found that Canada had breached a duty of care to on-reserve Status Indian and Inuit children placed with non-Indigenous families in Ontario by failing to take reasonable steps to prevent the loss of their cultural identity. A national settlement, announced in 2017 and approved in 2018, provided compensation for Status Indian and Inuit survivors, while Métis and non-Status individuals, who had been excluded, pursued their own litigation and advocacy. Provincial governments issued formal apologies, including Manitoba in June 2015 and Alberta in May 2018. The federal government reached the Sixties Scoop settlement approved by courts in 2018, but that compensation agreement should not be conflated with a formal national federal apology. Total numbers remain uncertain; commonly cited estimates run to tens of thousands of children across the 1950s through the 1980s, but reliable counts are hampered by incomplete records and by changing definitions, and historians treat all totals as approximate.

Historical Vocabulary
The Sixties Scoop

Definition: A term for the large-scale removal of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children from their families by provincial and territorial child welfare authorities in Canada, and their placement in foster care or adoption, frequently with non-Indigenous families, beginning in the 1950s and continuing for decades.

Historical context: The expression was popularized by Patrick Johnston's 1983 report. Removals continued beyond the 1960s, and the phrase refers to a pattern rather than a single policy. Compensation and apology processes have used the term and have defined eligible periods and groups in legal terms.

Why it matters: The term links the apprehensions to a broader history of state intervention in Indigenous family life, and its use in litigation and public apologies has shaped how the history is remembered.

Potential misunderstanding: The term suggests a brief, single-decade episode and a single policy; in reality it covered multiple provinces, decades, and administrative practices, and many removals were carried out under ordinary child welfare law rather than a special program.

Section 131.7

Childhood, Adoption, Identity, and Family Separation

To understand the effects of these practices, historians turn from institutions to individuals. Adoptees, birth parents, siblings, grandparents, and adoptive parents have recounted their experiences in court testimony, memoirs, oral histories, documentary films, and community gatherings. Many adoptees describe growing up in non-Indigenous communities where they were isolated from other Indigenous people, sometimes facing racism, sometimes without knowing their nation or family of origin. Language loss is a recurrent theme: children placed at young ages often did not learn their ancestral languages, and reconnecting as adults could be difficult. Others describe questions of identity, belonging, and the sense of being between worlds, as well as the search for birth families, which could be impeded by sealed adoption records, changed names, or incomplete documentation. When reunification occurred, accounts describe a range of experiences, from joyful reunions to painful discovery that parents or relatives had died or that years of separation could not easily be bridged.

The diversity of experiences must be acknowledged. Some adoptees and foster children report that they were raised in loving homes and are grateful to their adoptive families, while also affirming that they were entitled to know their heritage. Some adoptive parents sought to maintain a child's connection to Indigenous culture; others did not, or lacked the means. Some children experienced abuse or neglect in foster or adoptive placements, and some experienced multiple placements. Birth parents describe grief, coercion, and in some instances voluntary relinquishment under pressure of poverty or the advice of caseworkers, as well as in some cases decisions that they themselves made for reasons they considered best for the child. Historians treat these accounts as essential and as differing in their implications; they do not assume uniform outcomes, and they take care not to place on individual adoptive families a responsibility that belongs largely to the systems and policies under which placements were made.

The effects extended beyond individuals to communities. Separation of children reduced the number of young people growing up in communities, interrupted the transmission of language, ceremony, and kinship knowledge, and left grandparents and parents without children. Indigenous communities have described the grief and disruption caused by absent children, and later movements for reunification, language revitalization, and cultural renewal, discussed in Chapters 123 and 146, have often been bound up with efforts to bring adoptees home. Scholars and Indigenous commentators also emphasize that removal, adoption, and residential or boarding school attendance often affected the same families across generations, so that one generation's experience compounded another's. Where records permit, historians trace these linkages with care, aware that individual family stories differ.

Multiple Perspectives

Removal and Its Consequences: Children, Families, Indigenous Governments, and Agencies

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that testimony, hearings, and scholarship have documented. They are not quotations from any single named individual, and individuals within each group held differing views.

From the perspective of an Indigenous child placed in a non-Indigenous home: Experience varied widely. Some children grew up in caring homes yet felt cut off from language, relatives, and nation; others experienced discrimination, instability, or abuse. Many later sought their birth families and nations as adults, and some describe the search as an effort to recover an identity they had been denied the chance to learn.
From the perspective of birth parents, grandparents, and relatives: Families often experienced removal as sudden, unexplained, and beyond their control. Some had relinquished children under pressure of poverty or illness; others had children taken after investigations that they did not understand. They commonly describe grief, a lack of information about where children had been placed, and efforts to reach them across distance and legal barriers.
From the perspective of tribal and First Nations governments: Leaders came to regard unchecked removal as a threat to the survival of their communities and to their authority as governments. Many argued that decisions about their children belonged to them as an exercise of jurisdiction, and that agencies without roots in their communities could not make such decisions responsibly. They organized to claim authority through law, litigation, and the creation of their own services.
From the perspective of child welfare agencies and social workers: Many workers saw themselves as protecting children from real harm, working with limited resources, and applying professional standards of the time. Some later acknowledged that those standards embodied cultural bias and that agencies lacked knowledge of Indigenous communities. Agency administrators also worked within funding formulas and legal mandates that they did not design.

Note on balance: These perspectives did not carry equal power. Children had the least ability to shape decisions about their lives, and families faced agencies and courts with legal authority over them. The purpose of the comparison is to show the complexity of the historical situation without suggesting equivalence.

Section 131.8

Indigenous Resistance and the Movement to Protect Families

Indigenous families and communities did not accept the removal of children passively. Parents contested apprehensions in court where they could afford counsel or find legal aid; grandparents and other relatives sought to take children in; community members hid children, sought assistance from elders, or appealed to tribal councils and band councils. These individual acts of resistance were often not recorded in official files, and historians draw on oral histories and community memory to document them. As removals accumulated, resistance took more organized forms. Tribal governments began to pass resolutions, create child welfare committees, and demand consultation. In the United States, the Association on American Indian Affairs, working with tribal leaders, conducted surveys, assisted tribes in litigation, and provided legal and public support. Native social workers, attorneys, and community advocates formed networks that shared information across reservations and cities.

In Canada, First Nations leaders and organizations took parallel steps. In 1980 the Spallumcheen Band in British Columbia, led by Chief Wilf Adam, adopted a by-law asserting band authority over the placement of its children, which led to an agreement with the provincial and federal governments in the early 1980s. Dakota Ojibway Child and Family Services, established in Manitoba in 1981, was among the first child and family service agencies operated by First Nations, and Mi'kmaw Family and Children's Services in Nova Scotia was established in the 1980s. These initiatives showed that First Nations could organize and deliver services themselves, though they operated within delegated authority from provinces and within federal funding structures that limited their independence. Provincial inquiries, notably Manitoba's Kimelman inquiry, brought public attention to the practices.

The movement reframed child welfare. Instead of treating each removal as an isolated custody case, advocates argued that the systematic separation of children threatened the survival of Indigenous nations as peoples and that Indigenous governments held the authority to protect them. This argument drew on the political status of tribes and First Nations, discussed in Chapter 1 and Chapter 114, and on the broader assertion of self-determination in the 1960s and 1970s. It combined legal strategies, such as asserting tribal jurisdiction and challenging state or provincial authority; public investigation, including surveys and hearings; and the creation of community-based services. These strategies led, in the United States, to the congressional action described in the next division, and in Canada to a longer and more fragmented process of reform through inquiries, agreements, litigation, and eventually legislation.

Division III — The Indian Child Welfare Act and the United States Section 131.9

Congressional Investigations and the Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978

The path to federal legislation ran through investigation. The Association on American Indian Affairs, directed by William Byler, published surveys in 1969 and 1974 documenting the placement of Native children outside their families and communities. In April 1974, the Senate Subcommittee on Indian Affairs, chaired by Senator James Abourezk of South Dakota, held hearings on Indian child welfare. Tribal leaders, parents, social workers, attorneys, and psychiatrists testified about the removal of children, the role of state and private agencies, and the effects on families and communities. Calvin Isaac, then a leader of the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians, was among those who described the experience of tribes. Additional hearings followed in 1977 and 1978, and the American Indian Policy Review Commission, created by Congress, reported in 1977 on the state of Indian affairs, including child welfare. Tribal governments and national Indian organizations, including the National Congress of American Indians, lobbied for legislation.

The testimony and surveys led Congress to find that an alarmingly high proportion of Indian families were being broken up by removal of children by nontribal public and private agencies, that a high proportion of those children were being placed in non-Indian homes and institutions, and that state agencies and courts often failed to recognize the cultural and social standards of Indian communities. The Indian Child Welfare Act, Public Law 95-608, was signed into law on November 8, 1978. Congress based it on its constitutional responsibility for Indian affairs and on the trust relationship between the United States and Indian tribes. Its declared policy was to protect the best interests of Indian children and to promote the stability and security of Indian tribes and families by establishing minimum federal standards for the removal of Indian children and their placement in homes reflecting the unique values of Indian culture, and by providing for assistance to tribes in operating child and family service programs.

The act's significance lies partly in its premise. By recognizing that tribes had an interest in their children distinct from, and not simply derivative of, the interests of individual parents, it treated child welfare as a matter of tribal as well as individual rights. That premise was contested then and later, and it rests on the legal characterization of tribes as political entities rather than racial groups, a distinction central to the constitutional debates described in Section 131.12. Historians also note the roles of contingency and of political alliances: the legislation succeeded because tribal leaders, advocacy organizations, sympathetic members of Congress, and some child welfare professionals converged at a moment when federal policy was shifting from termination toward self-determination, a shift examined in Chapter 114.

Section 131.10

The Legal Protections Established by ICWA

ICWA applies to "child custody proceedings" involving an "Indian child." The statute defines an Indian child as an unmarried person under eighteen who is either a member of an Indian tribe or is eligible for membership and is the biological child of a member. The covered proceedings include foster care placements, terminations of parental rights, preadoptive placements, and adoptive placements; it does not generally cover custody disputes in divorce or placements resulting from juvenile delinquency. This definition is political, not racial: eligibility depends on tribal membership, which each tribe determines under its own law, a point developed in Chapter 144.

The statute's principal protections operate in several ways. First, it allocates jurisdiction. Tribal courts generally have jurisdiction exclusive of state courts over custody proceedings involving an Indian child who resides or is domiciled on the reservation, except where existing federal law vests jurisdiction in the state. A tribal court also retains exclusive jurisdiction over an Indian child who is its ward, irrespective of that child's residence or domicile. For foster care and termination proceedings involving other Indian children in state court, ICWA ordinarily requires transfer to tribal court when a parent, Indian custodian, or the tribe petitions, unless there is good cause to the contrary, either parent objects, or the tribal court declines transfer. Tribes also have a statutory right to intervene in state foster care and termination proceedings. Second, it requires notice to the child's tribe and parents in involuntary proceedings. Third, it imposes substantive and procedural standards: parties seeking foster care placement or termination of parental rights must show that active efforts have been made to provide remedial services and rehabilitative programs designed to prevent the breakup of the Indian family, and that those efforts have been unsuccessful; foster care placements require clear and convincing evidence, and terminations require evidence beyond a reasonable doubt, in each case supported by testimony of a qualified expert witness that continued custody by the parent is likely to result in serious emotional or physical damage to the child. Fourth, in voluntary proceedings, consent to termination or adoption must be given before a judge, with an explanation of consequences, and parents may withdraw consent within certain time limits.

Fifth, the statute establishes placement preferences. Absent good cause to the contrary, an Indian child in an adoptive placement is to be placed first with a member of the child's extended family, then with other members of the child's tribe, then with other Indian families. Foster care and preadoptive placements follow a parallel order, including placement in homes approved by the tribe. Tribes may alter the order of preferences by resolution. The statute also authorizes grants to Indian tribes and organizations to establish and operate child and family service programs, though appropriated funding has historically been limited. Taken together, these provisions displace default state practice by requiring heightened standards, giving tribes procedural rights, and creating a preference for placements that preserve the child's connection to family and nation.

Historical Vocabulary
Tribal Jurisdiction

Definition: The legal authority of a tribal government, typically exercised through its courts and agencies, to decide matters involving its members, territory, or both, including child custody and welfare.

Historical context: Tribal authority over family matters long predates the United States, and was acknowledged and limited in various ways by federal law and by court decisions. ICWA gives tribes exclusive jurisdiction over certain proceedings and the right to seek transfer or intervene in others. In Canada, the comparable concept is the exercise of Indigenous governing authority over child and family services under the 2019 legislation.

Why it matters: Jurisdiction determines which institution decides a child's future. Disputes over it were at the center of both the removals and the reforms.

Potential misunderstanding: Tribal jurisdiction does not mean a tribe acts without law or accountability; tribal courts apply tribal law and, under ICWA, interact with federal standards and state courts, and the extent of jurisdiction varies with the child's residence, the tribe's recognition, and the state.

Section 131.11

Tribal Authority, State Courts, and the Implementation of ICWA

A statute's text does not determine its effect; implementation does. ICWA's protections depend on state agencies and courts identifying children as Indian, giving timely notice to tribes, and applying the statute's standards. Practice has varied substantially by state, county, and judge. Some states and counties developed specialized procedures, trained caseworkers, and cooperative relationships with tribes. Others failed to ask whether a child might be Indian, did not notify tribes, or found "good cause" to depart from the placement preferences on grounds that critics considered inconsistent with the statute. Research and advocacy reports through the following decades documented continued noncompliance in some places, although the absence of comprehensive national data on ICWA cases has made it difficult to measure compliance precisely, and the Department of Health and Human Services only later moved to improve data collection.

Courts also shaped implementation. In Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield (1989), the Supreme Court held that twin infants born off the reservation to parents who were domiciled on it fell within the tribe's exclusive jurisdiction, despite the parents' decision to give birth away from the reservation and consent to adoption, emphasizing that the statute reflected the tribe's interest in its children. In Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl (2013), by contrast, a divided Court held, five to four, that certain ICWA provisions did not apply where a noncustodial biological father had never had legal or physical custody of the child, and that the adoptive placement preference did not apply where no other eligible party had sought to adopt. The decision narrowed ICWA's reach in some circumstances and was criticized by tribes and defended by those who emphasized the child's relationships with her adoptive family. In 2016, the Bureau of Indian Affairs issued binding regulations to clarify the statute's application, replacing guidelines first issued in 1979 that had lacked the force of law.

Institutional capacity matters as much as legal doctrine. Tribes must have staff to receive notices, determine membership, decide whether to intervene or seek transfer, and find or approve placements; many tribes, especially small ones with limited budgets, have found it difficult to respond to every notice. Federal grants under the statute have never been sufficient to meet needs. Cooperation agreements between tribes and states, discussed in Chapter 118's treatment of tribal courts and in Section 131.17 below, have helped in some places. Some states have enacted their own ICWA statutes, in part to reinforce the federal law. The differences among states show that, in practice, the statute's effectiveness depends on the commitment and capacity of tribal, state, and local institutions.

Section 131.12

The Legal Challenges to ICWA and Haaland v. Brackeen

ICWA has been the subject of recurring legal challenge. Critics have argued that it imposes race-based classifications, that it constrains state authority over family law in ways inconsistent with the Constitution, and that its placement preferences can delay or prevent permanent placements that serve a child's interests. Supporters have responded that the statute rests on the political status of tribes, that Congress's authority over Indian affairs is well established, and that the statute's standards protect children and tribes from the harms Congress documented. In 2017, the State of Texas and several individuals who had sought to adopt or foster Indian children brought suit in federal court in Texas against the federal government, naming the Secretary of the Interior and other officials, challenging ICWA and the 2016 regulations. Several tribes, including the Cherokee Nation, Navajo Nation, Oneida Nation, and Morongo Band of Mission Indians, intervened to defend the statute.

The litigation produced divergent rulings. A federal district court in 2018 held parts of the statute and regulations unconstitutional. A panel of the Fifth Circuit reversed in 2019, and the full Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc in 2021, issued a fractured decision that upheld much of the statute but found some provisions invalid. The Supreme Court granted review, and on June 15, 2023, in Haaland v. Brackeen, it ruled by a vote of seven to two, in an opinion by Justice Barrett, that the challenged provisions of ICWA were within Congress's constitutional authority, rejecting the argument that Congress lacked power to enact them under its authority over Indian affairs. The Court also held that the statute did not violate the anticommandeering doctrine, which restricts Congress from compelling states to enact or enforce federal regulatory programs. It concluded that the challengers lacked standing to press the equal protection claim and a related claim concerning tribal authority to alter the order of placement preferences, and so did not decide those claims on the merits. Justice Gorsuch wrote a concurrence emphasizing Congress's authority and the history of federal Indian law; Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately to note that the equal protection question remained open for a future case; Justices Thomas and Alito dissented.

The decision's significance is considerable but should be described precisely. It confirmed that Congress may legislate in this field, protecting a statutory framework that tribal governments had defended as essential, and it was welcomed by tribes and many child welfare advocates. It did not, however, resolve every possible constitutional dispute. Because the Court dismissed the equal protection claim for lack of standing, future litigants with proper standing may raise similar arguments, and the concurrences show that members of the Court differed in their reasoning about related issues. The decision also did not address the practical issues of implementation discussed above, which continue to depend on state and tribal institutions. After the ruling, tribes, states, and federal agencies worked to implement it, and several states moved to adopt or strengthen state-level ICWA legislation as a further safeguard.

Historical Debate

How Should the Best Interests of Indigenous Children Be Determined, and Who Has Legal Authority to Determine Them?

The question: Child welfare law in both countries uses the standard of the child's best interests. Historians, courts, and policymakers disagree about how that standard should be applied to Indigenous children, whether it should be assessed individually or take account of the child's connection to a nation and culture, and which authority, whether a court, an agency, a parent, a tribe, or an Indigenous government, should decide.

Interpretation A — Individual child-centered assessment: Some critics of rigid preferences argue that each child's circumstances, including existing attachments, should be the primary consideration, that delay in permanent placement can harm children, and that statutory preferences or tribal rights should not override a judge's assessment of what a particular child needs. They emphasize the child's relationships and stability.
Interpretation B — Collective and cultural dimensions of best interests: Supporters of ICWA and of Canada's 2019 framework argue that a child's best interests cannot be separated from the child's relationships to family, community, language, and nation, that historical removals show the risks of leaving the determination to outsiders, and that tribal and Indigenous governments hold legitimate authority and have knowledge of kin and community that courts and agencies often lack. They contend that the statutory standards protect, rather than override, children's interests.
Interpretation C — Procedural and institutional approaches: Some scholars and practitioners emphasize that the disagreement is often narrower than rhetoric suggests: most placements under ICWA go to relatives or tribal members without dispute, and the hard cases arise at the margins. They argue for focusing on early identification of relatives, timely notice, adequate services, and cooperative decision-making that reduces conflict.

Evidence: Participants rely on testimony and case studies, research on attachment and on the outcomes of different placements, data on placement patterns, and the legislative record. The research is mixed and sometimes limited by methodological difficulties, including the lack of comprehensive data.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and, in part, evidence: how to weigh individual attachment against collective interests, how to balance speed against the search for relatives, and whether the legal status of tribes as political entities justifies different treatment.

Why it matters: The answer determines whether statutes may prefer particular placements, how courts apply them, and whether Indigenous governments or non-Indigenous courts have the final word.

Student task: Identify one hard case, real or hypothetical, in which Interpretations A and B might lead to different outcomes, and explain what evidence would help a decision-maker choose. Then explain how Interpretation C might reduce the number of such cases.

Division IV — Canadian Child Welfare Reform and Indigenous Jurisdiction Section 131.13

Canadian Child Welfare After the Sixties Scoop

In the decades after the peak of the Sixties Scoop, Canadian child welfare changed but did not eliminate the overrepresentation of Indigenous children. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities and organizations worked to establish their own agencies and to reform provincial and territorial systems. By the 1990s, the federal government funded a growing number of First Nations child and family service agencies, delegated by provinces to provide services on reserves, under a national program that the federal government administered. Over subsequent decades, a substantial network of such agencies developed. Its size has varied with funding arrangements, provincial delegation, and changes in agency organization; the agencies differ widely in resources and relationships with provincial authorities. Delegated agencies operate under provincial legislation and standards, so that, despite First Nations control of management, they generally operate within provincial legal frameworks, and funding and authority questions have been continuing sources of tension.

Disparities persisted. Statistics Canada data from the 2016 census showed that Indigenous children aged fourteen and younger represented about 7.5 percent of all children in that age group but approximately 52.2 percent of foster children in private households; the census measure did not include every form of residential care. Later census data showed similar patterns. Researchers and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, whose 2015 report included calls to action on child welfare, linked these disparities to poverty, housing, intergenerational effects of residential schools, and funding structures. The Commission called on governments to reduce the number of Indigenous children in care, to fund prevention, to enact national standards, and to ensure that agencies take account of the history of residential schools. Provincial and territorial governments also reformed their laws, some adding provisions requiring notice to Indigenous communities and consideration of cultural identity in placement.

The circumstances of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities differ. On-reserve First Nations services historically depended on federal funding and provincial delegation; off-reserve First Nations children generally fell under provincial and territorial systems with less consistent attention to community connections. Inuit communities in the North, including Nunavut, Nunavik, and parts of Labrador, face remote geography, small and dispersed populations, and limited local services, and Inuit leaders have drawn attention to the placement of northern children in southern foster homes. Métis communities, long left in a legal gap because of disputes over federal jurisdiction, gained clarity when the Supreme Court of Canada held in Daniels v. Canada (2016) that Métis and non-Status Indians are "Indians" within the meaning of the federal legislative power over "Indians," though that decision did not itself create specific programs. These differences explain why a single national approach has required flexibility to accommodate distinct legal and political relationships.

Section 131.14

Funding Inequality, Jordan's Principle, and Human Rights Litigation

The financial architecture of First Nations child and family services became the center of a major human rights case. For on-reserve services, the federal government funded agencies through formulas that, critics argued, covered the costs of taking children into care while providing far less for prevention and family support, and that provided less funding than provinces provided for comparable services off reserve. In 2007, the First Nations Child and Family Caring Society of Canada, led by Cindy Blackstock, and the Assembly of First Nations filed a complaint with the Canadian Human Rights Commission alleging that the federal government discriminated against First Nations children by underfunding child and family services and by its handling of Jordan's Principle. The case followed years of preliminary litigation over jurisdiction and was heard by the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal beginning in 2013.

On January 26, 2016, the Tribunal issued its decision. It found that the federal government's funding for First Nations child and family services, and its implementation of Jordan's Principle, were discriminatory, and that they had adverse effects on First Nations children and families, including incentives to remove children from their homes and communities. It ordered the federal government to cease the discriminatory practices and to reform the program, and it later issued a series of further orders, including a broader interpretation of Jordan's Principle that required the government to address needs without regard to the usual jurisdictional disputes. In September 2019, the Tribunal ordered compensation for First Nations children removed from their homes under the child welfare system, and for certain parents and grandparents, as a remedy for the discrimination found. The federal government sought judicial review, and litigation and negotiation over compensation and long-term reform continued, resulting in a compensation settlement approved by the Federal Court on October 24, 2023, with negotiations over long-term funding reform continuing into 2024.

Jordan's Principle connects the human rights case to the individual tragedy that gave it a name. The principle, adopted by the House of Commons in 2007, provides that when a jurisdictional dispute arises over services for a First Nations child, the department of government first contacted should pay for the service and seek reimbursement afterward. The Tribunal found that the federal government's narrow application of it failed to prevent delays and denials, and ordered a broader approach. Applications for support under the principle, covering health, education, and social services, have since been made in large numbers. The relationship between this reform and child welfare lies in prevention: when families have access to services such as therapies, respite care, and educational supports, they may be less likely to face the pressures that lead to removal. The Tribunal's findings also gave advocates a legal foundation for arguing that the underfunding of services was not a neutral budgetary choice but a form of discrimination with consequences for family separation.

Section 131.15

Canada's 2019 Indigenous Child and Family Services Legislation

In January 2018, the federal government convened an emergency meeting on Indigenous child and family services, and later that year it announced its intention to introduce legislation. The result was An Act respecting First Nations, Inuit and Métis children, youth and families, commonly referred to by its bill number, C-92, which received royal assent on June 21, 2019 and came into force on January 1, 2020. The act was developed after consultations with Indigenous governments and organizations, and its preamble and purposes link it to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's calls to action and to the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, discussed in Chapter 143. Its stated purposes include affirming the inherent right of self-government of Indigenous peoples, which includes jurisdiction over child and family services, setting national standards for the provision of services to Indigenous children and families, and contributing to the implementation of the Declaration.

The act has three main features. First, it sets national principles and minimum standards, applying to all providers of child and family services to Indigenous children, whether provincial, territorial, or Indigenous: the best interests of the Indigenous child, understood to include cultural continuity, and substantive equality. It gives priority to preventive and family-support services, provides that a child should not be removed solely on the basis of socioeconomic conditions such as poverty or inadequate housing, requires notice to the child's family and Indigenous governing body in specified circumstances, and sets priorities for placements, favoring family members and members of the child's community. Second, it affirms that the inherent right of self-government includes jurisdiction over child and family services, and provides a process by which an Indigenous governing body may give notice of its intention to exercise legislative authority, may request a coordination agreement with the federal and provincial governments, and, if no agreement is reached within a set period, may have its law take effect with the force of federal law. Third, it provides that Indigenous laws, once in force and subject to specified conditions, prevail over conflicting provincial and federal law on certain matters.

The legislation did not settle the question of funding, which remained the subject of separate negotiation, and critics have noted that recognition of jurisdiction does not itself provide resources. Quebec's government challenged the act's constitutionality. In February 2022, the Quebec Court of Appeal held most of the act valid but considered sections 21 and 22(3), governing the priority and application of Indigenous child and family services laws in relation to other laws, constitutionally invalid. On February 9, 2024, the Supreme Court of Canada, in a unanimous decision on a reference from Quebec, held that the act is constitutionally valid, within Parliament's authority over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians." The Court's reasoning confirmed that Parliament may enact the framework, including the provisions giving Indigenous laws the force of federal law, and it treated the affirmation of the inherent right as consistent with the constitutional recognition of Aboriginal rights. The decision did not itself define the full scope of the inherent right of self-government, and implementation remains a matter of negotiation and practice.

Section 131.16

Indigenous Governments and the Reclamation of Child Welfare Authority

Statutory recognition of jurisdiction is a beginning, not an end. To exercise authority, an Indigenous governing body must draft laws, create or adapt institutions, recruit and train staff, secure funding, and negotiate relationships with provincial and federal governments and neighboring agencies. Under the 2019 legislation, a governing body may notify the federal and provincial governments of its intention to exercise jurisdiction, and the parties may negotiate a coordination agreement addressing matters such as funding, the transfer of cases, and the relationship between Indigenous and provincial services. The first coordination agreement under the act was signed on July 6, 2021, by Cowessess First Nation, Canada, and Saskatchewan. The Cowessess Miyo Pimatisowin Act had already acquired the force of federal law on April 1, 2021. These milestones distinguish the date an Indigenous law took legal effect from the subsequent conclusion of its coordination agreement. Other First Nations, including some in Ontario and British Columbia, have developed their own child and family services laws and have sought coordination agreements. Inuit and Métis governing bodies have also pursued arrangements, though their circumstances and the available institutions differ.

Practical obstacles are substantial. Communities must decide how their laws will define the child's best interests, which may draw on customary concepts of kinship and community responsibility, and how they will handle cases of abuse or neglect in a way that is accountable to families and to the community. Funding is critical: negotiations have often focused on whether federal and provincial funding will cover the real costs of operating services, including prevention, and whether it will be stable. Small communities face questions of capacity, including recruiting trained social workers, ensuring independence from political pressure, and handling sensitive cases among people who know one another. Some Indigenous governments have chosen to exercise jurisdiction in stages, beginning with prevention and customary care and building capacity over time; others have organized regionally or through tribal councils to pool resources.

For these reasons, historians and observers are cautious in assessing results. The act's framework is recent, and implementation remains in progress. The legal pathway has existed for only a few years, and early experiences show both promise and difficulty. Rather than treating legislative reform as automatic implementation, this chapter treats it as the opening of a long process in which Indigenous governments must build institutions, maintain accountability to their citizens, and secure resources. The comparison with tribal child welfare institutions in the United States, discussed in Section 131.17, shows that such institutions can function effectively over time but depend on sustained capacity and funding.

Division V — Family Preservation, Sovereignty, and Historical Consequences Section 131.17

Tribal Child Welfare Institutions in the United States

Since ICWA's enactment, many tribes have built social service departments, children's courts, and family-support programs. Their form varies with population, resources, and legal setting. Large nations such as the Navajo Nation operate extensive divisions of social services and have codified children's law. Small tribes may employ a single ICWA caseworker who coordinates with state agencies, relies on regional consortia, or contracts for services. Tribal courts, discussed in Chapter 118, hear child welfare cases involving children who reside on the reservation and, through transfer, some cases from state courts. Tribal codes often incorporate customary values, such as the importance of extended family and community, alongside rules on investigation, emergency removal, due process for parents, and placement.

Funding has been a persistent constraint. ICWA authorized grants to tribes, but appropriations have been limited. For decades, tribes lacked direct access to the largest federal source of foster care funding, Title IV-E of the Social Security Act, which flowed to states. A 2008 federal law, the Fostering Connections to Success and Increasing Adoptions Act, allowed tribes to apply for direct Title IV-E funding, though start-up costs and administrative requirements have limited participation, and some tribes continue to depend on agreements with states to obtain funds. Tribal-state agreements, which address matters such as notification, funding, licensing of tribal foster homes, and case transfers, have become important tools. Organizations such as the National Indian Child Welfare Association, founded in 1987, provide training and policy support.

Tribal institutions balance several responsibilities that can pull in different directions: protecting children from harm, respecting parents' rights and due process, preserving families, and sustaining cultural and community continuity. They also face jurisdictional limits. Whether a tribe may act depends on factors such as the child's residence, the status of the land, and the presence of state jurisdiction under laws such as Public Law 280 in some states, where tribes may have to share jurisdiction unless they seek to reassume exclusive authority as ICWA allows. These limits mean that even a well-resourced tribal program may operate within a complex legal environment, and that cooperation with state agencies is often necessary.

Section 131.18

Prevention, Kinship Care, Reunification, and Family Healing

Reform in both countries has increasingly emphasized keeping children safe while reducing unnecessary separation. Preventive services include family support, parenting programs, substance-use treatment, housing assistance, and respite care. Kinship care, formal or informal, offers placement with grandparents, aunts, uncles, or other relatives or community members, and has become a priority under ICWA's placement preferences and Canada's 2019 act. Many Indigenous programs combine such services with culturally grounded approaches, including support from elders, ceremonies, language and cultural programs, and community-based gatherings in which families and communities participate in planning for a child. Reunification services help families whose children have been placed in care to address the problems that led to removal and to restore contact. Adoptees seeking their origins have also been assisted by programs that locate birth family members, support reunions, and help them reconnect with their nations, and by changes in access to adoption records in some jurisdictions.

These approaches face practical difficulties. Kinship caregivers may lack resources and may face licensing standards designed for other kinds of homes, and many are elderly grandparents on fixed incomes. Prevention requires funding upstream, when agencies are often funded only when a child is in care. Reunification can be complicated when parents face the effects of earlier trauma, poverty, or addiction, and when placement decisions involve competing interests. Addressing intergenerational consequences of removal and of schooling is a long-term process; healing programs have emerged in many communities, but outcomes are difficult to measure and funding is often short-term. Evaluating programs also requires care, since evidence about outcomes is uneven and many Indigenous programs have not been formally studied.

Everyday Life and Social History

Raising, Losing, and Reclaiming Children

The history of child welfare is lived in households. A grandmother who takes in grandchildren when a parent is ill, a family that shares a small house among several generations, a mother who must travel hundreds of kilometres to reach a court hearing or a hospital, a social worker deciding in a single visit whether a home is safe, and an adoptee who first learns of a nation of origin as an adult are all part of this history. Their experiences were shaped by housing, transportation, employment, access to services, language, and the knowledge of the courts and agencies they encountered.

Records of these experiences are uneven. Agency files describe families through the eyes of investigators; court records emphasize legal findings; and Indigenous parents, children, and relatives are often heard only through later testimony, memoirs, and oral history. Historians read these sources together and recognize that each reflects its creator's purposes. They also recognize that many families who kept their children, or who had them returned, left little trace in the records, so that the documented cases may not represent everyday experience.

Modern reform programs also reshape daily life: kinship caregivers who receive support, communities that hold family gatherings to plan for a child's care, language nests and cultural camps that bring children and elders together, and reunions between adoptees and relatives. The success of these programs depends on the same daily realities, such as stable housing, transportation, and the time and patience of relatives, that earlier systems often overlooked.

Section 131.19

Regional, Urban, and Cross-Border Differences in Indigenous Child Welfare

Conditions differ across reservations, reserves, rural and northern communities, and cities. On reservations and reserves, services are often limited by remoteness and small populations, but communities may have closer links between agencies and families. In cities, where large numbers of Indigenous people now live, as Chapter 140 explains, children may be served by state or provincial agencies with little connection to their nations, even though ICWA and Canadian reforms apply to them. Urban Indigenous organizations, such as Native Child and Family Services of Toronto, and urban Indian organizations in the United States, have developed services to fill this gap. The disparities documented in the 2010s, such as the high proportions of Native children in foster care in states including South Dakota, Alaska, and Minnesota, and of Indigenous children in care in Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta, reflect regional patterns of poverty, history, and administration rather than a uniform national pattern.

Alaska presents a distinctive case. Alaska Native villages are federally recognized tribes, but because the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act extinguished most reservations, and because in 1998 the Supreme Court held in Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government that Alaska Native corporation lands were not "Indian country," tribal jurisdiction has depended on the tribes' authority over their members rather than on territory. The Alaska Supreme Court recognized in John v. Baker (1999) that Alaska Native tribes retain inherent authority to adjudicate child custody disputes among their members. In 2017, the State of Alaska and Alaska tribes signed a tribal child welfare compact to enable tribes to take on responsibilities for services. Among Inuit, Nunavut and the Northwest Territories have legislation recognizing customary adoption, and Nunavik in northern Quebec operates within Quebec's youth protection system with adaptations. Métis communities, especially in the prairie provinces, face challenges of recognition and access to services that differ from those of First Nations.

Families and networks also cross jurisdictions. Relatives may live on both sides of a provincial boundary or the Canada–United States border, as Chapter 142 discusses, and placements historically crossed provincial and national lines, as in the placement of Manitoba children with American families. Recognition status matters: ICWA applies to children connected to federally recognized tribes, so children connected to unrecognized communities may not receive its protections, while Canadian legislation covers First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children, though access to services varies with residence and provincial policies. Funding, geography, and the strength of local institutions interact to produce very different experiences.

Section 131.20

The Defense of Indigenous Families as an Exercise of Sovereignty

The American and Canadian histories converge on a common pattern with important differences. In both countries, colonial governments and later settler states claimed authority over Indigenous children through missions, schools, guardianship, and, in the twentieth century, modern child welfare systems; in both, poverty and unequal services contributed to separation; and in both, Indigenous parents, tribal and First Nations governments, and advocates mobilized to challenge removal and to assert authority. They differ in legal form. The United States addressed the problem through a statute grounded in federal authority over Indian affairs and the trust relationship, which sets standards for state courts and recognizes tribal jurisdiction. Canada has, after a long period of provincial control and federal funding disputes, enacted a statute that affirms an Indigenous right of self-government in child and family services and provides a pathway for Indigenous laws to operate, backed by human rights findings on funding equity.   • The conditions of poverty and unequal services that contribute to separation
  • The central role of Indigenous families and communities in defending children

Children and families experienced the harms most directly; communities and governments that built new institutions experienced change most visibly. The defense of Indigenous families developed from resistance to removal into a continuing effort to exercise political authority over the institutions responsible for children. That development connects child welfare to the broader history of Indigenous sovereignty traced in Volume X: the claim that a nation's future depends on its ability to raise, protect, and educate its children, and that the decisions most affecting those children should be made by those who are accountable to their communities. How fully that claim is realized remains an open question, and later chapters on contemporary governments, urban communities, and the relationship between Indigenous nations and the states of North America take it up further.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that child welfare in Indigenous North America is a question of jurisdiction, kinship, cultural continuity, and collective rights as well as individual custody. Indigenous societies raised children within extended families, clans, and communities, and practiced customary adoption and fostering that extended rather than severed relationships. Colonial governments, missionaries, and settler states claimed authority over Indigenous households through missions, schools, guardianship, and regulation, justified by assimilation policy.

In the twentieth century, modern child welfare systems, including juvenile courts, public agencies, adoption procedures, and foster care, expanded into Indigenous communities, aided in the United States by federal termination policy and Public Law 280 and in Canada by the 1951 Indian Act amendments. Professional standards of fitness, drawn from non-Indigenous households, were applied by social workers and judges with broad discretion. Poverty, housing shortages, remote locations, and unequal services were often treated as neglect, and funding structures rewarded removal more than prevention.

The chapter distinguished genuine child protection from unnecessary separation. Abuse and neglect occurred, and removal was sometimes necessary. But hearings, inquiries, and tribunals found that many children were removed for reasons connected to poverty, cultural difference, and institutional incentives, and that families who might have kept their children with support were instead separated from them.

Mid-twentieth-century practices included the Indian Adoption Project of 1958 to 1967, one documented element of a larger pattern of placement of Native children in non-Native families, and, in Canada, the Sixties Scoop, a term for removals that began in the 1950s and continued for decades across provinces. Estimates of numbers are approximate, and experiences varied; adoptees, birth parents, and adoptive families offered differing accounts, and historians attend to that diversity while recognizing that decisions were made without the participation of the children's nations.

Indigenous parents, grandparents, tribal and First Nations governments, and advocates resisted through individual acts, litigation, surveys, public hearings, and the creation of their own services, reframing the issue as one of collective survival and sovereignty. In the United States, this movement produced the Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978, which sets standards for custody proceedings involving Indian children, including tribal jurisdiction, notice, active efforts, evidentiary requirements, and placement preferences.

ICWA's effects depended on implementation by state courts, agencies, and tribal institutions, and on judicial interpretation in cases such as Holyfield and Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl. Constitutional challenges culminated in Haaland v. Brackeen, decided June 15, 2023, in which the Supreme Court upheld the challenged provisions as within Congress's authority and rejected the anticommandeering claim, while leaving the equal protection claim undecided for lack of standing.

Canada's path was different. Provincial control, federal funding disputes, inquiries, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and human rights litigation led to the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal's 2016 finding of discrimination in First Nations child and family services funding, the broadening of Jordan's Principle, and, in 2019, a national statute affirming Indigenous jurisdiction, in force in 2020 and held constitutionally valid by the Supreme Court of Canada on February 9, 2024.

The chapter also showed that recognition is not implementation. Tribal child welfare institutions in the United States and Indigenous governing bodies in Canada must build laws, staff, funding, and accountability, and they operate within jurisdictional limits that vary by region, recognition status, and residence. Prevention, kinship care, reunification, and healing programs aim to keep children safe while reducing unnecessary separation, but they face limits of funding, capacity, and evidence.

Finally, the chapter concluded that the defense of Indigenous families developed from resistance to removal into an ongoing struggle to exercise authority over the institutions responsible for children. Achievements and unresolved problems coexist, and the history illustrates a broader theme of Volume X: that Indigenous sovereignty is exercised, in significant part, through the care and protection of future generations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an apparently private matter, the raising of children, became one of the central arenas of Indigenous political history. Removal of children threatened the continuity of communities, and the defense of families was therefore a defense of nationhood. The history also shows how institutions that present themselves as neutral, such as child welfare agencies and funding formulas, can embody assumptions and incentives that produce unequal outcomes, and how evidence from hearings, tribunals, and testimony can expose them.

The chapter connects to earlier parts of the textbook. The assimilation policies of Volumes VII and VIII, including the schools examined in Chapters 87 and 88, and the termination and relocation policies of Volume IX, provide the background to child welfare intervention. It also connects to the movements of Volume X: self-determination, treaty and land rights, and the revival of language and culture. In turn, the debates and institutions described here bear on later subjects, including contemporary tribal and First Nations governments, urban Indigenous communities, and Indigenous control over data and knowledge.

The history also shows the limits of law. Statutes and court decisions have recognized Indigenous authority, and those achievements were won by communities, advocates, and litigants over decades. Yet disparities persist, funding is contested, and the capacity of governments to exercise recognized authority is uneven. Continuities of poverty, unequal services, and jurisdictional dispute run through the period, even as the institutions responsible for children have changed.

Chapter 132 turns to another dimension of colonial history: the struggle over historical memory, monuments, and decolonization. Students should carry forward the recognition that Indigenous authority over the care of children is not a peripheral social service issue but a measure of whether Indigenous nations can sustain themselves across generations, and that its exercise remains a continuing, contested, and unfinished historical process.
