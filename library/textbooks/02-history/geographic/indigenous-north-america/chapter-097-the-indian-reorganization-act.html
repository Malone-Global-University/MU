The Indian Reorganization Act

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History · The Indian New Deal

Chapter Opening

In June 1934 the United States Congress ended a policy that had dominated federal Indian administration for nearly half a century. It did not, however, hand governing authority back to Indigenous nations. It offered them a new arrangement, and then asked them, reservation by reservation, whether to accept it.

In the spring of 1934, while Congress was still debating the legislation that would become the Indian Reorganization Act, officials of the Office of Indian Affairs convened a series of regional congresses so that Indigenous delegates could hear and respond to the administration's proposals. Stenographers recorded much of what was said. Those transcripts, together with the hearing records and the correspondence of the Interior Department, allow historians to hear delegates ask questions that remain central to Indigenous politics: Who would hold final authority? What would happen to land already lost? Would councils be allowed to govern, or only to advise? These records matter because they show that the reorganization of federal Indian policy was not simply imposed from Washington on a silent population. Indigenous people argued, bargained, doubted, and disagreed.

The result of those debates was the Indian Reorganization Act of June 18, 1934, also called the Wheeler–Howard Act after its congressional sponsors. The law reversed the central premise of the General Allotment Act of 1887. It prohibited the further allotment of reservation land in severalty, extended the trust periods on land that remained in federal trust, authorized the return of some unsold surplus lands to tribal ownership, and created a mechanism for acquiring new land in trust. It also authorized tribes to organize governments under written constitutions and to receive federal corporate charters, created a revolving credit fund, and made provision for educational loans and Indian preference in the Indian Service. Together with other measures of the period, these provisions are often grouped under the name "the Indian New Deal."

The Act was nevertheless contradictory, and this chapter explains why. It stopped one engine of dispossession but did not recover most of the land that allotment had already removed from Indigenous hands. It authorized tribal governments but placed constitutions, charters, land acquisitions, and many decisions under the approval of the Secretary of the Interior. It was drafted by officials who believed they were protecting Indigenous community life, yet it often relied on models and procedures that were unfamiliar to the communities expected to use them. It let each reservation vote on whether the Act would apply, and the voters of the Diné Nation, the Crow Nation, the Seneca Nation, and many others used that right to say no, while those of the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes and scores of other communities said yes.

A single distinction governs the chapter. The Indian Reorganization Act recognized and reorganized aspects of tribal self-government; it did not create Indigenous nations and it did not originate their sovereignty. Indigenous nations possessed governments, laws, territories, and diplomatic histories long before 1934, as earlier volumes of this textbook have shown. What the statute changed was the way those governments could be organized and recognized within United States law, and the extent to which federal policy would work against, rather than for, the survival of tribal land and institutions.

The chapter also asks students to treat acceptance and rejection as political decisions rather than as measures of enlightenment or backwardness. Communities voted under unequal conditions, with imperfect information, under pressure from administrators, and in the shadow of the Depression. Even so, they weighed concrete experiences: land loss, trusteeship, boarding schools, livestock policy, relations with local agents, and the strength of existing councils and ceremonial authorities. The evidence base includes the statute itself, the congressional hearing record, the published transcripts of the 1934 congresses, federal election returns preserved in the National Archives, tribal constitutions and charters, administrative reports, scholarly compilations such as Theodore Haas's later summary of the elections, and oral histories collected from people who lived through the period. Most of these sources were produced by or through the federal government, and the chapter therefore asks continually whose voices they preserve and whose they omit.

Interpreting this legislation also requires attention to time. The text enacted in 1934 is not identical to the provisions later printed in modern compilations of federal law; Congress amended election procedures in 1935 and charter provisions in later decades. A tribe could vote about the Act’s application, adopt or decline a written constitution, and pursue or reject a business charter at different moments, each with its own political significance. Similarly, the protection of land was a legal possibility, not proof that an appropriation, acquisition, or local benefit had occurred. The chapter consequently separates the original statute, administrative practice, and documented choices by Indigenous voters. This approach allows the IRA to be assessed as a negotiated and unevenly implemented policy rather than as a single event whose meaning was settled on June 18, 1934.

The chapter follows the arc of the policy. Division I explains why federal policy changed and how the Act was drafted and enacted. Division II examines land, credit, and resource management. Division III turns to constitutions, charters, and referendums. Division IV compares Indigenous responses in the Confederated Salish and Kootenai, Diné, Hopi, and other communities. Division V considers Oklahoma and Alaska, the wider Indian New Deal, the scholarly debate over its meaning, and its legacy. The conditions that preceded the Act are treated in Chapters 95 and 96; the parallel history of First Nations in Canada belongs to Chapter 98; and the later reversals of termination and the rise of self-determination are the subject of Chapters 104 and 114.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the allotment policy came under sustained criticism by 1933 and how the administrative, economic, and political circumstances of the New Deal created an opening for reform.
  2. Trace the drafting, consultation, congressional debate, and compromise that produced the Indian Reorganization Act of June 18, 1934.
  3. Analyze the major provisions of the original statute, including Sections 1, 2, 3, 5, 16, 17, and 18, and identify the authority each conferred and the limits each retained.
  4. Distinguish between ending new allotments and restoring land already lost, and explain why the difference matters for assessing the Act.
  5. Distinguish among a tribal constitution, a corporate charter, a vote on the Act's application, and federal recognition.
  6. Assess the relationship between the Indian New Deal's conservation programs, especially Diné livestock reduction, and the statutory provisions of the Act itself.
  7. Evaluate the 1934–1936 referendum procedures, including the 1935 amendments, as evidence of Indigenous consent.
  8. Compare the responses of the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, the Diné Nation, and the Hopi to the Act, and explain how history and institutions shaped those responses.
  9. Describe the distinct arrangements made for Oklahoma and Alaska in 1936 and explain why the 1934 statute could not serve as a uniform model.
  10. Interpret primary sources, including statutory text, referendum returns, constitutions, and conference records, with attention to authorship, audience, and limitation.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether the Act restored self-government or established federally supervised tribal organization.
  12. Connect the Act to the longer history of Indigenous sovereignty, and explain why the statute recognized rather than created Indigenous governmental authority.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the United States abandon allotment as a central objective of Indian policy in 1934, and who pressed for that change?
  2. To what extent was the Indian New Deal a federal initiative, and to what extent did it build on Indigenous activism that predated the Roosevelt administration?
  3. How did the bill introduced in February 1934 differ from the statute enacted in June, and what do the differences show about the balance of power among Collier, Congress, and Indigenous delegates?
  4. What is the difference between preventing further land loss and recovering land already lost?
  5. In what sense did the Act "recognize" tribal government, and in what sense did it leave sovereignty untouched?
  6. Why do historians separate livestock reduction from the Indian Reorganization Act, and why did Diné voters nevertheless link them?
  7. Can an election conducted under federal procedures and subject to federal approval be considered an expression of Indigenous consent?
  8. Why did nations with similar histories sometimes vote differently, and why did nations with different histories sometimes make similar choices?
  9. How did Oklahoma and Alaska expose the limits of a single statutory model for Indigenous peoples?
  10. Should the Act be understood as a restoration of self-government, as a new form of federal supervision, or as a framework within which Indigenous nations pursued their own political purposes?

Historical Context

The Indian Reorganization Act cannot be understood without the policy it replaced. The General Allotment Act of 1887, examined in Chapter 86, authorized the division of reservations into individual allotments, the sale of "surplus" land to non-Indians, and a transitional trust period during which allotments were held by the United States. Its sponsors argued that private property would prepare Indigenous people for citizenship and dissolve tribal governments, which many officials regarded as obstacles to assimilation. The Burke Act of 1906 allowed the Secretary of the Interior to end trust status early, and later administrative practice accelerated the issuance of fee patents to people the government judged "competent." Scholarly estimates commonly place the reservation and trust land base in the early 1930s at roughly 48 million acres, down from roughly 138 million acres in 1887, although such totals vary with how reservations, allotments, and tribal holdings are counted.

By the 1920s, the consequences of allotment were widely documented. Allotted land that remained in trust passed to heirs, producing parcels divided among many owners that were difficult to use or lease. Land that had been sold or patented often ended up in non-Indian hands, creating checkerboard patterns of ownership within reservation boundaries. The Meriam Report of 1928, formally titled The Problem of Indian Administration and prepared by investigators working for the Institute for Government Research, described widespread poverty, poor health, inadequate schooling, and an administrative system that was often more concerned with supervising individuals than with promoting economic recovery. Chapters 95 and 96 trace this criticism and the conditions that preceded the New Deal; this chapter assumes that background and concentrates on what Congress and Indigenous communities did in response.

Indigenous political institutions had not disappeared. Despite policies that restricted ceremonies, supplanted hereditary authorities with agency-appointed judges and police, and favored "progressive" individuals over traditional leaders, communities continued to deliberate through councils, clan and village structures, ceremonial societies, church and fraternal organizations, and intertribal bodies. Some nations had written constitutions long before 1934, and others had business committees or councils that dealt with the agency on leasing and claims. The Indian Reorganization Act therefore entered a landscape of existing governments, many of them weakened but none empty.

The Great Depression and the New Deal created a distinctive political moment. The Roosevelt administration, which took office in March 1933, accepted federal responsibility for economic recovery, conservation, and public works on a scale previously unknown, and it placed reformers in key Interior Department posts. The appointment of John Collier as Commissioner of Indian Affairs gave a long-standing critic of allotment direct administrative authority. Yet Collier and the Interior Department still operated within a federal system in which Congress held plenary legal power over Indian affairs, a doctrine that Indigenous nations had long contested. This chapter therefore examines both the opportunities the New Deal opened and the structural limits within which the opportunities existed.

From Assimilation to the Indian New Deal

Section 97.1

The End of the Allotment Era and the Opening of the Indian New Deal, 1933

When the Roosevelt administration took office in March 1933, federal Indian policy rested on a legal structure assembled over several decades. Reservations still existed, but many had been cut into allotments and surrounded by non-Indian landowners. The Indian Service, a bureau of the Interior Department, exercised wide discretion over leasing, the sale of inherited land, the expenditure of tribal funds, schooling, and the appointment of agency personnel. Indigenous people had been recognized as citizens of the United States by the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924, but citizenship did not remove the federal trust relationship or the administrative authority that accompanied it. For most reservation residents in 1933 the practical experience of government was the agency superintendent, not a tribal council with independent legal authority.

The conditions described in Chapter 96 had become politically difficult to defend. Land loss was severe and well documented. Poverty and ill health were widespread, and the Depression deepened both, since wage work and leasing income declined at the same time that prices for farm and livestock products collapsed. Boarding-school education, discussed in Chapters 87 and 88, remained a target of criticism, while the Meriam Report of 1928 had urged more day schools, better health services, and attention to the economic basis of Indigenous communities. Chapter 95 traced how reformers, scholars, women's organizations, and Indigenous leaders had turned this evidence into a campaign against assimilationist administration in the late 1920s and early 1930s.

Indigenous demands for reform predated the Roosevelt administration and should not be treated as an afterthought to a federal initiative. Indigenous people had litigated, petitioned, organized councils, and lobbied in Washington for decades. The Pueblo communities of New Mexico, acting together through the All Pueblo Council formed in 1922, had mobilized against the Bursum bill, which threatened Pueblo land claims, and their campaign became a national cause. Leaders of the Society of American Indians in the 1910s and of many reservation councils in the 1920s had called for the protection of land, for the recognition of treaty obligations, and for greater control of community affairs. Tribal delegations also testified before congressional committees during the late 1920s and early 1930s. The ideas that the Indian New Deal would draw upon, including the protection of land, the revitalization of councils, and the toleration of ceremonial life, were therefore already in circulation among Indigenous people before 1933.

The election of Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1932 changed the administrative context. The New Deal rested on a belief that the federal government should intervene in the economy to stabilize it, create employment, and protect natural resources. This philosophy encouraged a reconsideration of Indian administration in at least three ways. First, public-works and conservation programs offered a means of bringing wages to reservations in a period of severe unemployment. Second, concern with soil erosion and overgrazing, already prominent in western land policy, linked Indian lands to a national conservation agenda. Third, the administration's reliance on planning and expertise encouraged officials to consider structural reform rather than incremental adjustment of existing practice.

Roosevelt's appointment of John Collier, confirmed as Commissioner of Indian Affairs in 1933, gave the reform movement an official leader. Collier took office with a broad program: ending allotment, protecting Indigenous land, supporting local organization and ceremonial life, and developing economic enterprises. He moved quickly on matters within administrative control. The Indian Emergency Conservation Work program, a reservation counterpart of the Civilian Conservation Corps that began in 1933, employed Indigenous workers on projects such as erosion control, fencing, and water development. In early 1934 the Interior Department issued a circular affirming religious freedom and cultural liberty, reversing earlier administrative attempts to suppress dances and ceremonies. But the most consequential changes required legislation, since allotment, trust restrictions, and the powers of the Secretary of the Interior were fixed by acts of Congress.

That fact explains the shape of the political effort that followed. Reform of land tenure and tribal government could not be accomplished by executive order alone, and the proposals that Collier took to Congress therefore combined a repudiation of allotment with an organizational program for tribal government, economic development, and education. How Indigenous communities would respond to this program, and how far Congress would go in accepting it, were still open questions in the spring of 1933. The sections that follow examine how those questions were answered.

Cause and Effect

How Allotment-Era Land Loss and Administrative Failure Contributed to the 1934 Legislation

Long-term causes

Allotment and the sale of surplus land reduced the Indigenous land base; heirship divided trust parcels among many owners; administrative supervision limited the development of community enterprises and tribal institutions.

Short-term causes

The Meriam Report of 1928, congressional investigations, the Depression's effect on reservation incomes, and organized criticism by reformers and Indigenous leaders created pressure for change.

Immediate trigger

The Roosevelt administration's willingness to expand federal intervention and the appointment of John Collier in 1933 created a political and administrative opening.

Event and effects

The Act of June 18, 1934 ended new reservation allotments and created new mechanisms for land and government. It did not restore lost land automatically or end federal supervision.

Historical complexity: The change was not inevitable. Earlier reform proposals had failed in Congress, and the Act that passed was a compromise between administrative reformers, congressional skeptics, and Indigenous delegates who disagreed among themselves.

Section 97.2

John Collier, Harold Ickes, and Competing Visions of Indigenous Self-Government

John Collier was born in 1884 and came to Indian affairs through social work, adult education, and, after 1920, direct experience of the Pueblo communities of New Mexico. In Taos he encountered a living system of communal landholding and ceremonial life that he came to regard as a model of social cohesion. When the Bursum bill of 1922 threatened Pueblo land titles, Collier joined the campaign to defeat it, and in 1923 he helped found the American Indian Defense Association, serving as its executive secretary. Over the following decade he published sharp criticisms of the Indian Bureau, of allotment, and of the restrictions on Indigenous religion. His public career thus preceded his appointment and gave him a reputation among reformers as a defender of Indigenous community life against assimilationist policy.

Collier's philosophy rested on two convictions. The first was that the destruction of communal institutions had produced poverty and social disorganization, and that policy should therefore protect and strengthen collective landholding, community economic organization, and cultural expression. The second was that the federal government, properly directed, could act as a guardian of those institutions. The first conviction aligned him with many Indigenous leaders who sought the protection of land and the toleration of ceremonial life. The second created tension, because it assumed that an administrator could design institutions to preserve community life. For many Indigenous nations, community life was already organized according to their own laws, and the question of who should design institutions was a political one.

Harold L. Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior, supported Collier's appointment and his program, though the two men did not always agree and Ickes retained final departmental authority. Ickes also directed major public-works spending, giving him leverage in cabinet competition for resources. Because the Act that Congress ultimately passed vested authority in the Secretary of the Interior, not the Commissioner, the relationship between Collier and Ickes mattered. Collier could propose and administer, but approval of constitutions, charters, and land acquisitions would rest on the department's legal and political judgment.

The reformers also relied on legal expertise within the department. Attorneys in the Solicitor's Office, including Felix S. Cohen, worked on the drafting of provisions and on the interpretation of existing law, and their reading of federal Indian law shaped how the statute would be understood. Their work later fed into Cohen's Handbook of Federal Indian Law, a systematic compilation of the legal doctrines that governed Indian affairs. This legal apparatus illustrates an important feature of the Indian New Deal: it was in significant part an exercise in administrative reconstruction by lawyers and social scientists who believed that law could be used to protect Indigenous communities.

Collier's program must be distinguished from the objectives of other participants. Congress, particularly the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs chaired by Burton K. Wheeler, was willing to end allotment but was skeptical of proposals that expanded federal administrative power or created new institutions such as a separate court for Indian affairs. Local officials and members of Congress representing districts adjoining reservations were concerned about taxation, land titles, and non-Indian interests. Missionary organizations, some of which opposed Collier's cultural policies, voiced their own objections. Indigenous people, finally, held a range of views. Some leaders welcomed the opportunity to strengthen councils and secure land. Others feared that a program devised in Washington would replace one form of control with another, or that organization under federal models would weaken traditional authorities. A group of Indigenous critics organized the American Indian Federation in 1934 to oppose Collier, charging that his program threatened individual rights and mistakenly favored communal arrangements. The Federation's charges were sometimes extreme, and its leadership included people with a range of political commitments, but its existence shows that opposition to Collier was not confined to non-Indigenous interests.

These differences make clear that "Collier's vision," "congressional intention," and "Indigenous objectives" cannot be treated as synonyms. Collier believed in preserving communal life; Congress wanted to limit costs and reduce administrative centralization; Indigenous leaders and communities wanted a range of outcomes, from land protection to greater control over resources to the recognition of treaty rights. The statute that emerged reflects negotiation among these positions, and its provisions must be read as the outcome of that bargaining rather than as the full expression of any single party's design.

Multiple Perspectives

The 1934 Negotiations: Collier, Congress, Administrators, and Indigenous Delegates

Commissioner Collier and reform-minded administrators: Allotment had undermined Indigenous communities, and federal law should instead protect land, support community organization, and allow tribes to manage economic enterprises. Collier favored a broad statute and expected federal guidance to continue.

Section 97.3

The Wheeler–Howard Bill: Indigenous Consultation, Congressional Debate, and Compromise

In February 1934, Senator Burton K. Wheeler of Montana and Representative Edgar Howard of Nebraska introduced companion bills that embodied Collier's program. The proposals were extensive. They would have ended allotment, restored land, and created mechanisms for tribal self-government and corporate organization, and they also contained titles on Indian education and on the creation of a federal court for Indian affairs. The bills were drafted within the Interior Department and presented to Congress by sponsors who held positions of influence on the committees that handled Indian legislation. Wheeler, whose state contained several reservations, would become a more skeptical manager of the proposals than their original shape suggested, and his influence on the Senate's version was significant.

While Congress held hearings, the Indian Service organized a series of regional congresses so that Indigenous delegates could discuss the proposals. They took place in the spring of 1934 at locations including Rapid City, South Dakota, and Chemawa, Oregon, among others across the country. Delegates came from reservations in the surrounding regions, and the meetings were recorded by stenographers. Vine Deloria Jr.'s documentary collection, The Indian Reorganization Act: Congresses and Bills, preserves much of this record together with the texts of the bills, and it remains the most accessible window into the debates. The records show Collier and other officials presenting the proposals, delegates asking about taxation, land, the powers of councils, the role of the Secretary, and the relationship between the new arrangements and existing treaties.

Consultation did occur, but its meaning should be assessed carefully. The congresses brought together delegates selected in various ways, and they could not represent every community equally. Time was short, distances were large, and the technical content of the bills was complex. Delegates sometimes pressed for more time or more information. In other cases, they spoke from direct knowledge of what allotment had done in their own communities. The congresses gave federal officials a means of gauging reaction and, in some cases, of making adjustments. They did not amount to a negotiation in which Indigenous delegates held a veto over the text, and communities that did not send delegates were not directly represented. The records therefore document a significant moment of Indigenous participation without establishing that every community was fully informed or satisfied.

The legislative process narrowed Collier's proposals. The Senate Committee on Indian Affairs held hearings in which Collier was questioned closely, and congressional members raised concerns about the cost of the program, about federal authority over local interests, and about the possible effects on taxation and on non-Indian landowners within reservations. Several features of the original bills were dropped or sharply reduced. The proposed court for Indian affairs did not survive, and the education title was removed from the enacted statute. Provisions concerning the organization of tribes and the conduct of elections on the Act's application were revised, and the Act included explicit protection of existing valid rights when land was restored to tribal ownership. In the end, Congress passed a more limited statute than the one Collier had introduced.

These changes have been interpreted in different ways. Some scholars emphasize Collier's compromises as a pragmatic strategy that preserved the core of the program, notably the end of allotment and the mechanisms for land and government. Others argue that the amendments reduced the capacity of the Act to deliver meaningful self-government, since the bills' institutional provisions were weakened while the Secretary's supervisory role remained. A third view stresses that the right of reservations to reject the Act, added during congressional consideration, was a significant concession to Indigenous consent. The sections that follow examine the statute's provisions in order to evaluate these claims.

Section 97.4

The Indian Reorganization Act of June 18, 1934: Structure, Purpose, and Legal Scope

President Roosevelt signed the Indian Reorganization Act on June 18, 1934. It was Public Law 73-383 and appears at 48 Statutes at Large 984. The statute is relatively short, and students should read the original enactment rather than relying only on the modern United States Code, because later amendments have changed wording and some sections have been redistributed. Its title described its purpose as conserving and developing Indian lands and resources, extending and strengthening trust protection, and establishing means of self-government and economic organization. The provisions fall into several groups, and the discussion below follows those groups before the next sections treat each in detail.

The first group concerns land. Section 1 provided that no reservation land would hereafter be allotted in severalty to any Indian. Section 2 extended existing trust periods and restrictions on alienation until otherwise directed by Congress. Section 3 authorized the Secretary of the Interior to restore to tribal ownership remaining surplus lands of Indian reservations that had been opened to entry, while protecting valid existing rights. Section 4 restricted the transfer of Indian trust land except to the tribe, or by inheritance or exchange. Section 5 authorized the Secretary to acquire land, water rights, or surface rights for tribes or individual Indians, with title held in trust by the United States and exempt from state and local taxation, and it authorized annual appropriations for that purpose. Section 6 directed the Secretary to promote the conservation of range and forest resources through regulation on a sustained-yield basis, and Section 7 permitted the proclamation of new reservations or additions to existing ones on lands acquired.

The second group concerns finance and administration. Section 9 authorized appropriations to defray the expenses of organizing Indian chartered corporations or other organizations, and Section 10 authorized a revolving credit fund of $10 million for loans to chartered Indian corporations. Section 11 authorized appropriations for loans to Indians for tuition and other expenses in recognized vocational and trade schools, high schools, and colleges. Section 12 directed that Indians be given preference in appointment to positions in the Indian Service. These sections authorized programs; whether they were funded and carried out depended on later appropriations and administrative decisions.

The third group concerns tribal organization and consent, and it contains the provisions for which the Act is best remembered. Section 16 provided that any Indian tribe or tribes residing on the same reservation had the right to organize for their common welfare and to adopt an appropriate constitution and bylaws, effective when ratified by a majority vote of the adult members at a special election called by the Secretary and approved by the Secretary. Section 17 authorized the Secretary to issue a corporate charter upon the petition of at least one-third of the adult members of a tribe, to become operative when ratified by a vote of the adult Indians living on the reservation. Section 18 provided that the Act would not apply to any reservation whose adult Indians voted against its application in a special election to be called by the Secretary within a year, held by secret ballot upon thirty days' notice. Section 19 defined "Indian" to include members of recognized tribes, their descendants residing on a reservation on June 1, 1934, and other persons of one-half or more Indian blood, and defined "tribe" to include Indians residing on one reservation.

The statute also established important geographic limits. Section 13 provided that certain sections did not apply in certain territories and states, with different treatment of Alaska and of a list of named Oklahoma tribes, and it was followed by separate legislation in 1936 for Oklahoma and Alaska, discussed in Section 97.17. The Act therefore did not establish uniform arrangements for all Indigenous peoples under United States jurisdiction. The original statute also applied only to the United States and its Indigenous peoples. The history of First Nations in Canada, which was shaped by the Indian Act and different legal institutions, is treated in Chapter 98.

Reading the statute requires attention to several legal distinctions that the following sections develop. Recognition of a tribal government is not identical to tribal membership, which the Act defined for purposes of the statute and which tribes later defined in their own constitutions. Trust ownership is not identical to land management, since a tribe might hold trust land but depend on the Interior Department for leasing approvals. Federal supervision persisted in the form of approvals, regulations, and appropriations. The statute's stated objectives and its operative powers also differ: Section 16 spoke of a "right to organize," but the content of the constitutions and the powers they exercised depended on provisions of existing law and the Secretary's approval. The statute is therefore best read both for what it enabled and for what it left unchanged.

Division II

Land, Resources, and Indigenous Economic Reconstruction

§§97.5–97.8 · Ending allotment, restoring land, developing credit, and managing resources
Section 97.5

Ending Allotment: Sections 1 and 2 and the Continuation of Trust Land

Section 1 of the Indian Reorganization Act reversed the principal direction of the General Allotment Act. Under the earlier statute and its amendments, reservation land could be divided among individuals, with remaining land declared surplus and opened to non-Indian entry. Section 1 prohibited further allotments in severalty on reservations. This was a fundamental change in the legal framework: it removed a standing mechanism by which more land could pass out of tribal ownership and, eventually, into private non-Indian hands. For Indigenous nations that still held substantial tracts in common, the provision protected the land that remained.

Section 2 addressed existing allotments. Under the Dawes Act and the Burke Act, trust periods on allotments were scheduled to expire, after which an allottee might receive a patent in fee and the land might become subject to taxation and sale. Section 2 extended the trust periods and restrictions on alienation on allotted land until otherwise directed by Congress. The effect was to halt the automatic movement of such land into unrestricted ownership. The change did not, however, make allotments the same as communal tribal land. Allottees and their heirs retained individual beneficial interests, held in trust by the United States, and those interests continued to descend through inheritance, with all the complexity that implied.

Students should compare these provisions with the earlier policy. The Dawes Act of 1887 and its sequels established allotment as the standard method of land tenure and assumed that tribal government would diminish. The Burke Act of 1906 had enabled earlier release from trust for allottees whom the Secretary judged competent. The Indian Reorganization Act reversed the presumption: land remaining in trust would stay in trust, and further allotment on reservations would end. This shift from compulsory individualization to protection of trust status was one of the most concrete expressions of the change in federal policy.

The limits of the provisions are equally important. The Act did not automatically recover land that had already passed out of Indigenous ownership. Where allotments had been sold, patented, or leased long-term, and where surplus lands had been entered by homesteaders, the title in non-Indian hands remained valid. The Act did not void such transactions, and Section 3 specifically preserved valid existing rights when surplus lands were restored. Even where land remained in trust, heirship continued to divide interests among descendants. Fractionation, the proliferation of tiny undivided shares in a parcel, was not solved by the Act. Section 4 restricted the transfer of trust land, including sale to non-Indians, but inheritance continued to multiply owners, and the problem persisted into later decades, as later chapters on land and law show.

Examples from Chapter 96 illustrate the distinction between preventing further losses and recovering past ones. On reservations where allotment had occurred early, such as those in the northern Plains and in parts of the Northwest, large portions of reservation land were already held in fee by non-Indians in 1934, and the Act's protection applied to what remained. On reservations where allotment had not been carried out, such as many in the Southwest, the prohibition on future allotment protected communal holdings from a policy that had not yet reached them. The same provision thus had different significance in different places, depending on how far allotment had already gone.

Historians have therefore described Sections 1 and 2 as a defensive achievement. They closed an avenue of loss and stabilized the land base at the level it had reached by 1934. Recovery of land required other mechanisms, which are the subject of the next section, and those mechanisms depended on appropriations and administrative decisions that were uneven. The distinction between ending allotment and restoring land helps explain why many Indigenous leaders could welcome the first while remaining dissatisfied with the second.

Historical Vocabulary
Allotment, Trust Tenure, and Land Restoration

Definition. Allotment was the assignment of individual parcels of reservation land to individuals. Trust tenure describes land whose legal title is held by the United States for the benefit of a tribe or an individual Indian, subject to restrictions on sale and taxation. Restoration refers to the return of land to tribal ownership, as under Section 3 for surplus lands not yet sold. Acquisition is the purchase of new land to be held in trust, as under Section 5.

Historical context. Allotment began with the 1887 Act; trust restrictions were intended to be temporary; the 1934 Act made them continuing and prohibited new reservation allotments while permitting restoration and acquisition.

Why it matters. The terms describe different actions with different results. Ending allotment prevented future loss; continuing trust prevented a class of existing land from passing automatically into fee; restoration and acquisition added land.

Potential misunderstanding. Students often assume that ending allotment returned land. It did not. Land already sold to non-Indians generally remained in non-Indian hands.

Section 97.6

Restoring Tribal Land: Sections 3 and 5, Trust Acquisitions, and Their Limitations

While Sections 1 and 2 protected existing holdings, Sections 3 and 5 aimed to expand them. Section 3 authorized the Secretary to restore to tribal ownership the remaining surplus lands of any Indian reservation that had been opened but not yet disposed of. The qualification matters: only lands still in the government's hands could be restored, and the provision explicitly preserved valid existing rights. In practice, restoration affected tracts that had been declared surplus but not entered or sold, and the amount varied sharply among reservations depending on how much had been claimed by homesteaders and others.

Section 5 authorized the Secretary, in his discretion, to acquire land and rights in land for Indians, with title taken in the name of the United States in trust for the tribe or individual Indian for whom it was acquired. The statute authorized appropriations of up to $2 million annually for this purpose. The importance of this mechanism lay in its reversal of the earlier presumption that Indigenous land would shrink. For the first time in decades, federal law provided a standing means for adding to the Indigenous land base. The authority extended to individual Indians as well as tribes, although the tribal acquisitions had the greater political significance.

The mechanics reveal why the program was constrained. The Secretary's authority was discretionary, so acquisitions depended on administrative priorities. Money had to be appropriated, and Congress did not consistently fund the program at the level the statute authorized. Land had to be available for purchase from willing sellers, and local opposition could be considerable where counties and non-Indian neighbors feared the loss of taxable property. This last point explains why Section 5 provided that trust land would be exempt from state and local taxation. The exemption protected tribal resources, but it also meant that acquisitions could reduce local tax revenues, an argument opponents used. The legal consequences were large: land taken into trust fell outside state taxing authority and generally within federal and tribal jurisdiction, a matter that would become central to later disputes over jurisdiction discussed in Chapter 118.

The distinction among forms of ownership is essential to understanding what land restoration meant. Tribal ownership refers to land held collectively for the tribe, with the United States holding legal title in trust. Individual beneficial ownership refers to allotments or acquired parcels held in trust for individuals. Federal legal title is the formal interest the United States holds in trust land, which underlies the Secretary's approval authority over leases, sales, and encumbrances. Because the United States held legal title, restored and acquired land remained subject to federal administration even as it came under tribal or individual benefit. Recovery of land was therefore also a continuation of trusteeship.

Students should also note that modern citations to the Act's land provisions often refer to the codified versions, such as 25 U.S.C. §5108 for the acquisition authority, and that the codified text has been amended since 1934. When studying the original program, the 1934 enactment is the proper source. The Act's land provisions produced real if modest gains, and some nations used them to consolidate fragmented holdings, to purchase tracts near communities, or to add pasture and timberland. But the total effect remained small in comparison with the land lost between 1887 and 1934, and the Act did not provide mechanisms for recovering land lost by sale, for settling claims based on treaty violations, or for resolving the checkerboard of mixed ownership that allotment had created.

For Indigenous communities, then, the land provisions offered both opportunity and frustration. Where the Secretary acted and Congress funded purchases, tribes could regain access to land and water. Where funds were short or local resistance strong, the potential went unrealized. The Act's land program thus illustrates a pattern found throughout the statute: it created authority, and the extent of its practical effect depended on appropriations, administrative priorities, and the political relationships that nations could build.

Section 97.7

Tribal Credit, Corporate Enterprises, and Economic Development under the Act

The reformers who drafted the Act recognized that protecting land would not by itself overcome poverty. Reservation economies in the 1930s were constrained by limited capital, the absence of credit, small and fragmented holdings, and the Depression's collapse of markets. The Act therefore included provisions for economic organization and finance. Section 10 authorized a revolving credit fund of $10 million for loans to chartered Indian corporations, to be used for the purpose of promoting the economic development of tribes and their members. The figure is the historical authorization in the 1934 statute; later amendments increased the amount to $20 million, and the original figure should be used in discussing the Act as passed.

The credit program was linked to the corporate charter provisions of Section 17. Loans under Section 10 were to be made to chartered Indian corporations, which meant that access to the revolving fund depended on a tribe's having organized a federal corporation. The logic was that a corporation could contract, borrow, hold property, and carry out enterprises in ways that a council operating under an older, informal structure could not. Officials also saw credit as a way to encourage community-owned enterprises, such as livestock herds, farming equipment, stores, and processing operations, which would employ members and distribute benefits. The next section explains why the charter mechanism was separate from governmental organization under Section 16.

Possible uses of credit were varied. Tribes might finance purchases of livestock to build herds, equipment for farming, improvements for irrigation or grazing, or small manufacturing and craft enterprises. The Indian Service encouraged cooperative marketing of products. Still, authorization was not the same as accomplishment. Congress did not necessarily appropriate funds at authorized levels, and tribes had to qualify for loans under conditions established by the Interior Department. Many communities lacked managerial experience in the kinds of enterprises envisioned, in part because decades of federal supervision had limited their opportunities to operate such enterprises. Federal administrators supervised loans and sometimes advised on enterprise decisions, which meant that economic choices remained subject to departmental approval.

The credit program also shows how the Act's goals intersected with local conditions. A tribe with substantial trust land, access to markets, and existing organizational capacity might use credit to expand a successful enterprise. A tribe with little land or remote location faced more difficult prospects. Economic organization depended on capital, management, local resources, and political stability, and the Act could supply only part of these. Moreover, the revolving fund was a federal loan program, not a grant. Communities that borrowed had to repay, and a failed enterprise could leave a tribe in debt. This point is important because the Act's economic provisions sometimes are remembered as simple benefits. They were instruments that could help or burden communities depending on circumstances.

Historians have also pointed out that the scale of the credit program was modest in relation to the needs the reformers identified. Even the authorized $10 million, spread across the many reservations that qualified, meant limited sums per community, and actual appropriations were uneven. Where loans were made, they often supported agricultural or livestock enterprises, reflecting the existing economy of many reservations. The Act did not create a broad program of industrial investment or ensure markets for products. It created a framework of finance and organization whose effect depended on the resources that tribes and the federal government brought to it.

The broader significance of the credit provisions lies in what they signaled. For decades, federal policy had treated Indigenous economic activity primarily as a matter of individual farming or wage labor. The 1934 Act recognized collective enterprise as a legitimate form of economic organization. This did not guarantee success, but it altered the policy framework, and in later decades, as Chapters 121 and 122 show, tribal governments would develop enterprises on foundations that included the legal and organizational precedents of the 1930s.

Section 97.8

Land Conservation, Grazing, and the Contradictions of Federal Resource Management

The Indian New Deal's attention to land was not only a matter of ownership but also of use. The conservation movement of the early twentieth century and the New Deal's emphasis on soil and range management meant that federal officials regarded the condition of reservation land as a policy problem. Erosion, overgrazing, and drought, aggravated by the Dust Bowl years, affected large parts of the West, including reservations. The Indian Reorganization Act itself contained a conservation provision, Section 6, directing the Secretary to make rules and regulations for the operation and management of Indian forestry units on the principle of sustained-yield management and for the protection of range from deterioration. Other measures, including the Indian Emergency Conservation Work program, put Indigenous workers on projects aimed at erosion control and water development.

These programs could bring real benefits. Wage employment during the Depression was valuable, and well-designed conservation and water projects improved some range and farmland. Many Indigenous participants valued the work and the income it brought. But the conservation agenda also generated conflict, because officials made decisions about land use that affected households' livelihoods, often with limited consultation. Where federal technicians identified overgrazing as the cause of erosion, the proposed remedy was to reduce livestock numbers, a measure that struck directly at the economy of pastoral peoples.

The central case is the Diné Nation. The Navajo Reservation, in Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah, was a large area of arid and semi-arid land on which Diné families depended on sheep, goats, horses, and cattle. Federal officials in the early 1930s concluded that the range was severely overgrazed and that erosion was damaging not only the reservation but the Colorado River drainage and the new Boulder (Hoover) Dam downstream. Federal agencies, including the Soil Conservation Service and the Indian Service, proposed and then carried out programs to reduce stock numbers, beginning in the early 1930s and expanding in the following years. The reductions took the form of federal purchases of animals and, increasingly, requirements to dispose of stock according to formulas applied to permit holders.

The livestock-reduction programs must be distinguished from the statutory provisions of the Indian Reorganization Act. The Act did not mandate stock reduction. The reductions were carried out through administrative authority and appropriations connected with the Indian Service and with emergency and conservation programs, and they began before the Act was passed. Nevertheless, for many Diné people the programs and the Act were part of the same federal effort. The Commissioner who proposed the Act was the same official whose administration directed the reductions, and the referendum on the Act in 1935 occurred in the midst of the stock-reduction campaign. The distinction is analytically important, but historians must also explain why Diné people drew the connection they did.

Officials described the measures as environmental protection and, in the longer term, as a means of preserving the pastoral economy by conserving the range. Diné families experienced them differently. Livestock was not only a source of income and food; it was wealth, a basis of household independence, and part of a social and cultural order in which herds had significance beyond market value. Reductions were often applied in ways that fell heavily on households with small herds, and the formula for reductions did not always match local knowledge of carrying capacity. Accounts collected from Diné participants in later decades describe the loss of animals as a profound economic and cultural injury. Historians who have examined the programs, such as Richard White in his study of Navajo economy, have emphasized how stock reduction created dependency on wage labor and federal programs.

The Diné case demonstrates the central tension of the Indian New Deal. The same federal reformers who wished to protect Indigenous community life also imposed resource-management policies designed by outside experts. Preserving a community's land base, in the officials' view, sometimes required changing how the community used it. The result was a clash between two understandings of protection. This tension prepares students to understand why some nations rejected federal reorganization despite the statute's stated goals, and why a vote on the Act could become, in practice, a vote on the whole relationship between the community and the Indian Service. Section 97.14 examines that vote.

Division III

Tribal Government, Constitutions, and the Meaning of Self-Government

§§97.9–97.12 · Federal constitutional models, corporate authority, referendums, and Indigenous governance
Section 97.9

Section 16: Tribal Constitutions, Elected Councils, and Federally Recognized Government

Section 16 is the part of the statute most closely associated with the idea of reorganization. It gave any tribe, or tribes residing on the same reservation, the right to organize for their common welfare and to adopt a constitution and bylaws. The document would take effect when ratified by a majority vote of the adult members at a special election called by the Secretary of the Interior, and it required the Secretary's approval. Section 16 also confirmed that an organized tribe's council would retain powers vested in it by existing law, and it added further powers: to employ legal counsel, subject to the Secretary's approval of the choice and fee; to prevent the sale, disposition, lease, or encumbrance of tribal lands, interests in land, or other tribal assets without the consent of the tribe; and to negotiate with federal, state, and local governments.

The process combined local and federal steps. A community that wished to organize typically requested assistance from the Indian Service, which provided advisers and drafting help. The Interior Department circulated model constitutions and bylaws that served as starting points, and Indian Service staff and field representatives worked with committees to prepare drafts adapted, to a greater or lesser degree, to local circumstances. The draft was then submitted to a vote of eligible adults at an election supervised by the Secretary's representatives, and, if ratified, sent to the Secretary for approval. The tribal vote and the federal approval were both required, and the second step gave Interior authority to shape or reject provisions even after a community had voted.

Early constitutions under the Act commonly included certain recurring features. They established a tribal council, usually elected by the adult members, sometimes by district or community. They defined membership, often by reference to a base roll, descent, residence, or a combination of these. They specified the territory to which the government's authority applied, set out the council's powers to make rules for the management of tribal affairs, provided for elections and terms of office, and established amendment procedures. Many contained clauses that required the Secretary's approval of certain ordinances or actions, especially those concerning land and funds. The resulting documents were written constitutions in a form familiar to American political culture, with elected representative bodies as their centerpiece.

Students should compare these arrangements with existing Indigenous governments. Many nations had long possessed their own institutions: hereditary chiefs and councils, clan and lineage systems that allocated responsibilities, village councils that governed through consensus, religious societies with political functions, and treaty-based bodies with recognized authority to speak for a people. Some of these institutions had been weakened by agents who recognized only selected leaders or by courts of Indian offenses that displaced traditional dispute resolution. Others remained strong. The model constitutions assumed a particular form of political organization, centered on a representative council and majority elections, that did not correspond neatly to all such arrangements. Communities with strong traditional institutions sometimes had to decide whether to adapt the model, preserve older systems alongside it, or decline to organize.

The key historical distinction is that the federal statute offered a procedure for organizing governments that already belonged to distinct political peoples. It did not create those peoples or originate their authority. The federal government's role was to prescribe a method of recognition and approval within its own legal system. Indigenous nations' sovereignty, as discussed in Chapter 1 and later in Chapters 114 and 118, had existed prior to and apart from the statute, and the Act itself did not purport to confer it. But the practical effect of the approval requirement was that a constitution adopted under Section 16 operated within a federal framework, in which the Secretary retained supervisory authority.

Historians have asked whether the model constitutions facilitated political representation or imposed unfamiliar institutions. The evidence supports both claims, depending on place. Some communities adapted the model creatively, adding provisions suited to their traditions. Others adopted drafts closely resembling the model, sometimes because they lacked the time, expertise, or confidence to draft alternatives. In some places the new councils provided an effective means of acting collectively, negotiating with agencies, and managing tribal resources. In others, they created divisions between elected representatives and traditional authorities or between groups that had been advantaged or disadvantaged by earlier policies. The next sections consider how these tensions played out in practice.

Section 97.10

Section 17: Corporate Charters and the Separation of Government from Enterprise

Section 17 created a different instrument from Section 16. It authorized the Secretary of the Interior to issue a charter of incorporation to a tribe, upon petition of at least one-third of the adult Indians of the reservation, to be operative when ratified by a vote of the adult Indians living on the reservation. The charter could confer powers in addition to those vested in a tribe or council by existing law, including the power to purchase, take by gift or bequest, own, hold, manage, operate, and dispose of property of every description, to purchase restricted Indian lands and issue interests in corporate property in exchange, and to exercise such incidental powers as business operations required. The statute also provided that the charter could not authorize the sale, mortgage, or lease for a period exceeding ten years of any land included within the limits of the reservation.

This ten-year ceiling belongs to the original 1934 statute (48 Stat. 988, §17). It should not be confused with the twenty-five-year restriction appearing in later codifications after the 1990 amendment to Section 17. Reading the 1990 wording backward into the 1934 Act would distort the scope of the economic authority Congress first authorized. Contemporary approval and financing constraints also operated alongside this limit; a charter did not grant a tribe unrestricted power to alienate trust land.

The distinction between constitution and charter matters. A Section 16 constitution created a government: it determined who could vote, how a council would be chosen, what authority the council would exercise, and how decisions would be made. A Section 17 charter created a legal entity, a federally chartered corporation, with capacity to enter contracts, hold property, borrow money, and conduct enterprises. The two instruments could be held by the same people, but they served different purposes and required separate procedures. A tribe could adopt a constitution without seeking a charter, and the charter could not substitute for a governing document.

Federal officials favored the corporate form for several reasons. They believed that a corporation could manage property and business with greater flexibility than a council whose powers derived from older legal authority. They expected that incorporation would facilitate borrowing from the revolving credit fund, since Section 10 loans were designated for chartered corporations, and that it would encourage collective economic projects such as cooperatives, herds, and processing enterprises. They also thought a legally defined corporate entity would offer protection to creditors and tribe alike by separating enterprise liabilities from other tribal assets. The corporate form thus responded to the reformers' view that Indigenous economic development required organizational tools adapted to modern business.

The requirements for issuance placed the Department of the Interior at the center. The Secretary issued the charter, which meant that the department could specify terms and conditions, and charters commonly included provisions that kept certain transactions subject to departmental approval. Tribal ratification by vote was also required, so the charter was not valid without community consent. Ratification of a charter was a separate decision from the earlier vote on the Act's application and from the adoption of a constitution, and a community might approve one without approving the others.

The Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes provide a clear example of separate instruments. Their constitution was ratified in October 1935, and their corporate charter was ratified separately in April 1936. Different timing and procedures meant that the community had addressed two distinct questions, one concerning how to govern and the other concerning how to organize business. It would be a mistake, however, to assume that all Indigenous nations receiving benefits under the Act became Section 17 corporations. Many organized under constitutions without seeking charters, some sought charters later, and others did not use that mechanism at all. The charter provisions were available to tribes, not required of them.

The separation of government from enterprise has had a long afterlife. The idea that a tribal government might charter a corporate entity to manage business, with some separation between governmental functions and commercial risk, remained influential in later tribal economic development, a subject taken up in Chapters 121 and 122. At the same time, historians note that the charter's reliance on federal issuance and the Secretary's oversight limited the independence of enterprises in the 1930s, and the extent to which the corporate form increased Indigenous control over resources varied with circumstances.

Historical Vocabulary
Tribal Constitution, Corporate Charter, and Federal Recognition

Definition. A tribal constitution (Section 16) is a written governing document adopted by ratification and approved by the Secretary. A corporate charter (Section 17) is a federal instrument that creates a tribal business corporation with specified powers. Federal recognition refers to the United States' acknowledgment of a tribe's political status and its government-to-government relationship, a concept discussed in Chapter 1 and later in Chapter 118.

Historical context. The statute established procedures for organization and incorporation. It did not itself grant or create recognition for tribes that had not previously been recognized, and many nations were already recognized through treaties, statutes, and long administrative practice.

Why it matters. The three concepts refer to different things: governance, business organization, and federal acknowledgment. Confusing them leads to errors such as assuming that a tribe without a charter lacked a government or that organization under the Act created a tribe's sovereignty.

Potential misunderstanding. Federal recognition does not mean the United States created the nation. It means the United States acknowledges an existing political entity for legal purposes.

Section 97.11

Section 18: Tribal Referendums, Rejection Rights, and the 1935 Amendments

Section 18 contains the Act's most unusual feature for the period: a provision allowing each reservation to decide whether the Act would apply to it. The original provision stated that the Act would not apply to any reservation in which a majority of the adult Indians, voting at a special election called by the Secretary of the Interior, voted against its application. The Secretary was directed to call such an election within one year of the Act's approval, to hold it by secret ballot, and to give thirty days' notice. The provision originated in congressional concern that the law should not be forced on communities that did not want it, and it was a significant departure from earlier policy, in which Indigenous consent had rarely been required for legislation affecting their land and government.

The election procedure raised immediate problems. Who counted as an "adult Indian" eligible to vote? How would notice be given on large and remote reservations, and in what languages? Would those who did not vote be counted at all? The statute's language, a majority of the adult Indians "voting" against, left room for dispute over whether abstentions affected outcomes. In some early interpretations, the focus was whether a majority of eligible adults had voted to reject; in others, results were read as the majority of those who voted. Differences in interpretation could change the outcome in a close election and shaped how Interior treated the results.

Congress addressed these questions in amendments of June 15, 1935. The amendments altered the election procedures and established that the decision would turn on a majority of the votes cast, with a minimum participation requirement of 30 percent of eligible voters for the specified elections. The amendment's effect was to prevent a small group of voters from deciding the outcome in an election with very low turnout, while also settling that abstention would not by itself defeat the Act's rejection. The amendments were adopted partway through the election period and so affected many of the contests held in 1935 and later, and students must keep track of which standard governed the election they are studying.

Students should keep three decisions distinct, since they are frequently confused. The first was the vote on the Act's application under Section 18, in which eligible voters decided whether the reservation would be covered by the statute. The second was the adoption of a tribal constitution under Section 16, a separate election in which voters ratified a governing document. The third was the ratification of a corporate charter under Section 17. A reservation that voted to accept the Act might never proceed to a constitution or charter, and a reservation that voted to reject the Act forfeited the statutory mechanisms for constitutions and charters, although it did not thereby lose all other benefits of the Indian New Deal, which included programs not dependent on the Act.

The elections raise the question of whether federally established procedures captured Indigenous political consent adequately. Several issues are relevant. Voter eligibility was defined by federal regulation. Notice and campaign periods were short in some places. Federal officials, including superintendents, promoted the Act, and critics charged that agency staff exerted pressure or presented information in a one-sided way. Turnout varied, and the 30 percent threshold meant that an election could be valid even when most eligible voters did not take part. Illiteracy, language barriers, and distance affected participation. At the same time, the elections gave communities a direct opportunity to say no, and many did. Historians therefore often treat the referendums as meaningful but imperfect indicators of Indigenous opinion, to be interpreted in light of local circumstances and not as simple measures of support.

Historical Vocabulary
Referendum, Ratification, and Tribal Consent

Definition. A referendum here means the Section 18 election on whether the Act applied to a reservation. Ratification is a vote approving a specific document, such as a constitution or charter. Consent refers to the community's authorization, which depends on who could vote, what information they had, and what choices were presented.

Historical context. Procedures changed in 1935, and the voting population, notice, and participation threshold were all defined by federal law and regulation.

Why it matters. The population entitled to vote, the standard for counting results, and the number of separate decisions determined what an outcome could be said to mean.

Potential misunderstanding. A "yes" vote on the Act's application was not approval of a particular constitution, and an election result was not necessarily a unanimous or fully informed expression of community will.

Section 97.12

Indigenous Government and Federal Supervision: Authority, Representation, and Internal Disagreement

After a community adopted a constitution, the practical questions of governing began. The council could meet, pass resolutions and ordinances, appoint committees, manage tribal funds, represent the tribe to outside governments, and exercise the powers granted in the constitution and by Section 16. But most reorganized governments operated alongside continuing federal authority. Leases of tribal land, expenditures of certain funds, and specified ordinances often required the Secretary's approval, either under the constitution or under other law. The superintendent of the agency remained an important figure, and in many places the relationship between council and agency was one of negotiation and sometimes conflict.

Comparing the powers of tribal councils with those retained by federal officials shows the mixed character of reorganization. Councils gained clearer authority to speak for their communities, to employ counsel, and to resist the disposal of tribal land without consent. Federal officials continued to control the trust land and resources, to approve major contracts, to administer tribal funds in many cases, and to supervise the relationship between tribes and states. The practical balance varied with the strength of the council, the character of the superintendent, the resources of the tribe, and the political resources of its leaders. In some places councils used their new standing to bargain effectively. In others, they found that their authority was limited by the very approvals they had sought to loosen.

Representation within communities was not uniform. Elected councils often drew on individuals who had been active in earlier business committees or who were literate in English and familiar with agency procedures, and traditional leaders sometimes found themselves outside the new structures. Studies of reservations in the northern Plains, such as work on the Lakota reservations by Thomas Biolsi, have documented tensions between elected councils and groups that regarded themselves as more traditional, and between people classified as "mixed-blood" and "full-blood." Women's participation varied. Many constitutions extended the vote to women, in line with national practice, but in nations with matrilineal or women-centered political traditions, written constitutions that centered electoral politics could sit uneasily with existing roles. Younger activists, returned students, and families affected by past policies each had different stakes in the new arrangements.

Membership provisions deserve particular attention. Constitutions had to define who was a member of the tribe for voting and other purposes. Many drew on census rolls compiled by the agency, sometimes combined with residence requirements and descent criteria, including in some cases minimum degrees of Indian ancestry. These choices incorporated federal classifications into tribal governing documents and sometimes defined membership differently from older kinship-based understandings. The consequences were long-lasting, because membership rules determined access to voting, services, and resources, and later controversies over enrollment and blood quantum, discussed in Chapter 144, often trace their origins to documents written in this period.

Examples of success and dispute coexisted. Some reorganized councils strengthened Indigenous bargaining power, using their authority to negotiate leases, to challenge unfavorable agency decisions, and to press claims in Washington. Others became arenas of factional disputes over representation, over the control of tribal funds, or over the relationship between the elected council and traditional authorities. The point for students is not that reorganization uniformly succeeded or failed but that it created a political terrain on which Indigenous people contested their own futures.

These observations lead to the chapter's central constitutional question: how much self-government is restored when the legal structure and important decisions remain subject to federal approval? The answer depends on what is meant by self-government. If it refers to a tribe's inherent authority, the Act did not grant it and could not remove it. If it refers to the practical ability to govern within the United States legal system, the Act provided a framework that expanded some powers, such as organizing a government recognized by the Interior Department, while leaving others subject to supervision. The next division examines how Indigenous communities themselves answered the question.

Cause and Effect

How Referendum Rules, Federal Approval, and Local Politics Shaped Reorganization

Long-term causes

Earlier relations with agents, experiences of allotment and land loss, the strength of existing councils and traditional authorities, and differences in economic circumstances shaped how communities viewed federal proposals.

Short-term causes

Rules about voter eligibility and participation, the amendments of June 15, 1935, the quality of information, and local campaigns for and against the Act influenced outcomes.

Immediate trigger

Each reservation's Section 18 election, held under the Secretary's supervision within the period fixed by the statute and its amendments.

Effects

Acceptance opened the way to constitutions and charters; rejection excluded a reservation from the Act. Constitutions needed federal approval and often retained supervisory requirements.

Historical complexity: Outcomes cannot be explained by a single factor. Local history, federal practice, and the procedures themselves all mattered, and the same voting result could reflect different motives among different voters.

Section 97.13

The Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes: An Early Example of Reorganization

The Flathead Reservation in western Montana was established under the Hellgate Treaty of 1855 and became the home of the Salish, Pend d'Oreille, and Kootenai peoples, who are now organized as the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes. The reservation lay in a region of mountain valleys, rivers, and Flathead Lake, with fisheries, timber, grazing land, and farmland. The Bitterroot Salish had been pressed to leave their valley for the reservation in 1891, and the reservation was reshaped by later federal policy. The Flathead Allotment Act of 1904 provided for allotment and for the sale of surplus land to non-Indians, and the reservation was opened to entry in 1910. The result was a mixed landscape, with Indigenous allotments and tribal holdings alongside land owned by non-Indian settlers and by the federal government for irrigation and other purposes.

The political conditions leading into the Indian New Deal included the effects of this land loss, the operation of an irrigation project that charged landowners, and the question of the Flathead River's hydroelectric potential. A dam site on the river was licensed in 1930 to a private power company, and the use of the river's water power was an economic and political matter of lasting consequence for the tribes. The Flathead community had also long maintained a tribal council that dealt with the agency, and individuals from the reservation, including D'Arcy McNickle, who later joined the Indian Service in the mid-1930s and became a significant figure in twentieth-century Indigenous politics, were connected to the reform discussions. These circumstances gave the reservation both an incentive to seek land and economic tools and an existing political base from which to act.

The Flathead voters accepted the Act's application, and the community proceeded to draft and ratify a constitution. The Confederated Salish and Kootenai Constitution was ratified by tribal voters on October 4, 1935, and approved by the Secretary of the Interior on October 28, 1935. The Library of Congress preserves the published text. The document established a tribal council representing the reservation's districts, defined membership, set out the council's powers, and provided procedures for elections and amendment. The title and structure indicate that the three peoples who made up the Confederated Tribes were organized as a single constitutional government.

The corporate charter followed separately. It was ratified on April 25, 1936, and created a federally chartered corporation for the Confederated Tribes to conduct business and manage property. The dual instruments illustrate the distinction explained in Section 97.10. The constitution established the tribes' government. The charter provided a legal entity for enterprise. The tribes' use of the charter allowed access to the credit and organizational possibilities that the Act offered, subject to the limitations discussed earlier.

The relationship between the new council and national identity deserves attention. For the Salish, Pend d'Oreille, and Kootenai, whose distinct languages and histories predated the reservation, the constitution made it possible to act as a confederation with a shared governing body while maintaining separate identities. The council became the body through which the tribes negotiated with the agency, managed resources, and, over time, pursued claims. Historians note that the reorganization did not create these peoples' identities; it provided a recognized legal form for their collective action.

The case should not be read as a simple success story. Adoption of the constitution did not restore lost land, resolve the checkerboard pattern of ownership on the reservation, or end the Interior Department's supervisory authority. The tribes continued to face economic difficulties in the Depression, limited capital, and dependence on federal approvals. What reorganization created was an opportunity: a legally recognized council and corporation that could be used to pursue those aims. Later developments, including the tribes' efforts to assert greater control over the river, land, and services, took place on the foundation laid in these years, and they illustrate how Indigenous nations used the new framework for their own purposes within, and sometimes against, federal limits.

Section 97.14

The Diné Nation and the Rejection of the Indian Reorganization Act, 1935

The Diné Nation (Navajo Nation) was the largest reservation population in the United States, and its decision carried unusual weight. The reservation had grown through successive treaties and executive orders after the Treaty of 1868, discussed in Chapter 71, and by the 1930s encompassed a vast area of the Colorado Plateau. Diné political life included local leaders, family and clan networks, and the Navajo Tribal Council, established in 1923 under Interior Department regulations at a time when oil discoveries required an authorized body that could approve leases. The Council's early history was thus tied to the Department's interest in resource development, and many Diné people regarded it as an instrument that had been created by outsiders and not an expression of Diné political traditions.

The early 1930s brought the livestock-reduction programs described in Section 97.8. Diné households were still absorbing the effects of drought and the Depression when federal officials began to purchase and then to require the reduction of animals. Collier and Indian Service officials toured the reservation, held meetings to explain the Act and the conservation program, and sought support from Council members and community leaders. Opinion among Diné leaders was divided. Some supported reorganization, believing that it offered protection of land and a stronger council. Others opposed it or distrusted the Indian Service, and Jacob C. Morgan, a Diné leader with experience in mission and school settings, became a prominent critic of Collier's policies. The reasons for support and opposition varied by individual and household, and the historical record does not permit a single explanation.

The referendum took place in June 1935, on June 14 and 15 according to the chapter's chronology. Theodore Haas's official compilation, prepared by an Interior Department official after the elections, records approximately 7,608 votes in favor and 7,992 against. The margin was narrow, and the rejection meant that the Diné Nation would not organize under Sections 16 and 17 of the Act. The vote preceded by a day the June 15 amendments to the election provisions, a circumstance that complicates efforts to apply a single standard retrospectively, and it should be read with the precise rules and returns in view. Students should distinguish the raw returns, which are documentary evidence, from interpretations of why voters chose as they did.

Historians have emphasized the connection between the narrow rejection and resentment over livestock reduction. Many voters appear to have linked the Act with the commissioner's administration and its stock policies, and the vote was, for many, an occasion to register opposition to the Indian Service's program. But the unsupported assumption that every opposing voter had the same motive should be avoided. Some opposed because they feared that organization would increase federal control. Some objected to particular officials. Some did not understand the Act's provisions, in a period when information was transmitted orally and in translation. Some favored the Act but were outvoted. And some who opposed reorganization supported other forms of assistance.

It is also important to distinguish refusal of the Act from rejection of all future institutional change. The Diné did not cease to govern themselves after 1935. The Navajo Tribal Council continued to operate, and later in the decade the Interior Department approved revised procedures for the Council. The Nation continued to seek economic assistance, education, and recognition of its land claims. What the referendum decided was narrower: that the Diné would not accept the specific framework of the Indian Reorganization Act, as it then stood and in the context of the stock-reduction campaign. In subsequent decades, the Nation developed its own governmental structures, including a tribal court system and a larger administrative apparatus, which later chapters examine.

The Diné case therefore teaches several lessons. Acceptance of the Act was not universal, and a nation could reject it without ceasing to be a political community. The referendum could become an expression of broader grievance, and the conditions under which voters decided shaped the meaning of their choice. And the right to say no, built into Section 18, had real consequences: the largest reservation population in the country remained outside the Act's organizational framework.

Everyday Life and Human Experience

Diné Households and Federal Conservation Policy

To understand why the referendum was bound up with livestock reduction, students should consider how stock reduction entered daily life. Documentation of this experience includes the collection of oral testimonies assembled by Ruth Roessel and Broderick H. Johnson, Navajo Livestock Reduction: A National Disgrace (1974), as well as scholarly studies such as Richard White's The Roots of Dependency (1983) and Donald Parman's The Navajos and the New Deal (1976). This feature does not recreate any individual's words; it summarizes patterns found in those sources and directs students to the testimonies themselves.

For many households, sheep and goats supplied meat, wool for weaving, and a source of cash through sales to traders. Herds were also held by families across generations, and the number and condition of animals marked a household's standing and security. Women's ownership and management of flocks and the weaving economy that depended on wool meant that reductions affected women's work and income in particular ways. When federal programs required families to sell or dispose of animals, households had to decide how to meet obligations, which animals to give up, and how to replace lost income. Some testimonies describe distress at the loss of animals that had been built up over many years. Others describe the economic strain that followed, as families turned toward wage employment and federal relief.

These experiences help explain why many Diné people associated the federal program with the Indian Service as a whole, and why a referendum organized by that same service in 1935 could be received with suspicion. The historical task is to treat these sources with care: oral testimonies recorded decades later reflect memory and the circumstances of collection, and the specific circumstances of stock reduction varied among districts and households.

Section 97.15

Hopi Political Institutions, Pueblo Communities, and the Problem of Representation

The Hopi villages of northeastern Arizona present a distinctive test of the Act's assumptions. The Hopi are organized in separate villages, each with its own leadership, clans, and ceremonial responsibilities, and historically no single governing body spoke for all of them. Village authority rested on clan and ceremonial roles, with village leaders known as kikmongwi holding positions connected to the ritual and social order. The Hopi had experienced intense factional conflict in the early twentieth century, including the split at Oraibi in 1906, in part over responses to federal policy. By the 1930s the question of how a single constitutional government could relate to autonomous villages was a practical difficulty.

Students should keep the Hopi decisions separate. On June 15, 1935, the Hopi voted on whether the Act would apply; Theodore Haas’s later compilation records 519 votes for application and 299 against. Many eligible voters did not take part, and some traditionalist communities questioned the federal process. The distinct constitutional referendum took place on October 24, 1936: 651 voters ratified the constitution and 104 voted against, according to the document’s certification. Secretary of the Interior Harold L. Ickes approved the constitution on December 19, 1936. These were two different decisions, with different electorates and different legal effects; neither demonstrated unanimous agreement among Hopi villages.

The 1936 Hopi constitution explicitly made provision for continued traditional village government. It created a tribal council with representatives from the villages and specified powers for that body, while recognizing the authority of villages to manage their own affairs according to their own customs. This arrangement shows that a written constitution could incorporate existing institutions even while creating a broader council structure. In effect, it established a federated model suited to a people whose political life was organized by village.

The certification attached to the published constitution also records the threshold required by the amended 1935 electoral law. This makes the document especially valuable as evidence of federal procedure: it establishes a count and an approval date but does not establish why individuals abstained. A later claim that an entire village endorsed the council cannot be inferred from a reservation-wide total. The adopted constitution should therefore be read alongside village records and later accounts of representation, including the decisions of villages that did not send representatives.

The experience also reveals the difficulties of representation. Some villages treated the Council as an alien creation, especially where leaders regarded the new government as a means of federal control or as an institution that bypassed the kikmongwi. Participation in the Council was uneven, and in the following years the Council's legitimacy was contested. The land question also complicated matters, since the 1882 executive-order reservation for the Hopi had been shared with Navajo residents, and the Hopi leaders' claims about boundaries and grazing were mixed with questions of who could speak for the Hopi in dealings with the federal government. Students should avoid the claim that approval by eligible voters necessarily represented unanimous village consent. The record shows that a significant number of Hopi did not participate and that some communities rejected the process.

A comparison with Pueblo communities in New Mexico illuminates the range of political traditions. The Rio Grande and western Pueblos were governed by systems combining civil officers, such as governors and councils, with religious leadership, and they had a history of collective action through the All Pueblo Council in defense of land. Many Pueblos did not adopt constitutions under the Act, preferring to retain existing systems of government, and the Act's land protections and the Pueblo-specific legislation of the 1920s and 1930s mattered more to them than the constitutional mechanism. A smaller number, including Santa Clara Pueblo, adopted constitutions. The Santa Clara constitution of 1935 later became a subject of legal controversy because of membership rules adopted by the Pueblo's council, a matter that reached the Supreme Court in 1978 and is discussed in Chapter 118. These contrasts show why nations with different political institutions evaluated reorganization differently, and why a model designed around elected councils did not fit all of them equally.

Section 97.16

The Geography of Acceptance and Rejection: Crow, Plains, and Other Tribal Decisions

The three principal cases should be set within the broader pattern. Haas's historical compilation records 258 IRA elections in 1934–1936, of which 181 accepted the Act and 77 rejected it. These figures refer to decisions on the Act's application and not to an identical number of ratified constitutions or corporate charters, since not every accepting reservation went on to adopt both instruments. They also count elections, which sometimes involved small communities, so they should not be read as a measure of Indigenous population percentages.

Regional patterns appear in the returns without being uniform. Many Plains reservations accepted the Act and proceeded to adopt constitutions. The Blackfeet of Montana adopted a constitution in 1935, and the Oglala Sioux Tribe of the Pine Ridge Reservation adopted one in 1936. In the Southwest, the Mescalero Apache adopted a constitution in 1936 and the Papago, now the Tohono O'odham Nation, in 1937, while the Diné rejected the Act and many Pueblos accepted its application without adopting constitutions in the standard form. But other communities rejected it. The Crow voters in Montana rejected reorganization, and prominent Crow leaders, among them Robert Yellowtail, who became Crow Agency superintendent on August 1, 1934, were critics of Collier's program who feared that it would concentrate authority in the Interior Department and undermine the Crow General Council. The Klamath of Oregon rejected the Act, and the Seneca Nation of Indians in New York, which already governed itself under a written constitution adopted in the nineteenth century, also rejected it.

The election tables make regional comparison more concrete. Haas records the Crow decision as 112 votes for and 689 against, and the Klamath decision as 56 for and 408 against. The Blackfeet vote was 823 for and 171 against; the Mescalero Apache, 273 for and 11 against; and Santa Clara Pueblo, 134 for and 34 against. These are counts of ballots on applicability, not constitutional ratifications. Even these striking numerical contrasts cannot reveal the thinking of individual voters without community-specific petitions, meetings, newspapers, and other testimony. For the Seneca communities of New York, the federal compilation lists constituent voting places separately, a reminder that calling a single outcome simply “the Seneca vote” can conceal local distinctions.

Why did official support vary? Historians have identified several overlapping factors, none of which suffices alone. Land tenure mattered. Communities with extensive allotment and land loss often saw value in the provisions ending allotment and permitting land acquisition, while those with large communal holdings, valuable timber, or leasing income sometimes feared that new federal involvement would endanger arrangements they valued. Political traditions mattered. Where a council or general assembly had an established record of dealing with the agency, as in Crow and Seneca cases, the Act might look less like an opportunity than a displacement. Local economic circumstances mattered, since credit and land programs appealed more to communities with a prospect of using them. And prior relations with federal administrators mattered, because trust or distrust of a particular superintendent affected how the Act was received.

Students must also be cautious about interpreting vote totals as a referendum on Indigenous sovereignty in the abstract. A "no" vote did not necessarily express a rejection of tribal self-government; in some cases it expressed confidence in existing institutions, and in others it expressed distrust of the federal sponsor. A "yes" vote did not necessarily express acceptance of federal supervision; in some cases it expressed a calculation that the Act's land and organizational provisions would serve the community's interests. The voters made choices about a specific federal statute, under procedures defined by the federal government, at a particular moment in the Depression.

The synthesis is that acceptance and rejection were both political decisions made under unequal historical circumstances. Indigenous communities did not choose freely among all imaginable options. They chose among those the federal government had placed before them. Within those limits, they exercised judgment, and their choices reflected distinct histories, institutions, and priorities. The decisions made in 1934–1936 shaped the next generations of tribal government in each community, and they remain part of how nations remember and interpret their relationship with the federal government.

Section 97.17

The Indian New Deal beyond the Original Act: Oklahoma and Alaska, 1936

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 did not establish uniform arrangements for all Indigenous peoples under United States jurisdiction. Section 13 excluded certain provisions from application in specified places, and Congress handled Oklahoma and Alaska through separate legislation in 1936. The reason lay in the distinct legal and historical circumstances of each. The statute had been written primarily with western reservations in mind, where a defined land base, a federal agency, and a tribal population residing together formed the basis of organization. Neither Oklahoma nor Alaska fit that pattern.

Oklahoma's situation reflected a particular history. Indian Territory had been the destination of removals in the nineteenth century, discussed in Chapters 65 and 66, and its lands were later allotted under special legislation. The governments of the Five Tribes (Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee, and Seminole) had been sharply curtailed by the federal legislation that preceded Oklahoma statehood in 1907, and the territory's reservations were largely dissolved as political jurisdictions. The result was a population of Indigenous people holding allotments scattered among non-Indian neighbors, with few of the compact reservations that characterized many western states. The 1934 Act listed numerous Oklahoma tribes among those for which certain sections, including the organizational provisions, did not apply, and Oklahoma's congressional delegation sought tailored legislation.

The Oklahoma Indian Welfare Act of June 26, 1936, responded. It authorized recognized tribes and bands in Oklahoma to organize for their common welfare, to adopt constitutions and to receive charters of incorporation, and it provided for land acquisition in trust and for credit. The act's organizational provisions did not depend on a reservation as the territorial base, which reflected the absence of reservations in Oklahoma. Several tribes organized under the statute in the following years. The Five Tribes, whose governments operated under the special limitations of earlier legislation, followed a different path, and the restoration of their elected principal chiefs and constitutional governments came much later, a development covered in Chapter 114. The Oklahoma example shows that the Act's land and governance model was not simply portable and that nations with distinct histories required distinct legal instruments.

Alaska presented another set of problems. Alaska Native peoples, including the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Iñupiat, Yupik, Unangax̂, Athabascan, and others, lived in hundreds of villages across a vast territory, with relatively few treaties and, in most places, no reservations in the continental sense. Their legal status under federal law was less clearly defined than that of reservation Indians in the contiguous states, and federal administration, including education and health, had developed through different channels. Alaska Native organizations, such as the Alaska Native Brotherhood, had been active in political advocacy, and Chapter 99 treats Alaska Native political history more fully.

The Act of May 1, 1936, extended additional provisions of the Indian Reorganization Act to Alaska. It allowed groups of Alaska Natives to organize under constitutions and corporate charters on bases that did not depend on tribal or reservation membership in the continental sense. Residents of a village or other defined community, or persons sharing a common bond of occupation or association, could organize. The statute also authorized the Secretary of the Interior to designate certain lands as reservations for Alaska Native use, and several such reserves were later established. The community-based criteria of residence and association were important because they allowed Alaska Native villages to organize without a prior federal designation as a tribe.

Neither law duplicated the 1934 statute. Oklahoma's law adapted organizational and land provisions to a state without reservations, and Alaska's law substituted community-based criteria for the reservation and tribal membership concepts of the original. Both illustrate a recurring theme: federal policy had to be adapted to Indigenous peoples' actual circumstances, and the legal categories developed in one region did not always fit another. Together with the cases of the Flathead, Diné, and Hopi, they show that the Indian New Deal was not a single uniform program but a family of related arrangements.

Section 97.18

The Indian New Deal in Practice: Education, Employment, Culture, and Administrative Change

The Indian New Deal encompassed more than the Indian Reorganization Act, although the Act remains this chapter's central subject. Alongside the statute, the administration used emergency programs, executive action, and other legislation. The Johnson–O'Malley Act of April 16, 1934, for example, authorized the Interior Department to contract with states and other entities for the provision of education, medical care, and other services to Indigenous people. Emergency work programs, which put Indigenous labor to use on reservation projects, operated under authorities distinct from the 1934 Act. Understanding the full program requires distinguishing statutory authorizations from the appropriations and administrative action that put them into effect.

Within the Act itself, two provisions addressed education and employment. Section 11 authorized appropriations for loans to Indians to pay tuition and other expenses at vocational and trade schools, high schools, and colleges, and Section 12 directed that Indians be given preference in appointment to positions in the Indian Service. These sections authorized programs, and their results depended on later appropriations and implementation. The preference provision increased the number of Indigenous employees in the Indian Service over time, a change that affected the composition of the bureaucracy through which policy was carried out, though the pace and extent varied.

The Indian Emergency Conservation Work program, which began in 1933 and continued in various forms until the early 1940s, provided wages and training to Indigenous workers. Projects included building fences, dams, and roads, controlling erosion, and fighting fires. For many families the wages were a significant source of cash income during the Depression, and the program also taught skills. Its administration remained in the hands of Indian Service and conservation officials, and, as the Diné case shows, conservation goals could conflict with local priorities.

Educational administration changed during the decade. The Indian Service reduced emphasis on distant boarding schools, expanded day schools, and promoted curricula that took account of Indigenous languages and cultures, an approach associated with Willard Beatty, who became director of Indian education in 1936. These changes responded to the criticisms of the Meriam Report and of Indigenous parents. They did not end boarding schools, and chapters in Volume VIII, particularly Chapters 87 and 88, document the continuing history of institutional schooling and its consequences. The reforms were real but partial.

Federal support for Indigenous arts and crafts took institutional form in the Indian Arts and Crafts Board, established under legislation of 1935 within the Interior Department. The Board's purposes included promoting the economic welfare of Indigenous people through the development of arts and crafts, encouraging quality, and creating markets for authentic Indigenous-made goods. It had limited resources and an advisory character, and its effects were uneven; later legislation in the late twentieth century strengthened protections against misrepresentation of goods as Indigenous-made. Nevertheless, the Board indicated a shift in federal attitudes, from treating Indigenous cultural production as a relic to treating it as an economic and cultural asset.

The practical constraints of reform were considerable. Funding was inadequate compared with the needs identified, and appropriations fell short of authorizations. Administrative capacity was uneven, and the Indian Service had to carry out new tasks with a staff and structure inherited from earlier policy. Officials disagreed among themselves about priorities, and congressional critics, including members who distrusted Collier, pressed for reductions. The Depression's continuing effects limited the resources available for development. By 1940, these limits were evident, and the Indian New Deal's accomplishments were therefore mixed.

Section 97.19

Historical Debate: Indigenous Self-Government or Federally Directed Reorganization?

Assessments of the Indian Reorganization Act have never turned on a single test of success. The statute intervened in several historically distinct systems at once: reservation land tenure, tribal political organization, access to capital, and relations with federal administrators. A land-protection measure could be important even where a tribal constitution was never adopted; a constitution might strengthen bargaining capacity while leaving other powers under Secretarial supervision. To ask whether the IRA “restored self-government” without identifying the dimension being measured is therefore to risk an answer that is too broad for the evidence.

One interpretation emphasizes the break with allotment. The explicit prohibition on new reservation allotments and continuation of trust restrictions halted a policy that had helped transfer extensive acreages away from Indigenous communities. Sections 3 and 5 supplied tools for land restoration and acquisition, although their use depended on executive decisions, appropriations, and competing claims. In this reading, the law mattered because its central legislative purpose no longer required the destruction of the collective land base. Its impact can be measured in preserved trust interests and in the institutional capacity of nations that put its organizational provisions to work.

A second interpretation is attentive to the survival of federal supervision. Congress did not transfer unrestricted title to all remaining land or abolish the authority of the Interior Department. The Secretary retained important approval powers over constitutions and charters, while Indian Service administrators continued to shape budgets, personnel, and resource policy. The existence of a written constitution might also shift influence from clan, village, or general-council institutions to a federally recognized elected council. The key issue is not whether the new bodies were “authentic” by a single external standard, but who controlled their construction, whose interests they represented, and what the law allowed them to decide independently.

A third approach begins with the choices of Indigenous nations rather than federal declarations. The Diné majority rejected applicability in 1935; the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes accepted reorganization and later ratified separate governing and corporate instruments; the Hopi ratified a constitution while significant numbers of eligible voters abstained and some village leaders remained opposed. These decisions should not be placed on a simple scale from resistance to progress. The comparison is meaningful because communities faced different local histories of land tenure, livestock management, federal interference, and political authority. The same statutory option could be a practical resource in one setting and a threat to established institutions in another.

The documents used in this chapter support different claims with different degrees of certainty. The original statute proves what Congress authorized in 1934, not the quality of local implementation. Election returns record ballots cast, not the preferences of nonvoters or the reasons behind each choice. Constitutions establish formal institutional arrangements; they cannot show that officeholders enjoyed general confidence or that federal approval power was rarely exercised. The debate must therefore combine law, fiscal and land records, community testimony, and the subsequent operation of tribal institutions. It is precisely this combination of legal change and continued political negotiation that makes the IRA a turning point without making it a final settlement.

Historical Debate

Did the Indian Reorganization Act Restore Indigenous Political Authority, or Replace Paternalism with Another Form of Federal Supervision?

The question: Historians, legal scholars, and Indigenous commentators have long disagreed about the meaning of the Act. The disagreement concerns not what the statute said, which is documented, but how its provisions worked in practice and how they should be weighed against the longer struggle for Indigenous sovereignty.

Interpretation A — A significant reform: Supporters emphasize that the Act ended new reservation allotments, extended trust protection, authorized land restoration and acquisition, and gave tribes a legal route to organize governments and corporations. Section 16's powers, including the right to prevent disposal of tribal land without tribal consent, were practical tools, and the Act ended the official policy of dissolving tribal institutions. On this view the Act was a fundamental departure from assimilationist policy and created foundations for later tribal government.
Interpretation B — A new form of supervision: Critics emphasize that constitutions, charters, and many decisions required the Secretary's approval, that the Interior Department circulated model constitutions, that elected councils might displace traditional authorities, and that federal control over resources, including livestock policy in the Diné case, remained strong. On this view, the Act replaced compulsory individualization with a standardized, federally supervised model, and the self-government it offered was limited by the department's authority.
Interpretation C — Indigenous political agency: A third approach emphasizes that communities decided for themselves whether to accept the Act, shaped constitutions to local needs, rejected or amended federal arrangements, and used the new institutions for their own purposes. The Hopi constitution's provision for village government, the Diné rejection, and the Salish and Kootenai use of constitution and charter illustrate this range. On this view the question is less what the Act gave than what Indigenous nations made of it.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same sources: the statute, the hearings and congress transcripts, election returns, constitutions and charters, administrative records, and later accounts by participants and historians. They differ in which evidence they emphasize. Interpretation A stresses the land provisions and the legal powers in Sections 1, 2, 3, 5, and 16. Interpretation B stresses the approval requirements, the model documents, and cases of conflict. Interpretation C stresses referendum outcomes and local variation.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ on interpretation and emphasis more than on the basic facts, particularly regarding how to weigh the Act's protective provisions against its supervisory structure, how far to treat the elections as consent, and whether the Act should be judged by its own stated goals or by the standard of full Indigenous sovereignty.

Evaluating the interpretations with specific evidence: Section 1 and Section 2 support Interpretation A, since they closed a mechanism of land loss. The requirement in Section 16 that constitutions be approved by the Secretary supports Interpretation B, while the Section 18 right to reject supports Interpretation C. The Diné and Hopi cases illustrate how all three can be true at once: the Diné used the right to reject, the Hopi constitution adapted the model, and the Secretary's role persisted in both contexts. The interpretations are better understood as emphasizing different aspects of a single complex law than as exclusive alternatives.

Why it matters: How historians understand the Act affects how they understand later developments, including termination, self-determination, and contemporary debates over tribal constitutions, discussed in Chapters 104, 114, and 118.

Student task: Select three provisions of the statute and one community case. For each, decide which interpretation the evidence supports most strongly and explain why. Then identify one additional kind of evidence that would help to resolve the disagreement.

Section 97.20

The Legacy of the Indian Reorganization Act: Gains, Limitations, and the Future of Tribal Sovereignty

By the late 1930s and early 1940s the principal results of the Act were visible. The most significant changes were the end of new reservation allotments, the preservation of existing trust land, the availability of land restoration and acquisition, the establishment of organized tribal constitutional governments in many communities, and the expansion of possibilities for collective economic action. Each represented a departure from the assumptions of the allotment era. The legal framework of tribal governance, which would later be extended and reinterpreted, had been laid.

These gains should be set beside continuing limitations. Capital remained scarce, and the credit and land programs fell short of what many communities needed. Poverty persisted on many reservations, and the Depression's effects lingered. Federal supervision continued through the approval requirements and the powers of the Interior Department. Disagreements over constitutional forms persisted within communities, between elected councils and traditional leaders, and between reorganized governments and those who had rejected the Act. Fractionation and checkerboard ownership were not resolved. And the Act's reliance on federal approval meant that decisions that mattered to tribes remained, in many cases, subject to a federal veto.

It is also important to distinguish the Act's institutional importance from the broader struggle for Indigenous sovereignty. That struggle began long before 1934 and continued after it. Indigenous nations' claims to inherent authority rested on their own histories and on treaties, not on the 1934 statute. The Act recognized and organized certain forms of government within United States law, and in doing so it provided a legal vocabulary and institutions that later generations would use, challenge, and rework. But Indigenous peoples' efforts to define and defend their political status, through litigation, organizing, and diplomacy, would continue in the following decades, as later chapters on intertribal organization, self-determination, and sovereignty doctrine show.

An assessment of the first decade must also distinguish institutional availability from institutional effectiveness. Congress permitted a credit program, but access to a loan was contingent on organization, federal administration, and financial resources; the mere creation of a credit fund did not demonstrate a functioning enterprise in every community. Section 5 allowed the federal government to acquire land in trust, but actual restoration depended on available appropriations, willing sellers, and departmental choices. Likewise, the number of constitutions approved measures the spread of one legal instrument, not the strength of local representation or the scope of independent authority exercised under it.

The regional extensions underline the Act’s uneven geography. Oklahoma required legislation adapted to tribes whose land tenure and political arrangements differed from those of western reservations. Alaska Native communities were given organizational pathways grounded in neighborhood, residence, occupation, and association, language that reflected the inadequacy of the original reservation-centered framework. Those innovations widened the federal organizational menu; they did not eliminate the need for Indigenous communities to define their collective membership, choose their representatives, and negotiate with administrators. Any general account of the IRA should therefore be tested against cases that Congress treated differently.

Nor did an election give a historian license to treat a nation as politically homogeneous. The narrow Diné rejection, the separate votes on application and constitution at Hopi, and the different decisions made in Plains communities reveal internal debate as well as jurisdictional differences. A legislative account that records only “acceptance” or “rejection” risks erasing the process through which families, village leaders, pastoralists, council members, and other participants reached their views. The most durable legacy of reorganization lies in a contested institutional framework that Native peoples could employ, criticize, amend, or decline; its effectiveness must be traced in each nation’s subsequent history.

The coming of the Second World War and later changes in federal policy altered the environment in which the reorganized governments operated. Wartime brought military service, migration to defense jobs, and budget pressures that reduced funding for Indian Service programs, as Chapter 101 explains. Congressional critics of Collier gained influence, and after the war the policy of termination, treated in Chapter 104, sought to end federal recognition of some tribes, including some, such as the Klamath, that had rejected the Act. Later reversals in the era of self-determination, treated in Chapter 114, built in part on the institutions created in the 1930s. This chapter does not narrate those later developments; it notes only that the framework of 1934 was neither the end of the story nor a stable settlement.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Indian Reorganization Act of June 18, 1934, as a turning point in United States federal Indian policy. The Act ended the compulsory allotment of reservation land that the General Allotment Act of 1887 had established, and it replaced the assumption that tribal governments should dissolve with a statutory framework for protecting trust land, restoring and acquiring land, extending credit, and organizing tribal governments and corporations. Its central argument is that the Act changed the terms of federal policy without fully restoring Indigenous self-government.

The policy shift had deep roots. Allotment had reduced the Indigenous land base from roughly 138 million acres in 1887 to roughly 48 million by the early 1930s, according to commonly cited estimates, and had produced fractionated heirship and checkerboard ownership. The Meriam Report of 1928 and the Depression intensified criticism of the system. Indigenous activism, including the Pueblo campaign against the Bursum bill and decades of petitioning by councils and delegations, predated the Roosevelt administration, and the Indian New Deal built on ideas already circulating among Indigenous people.

John Collier, appointed Commissioner of Indian Affairs in 1933 with the support of Secretary of the Interior Harold Ickes, brought a philosophy of protecting communal life and a long record of reform advocacy. His program, however, differed from the aims of Congress and from the diverse objectives of Indigenous communities. The Wheeler–Howard bills of February 1934 were narrowed in Congress, which removed the proposed court for Indian affairs and the education title while adding protections for existing rights and the right of reservations to reject the Act.

The regional congresses of spring 1934 gave Indigenous delegates a voice in the drafting debate, and their records, preserved in published collections, document concerns about land, taxation, the powers of the Secretary, and the effect on existing governments. Consultation did occur, but it was incomplete and conducted under federal auspices, and it did not give delegates control over the text. The enacted statute, Public Law 73-383, 48 Stat. 984, was a compromise.

The Act's land provisions were defensive and limited. Sections 1 and 2 stopped new reservation allotments and extended trust periods, while Sections 3 and 5 authorized restoration of unsold surplus lands and acquisition of new land in trust. These provisions did not recover land already alienated, void valid private ownership, or end fractionation, and their practical effect depended on appropriations, Secretarial discretion, and local politics. Credit under the $10 million revolving fund authorized in 1934 and conservation programs similarly offered possibilities limited by funding, experience, and federal supervision.

Sections 16 and 17 created distinct instruments: constitutions establishing governments, and corporate charters establishing business entities. Both required ratification and the Secretary's approval. Constitutions often followed models circulated by the Interior Department and sometimes imposed unfamiliar structures, though communities adapted them in various ways. The statute recognized and organized aspects of tribal government but did not create Indigenous nations or originate their sovereignty. Membership provisions often incorporated federal classifications that would later produce disputes.

Section 18's referendum was an unusual grant of choice, though conducted under federal procedures and modified by the amendments of June 15, 1935. Haas's compilation records 258 elections between 1934 and 1936, of which 181 accepted and 77 rejected the Act's application. Students must distinguish the vote on application from constitution ratification and charter approval. Whether the elections captured Indigenous consent adequately depends on voter eligibility, information, participation, and the context of federal pressure.

The case studies showed the range of responses. The Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes accepted the Act, ratified a constitution in October 1935 and a separate charter in April 1936. The Diné Nation narrowly rejected the Act in June 1935, by approximately 7,992 to 7,608, amid resentment of livestock reduction, though without ceasing to govern itself. The Hopi accepted application, then ratified a 1936 constitution that preserved village government, with uneven participation. Crow, Klamath, and Seneca voters rejected the Act, while many Plains and Southwestern communities accepted it.

The Oklahoma Indian Welfare Act of June 26, 1936, and the Alaska act of May 1, 1936, adapted the model to conditions that the original statute did not fit. Together with the Johnson–O'Malley Act, Indian Emergency Conservation Work, educational reform, and the Indian Arts and Crafts Board, they formed the wider Indian New Deal. Historians debate whether the Act was a significant restoration, a new form of supervision, or a framework within which Indigenous agency operated; the evidence supports elements of all three.

By 1940 the Act's legacy was visible and mixed: allotment ended, trust land protected, tribal governments organized in many communities, and economic tools made available, yet limited by scarce capital, persistent poverty, supervision, and internal disagreement. The statute's lasting significance lies in the institutions and legal vocabulary it established, which later generations of Indigenous nations used and contested. The extent of Indigenous self-government continued to depend on the authority nations could exercise within, and sometimes against, the new administrative framework.

Chapter Reflection

The Indian Reorganization Act matters because it shows how a state can reverse a policy without surrendering its authority. Congress ended compulsory allotment, yet it kept the power to approve, fund, and supervise. The chapter's lesson is that policy reversals must be read for what they changed and what they left intact.

It also matters because it reveals Indigenous political agency within a constrained setting. Communities argued over the bill, voted on its application, drafted or declined constitutions, and adapted institutions to their own histories. The Diné rejection, the Salish and Kootenai constitution and charter, and the Hopi constitution show that a single statute produced different results because nations were different, not because some were more advanced than others.

The Act shaped later developments. Its constitutions and charters became the institutional base for later tribal governments, and its membership provisions and approval requirements shaped later disputes. Termination in the 1950s, examined in Chapter 104, targeted nations that had been recognized in the 1930s, while the self-determination era, examined in Chapter 114, expanded the powers that tribal governments exercised. In Canada, a different legal framework governed First Nations in these years, as Chapter 98 explains.

Finally, the chapter contributes to this textbook's central argument that Indigenous nations are continuing political communities. The Act recognized and reorganized some aspects of their government but did not create them. Their sovereignty rested on their own histories, and the history of 1934 is best understood as one episode in a longer struggle to define how that sovereignty would be exercised within and against the institutions of the United States.
