Chapter Opening

In October 1869 a survey crew was stopped on a Métis farm beside the Red River. In May 1885 a Métis community on the South Saskatchewan River was overrun after four days of fighting. Between those two events lay sixteen years, one negotiated province, and a long series of promises that many Métis came to regard as unkept.

On October 11, 1869, a survey party working for the Dominion of Canada was laying out lines on hay land belonging to the Métis farmer André Nault, near the Red River in what is now southern Manitoba. Local people, led by a group of younger men, confronted the surveyors. Louis Riel, then a twenty-five-year-old returned from schooling in Montreal, is traditionally remembered as having stepped onto the surveyors' measuring chain to halt the work. The incident was small, and no one was hurt. But it began a sequence of events that led within a year to the creation of a Canadian province, and that shaped Canadian politics for generations.

On May 12, 1885, the final day of the Battle of Batoche, a force of roughly eight hundred Canadian militia and soldiers, supported by artillery and a Gatling gun, overran the rifle pits of a few hundred Métis and First Nations defenders. Batoche was a settled community of river-lot farms, a church, a ferry, stores, and extended families, built after earlier Métis families left Manitoba for the Saskatchewan valley. The defeat of the Saskatchewan resistance, followed by the hanging of Louis Riel on November 16, 1885, is often treated as the end of the story. This chapter argues that it was not the end, and that it cannot be understood without the sixteen years that came before it.

The chapter is organized around a central question: how did the Métis seek to defend their homeland, political institutions, and territorial rights against Canadian expansion, and why did the negotiated settlement that created Manitoba in 1870 fail to prevent another major resistance in 1885? The answer developed here has three parts. First, the Red River Resistance of 1869–1870 was an act of organized political self-government by a people with established institutions, and it produced a significant constitutional settlement. Second, between 1870 and 1884 the implementation of that settlement, together with the collapse of the buffalo economy, the imposition of standardized land surveys, and the absence of effective political representation, produced new grievances in Manitoba and in the Saskatchewan settlements. Third, the resistance of 1885 grew out of a petition movement that was ignored or answered too slowly, and it involved Métis leaders and also First Nations communities whose own treaty and survival crises overlapped with, but were not identical to, those of the Métis.

The chapter's terminology is itself part of the history. Canadian official and popular writing long used "rebellion" for both events, a word that implies disloyalty to a legitimate government. Métis writers, many historians, and public institutions now commonly use "resistance," a term that emphasizes the defense of an existing homeland and political order. Neither word settles the historical questions, and the Historical Debate in Section 83.20 returns to them. This textbook uses "resistance" in its chapter title and general narrative, and it uses "rebellion" where it quotes or describes the language of nineteenth-century officials and later historiography.

Historians reconstruct these events from an unusually rich but uneven record. The Métis Lists of Rights, the Manitoba Act, the petitions of 1884, the official report of Riel's trial, and the 1886 land-claims and other records concerning losses filed by Saskatchewan families are documents that survive in print or archives. Parks Canada and Métis institutions such as the Gabriel Dumont Institute have preserved and interpreted the Batoche landscape and community history. Oral history, family testimony, and the dictated recollections of participants such as Gabriel Dumont add Métis perspectives that Canadian military reports and newspaper accounts omit. Each type of source reflects its creator's position, and the chapter asks students repeatedly whose voice a document represents and whose is missing.

The chapter also has a place in the larger textbook. Chapter 52 traced the formation of Métis communities from fur-trade networks, Chapter 81 explained the destruction of the buffalo economy, and Chapter 82 examined the numbered treaties that Canada negotiated with First Nations. Chapter 83 draws on all three to explain the political environment of 1885 without retelling them. It then leads into Chapter 84, which compares the reserve and reservation systems used to confine and administer Indigenous peoples across the United States and Canada, and Chapter 85, which examines the expansion of Canadian colonial administration under the Indian Act.

  1. In what sense were the Red River Métis a politically organized people before 1869, and what institutions supported that claim?
  2. Why did a land transfer negotiated between Britain, the Hudson's Bay Company, and Canada provoke organized resistance among people who were not party to it?
  3. How did the provisional government of 1869–1870 balance claims of local authority against its declared willingness to enter Canada?

Historical Context

The vast drainage basin of Hudson Bay, known to Europeans as Rupert's Land after the royal charter granted to the Hudson's Bay Company in 1670, was never an empty territory awaiting administration. Cree, Anishinaabe, Assiniboine (Nakoda), Dakota, Blackfoot, and other nations held the lands, and from the late eighteenth century the fur trade produced new communities of mixed Indigenous and European descent, whose formation is the subject of Chapter 52. By the early nineteenth century the Red River valley had become the center of one such community. The Company's chartered authority existed alongside Indigenous territorial claims, and its actual power depended on cooperation with the people who supplied furs, provisions, labor, and transport.

The Red River Settlement, begun in 1812 under the sponsorship of the Earl of Selkirk, brought Scottish and other colonists into a region already inhabited by Métis and First Nations people. In 1816 the conflict between Selkirk's settlers and the rival North West Company culminated in the clash at Seven Oaks, in which Métis fighters under Cuthbert Grant defeated a party of settlers and Company officials. The episode remained a memory of Métis collective action for generations. In 1849 the trial of the Métis trader Guillaume Sayer for violating the Company's trade monopoly ended in a guilty verdict with no penalty, after a large gathering of armed Métis outside the courthouse. Historians generally regard that outcome as a practical end to the Company's ability to enforce its monopoly at Red River, and as evidence of the Métis capacity to act collectively.

The economy of the settlement combined river-lot farming, the semiannual buffalo hunt, the production of pemmican and hides, and freight transport by Red River cart along trails connecting Red River to Saint Paul in Minnesota and to the Saskatchewan country. These activities were organized collectively through hunt governance, parish life, and extended kin networks. Chapter 81 explained how the destruction of the bison herds undermined this economy across the northern Plains. In the 1870s and early 1880s that collapse was one of the principal reasons Métis families reorganized their lives around settled agriculture and wage labor, often at a distance from Red River.

Political circumstances changed sharply in the 1860s. The British North America Act created the Dominion of Canada in 1867, and the new government, dominated by Ontario and Quebec, wished to acquire the Northwest for agricultural settlement and for a transcontinental railway. Chapter 82 examined the numbered treaties by which Canada, beginning in 1871, sought agreements with First Nations of the Northwest to open lands to settlement. The Red River Resistance preceded Treaties 1 and 2 and shaped the political context in which they were made. The Canadian government's approach to the Métis differed from its approach to First Nations: rather than treaties, it relied on a constitutional statute, the Manitoba Act, and on land grants and later scrip. This difference in legal form, and its consequences, is a theme that runs through the chapter.

Students should avoid two simplifications. The first treats the Métis as a single people with a single political position; in 1869 French-speaking Catholic Métis, English-speaking Protestant people of mixed descent, Scottish settlers, and newly arrived Canadians held diverging views, and in 1885 Métis communities differed over strategy. The second treats the resistances as inevitable. Neither the success of 1870 nor the defeat of 1885 was predetermined, and in each case the decisions of Métis, First Nations, Canadian, and church leaders shaped outcomes in ways the following sections examine.

Division I · Section 83.1

The Red River Métis: Homeland, Nationhood, and Political Organization

By 1869 the Red River Settlement had roughly twelve thousand inhabitants, a large majority of whom were of mixed Indigenous and European ancestry. Estimates vary, but historians commonly describe the population as divided broadly between French-speaking, largely Catholic Métis and English-speaking, largely Protestant people of mixed descent, with smaller numbers of Scottish settlers, Canadians, Americans, and Hudson's Bay Company personnel. This division matters because the word "Métis" had more than one use. In the narrow sense used by Métis political leaders and by this textbook, it refers to a people with its own collective identity, history, kinship networks, and political traditions. English-speaking people of mixed ancestry were often called "Country-born" or "English half-breeds," and many identified more closely with their Protestant parishes and Hudson's Bay Company ties than with the Métis national movement. Some Country-born residents supported the Red River resistance, some opposed it, and many sought a negotiated course between the two. Chapter 52 treated the origins of Métis communities; this section concentrates on the institutions that made organized political action possible.

The first of these was settlement structure. Métis families lived on narrow river lots, usually fronting the Red or Assiniboine River and extending back across the prairie, with timber and hay privileges beyond. The pattern, inherited from French-Canadian practice in the St. Lawrence valley, placed households close together along the river, near the parish church, and within easy reach of relatives. Parishes such as Saint-Boniface, Saint-Norbert, Saint-Vital, and Saint-François-Xavier (White Horse Plains) were centers of religious life, education, and community decision-making. Priests, notably Noël-Joseph Ritchot, had significant influence, though the clergy were not always aligned with the most active Métis political leaders.

The second institution was the buffalo hunt. Each summer and fall, Métis hunting parties formed large brigades of carts and riders, often involving hundreds of families, to travel onto the Plains. These expeditions were governed by rules agreed in advance: captains were elected, councils of respected hunters resolved disputes, hunters could not charge the herd before the captain's signal, and violations were punished by community sanction. Historians regard the hunt not merely as an economic activity but as an exercise in collective government, because it required elected leaders, enforceable rules, and a shared understanding of authority. These practices later informed the organization of the Saint-Laurent council and of the provisional governments of 1869 and 1885.

A third element was the experience of conflict and negotiation with outside authority. Métis participation at Seven Oaks in 1816, the free-trade agitation that culminated in the Sayer trial of 1849, and the intermittent armed conflicts with Dakota and other Plains nations over the hunting grounds all contributed to a political memory of collective action. The Hudson's Bay Company, whose Council of Assiniboia nominally governed the settlement, had accommodated Métis interests partly because it lacked the means to coerce them. Métis leaders were therefore accustomed to dealing with external governing authorities as a people with leverage, rather than as petitioners without it.

Language and economy completed the picture. Many Métis spoke French, Cree, Anishinaabemowin, or English, and many spoke Michif, a language combining French noun phrases with Cree verb structures that is distinctive to Métis communities. The economy combined river-lot farming, hunting, freighting by cart to Saint Paul, seasonal wage work, trade, and household production. The settlement thus had a mixed economy and a mixed political culture, with a strong sense of local custom and landholding. When Canada began to treat the region as territory to be surveyed and settled without consultation, the Métis response drew on institutions already in place.

Historical Vocabulary
Métis Nation, Michif, and River-Lot Settlement

Métis Nation refers to the Indigenous people who emerged from fur-trade families and communities in the northwest, with a shared history, collective identity, and political institutions centered on the Red River homeland and extended across the Plains. The term is used by Métis governments and has legal significance in Canada today. It does not mean everyone of mixed ancestry; it describes a distinct people with a continuing community. In nineteenth-century sources, writers also used "half-breed," "Métis," "Bois-Brûlé," and "Country-born" with varying meanings.

Michif names the Métis language that combines French nouns and noun phrases with Cree verb forms and grammar, and also refers more broadly to Métis cultural identity. It is one of the best documented signs of Métis distinctiveness, although many Métis communicated daily in French, Cree, English, or other languages.

River-lot settlement describes the pattern of long, narrow farms fronting a river, usually organized in parishes. At Red River and later on the Saskatchewan, it was both an agricultural system and a social one, giving each family river access while keeping households close. The pattern was distinct from the rectangular township grid used by the Dominion survey after 1871.

Potential misunderstanding: Treating "Métis" as a general term for "mixed race" obscures the political claims made in 1869 and 1885, which were claims made by a people about land, self-government, and recognition, not about ancestry alone.

Division I · Section 83.2

Confederation, the Hudson's Bay Company Transfer, and the Crisis of 1869

Canadian Confederation on July 1, 1867, united Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick under a federal government. Its architects, including John A. Macdonald and George-Étienne Cartier, treated western expansion as a central goal. Ontario farmers and politicians were interested in the agricultural lands of the Northwest, and Canadian leaders feared that the United States, which had purchased Alaska in 1867, might extend its own influence into the region. The Dominion also envisioned a transcontinental railway linking the Pacific to the east, which required control of the land between.

The legal route to expansion ran through the Hudson's Bay Company. The Company claimed proprietary rights over Rupert's Land under its 1670 charter and administered the North-Western Territory beyond. In 1868 the British Parliament passed the Rupert's Land Act, enabling the Company to surrender its claims to the Crown. Negotiations among Britain, the Company, and Canada continued into 1869 and produced an agreement under which Canada would pay £300,000 to the Company and the Company would retain its posts and a portion of the land in the settled districts. The deed of surrender was executed in November 1869, and the transfer to Canada was scheduled for December 1. No Métis or First Nations leaders took part in these negotiations. The agreement treated the transfer as an exchange between governments and a company, and the legal claims of the people living on the land received little formal consideration.

The Canadian government began acting before the transfer was complete. In 1869 it dispatched survey parties under Colonel John Stoughton Dennis to lay out township lines, and it appointed William McDougall, a leading Ontario expansionist, as lieutenant-governor designate of the North-West Territories. The surveyors worked in the Red River district, and the township system they used ran in straight lines of rectangular blocks irrespective of existing river-lot boundaries. To Métis farmers, whose title in many cases rested on occupation and custom rather than formal documents, the survey seemed to threaten their land. Newspapers such as the Nor'Wester, edited by John Christian Schultz, and letters from Canadian newcomers sometimes used contemptuous language toward the Métis, which fed alarm about the character of the coming administration.

The fear was not only of lost land. People worried about language rights, since French was widely spoken; about church schools and the position of the Catholic Church; and about political participation, since the Canadian government proposed to govern the Northwest through an appointed council rather than an elected assembly. Archbishop Alexandre-Antonin Taché was absent in Rome during these months, and the Métis lacked their most influential ecclesiastical advocate. Prominent figures among the Red River English-speaking population were also uneasy. They did not necessarily share Métis tactics, but many agreed that the people of Red River should be consulted about their future government. The Canadian Museum of History identifies the planned transfer without Métis participation as a central cause of the resistance, and most historians agree.

Cause and Effect

How Confederation and Territorial Expansion Produced the Red River Crisis

Long-term causes

Métis political experience since 1816 and 1849; an economy and land system based on river lots and custom; Hudson's Bay Company governance that depended on accommodation; Canadian ambitions for agricultural settlement and a railway.

Short-term causes

Confederation in 1867; the Rupert's Land Act of 1868; negotiations over the transfer without local participation; the appointment of an outside lieutenant-governor and an appointed council; the arrival of survey parties in 1869.

Immediate trigger

The survey party's work on Métis land in October 1869, and the news that McDougall was traveling to take up his post. Local leaders chose to block both.

Immediate effects

The National Committee, the barricade on the road from the border, the occupation of Upper Fort Garry, and the demand for negotiations over entry into Canada.

Long-term consequences: The creation of Manitoba in 1870, the beginnings of Métis constitutional claims, and a precedent for later land and rights disputes.

Historical complexity: The crisis cannot be reduced to survey lines, to Riel's leadership, or to religious and linguistic tension. It arose from the combination of a legal transfer that ignored local people, practical threats to landholding, and a community with the institutions and experience to resist.

Division I · Section 83.3

Louis Riel, the National Committee, and the Occupation of Upper Fort Garry

Louis Riel was born in 1844 in the Red River Settlement to a prominent Métis family. His father, Louis Riel senior, had been involved in the free-trade agitation preceding the Sayer trial. Riel was educated in Montreal for the priesthood but left the program, and returned to Red River in 1868, bringing legal and political training and fluency in French and English. He was one of several young Métis and educated men active in the 1869 crisis, and he should be understood as a leader within a larger movement rather than its sole creator. Older Métis hunt leaders, local priests, and the Red River parishes contributed to the mobilization.

After the survey confrontation of October 11, a group of Métis formed the National Committee of the Métis at Saint-Norbert on October 16, 1869, with John Bruce as president and Riel as secretary. The committee announced that it would prevent the entry of McDougall and his party unless Canada negotiated with the people of Red River. On October 21, Métis horsemen set up a barricade on the road at the Rivière Salle (also written Sale), south of Fort Garry, to block the party traveling north from the border. When McDougall arrived at the border area at the beginning of November, he was refused entry and ordered to return, and he withdrew to Pembina on the American side.

On November 2, 1869, Métis forces under Riel's direction occupied Upper Fort Garry, the Hudson's Bay Company's administrative center. The occupation was largely peaceful. The Company's governor, William Mactavish, who was seriously ill, did not resist with force. The fort mattered in several ways. It held stores of provisions, arms, and funds, so its possession allowed the Métis to feed and equip their forces. It was the seat of the Council of Assiniboia, the existing governing authority, which meant that control of the fort created a physical basis for assuming administrative authority. And symbolically, the fort represented the settlement's government, so its occupation signaled that authority now rested with the local people.

Reactions in the settlement varied. Most French-speaking Métis supported the National Committee, though some priests urged caution. English-speaking residents, many of whom were also uneasy about Canadian plans, were divided between those who supported negotiation and those who rejected Métis control. On November 6 Riel invited the English-speaking parishes to send delegates to a joint council, an important step because it extended the movement beyond the French-speaking community. Meanwhile a small group of Canadians, led by Schultz and Dennis, favored opposition by force. They are examined in Section 83.5. The result was a political movement that was strong enough to control the fort and block the lieutenant-governor, but that depended on maintaining cooperation across linguistic and religious lines.

The events of late 1869 were documented by Métis historical accounts, church records, the Dictionary of Canadian Biography, and the reports and letters of Canadian officials. A reasonable assessment is that political grievances became organized resistance through a combination of local institutions, committee governance, and control of strategic ground. The resistance also had a limited and defensible stated aim at this stage: it did not declare independence from Canada but insisted that Canada negotiate terms of entry with the people of the settlement.

Division I · Section 83.4

The Provisional Government, the Convention of Forty, and the Lists of Rights

McDougall's attempt to assert authority in early December deepened the crisis. Although the transfer had been postponed because of the unrest, he issued a proclamation as though it had taken effect and commissioned Dennis to raise an armed force among loyal residents. When news of the proclamation became known, Riel and the Métis reacted by claiming that McDougall's commission had no legal basis. A small armed party of Canadians gathered at Schultz's house and warehouse, and after a standoff they surrendered on December 7. On December 8 the Métis issued a Declaration of the People of Rupert's Land and the North-West, asserting that, with the transfer of authority uncertain, the people had the right to form a provisional government, and a council was established with Riel as its leading figure. He became president of the provisional government late that month.

The new government was not only a Métis body. Riel had invited English-speaking parish delegates to participate in earlier councils, and in January 1870 the process was widened. Donald A. Smith, a senior Hudson's Bay Company official acting as a special commissioner for the Canadian government, arrived in late December. He addressed large open-air meetings in January, and the settlers agreed to a convention to consider his message and decide on terms. The resulting Convention of Forty, with twenty French-speaking and twenty English-speaking delegates elected from the parishes, met from late January into early February 1870. It debated the future status of the settlement, the legitimacy of the provisional government, and the terms to demand from Canada.

The convention produced a revised list of demands and chose delegates to carry them to Ottawa. Disagreement over key points was real. English-speaking delegates were generally more willing to accept territorial status within Canada, while Riel and many French-speaking delegates pressed for provincial status. Disagreement also arose over whether to demand control over public lands and over how forcefully to bargain. Consistent with the movement's declared aims, the provisional government's claim to legitimate local authority was paired with a willingness to negotiate entry. The Métis did not seek to detach the Northwest from Canada; they sought to enter on negotiated terms.

It would be a mistake to treat the "List of Rights" as a single unchanging text. Historians and the Métis Museum of the Gabriel Dumont Institute identify several successive versions, commonly counted as four, drafted between late 1869 and early 1870 by the National Committee and then by the conventions. Early lists emphasized local control, protection of existing property and customs, and representation. Later lists, shaped by the convention discussions and by the consultation of English-speaking delegates, added demands for provincial status and for guarantees of language and religion. In general, versions differed in the status they claimed for the settlement, the control over lands they requested, and the number and specificity of demands. Students should therefore read the versions as stages in a negotiation among different groups, rather than as a fixed statement of Métis positions.

The procedural history helps explain why later references to a single List of Rights obscure the record. A list debated by representatives of both language communities in December 1869 was not identical in authorship or political mandate to the proposals produced by the Convention of Forty in early 1870. The December meeting demonstrated agreement on some protections but disagreement over the authority of delegates to approve a provisional government. After Donald Smith’s public meetings in January, the larger convention could consider more concrete proposals concerning the future province, legislative institutions, and public land. The lists were therefore instruments of coalition-building as well as bargaining papers directed toward Ottawa. Their evolving language reveals not only what the delegates wanted Canada to guarantee but how local political consensus had to be assembled.

The fourth iteration carried by Ritchot, Black, and Scott to Ottawa in March was an outcome of this sequence rather than a document written by Riel alone. It combined a demand for provincial government with proposals concerning local control of lands, minority protections, representation, and amnesty. Some demands subsequently appeared in altered form in the Manitoba Act; others, particularly comprehensive control of public lands and a written amnesty, did not. A responsible comparison should label each version by its meeting or compiling body and date, acknowledge discrepancies among surviving copies, and avoid silently transferring a clause from the final list into an earlier one. Provincial historical chronologies, the Gabriel Dumont Institute’s four-iteration study, and the contemporaneous Convention of Forty debates provide complementary documentary starting points.

Division II · Section 83.5

Political Division, Thomas Scott, and the Crisis of the Provisional Government

The provisional government never commanded unanimous support. A group of recent arrivals from Ontario, called the Canadian Party, rejected its authority and favored annexation on Canada's terms. Its best-known figure was John Christian Schultz, a physician, merchant, and newspaper proprietor who had long been critical of the Hudson's Bay Company and of the Métis. After the confrontation at his premises in December 1869, Schultz was imprisoned, escaped in January, and rallied supporters. In February 1870 a party of Canadians, including Major Charles Boulton, set out from the Portage la Prairie district to free prisoners and challenge the government, and they were arrested on February 17. Boulton was sentenced to death by a council, and was spared after intervention by Donald Smith and others.

Thomas Scott was a young laborer from Ontario who had worked on the road-building project connecting the Lake of the Woods to Red River. He had been among those detained in December and escaped in January. Contemporary accounts depict him as defiant and abusive toward his guards, which later critics and defenders of the provisional government cited in different ways. After recapture in February he was tried before a council of the provisional government presided over by Ambroise Lépine. Scott was convicted of resisting the authority of the provisional government and was sentenced to death. He was shot on March 4, 1870, at Fort Garry. Accounts describe an unnerving execution, and his body was never recovered.

The government justified the execution as necessary to maintain order in a settlement that had no police force and faced armed opposition, and Riel is said to have argued that Canada needed to learn that the people of Red River must be respected. Others, including Smith and members of the English-speaking community, urged clemency. The proceedings were criticized then and afterward on legal grounds: the council's authority was disputed, the trial was brief, and Scott had no counsel in the modern sense. At the same time, supporters of the provisional government argued that it was a de facto authority with the right to protect itself during a period of unresolved sovereignty. Historians treat the execution as a serious act whose legality and wisdom are contestable, and which cannot be defended by simple appeal to either side's claims. It was not merely an act of individual malice, and it was not a mere justified exercise of authority.

The political effects were immediate and long-lasting. In Ontario, news of the execution, amplified by the press and the Orange Order, to which Scott belonged, produced outrage and demands for retribution. The Ontario government eventually offered a reward for the arrest of those involved. In Quebec, there was sympathy for the Red River Métis as French-speaking Catholics, and opposition to treating the matter as simple murder. In Ottawa, Macdonald's government tried to hold a middle course, committed to peaceful entry of Manitoba but unable to ignore Ontario's anger. In Red River itself, opponents of the provisional government were emboldened, and the Métis later faced reprisals. Later national memory simplified these views: English Canadian tradition often presented Scott as a martyr and Riel as a murderer, while French Canadian and Métis tradition often emphasized the negotiated settlement and Riel's role as founder of Manitoba. Both simplifications omit contested detail.

Division II · Section 83.6

The Ottawa Negotiations and the Manitoba Act of 1870

The convention chose three delegates to negotiate with Ottawa: Father Noël-Joseph Ritchot, a priest with extensive local influence; John Black, a lawyer representing the English-speaking community; and Alfred Henry Scott, an American-born resident who acted for the provisional government. They reached Ottawa in April 1870 carrying a list of demands. Soon after arrival, Ritchot and Scott were briefly arrested on a warrant sworn by a relative of Thomas Scott, though the charge was soon withdrawn. The incident showed how heated the political climate in Ontario had become. The delegates then negotiated with Prime Minister John A. Macdonald and George-Étienne Cartier, the government's senior Quebec minister.

The negotiations were substantial and, for the Canadian side, politically fraught. The delegates pressed for provincial status with local control of public lands, which the government resisted because it regarded control of western lands as essential to its plans for railways and settlement. The compromise was a province much smaller than the later one, sometimes called the "postage stamp" province, with a lieutenant-governor, an appointed upper house and a twenty-four-member elected assembly, and representation in the Canadian Parliament. Ottawa retained control of public lands, which meant that it, rather than the new province, would decide how lands were distributed. The delegates did not obtain a formal amnesty in the text of the Act, though Ritchot maintained for the rest of his life that he had received verbal assurances of one. The Manitoba Act was passed in May 1870 and took effect when Manitoba entered Confederation on July 15, 1870.

The act contained several provisions of lasting importance. Section 22 protected denominational schools in so far as they existed by law or practice, a provision that later became the subject of political and legal conflict in Manitoba. Section 23 permitted the use of English and French in the legislature and courts and required the records and journals of the legislature to be kept in both languages. Together these provisions reflected the demands of both language communities, and they meant that the new province possessed express bilingual institutions and legally protected denominational-school interests, even though the groups living within its boundaries were more varied than any two-community description suggests. Provincial status gave Red River an elected government of its own, a notable outcome for a settlement that, months earlier, had no direct representation.

Two land provisions are especially important and must be distinguished. Section 31 set aside one million four hundred thousand acres of ungranted land in the province for the benefit of the families of Métis residents, described in the Act as a measure connected with the extinguishment of Indian title. This was a collective land grant to be allocated to Métis children. Section 32 addressed existing landholdings, providing a process by which people who occupied land, including river lots held by custom, could have their claims confirmed and granted title. The first provision concerned a new grant; the second concerned recognition of existing possession. The two were administered by different mechanisms and led to different disputes. The Supreme Court of Canada, in Manitoba Métis Federation v. Canada (2013), treated them separately.

The delegates and Métis leaders considered the agreement a meaningful political achievement: it created a province, protected language and religion, and secured land provisions. But unresolved questions remained. The delegates had not obtained an amnesty or control of public lands, and the land provisions depended on administration by a federal government whose commitment would be tested. Parliament's 1871 amendment to the British North America Act confirmed the Manitoba Act's validity, giving it constitutional force. The Act also coincided with Canada's first numbered treaties with First Nations in 1871 (Chapter 82), reflecting a government approach in which Indigenous claims were handled in different legal forms for different peoples.

The distinction between an elected provincial legislature and control of Crown lands was consequential. A new province could enact local laws and elect representatives, but without the public-domain portfolio it did not possess the principal territorial asset on which a settler state planned to raise revenue, encourage immigration, and direct construction. Macdonald and Cartier treated a federally controlled western domain as strategically necessary; Red River delegates understood local land authority as a safeguard for existing communities. The compromise accordingly divided political recognition from substantial territorial control. This distinction should inform every subsequent discussion of why Métis land assurances depended on federal administrators even after provincial status had been conceded.

The school and language guarantees also did different legal work. Section 22 addressed rights or privileges concerning denominational schools existing by law or practice and furnished procedures for federal intervention if those rights were prejudiced. Section 23 dealt instead with the use of English and French in provincial legislative debate and the courts, together with requirements for bilingual official legislative records. Neither clause should be read as promising an unlimited language or education regime without later disputes over interpretation. Both, however, show why delegates who sought protection for their institutions considered provincial arrangements inseparable from the security of their communities. Later controversies over Manitoba schools and language obligations cannot be understood without distinguishing the text and functions of these sections.

Sections 31 and 32 created an equally sharp legal contrast. The 1.4-million-acre provision of Section 31 was expressed as a special allotment connected to the families of Métis inhabitants and required later decisions about eligibility, allocation, and grant procedures. Section 32 dealt principally with the confirmation of pre-existing titles and occupancy; it was not another 1.4-million-acre distribution. A family might therefore encounter two separate bureaucratic processes: securing recognition of a river lot occupied under local arrangements and seeking an allotment for an eligible child. They carried different proofs, timelines, and risks. The Supreme Court’s 2013 judgment later held that the constitutional character of the Section 31 promise engaged the honour of the Crown, but it did not convert every difficulty in property administration into the same legal violation.

Division II · Section 83.7

The Wolseley Expedition, Amnesty, and Louis Riel's Exile

While the Manitoba Act was being negotiated, the Canadian government organized a military expedition to Red River under Colonel Garnet Wolseley. About twelve hundred men, drawn from British regulars and Canadian militia, left central Canada in May 1870 and traveled by water and portage through the Lake Superior country. The government's stated objective was to maintain order and assert Canadian authority during the transfer, not to wage war against the Métis. But many Métis understood the expedition as a punitive force, particularly since a number of volunteers from Ontario were openly hostile to Riel and his associates. The journey took about three months, and the force reached Fort Garry on August 24, 1870.

Riel, Lépine, and other leaders left Fort Garry before the troops arrived, so the soldiers entered an unopposed fort. Lieutenant-Governor Adams Archibald arrived in early September and took up the work of organizing the new province. The government's stated policy was peaceful administration, and Archibald attempted to reconcile the communities. But the arrival of the expedition coincided with harassment of Métis residents. Several were assaulted, and in September Elzéar Goulet, a member of the provisional government's council, drowned after being chased by a group of soldiers and volunteers. The historical record distinguishes between government policy, which was formally conciliatory, military conduct, which was disciplined in some respects but permissive of intimidation in others, and violence by individuals, which was not effectively punished.

The question of amnesty complicated everything. The Governor General had issued a proclamation in December 1869 promising that those who ceased resistance would not face reprisals, and Ritchot returned from Ottawa believing that an amnesty had been assured. The government, however, regarded amnesty as an imperial matter, and it was not granted promptly. For years, Riel and Lépine faced the threat of prosecution. Lépine was tried in 1874 for the murder of Scott, convicted, and had his sentence commuted to a period of banishment. Riel, elected to the House of Commons for the district of Provencher in 1873 and again in 1874, was unable to take his seat; he signed the members' roll in Ottawa in early 1874 and then left, and was expelled. In 1875 he received an amnesty conditional on five years of banishment from Canada.

The consequences of exile were political and personal. The Métis lost their most prominent political leader at the moment their constitutional settlement most needed defending. Riel suffered a period of severe psychological and spiritual distress in the mid-1870s, and was hospitalized in Quebec under assumed names between 1876 and 1878. He later settled in Montana, worked as a teacher, married, and became a United States citizen in 1883. Other Métis leaders remained in Manitoba but faced a political landscape in which new settlers, mostly from Ontario, were increasingly dominant. The formal creation of Manitoba did not therefore produce an immediately secure political settlement: its institutions were in place, but the Métis experienced insecurity, hostility, and delayed implementation of the provisions that mattered to them most.

Division II · Section 83.8

The Manitoba Land Settlement, Administrative Failures, and Métis Displacement

The land provisions of the Manitoba Act were the center of Métis hopes, and their administration is among the most studied episodes in Métis history. Section 31's grant of 1.4 million acres was to be allocated to the children of Métis families, which in practice meant that each eligible child received a specified amount, commonly stated as 240 acres. This required the government to identify eligible children, survey and select land, and issue patents, the formal legal titles. The work took years. Land was allocated in stages through the 1870s, and many patents were not issued until the end of that decade or later. The delay mattered because Métis families faced economic pressure and a rapidly growing influx of settlers, and because land values and rules changed during the interval.

Several problems developed. Lands were drawn by lottery in ways that sometimes scattered family allotments, and some children received land at a distance from their homes. Changes in the law and in administrative practice, including measures in the 1870s affecting how minors' lands could be sold or leased, made it easier for speculators to acquire holdings. Many Métis families sold land or scrip, often at low prices, to buyers who profited when the land's value rose. Parliament also authorized separate scrip for heads of families and others, which was likewise subject to sale and speculation. Meanwhile, the confirmation of existing river lots under Section 32 was often slow, and some families struggled to prove occupation or to obtain patents in the face of competing claims by newcomers. Hostility toward Métis residents after 1870, including pressure from incoming settlers and disputes over claims, added to the insecurity.

Families experienced these developments differently. Some retained their river lots and farmed successfully; some sold land and moved to join relatives elsewhere; some continued to hunt and freight and left the province for the Saskatchewan valley, where they established the communities discussed in Section 83.9. Historians debate the relative importance of different causes. Some, notably D. N. Sprague, emphasize the role of government policy and administrative failure, arguing that the pattern of delay and legal change effectively dispersed Métis landholders and was shaped by a federal preference for rapid settlement. Others, including Gerhard Ens and, in some respects, Thomas Flanagan, give greater weight to market circumstances and to choices made by Métis families, who could receive cash and move on to new opportunities. Most recent historians accept that several factors operated together, and that these interpretations differ about emphasis rather than disagreeing about whether many families lost land.

Students should distinguish carefully among the land mechanisms. The Manitoba Act's land grants (Section 31) and the confirmation of existing holdings (Section 32) were constitutional provisions in a statute. Métis scrip was also used in Manitoba before 1885: federal legislation in 1874 authorized a separate benefit for heads of Métis families, implemented through scrip or land provisions; later scrip also covered certain children left out of the Section 31 distribution. A distinct territorial scrip system was authorized under later legislation and operated through the 1885 and subsequent North-West commissions. The two should not be conflated. In its 2013 decision in Manitoba Métis Federation v. Canada, the Supreme Court of Canada held that Canada had failed to implement Section 31 with the diligence required by the honour of the Crown. The Court did not find the claim based on Section 32 to be established, and did not find a fiduciary duty in the circumstances. The decision is an authoritative later assessment, but it is a judgment about legal obligations, not a complete account of what happened to families.

A separate Manitoba scrip chronology is essential. Federal legislation in 1874 made provision for Métis heads of families who had not been the direct beneficiaries of the children’s land-grant formula. The associated program could provide a land claim or money-denominated scrip, commonly described in the historical literature as 160 acres or 160 dollars for qualifying heads. Transferable certificates were much easier for merchants and land buyers to accumulate than secure, occupied river lots. Their circulation could therefore produce a legal transaction that satisfied a federal administrative account while leaving the household with little land in hand. This was not the 1885 territorial program, even though federal officials later employed similar paperwork and market mechanisms in the Saskatchewan and Alberta regions.

A further complication arose when children eligible under Section 31 had not received their intended shares by the time the reserve of land was exhausted. Federal officials issued scrip to some of these unprovided-for claimants. This remedial process should not be confused with the 1874 grant for adult heads of families, and neither should be confused with confirming an already occupied lot under Section 32. These distinctions are more than formal. A child waiting for an allotment, a parent offered transferable scrip, and a river-lot occupant attempting to establish a patent faced different administrative incentives. The legal form of a benefit affected its vulnerability to sale, encumbrance, fraud, and loss.

The question of whether land sales were voluntary therefore cannot be answered merely by counting signed transfers. Some Métis owners sold property to meet immediate economic needs or to move near kin; others negotiated under conditions of delayed patents, disputed boundaries, unfamiliar legal procedures, and pressure from newcomers. Purchasers could sometimes gain from information and capital that sellers lacked. Conversely, the existence of coercive conditions does not mean every seller lacked judgment or agency. Historical explanation must hold these possibilities together and assess specific records rather than using either individual-choice arguments or a general claim of dispossession to explain every family. The Supreme Court’s ruling concerns Canada’s diligence in implementing a constitutional promise; historians must additionally examine household decisions, social relations, and land-market outcomes.

Historical Vocabulary
Land Grant, Land Patent, and Métis Scrip

Land grant here refers to the allocation of lands to Métis children under Section 31 of the Manitoba Act, a collective allotment of 1.4 million acres. It was a one-time provision within the statute, administered by federal officials.

Land patent is the formal document by which the Crown transfers title to land to an individual. The delay in issuing patents after allotment was one of the main sources of insecurity.

Métis scrip was a certificate, issued under later federal programs, that entitled the holder to claim land or, in some cases, money. It was transferable, and as a result many certificates were sold to speculators. Scrip was an administratively distinct and transferable mechanism; its Manitoba head-of-family authorization in 1874, subsequent remedial issues, and later territorial commissions must be separated from the constitutional promise made by Section 31 of the Manitoba Act.

Potential misunderstanding: Treating all Métis land policy as "scrip" obscures the separate constitutional origin of the Manitoba grants and the different legal questions each raised.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Political Recognition in 1870 to Territorial Insecurity in the Following Decade

What Changed

  • The Métis moved from an effective local government to a minority within a province governed by a growing newcomer majority.
  • Land security shifted from custom and occupation to formal title, delay, and market sale.
  • Many families migrated west, relocating the center of Métis political life from Red River toward the Saskatchewan.

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship networks, parish life, and Michif and French language use persisted across relocation.
  • Métis leaders continued to use petition and negotiation as political tools.
  • The claim to recognition as a distinct people with collective rights remained at the center of Métis politics.

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual and uneven rather than abrupt. Families who sold land experienced the settlement differently from those who kept it, and migrants differed from those who stayed. The shift from recognition to insecurity explains why the Saskatchewan settlements, which received many Red River families, carried memories of 1870 into their own grievances in the 1880s.

Division III · Section 83.9

Métis Communities along the South Saskatchewan River

The years between 1870 and 1884 were not an empty interval. While Manitoba's land settlement unfolded, Métis families, including many from Red River, established or expanded communities along the South Saskatchewan River, in what was then part of the North-West Territories. Saint-Laurent (Saint-Laurent-de-Grandin), founded in the early 1870s, became a center of settlement and of Catholic mission life. Batoche, a few miles upriver, developed around a ferry, a store, and the farms of families such as the Letendres, Caron, Tourond, and others, and took its name from Xavier Letendre dit Batoche, a trader and ferry operator. Nearby communities, including Saint-Louis, Duck Lake, and Fish Creek (Tourond's Coulee), formed a settled district whose population, with that of the wider Saskatchewan valley, numbered in the low thousands. Estimates vary, and the figures depend on whether English-speaking Métis around Prince Albert and nearby First Nations communities are counted.

The river was the organizing feature of the economy. Families laid out river lots along the bank in the same pattern used at Red River, and they farmed grain and gardens, kept cattle and horses, and cut hay. The Carlton Trail connected the district to Fort Garry in the east and Fort Edmonton in the west, and ferry crossings at Batoche and elsewhere gave the community strategic importance and income. Freighting, trading, ranching, and wage work for the Hudson's Bay Company and others complemented farming. The communities had stores, a school, and a church, Saint-Antoine-de-Padoue at Batoche, served by Oblate missionaries. Families were linked by extended kinship ties, often reaching back to Red River and the buffalo hunt, so the settlements were networks of relatives rather than collections of unrelated households.

Gabriel Dumont was the best-known community leader. Born in 1837, he gained reputation as a hunter and as an elected captain of the buffalo hunt, and he spoke several languages including Cree and Michif. In December 1873, the residents of Saint-Laurent established a council, with Dumont as president, to govern the settlement. The arrangement drew on the hunt's tradition of elected leadership and enforceable rules, and it dealt with matters such as land claims, property disputes, and the regulation of hunting. The council functioned for a few years, until the growing presence of Canadian authority, including the North-West Mounted Police established in 1873, made the arrangement difficult to sustain. Historians regard it as clear evidence that Métis communities in the Saskatchewan sought to continue self-government in the absence of effective Canadian institutions.

The Parks Canada history of Batoche and the research of historians such as Diane Payment establish that the community had a substantial economy and an active political and social life before 1885. This point is essential. If Batoche were treated merely as the place where Riel and Dumont made their stand, its history would be reduced to the final four days of fighting. In fact, it was a community of farmers, freighters, merchants, and families, with a church, a school, local institutions, and a population that would remain after the battle. The resistance of 1885 was an act of a settled community defending its land, not the creation of a military camp around a leader.

Division III · Section 83.10

The Collapse of the Buffalo Economy and the Transformation of Métis Livelihoods

Chapter 81 explained the destruction of the bison herds on the northern Plains. The consequences for the Métis can be summarized briefly here. By the late 1870s the herds on the Canadian side had declined sharply, hunting parties had to travel farther and found fewer animals, and by the early 1880s organized hunts had largely ended. The effects spread beyond the hunt itself. Pemmican production, which had supplied the fur trade, disappeared. Freighting declined with the arrival of the railway on the southern Plains. The commercial relationships that tied Métis families to the Company and to Plains communities weakened. Households that had divided the year between farming and the hunt had to depend on other sources of income.

Many families responded by expanding agriculture. They grew barley, oats, wheat, and potatoes on their river lots, kept cattle and horses, and worked as freighters, laborers, and traders as opportunities allowed. Agriculture in the district was not easy: early frost and poor harvests in some years, limited access to capital and machinery, and uncertainty about land title made it a risky basis for a livelihood. The adaptation was therefore uneven. Some households became relatively stable farmers and ranchers, others combined farming with seasonal wage work, and others experienced severe hardship, particularly those that had depended mostly on the hunt. Settlements also differed: communities with better land and access to markets fared differently from those with less.

Women played central roles in this transition. Métis women managed household production, including gardens, the preparation of food, clothing, and hides, and the care of children and the sick. They contributed to trade through the exchange of goods and the sale of products, and they maintained the kin networks that supported families in difficult years. As hunting declined, household production and women's work took on increased importance for family survival. Métis women also held social authority within families and parishes, and, as later sections show, took part in the political events of 1885. Métis historical research identifies women such as Marguerite (Dumas) Caron and Josephte Tourond among those whose contributions to the community are documented.

Economic transition did not occur at the same pace for every family. A household that owned horses and carts could continue hauling goods while developing fields, whereas one that depended heavily on seasonal hunt income might face a more severe interruption when the herds vanished. Agricultural settlement required tools, animals, seed, fences, and a period before crops yielded returns; wage labor could generate cash but also remove workers during planting or harvest. River lots maintained access to water and proximity to relatives, yet their narrow form did not solve every problem of soils, weather, or markets. These choices must be measured against changing transport routes and the relative power of trading firms, government agents, and incoming settlers.

Women’s work also complicates any division of the economy into a male hunting past and a farming future. Women had processed buffalo products, organized household provisioning, and sustained exchange before the decline. On the Saskatchewan they continued to produce and store food, manage domestic economies, cultivate gardens, raise children, and maintain mutual aid across extended families. Their labor could cushion shortages but could not substitute for secure land, livestock, and reliable access to markets. Diaries, parish records, testimony, and Diane Payment’s community reconstruction illuminate these changes more directly than military correspondence. A chapter centered on politics must nevertheless explain the ordinary resource decisions that made the petition demands urgent.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Métis Household at Batoche during the Transition from Buffalo Hunting to Settlement Agriculture

Documented community history shows that households in the Batoche district typically occupied a river lot with a log house, a stable or barn, a garden, and fields of grain, near relatives on adjoining lots. Work was divided by season and by household. Men and older boys cleared land, plowed, cut hay, tended livestock, and took freighting and wage jobs when available. The loss of the hunt changed this routine. Meat and hides that had once come from the Plains had to be replaced by farming, livestock, and purchase. Households that kept strong kin networks could share labor, tools, and food, while those with fewer relatives or less land were more vulnerable. After the events of 1885, many families filed claims for property taken or destroyed during the campaign. The claims records, as Payment's research shows, list houses, outbuildings, furniture, tools, crops, and livestock, and thus provide detailed evidence of the material life of households that left few other written records.

Division III · Section 83.11

Land Surveys, Settlement Policy, and the Growth of Political Grievances

For Métis farmers on the Saskatchewan, as for those at Red River earlier, the key issue was land. Families had taken up river lots by occupation, and they expected the federal government to survey and recognize their holdings in the form in which they were held. The Dominion government, however, applied a standard system of rectangular townships to the Northwest. Under this system, land was divided into square townships six miles on a side, subdivided into numbered sections of 640 acres, with quarter-sections of 160 acres as the basic homestead unit. The system was efficient for settlement and sale, but it ran across river lots and could split a Métis farm among several sections or place a house outside the lines of any single quarter-section.

Métis settlers petitioned repeatedly for the survey of their holdings in river-lot form and for the issue of patents. Federal officials responded slowly and inconsistently. Uncertainty over legal title persisted in the district through the early 1880s. Land-office procedures were distant and slow, with the seat of territorial government moved to Regina in 1883, farther from the northern settlements, and the choice of a southern route for the Canadian Pacific Railway in 1881 left the Saskatchewan valley off the main line, reducing the district's prospects. New settlers arrived, including English-speaking farmers around Prince Albert, and competition for land and for official attention grew. Because federal land administration for the North-West Territories was centralized and responsive mainly to settlement policy, local concerns tended to be handled as individual cases rather than as questions of principle.

Grievances included more than land. Farmers wanted markets and credit, freighters and merchants lost business as railways replaced river and cart transport, and community leaders sought representation. The North-West Territories had limited elected representation and no seats in the federal Parliament. Métis, English-speaking settlers, and Indigenous communities all had complaints, but their circumstances differed. First Nations in the district, including those party to Treaty 6 (1876), had separate histories: their treaty rights, reserves, and the supply of rations were governed by the Indian Department, and the hunger they faced in the early 1880s had its own origins, which Section 83.15 examines. Mixing these claims would obscure the particular standing of each. For the Métis, the land question was linked to the memory of Manitoba, where recognition in 1870 had not secured their holdings.

The important point is that dissatisfaction developed through repeated administrative decisions over years, not through a single provocation. Each unanswered petition, delayed survey, and unresolved claim reinforced the view that the government would not respond to ordinary requests. That cumulative pattern, as much as any single event, explains the growth of the petition movement and, eventually, the decision to recall Riel.

A river-lot claim was not just a preference for one surveying pattern over another. The arrangement represented a working relationship among household land, river access, ferry crossings, grazing areas, gardens, and the placement of neighbors along the bank. When a federal survey imposed township sections without accounting for actual occupation, a family could fear that an existing house or field would fall on land legally assigned to someone else. Even where officials later recognized local lots, an uncertain interval before formal confirmation could discourage improvements and expose families to claims by newcomers. The practical question was therefore not whether surveying was modern or traditional; it was whose evidence of prior ownership the administration would recognize and how quickly it would act.

Métis grievances also involved representation and the location of public institutions. A distant land office might require travel, fees, witnesses, and familiarity with unfamiliar procedures. New homestead regulations could benefit settlers entering under a predictable system while earlier local occupants were still waiting for documentary proof of their rights. Residents complained that an appointed territorial administration lacked accountability to the people most affected. Some English-speaking settlers shared these procedural objections without sharing a Métis national claim. Keeping their grievances distinct makes the breadth—and fragility—of the subsequent petition coalition easier to explain.

Division III · Section 83.12

Petitions, Political Organization, and the Recall of Louis Riel, 1884

Between the late 1870s and 1884, residents of the Saskatchewan valley sent numerous petitions and delegations to Ottawa and to territorial officials. Métis settlers, English-speaking Métis and settlers around Prince Albert, and others asked for recognition of their land holdings, the survey of river lots, patents, and representation in government. Public meetings were held at Batoche, Saint-Laurent, and nearby communities. Until then, these efforts remained constitutional in form and relied on the means that the Dominion government itself recognized: petition, delegation, and press.

By 1884 the lack of response had led community leaders to seek a more prominent advocate. In June, a delegation including Gabriel Dumont, Michel Dumas, Moïse Ouellette, and the English-speaking Métis James Isbister traveled to Montana, where Louis Riel was teaching at the mission of Saint Peter's. They asked him to return and help present their claims. Riel agreed, and he arrived at Batoche in July 1884, accompanied by his family. The decision to invite him reflected his record as the leader of the Red River provisional government, his experience in constitutional negotiation, and his skill in French and English. It was a decision taken by community leaders, and Dumont was central in making it.

Riel's early activity in 1884 focused on assembling a broad coalition. He supported petitions on behalf of Métis, settlers, and, to some extent, First Nations, and he sought support among English-speaking residents of Prince Albert. Demands included recognition of land claims and titles, representative government for the territories, possibly provincial status, acknowledgment of Métis rights as in 1870, and changes in federal administration. A petition was sent to Ottawa in December 1884. Cabinet authorized the proposed inquiry on January 28, 1885; its terms were then communicated to petitioners in February. The authorization was narrower than the petitioners’ combined demands for land security and representative government, and the delayed communication increased mistrust. For Riel and many Métis leaders, the proposal was an inadequate answer to grievances that had been repeatedly presented for years.

Not all members of the Métis movement favored the same strategy. Some, including many local clergy and merchants, preferred continued petitioning and negotiation. Others, including some English-speaking allies, withdrew from the movement as Riel's rhetoric took a more religious direction in late 1884. The Catholic clergy initially supported the movement's land claims but grew uneasy as Riel's political and religious ideas developed. The movement thus contained different strategies and voices, and its path to armed resistance was neither unified nor automatic.

The sequence of government correspondence matters. The December 1884 petition was not followed by an immediate settlement of land rights. The Cabinet decision of January 28, 1885, authorized a limited inquiry or enumeration; communication to the Saskatchewan petitioners followed in February. A federal official might characterize the January decision as evidence that the government had begun to respond, while people on the ground could fairly observe that no patents had been delivered and the wider demands for responsible representation remained unsettled. The interval between internal authorization and effective communication thus affected both sides’ understanding of how negotiations were proceeding.

The delegation that brought Riel home should not be mistaken for the origin of the movement. Community leaders had met, petitioned, and organized for years. Dumont brought an established reputation from the buffalo hunts and Saint-Laurent council; Riel brought knowledge of the political settlement of 1870 and the ability to connect disparate constitutional claims into a public program. Their skills were complementary but not identical. Some residents welcomed Riel as a negotiator while remaining reluctant to endorse a military confrontation; priests and merchants who agreed about title insecurity could disagree sharply over later tactics and religious pronouncements. Such differences help explain why the campaign of 1885 attracted support from some communities and caution or refusal from others.

Division IV · Section 83.13

The Provisional Government of Saskatchewan and the Decision to Resist

In early 1885 the movement entered a more radical phase. The announcement of a commission did not satisfy leaders who had waited years for action, and Riel's political and religious ideas had grown more prominent. On March 18, 1885, the Métis at Batoche proclaimed a provisional government. Pierre Parenteau presided over a council known as the Exovedate, a name Riel coined, and Gabriel Dumont was named adjutant-general, responsible for military affairs. The council claimed authority for the settlement and prepared to demand the recognition of its rights, drawing on the precedent of the Red River provisional government of 1869.

The step toward armed resistance was neither unanimous nor straightforward. The Exovedate debated how far to go, and members differed over the use of force. Some, including local clergy outside the council, urged restraint. Within days the movement took an assertive turn: on March 19 armed men took several people into custody and seized goods at a local store, and on March 21 Riel demanded the surrender of Fort Carlton, the nearby police post, offering safe withdrawal. Superintendent Leif Crozier of the North-West Mounted Police refused. These steps converted a political movement into one holding hostages and demanding the surrender of a government post, and they reduced the room for negotiation.

Riel's religious ideas shaped the movement and created tension within it. He believed that he had a prophetic mission, and he envisioned a reformed Catholic church for the Métis and new settlements in the Northwest. Priests such as Alexis André, who had sympathized with the land claims, became critical, and Riel's relations with the church deteriorated. Historians disagree about how much weight to give his religious views in explaining the events of 1885, and it would be inaccurate to reduce the political movement to Riel's personal beliefs. The land claims, economic crisis, and petitions preceded them, and many participants acted from those grievances, not from his religious program. At the same time, his ideas influenced decisions, including his preference for a fixed defense at Batoche. According to later accounts, Dumont had favored a more mobile guerrilla strategy.

The central historical question is why a movement based on petitions and constitutional recognition escalated to armed resistance. Part of the answer lies in the repeated failure of petitions; part in the belief that the government would respond only to pressure, as it had in 1869–1870; part in Riel's leadership and expectations; and part in the misjudgments of both sides about how the other would react. Some participants may have expected that a show of force would produce negotiation, as in 1870, without recognizing that the government now had a railway, a police force, and the ability to send troops quickly.

Historical Vocabulary
Provisional Government, Exovedate, and Political Authority

Provisional government refers to a temporary government established when an existing authority is absent, disputed, or unresponsive. In 1869 and 1885, Métis leaders used the term to claim authority to govern their settlements until their rights were recognized, and not necessarily to declare permanent independence.

Exovedate was the name given to the council of the 1885 government. The word was coined by Riel, and it is generally understood to indicate "from the flock." It refers to a body of community representatives that advised and made decisions with Riel, Parenteau, and Dumont.

Political authority here means the capacity to command obedience and make decisions in a community. The Métis based it on community consent, councils, and hunt-style leadership, while Canada based its claim on statute and sovereignty.

Potential misunderstanding: "Provisional" does not mean the governments were informal gatherings. Each had councils, officers, and procedures, even though their legal status was disputed by Canada.

Division IV · Section 83.14

The Battle of Duck Lake, March 26, 1885

After Riel's demand for the surrender of Fort Carlton, tension escalated around the supplies at Duck Lake. On March 26, 1885, Superintendent Leif Crozier left the fort with about one hundred North-West Mounted Police and volunteers from Prince Albert, with a small field gun, to secure stores at Duck Lake. They met a group of Métis and First Nations men, probably two hundred or more, led by Gabriel Dumont on the trail. Accounts of the number on each side vary, and sources differ because the participants were dispersed and the records were written afterward.

A parley was attempted. Interpreters on both sides, including the police interpreter John McKay, went forward, and the discussion turned tense. In the exchange, an Indigenous man, Assiyiwin (often written Asiyiwin), who accompanied the Métis delegation, was shot, and the shooting then became general. The immediate cause of the first shot is disputed, because accounts by Crozier's men and by the Métis differ, but sources agree that the exchange broke down in a few moments. The Métis and their allies, firing from cover along the trail and in the brush, forced the police to retreat. Twelve on the government side were killed, and several others wounded. On the Métis and allied side, about five were killed, among them Gabriel Dumont's brother Isidore and Assiyiwin. Dumont himself was grazed by a bullet.

Dumont's tactical leadership was important. He used the ground and the mobility of mounted men to flank the police, and he sought to prevent a prolonged engagement. Accounts indicate that Riel and others urged restraint when Dumont wished to pursue the retreating column, and the government force reached Fort Carlton in order. Within days Crozier abandoned the fort, which later burned, and the police withdrew to Prince Albert. The Métis had won a clear local victory.

Yet the victory had strategic costs. A fight that killed a dozen government men converted the crisis from a political dispute into a military confrontation, and it provided the Canadian government with a reason to mobilize large forces. Within days, Major-General Frederick Middleton, commander of the Canadian militia, was ordered west, and troops began moving by the Canadian Pacific Railway from eastern Canada. The first engagement thus closed off the possibilities of negotiation just as it showed Métis military skill. The relationship between armed action, mobilization, and diminishing room for negotiation is central to understanding the rest of the campaign.

Division IV · Section 83.15

First Nations Grievances and the Wider Crisis of 1885

The events of 1885 were not only a Métis story. At the same time, Cree and Nakoda (Assiniboine) communities in the North Saskatchewan and Battleford districts were facing their own crisis, rooted in the collapse of the bison, the circumstances of Treaty 6 (1876), and the administration of the Indian Department. Chapter 81 described the loss of the herds and Chapter 82 the treaty negotiations; here the consequence is that by the early 1880s many families depended on government rations, which were often inadequate and administered in ways that gave officials considerable power. Policies of limiting rations as leverage, and efforts to confine people to reserves, aroused resentment, while people who felt that treaty promises of assistance in hardship had not been honored pressed their claims.

The leaders involved held different positions. Big Bear was a prominent Cree chief who had delayed signing Treaty 6 until 1882, hoping to obtain better terms and to keep Cree people together; by 1885 he had few followers who held to a policy of peace and a growing number of younger men who were impatient. Poundmaker, a Cree leader adopted by the Blackfoot leader Crowfoot, headed a reserve near Battleford, and he had a reputation as an orator and negotiator. Fine Day was a respected Cree war leader and strategist who played a major role at Cut Knife. Wandering Spirit was war chief of Big Bear's band, and held authority over the young men who acted at Frog Lake. These leaders did not act under Riel's orders, and their communities took different decisions.

On April 2, 1885, at Frog Lake, members of Big Bear's band led by Wandering Spirit killed nine men, including the Indian agent Thomas Quinn and two Oblate priests. Big Bear opposed the killings, according to most accounts, but he could not control the war chief, and he was held responsible by the government. The group went on to Fort Pitt, which was surrendered by the police on April 15. The killings were, in historical terms, a violent act with its own causes, in particular the anger over rations and treatment, and the role of Quinn, whose actions toward the community had caused grievance, and they were not a Métis operation.

On May 2, a column under Lieutenant-Colonel William Otter attacked the camp of Poundmaker's people, a Cree and Nakoda encampment, at Cut Knife Hill. Parks Canada describes the attack as an attack on a camp. Under Fine Day's direction, the defenders took positions on the surrounding hills and the coulee and, after several hours, forced Otter's column to withdraw. Poundmaker is credited by many accounts with restraining his men from pursuing and destroying the retreating soldiers. After the defeat at Batoche, he surrendered to Middleton on May 26, seeking a political resolution. Poundmaker was tried and imprisoned, and died shortly after his release in 1886. In 2019 the Government of Canada formally exonerated him.

The Cree movements were therefore not simple subordinate units of Riel's army. They had their own leaders, causes, and strategies: Big Bear sought to keep his people together and avoid conflict, Poundmaker negotiated and tried to protect his camp, and Wandering Spirit directed a violent course that Big Bear opposed. Their grievances overlapped with those of the Métis, because both faced the end of the bison economy and unresponsive administration, but their political objectives were not the same. The Métis were demanding recognition of land and rights within the Canadian constitutional order; the Cree were pressing to honor treaty obligations and to survive. Afterward the federal government hanged eight First Nations men at Battleford on November 27, 1885, imprisoned Big Bear and Poundmaker, and tightened control over Indigenous communities, developments to which Chapters 84 and 85 return.

Division IV · Section 83.16

Gabriel Dumont, Fish Creek, and the Canadian Military Campaign

Gabriel Dumont's military experience came from decades on the buffalo hunt and in conflicts on the Plains. The Métis could field mounted riflemen who knew the ground, rode well, and shot accurately, but they lacked artillery, large reserves of ammunition, and a way to replace losses. Dumont recognized these limits, and he is generally thought to have favored harassing the advancing Canadians with rapid raids, ambushes, and night attacks rather than a static defense. The geography of the South Saskatchewan, with its steep banks, coulees, wooded ravines, and river crossings, gave defenders opportunities for concealment and surprise.

Major-General Frederick Middleton assembled a force of regular and militia units, several thousand men in all, and moved it west by railway as far as the line allowed and then overland. The Canadian Pacific Railway, though incomplete in sections north of Lake Superior, brought troops from eastern Canada to the Plains within days or weeks, a speed impossible in 1870 when Wolseley's expedition took months. Steamboats and wagon trains carried supplies, the telegraph linked commanders to Ottawa, and the government had modern rifles, artillery, and a Gatling gun. Middleton advanced on Batoche with roughly 800 men in columns on both sides of the river; his right-hand column of approximately 400 directly encountered the Fish Creek defenders.

On April 24, 1885, Dumont's men met Middleton's advance at Tourond's Coulee, known as Fish Creek, a ravine crossed by the trail south of Batoche. Approximately 150 Métis, Dakota, and Cree defenders, according to Parks Canada’s site designation, positioned themselves in rifle pits dug into the sides of the coulee and in the surrounding brush, with the intent of ambushing the column as it descended. The accounts of the battle differ in detail, but they agree that the ambush was partly detected, that the Métis held their ground for hours using cover and mobility, and that the larger Canadian force suffered significant casualties. Parks Canada’s national historic-site designation records 55 casualties in Middleton’s force and six among Dumont’s defenders; these combined casualties should not be confused with the number killed. Middleton's force was forced to halt and regroup for roughly two weeks before moving again.

Fish Creek is often cited as a Métis tactical success, because a small force delayed a larger one. But it did not change the strategic balance. The Canadian force had lines of supply, reinforcements, and new units on the way, while the Métis had limited ammunition and could not replace men lost. Parks Canada's battlefield designation documents how Dumont's force delayed Middleton's advance. The delay gave defenders time to prepare Batoche, but it also gave Middleton time to bring up forces. As in the earlier engagements, a local victory left the larger situation unchanged, and the campaign increasingly turned on logistics, transport, and technology rather than on battlefield tactics alone.

The Parks Canada designation provides a useful way to distinguish force sizes from combat losses at Fish Creek. Middleton was moving approximately 800 men along both banks of the South Saskatchewan, but it was the right-hand column of about 400 that directly confronted roughly 150 Métis, Dakota, and Cree fighters concealed at Tourond’s Coulee. The site’s historical designation records 55 Canadian casualties against six among the defenders; the figure is a total of killed and wounded, not a death toll. Numbers elsewhere may differ because authors count different detachments or categories of loss. The scale of the disparity, rather than an incorrectly precise killed-only comparison, supports the analysis that the smaller defending force inflicted a severe local check.

That check resulted from terrain and preparation, not from a reversal of the overall balance of military capacity. Men sheltered by broken ground could fire from dispersed positions, while advancing soldiers had difficulty locating targets and coordinating movement. Yet a battle fought from prepared cover also limited the defenders’ freedom to strike the enemy’s supply lines elsewhere. Middleton’s pause after the engagement was operationally costly and gave Batoche time to prepare, but his men could be reinforced and resupplied. The defenders could not create comparable ammunition stores or replace experienced marksmen as easily. The contrast between tactical resistance and strategic endurance is central to understanding the next engagement.

Division V · Section 83.17

The Battle of Batoche, May 9–12, 1885

Batoche was the culmination of the campaign. Parks Canada records the battle as lasting from May 9 to May 12, 1885, with fewer than three hundred Métis and First Nations defenders facing about eight hundred Canadian troops. The numbers are approximate, and defenders were joined and left over the four days as families and fighters moved. The defenders dug a network of rifle pits along the ridges, ravines, and bluffs overlooking the settlement and the river, placing them to cover the approaches from the south and east. The pits were well sited and hard to see, and the civilian community, including women, children, and the elderly, sheltered in pits and caves near the river and around the settlement.

On the morning of May 9 the Canadian force opened fire on the village with artillery and the Gatling gun, and the steamer Northcote, carrying troops and supplies, attempted to descend the river past Batoche. Métis defenders had lowered a ferry cable across the river, and it tore away the steamer's mast and funnels and forced it aground. The Northcote episode was a small victory, but it removed a planned element of Middleton's attack, which had expected a coordinated assault from two directions. In the following days, Middleton conducted cautious advances and reconnaissance, which allowed the defenders to hold their positions. Canadian artillery and the Gatling gun, operated by Captain Arthur Howard, a former American militia officer, inflicted limited damage on a dispersed and entrenched defense, but they put pressure on the defenders' morale and ammunition.

The relationship between military and political leadership was complex. Dumont directed the fighting, using small mobile parties to harass the Canadian flanks and rear, and he is said to have wanted still more mobile tactics. Riel stayed close to the defenders, and according to many accounts he moved among the rifle pits carrying a crucifix, encouraging men to resist. The defense also depended on the civilian community: families supplied food, tended the wounded, and supported the fighters, and the battle took place in and around their homes, church, and fields. Fighters began to run short of ammunition during the second and third days, and accounts describe the use of improvised shot made from nails and stones.

On May 12, Canadian units, including the Midland Battalion and the Grenadiers and the Winnipeg Rifles, advanced in a concerted attack that moved faster than the defenders had expected. The Canadian infantry overran the rifle pits, and the defense collapsed. Casualties are difficult to state precisely: Canadian forces lost about eight killed at Batoche, with several dozen wounded, and the Métis and First Nations dead are usually estimated in the dozens, with figures varying by source. Dumont escaped and eventually reached Montana. On May 15, Riel surrendered to scouts, and other participants were captured or fled. The village was looted, and many homes were damaged.

Students should note differences in the evidence. Canadian officers such as Middleton wrote official reports and later accounts that emphasized discipline, strategy, and gallantry and played down mistakes and looting. Métis and First Nations perspectives come from later recollections, including Dumont's dictated memoir, published in translation as Gabriel Dumont Speaks, and from community oral history. Each source was shaped by its author's position and purpose. The outcome, in any event, should be explained through military circumstances, including the superior numbers, artillery, supply, and ammunition of the Canadian force, and political decisions, including the choice of a static defense and the decision to confront a government with the railway and the means to send an army. Defeat was likely in a prolonged contest but was not inevitable at the outset, because a negotiated settlement or a more mobile strategy might have changed the course of events.

The opening day reveals why the battle cannot be described simply as artillery overcoming rifle pits. Middleton envisaged pressure from the landward approach and a movement by the steamer Northcote on the river. The lowered ferry cable disrupted the vessel’s passage and upset the timing of that plan. On land, defenders contested the ridges and approaches around the church, rectory, and village rather than remaining in a single fortified line. The attackers had to advance against positions that were hard to identify from a distance. Temporary Métis success in frustrating particular maneuvers should not be mistaken for control of the battle as a whole: the Canadians still possessed organized supply, heavy weapons, and the ability to hold ground beyond the defenders’ reach.

On May 10 and 11 the fighting became a contest of reconnaissance, concealment, fatigue, and ammunition. The defenders’ rifle pits made a direct rush costly; the attacking command’s caution limited its early gains. But the defenders had to distribute scarce ammunition over several threatened approaches while also protecting families, injured fighters, and households. Eyewitnesses and later historians disagree over the precise sequence and effect of individual assaults. A sound reconstruction therefore separates what can be located securely by day and terrain from remembered incidents whose timing has shifted in retelling. Photographs of the surviving ground, military plans, and Parks Canada’s field interpretation can assist students, but none supplies an unmediated account of the people fighting from the pits.

The final assault on May 12 involved Canadian infantry pressing into defensive positions amid weakening Métis resistance. Contemporary accounts sometimes emphasize the initiative of particular units or officers; Métis histories place equal emphasis on exhausted defenders and depleted ammunition. The narrative should avoid explaining the result through a single heroic charge. The troops’ numbers and reserves allowed an increasingly concentrated assault, while defenders who had endured several days of pressure could not replace ammunition or move civilians readily. The loss of the pits thus represented both a military defeat and the exposure of an inhabited settlement to occupation. Looting, household damage, and later claims for compensation make clear that the military event continued to affect families after firing ceased.

Battlefield casualties must be reported with caution. Parks Canada describes fewer than 300 defenders confronting about 800 Canadian troops over May 9–12, while other commemorative descriptions round the defending force to about 300. Some accounts supply killed-only figures for the final day; others count wounded across the entire four-day battle. These cannot legitimately be combined into one apparently exact total. The chapter’s historical conclusion does not depend on a manufactured count. It depends on explaining how a relatively small defending force could resist for four days and why the Canadian state, with more secure transport and supply, could eventually concentrate the force needed to overrun it.

Division V · Section 83.18

The Wider Campaign, Indigenous Resistance, and the Human Consequences of War

The fall of Batoche did not end the fighting. In the Fort Pitt and Frog Lake districts, Big Bear's band, which held a number of captives, moved northward pursued by Canadian columns. On May 28, at Frenchman's Butte, a force under Major-General Thomas Strange engaged Cree fighters entrenched on the heights. The engagement ended without decisive result, and the Cree withdrew. On June 3 a smaller engagement at Loon Lake between a Canadian detachment and the Cree rearguard became the last armed clash of the campaign. Big Bear surrendered at Fort Carlton on July 2. Poundmaker had surrendered on May 26. By midsummer, organized armed resistance was over.

The human consequences were severe and fell unevenly. Métis families at Batoche and nearby settlements lost homes, livestock, crops, furniture, and tools, to damage in the fighting and to looting by troops and others. Many people had fled into the countryside before or during the battle and returned to ruined properties. Some Métis fighters were killed, others were imprisoned, and dozens faced trial. Families were separated, with men in custody or in exile and women responsible for households and children. The campaign also dispersed families, and some left for Montana and elsewhere. The fear of reprisal, the loss of property, and the closure of political avenues deeply affected the community.

Women played significant roles throughout. They took part in discussions about whether to resist, cared for the wounded and the sick, protected children and the elderly during the fighting, prepared food, and kept households and farms functioning. Métis historical research identifies women such as Marguerite (Dumas) Caron and Josephte Tourond among those whose actions during and after the events are recorded. After the battle, women often took the lead in dealing with officials, pursuing compensation claims, and rebuilding. The claims for losses filed by families after 1885 are in many cases documents that reflect their efforts, and they remain important evidence of the war's material costs.

The military defeat affected Métis and First Nations participants differently. Métis leaders and fighters faced trials for treason-felony or related offenses, a number received prison sentences, and Riel faced a trial for high treason. First Nations leaders, including Big Bear and Poundmaker, were convicted and imprisoned, and eight men were hanged at Battleford on November 27, 1885 for killings at Frog Lake and elsewhere. For First Nations communities, the consequences continued after the trials, as the government tightened controls over movement, rations, and band governance, and reduced the ability of leaders to act independently. Chapters 84 and 85 examine those wider structures of administration. For the Métis, who did not have reserves or treaty status in the same way, the consequences took the form of land loss, dispersal, and political marginalization, topics taken up in Section 83.20.

Division V · Section 83.19

The Trial and Execution of Louis Riel, 1885

Riel surrendered on May 15, 1885, and was taken to Regina. The government chose to try him for high treason, a charge that carried the death penalty, before a stipendiary magistrate, Hugh Richardson, and a justice of the peace, with a jury of six men. The trial opened on July 20 and ended on August 1 with a verdict of guilty and a jury recommendation of mercy. The venue in Regina, the composition of the jury, which was made up of English-speaking men, and the choice of the North-West Territories' procedures, rather than a trial in Manitoba or Quebec, were criticized then and later. The Crown prosecution team included prominent lawyers, and the defense was led by François-Xavier Lemieux and Charles Fitzpatrick, among others.

The legal arguments centered on jurisdiction, on the question of whether Riel's actions constituted treason, and on his mental state. Defense counsel argued, among other points, that the court lacked proper jurisdiction and that Riel was insane and therefore not responsible. Riel himself rejected the insanity defense, insisting that his actions were rational and politically motivated, and he addressed the court at length, defending the grievances of the Métis, his role in the events of 1869–1870, and his conduct in 1885. Medical witnesses disagreed about his condition, but the court held that he was legally responsible. The jury found him guilty, and he was sentenced to hang, a sentence upheld on appeal to the Manitoba Court of Queen's Bench and by the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, which declined to grant leave to appeal.

The trial became a major controversy in Canada. In Quebec, many people saw Riel as a French-speaking Catholic defending a minority and condemned the execution, and mass protests were held in Montreal and elsewhere. In Ontario, many English-speaking Protestants saw him as a murderer and traitor, remembering the execution of Thomas Scott. The controversy exposed divisions of language and religion within Canada and affected the fortunes of political parties, with the Conservative Party weakened in Quebec and the Liberal Party under Wilfrid Laurier strengthened there. Laurier is remembered for saying publicly that, had he been born on the Saskatchewan, he might himself have taken up arms, a statement that illustrates how Riel's case became a debate about justice and belonging. The execution on November 16, 1885, in Regina closed the case, but not the argument.

Students should avoid two simplifications. Riel was not a flawless martyr: he led an armed movement that resulted in deaths, his decisions in 1885 were contested within the movement, and his religious ideas divided supporters. Neither was he simply a criminal defendant detached from political context. His trial must be read alongside the demands that had motivated the resistance, including land titles, representation, and recognition of Métis rights, which the government had failed to address, and alongside the history of the Red River Resistance, in which he had helped negotiate the creation of Manitoba. Later interpretations and commemorations, including the federal and provincial recognition of Riel as a founder of Manitoba, should be distinguished from the original trial proceedings, whose record reflects the legal and political world of 1885.

The printed record is not a simple window into what the court objectively established. The 1886 Queen’s Printer compilation, The Queen vs. Louis Riel, Accused and Convicted of the Crime of High Treason, gathered the Regina proceedings alongside subsequent appeal material and petitions concerning sentence and medical examination. Its organization shows how contemporaries made the trial into both a judicial and public political dossier. It is valuable for identifying the formal charge, testimony, counsel’s arguments, and Riel’s own courtroom speeches. It cannot show every exchange with the same fullness, independently verify testimony, or reveal how every Métis family interpreted the trial.

The jury’s recommendation of mercy, the defense’s disputed insanity strategy, and Riel’s insistence that his conduct had political meaning created different possibilities for the executive government after the verdict. Legal responsibility, executive clemency, and political recognition were separate questions; the court’s sentence did not compel a single historical interpretation of Red River or Batoche. The political controversy was intensified by regional and confessional tensions, but those divisions should not erase the Métis claims that had prompted the movement. A careful account presents the case as a prosecution in 1885, then traces how later commemorations altered Riel’s public standing without pretending that those commemorations retroactively rewrote the trial record.

Aftermath, Métis Survival, and the Historical Meaning of the Resistances

After 1885 the federal government punished participants, imprisoned some leaders, and reorganized the administration of claims in the Northwest. The 1885 North-West scrip commission processed Métis claims within a territorial program whose legislative antecedents predated the resistance; further commissions extended the process in later decades. This must be distinguished from Manitoba’s 1874 scrip for heads of families and from later remedial scrip for some children who had not received a Section 31 allotment. As in Manitoba, scrip was transferable and often sold cheaply, so it did not secure land for many families. Some Métis moved to Montana, Alberta, and other places, and some remained at Batoche and in the surrounding district. Gabriel Dumont escaped to the United States and received an amnesty in 1886. He joined Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show in Philadelphia in July of that year, returned to Canada later in the decade, and died in 1906.

It would be wrong to say that Batoche ceased to exist after 1885 or that the Métis disappeared as a political people. Diane Payment's research shows that families continued to farm and live at Batoche, that the church and school remained, and that community life, language, and kin networks endured, even under conditions of land loss, poverty, and prejudice. In Manitoba and Saskatchewan, many Métis families, pushed from their lands, lived on road allowances, strips of Crown land along roads, in what became known as road-allowance communities. These communities, often without legal title, maintained Michif, family networks, and cultural traditions. Their persistence is one reason that historians reject accounts of total Métis political disappearance. The twentieth-century story of Métis organization is beyond this chapter, but its foundations lay in community continuity.

Later legal and political developments show that the issues raised in 1869 and 1885 did not end. Canada's Constitution Act, 1982 recognized the Métis among the Aboriginal peoples of Canada. In R. v. Powley (2003), the Supreme Court recognized a Métis right to harvest, and in Manitoba Métis Federation v. Canada (2013) it found that the federal government had failed to implement Section 31 with the diligence required by the honour of the Crown. In Daniels v. Canada (2016) the Court held that Métis and non-status Indians are "Indians" for the purposes of federal jurisdiction. A proposed federal Louis Riel bill in 2010 did not become law. Manitoba’s provincial Louis Riel Act, enacted on December 7, 2023, bestowed the honorary title of first premier of Manitoba upon Riel; it was not a federal exoneration or a reversal of the 1885 conviction. The federal government formally issued a posthumous exoneration and apology for Poundmaker on May 23, 2019, and Canada and the Manitoba Métis Federation signed a self-government recognition and implementation agreement on July 6, 2021. Students should treat these as later developments and not as a resolution of the nineteenth-century grievances.

Historians have interpreted the resistances very differently. Older English-Canadian tradition often described them as rebellions against legitimate authority and as the last resistance of a primitive frontier society to modern civilization, a view reflected in works such as George Stanley's The Birth of Western Canada (1936), which emphasized a clash between settled and frontier ways of life. Thomas Flanagan's work questioned the legitimacy of Métis claims and emphasized Riel's decisions. Historians such as D. N. Sprague stressed government policy and the dispossession of the Métis, Diane Payment emphasized community continuity, and Gerhard Ens and Joe Sawchuk examined Métis political institutions and economic choices. Métis writers and institutions, for their part, emphasize the defense of a distinct nation and homeland.

The administrative aftermath generated several kinds of record that should not be merged. A federal investigation of land claims and eligibility for scrip was concerned with property and status under a particular government program. Claims for civilian losses during the campaign addressed destroyed houses, livestock, stores, or crops. William Pearce’s 1886 detailed report about land claims in the South Saskatchewan settlements was a government account of land administration, not a blanket register of war-damage payments. Those who filed a claim did not necessarily receive its requested value, and the presence of a name in a register does not prove that an award was paid. Comparing the categories permits a more precise understanding of the economic costs of defeat.

Later recognition likewise requires legal precision. Manitoba’s 2023 Louis Riel Act grants a posthumous honorary provincial title and specifies curricular recognition; it does not establish that a 2010 federal proposal became law, nor is it a judicial acquittal. The 2019 federal statement concerning Poundmaker is a formal governmental exoneration and apology, distinct from an appellate court reversing the nineteenth-century conviction. The July 2021 agreement between Canada and the Manitoba Métis Federation recognizes an existing Métis government and sets a course for implementation; it does not by itself settle every historical land claim arising from 1870 or 1885. These developments demonstrate continued political claims, but the chapter’s principal task remains explanation of nineteenth-century events.

Historical Debate

Were the Red River and North-West Resistances Failed Rebellions, Constitutional Struggles, or Defenses of Métis National Rights?

The question: How should historians characterize the two resistances? The label chosen shapes how the events are understood, taught, and remembered. The debate concerns terminology and interpretation more than the sequence of events, which is broadly agreed.

Interpretation A — Rebellions against legitimate authority: Some historians, and much older Canadian tradition, argue that Canada held lawful sovereignty after the transfer and that armed action against it, particularly in 1885, was rebellion. They stress that Riel's government in 1869–1870 executed a prisoner and that the 1885 movement used force while negotiations were still possible.
Interpretation B — Constitutional struggles: Others emphasize that the Red River Resistance produced a negotiated constitutional settlement, that the Lists of Rights and petitions used legal and political means, and that the use of force was a response to failures of the constitutional process. They treat the resistances as part of the making of the Canadian federation.
Interpretation C — Defenses of Métis national rights: Métis scholars and institutions, and many historians, argue that the Métis were a distinct people defending a homeland and self-government, with a right to be consulted about the transfer and to negotiate its terms. In this view, Canadian sovereignty over the Northwest was itself contested by the Métis and other Indigenous peoples.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same documents: the Lists of Rights, the Manitoba Act, the petitions of 1884, the records of Riel's trial, military reports, and community testimony. They differ in how they weigh those sources.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology, the legitimacy of Canadian authority in 1869 and 1885, and interpretation of Riel's decisions, rather than the occurrence of the events themselves. The interpretations are not always mutually exclusive: a movement could be both a constitutional struggle and a defense of national rights.

Why it matters: The label affects whether the Métis are treated as dissidents, as founders of a province, or as a people with continuing claims, and it informs modern legal and political questions about recognition, land, and self-government.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Métis Political Organization from Red River through the Aftermath of 1885

What Changed

  • Métis political authority moved from effective control of a settlement in 1869–1870 to a minority position within a province, and then to petitioning, armed resistance, and repression in the Saskatchewan.
  • Political organization shifted from hunt councils and provisional governments toward later associations, conventions, and legal advocacy.
  • Settlement patterns changed from river-lot parishes to dispersed communities, including road-allowance communities.

What Stayed the Same

  • The claim to collective rights, land, and recognition as a distinct people persisted.
  • Kinship networks, language, and community institutions, including the parish, endured.
  • Leaders continued to use councils, petitions, and negotiation, and the memory of 1870 and 1885 informed later organizing.

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven and contested. Leaders such as Riel and Dumont experienced exile and loss most directly, while many families experienced continuity through kin and community. The pattern shows that the military defeat of 1885 ended one form of political action without ending Métis political life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined two Métis resistances, sixteen years apart, as expressions of a continuing effort to defend a homeland, political institutions, and territorial rights against Canadian expansion. It has argued that the Red River Resistance of 1869–1870 achieved a significant constitutional settlement, that the settlement's land and political promises were inadequately fulfilled, and that renewed grievances, together with economic transformation and unresponsive administration, produced the North-West Resistance of 1885.

The Red River Métis were not a loose population of mixed ancestry but a politically organized people. Parish settlements, river-lot farms, kinship networks, the governance of the buffalo hunt, and a memory of collective action at Seven Oaks and in the Sayer affair gave them institutions through which to act. The distinction between the Métis nation and the wider Country-born community mattered, because the resistance depended on cooperation across linguistic and religious lines that was never complete.

The crisis of 1869 arose from the transfer of Rupert's Land to Canada without consultation, the arrival of survey parties that threatened river-lot holdings, and the attempt to install an appointed lieutenant-governor. The National Committee, the barricade, and the occupation of Upper Fort Garry converted grievance into effective authority. Louis Riel was an important leader within a larger movement that included older hunt leaders, local clergy, and parish representatives.

The provisional government, the Convention of Forty, and the successive Lists of Rights show a movement that built representative institutions and negotiated demands across language communities. Its claim to local authority was paired with a stated willingness to enter Canada on negotiated terms. The execution of Thomas Scott on March 4, 1870, was a grave act that had lasting political consequences, and historians neither excuse it nor treat it apart from the unsettled conditions in which it occurred.

The Manitoba Act created a province, provided for French and English in the legislature and courts, protected denominational schools, and included two land provisions that must be distinguished: Section 31's grant of 1.4 million acres for Métis families and Section 32's confirmation of existing holdings. Federal control of public lands and the absence of a written amnesty were unresolved issues. The Wolseley expedition and the hostility that followed it showed that the formal settlement did not provide immediate security.

In the following decade, delays in patents, changes in rules, speculation, immigration pressure, and hostility contributed to the loss of land by many Métis families, and many moved west. Historians weigh the causes differently, some emphasizing government policy and others market conditions and family choices. The Supreme Court's 2013 decision found a failure in the implementation of Section 31 under the honour of the Crown, and did not find the Section 32 claim established. The settlement of 1870 is therefore best understood as a real political achievement whose implementation was inadequate.

On the Saskatchewan, Métis communities such as Saint-Laurent and Batoche developed established economies and local government, including Dumont's council. The collapse of the bison herds transformed livelihoods and increased the importance of agriculture, wage work, and women's household economies. Dominion township surveys, uncertain title, the railway route, the location of the territorial capital, and limited representation produced grievances that accumulated through administrative decisions, and the petitions of 1878–1884 show a long constitutional effort before arms were taken up.

The recall of Riel in 1884 was a community decision, and the provisional government of March 1885 escalated the movement from petition to assertion of authority. The battles of Duck Lake, Fish Creek, and Batoche showed Métis tactical skill, but the railway, artillery, supply, and numbers of the Canadian force determined the campaign. The Cree and Nakoda actions at Frog Lake, Fort Pitt, Cut Knife Hill, and Frenchman's Butte arose from treaty and subsistence crises, were led by their own leaders, and were not subordinate to Riel's command.

The defeat at Batoche was followed by trials, hangings, imprisonments, the loss of property, and tighter administrative control over First Nations. Riel's trial and execution became a lasting controversy over law, language, religion, and the treatment of Métis claims. Yet the aftermath was not disappearance. Families, language, parish life, and political organizing endured at Batoche, in road-allowance communities, and elsewhere, and later legal developments show that the questions raised in 1869 and 1885 remained live.

Interpretations differ on whether the events should be called rebellions, constitutional struggles, or defenses of national rights. This chapter has treated them as connected but distinct episodes: one that produced a negotiated constitutional settlement and one that ended in military defeat, with consequences extending beyond the battlefield. Chapter 84 turns from these confrontations to the wider systems of reserves and reservations that confined and administered Indigenous peoples.

Comparing the Manitoba Settlement of 1870 with the Saskatchewan Grievances of 1884–1885

This comparative project asks you to examine how the demands and constitutional settlement of 1870 related to the grievances raised by Saskatchewan Métis communities in 1884–1885.

  1. Assemble sources. Collect government records, Métis historical accounts, and at least one identifiable primary source for each period, for example the Manitoba Act and a version of the Lists of Rights for 1869–1870, and the petitions of 1884 or claims records for 1885–1886.

Chapter Reflection

The two resistances matter because they show Indigenous people acting as political communities within the formation of the Canadian state. In 1869–1870 the Métis used councils, conventions, and negotiation to compel recognition of local rights, and the result, the Manitoba Act, is part of Canada's constitutional history. Their experience contradicts any picture of the Northwest as a blank space awaiting government.

The chapter also shows how formal recognition can be undermined by weak implementation. The land provisions of 1870 promised security that administrative delay, speculation, and changing rules failed to deliver, and the Supreme Court's 2013 decision confirms that these were matters of constitutional obligation. The failures helped drive families west and fed the grievances of the 1880s, so the chapter links the two resistances through a causal chain rather than treating them as separate episodes.

The events of 1885 reveal the limits of resistance against a government with a railway and an army, while also showing the independent decisions of Cree and Nakoda communities facing their own crisis. They shaped later policies of administration over First Nations, which Chapters 84 and 85 examine, and they shaped Canadian politics through the controversy over Riel.

What survived was as significant as what was lost. Families, language, parishes, and political traditions persisted at Batoche and in many other communities, and later generations used the memory of 1870 and 1885 in organizing and in litigation. This continuity is the central answer to narratives of disappearance, and it is why the history of these resistances belongs within a continental history of Indigenous nations as continuing political communities.
