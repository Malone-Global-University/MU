Norse Greenland and Vinland

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Around the year 1021, people using iron tools cut down trees on the northern tip of the island of Newfoundland. The wood they worked can now be dated to that single year. It is the earliest securely dated evidence of Europeans on the North American side of the Atlantic, and it belongs to a short-lived outpost, not to the beginning of a continuous colonial history.

Chapter 36 closed with an Indigenous continent already connected by its own systems of trade, diplomacy, migration, language, and ecological knowledge. This chapter begins the next movement of the textbook by asking what happened when the first Europeans to reach that continent crossed the North Atlantic. The answer is more modest, and more instructive, than popular memory suggests. The Norse, Scandinavian-speaking seafarers and farmers whose settlements spread west from Norway through Iceland, did establish communities in Greenland that lasted roughly four and a half centuries. From Greenland they made voyages farther west. At L'Anse aux Meadows, archaeologists have found the remains of a Norse encampment that is the first securely documented European site in North America.

It would nevertheless be a serious distortion to call this episode the "discovery" of America. The people of Newfoundland, Labrador, Baffin Island, and Greenland had histories reaching back thousands of years before any Norse ship crossed Davis Strait. Those histories are treated in Volumes I through III, particularly Chapters 11 and 35. The Norse entered a world that Indigenous peoples already inhabited, named, hunted in, and understood. What the Norse sagas called empty or abundant lands were, in many cases, regions through which Indigenous people moved seasonally or lived in settled communities. The sagas themselves record meetings with those people.

The chapter has three assigned tasks. The first is to explain Norse settlement in Greenland, including its economy, its institutions, and its eventual end. The second is to examine encounters between the Norse and Indigenous peoples, both in the western voyages the sagas call Vinland voyages and in Greenland itself, where Norse and Thule-tradition Inuit communities came to share a landscape. The third is to evaluate the archaeological evidence for Norse activity in northeastern North America, distinguishing what is firmly established from what is plausible, disputed, or discredited.

The chapter also teaches a method. The Norse case combines unusually different kinds of evidence: Icelandic sagas written down generations after the events, farm ruins and church foundations in Greenland, excavated buildings at L'Anse aux Meadows, ice-core and tree-ring science, medieval annals, and Indigenous archaeological traditions. These sources do not always agree, and they do not carry equal authority. Students should come away able to explain how a late and literary source differs from a radiocarbon-dated timber, and why historians treat the two differently while using both.

The central argument is this: the Norse voyages were the first securely documented European crossing into Indigenous North America, but they did not create the continent's networks or begin continuous European colonization. Norse settlers entered Arctic and northeastern worlds already inhabited by Indigenous peoples. Their western ventures remained limited, their encounters included both exchange and conflict, and their foothold in the Americas disappeared while Indigenous societies continued their own histories.

That argument depends on a distinction the chapter maintains throughout. Greenland is part of North America geographically, and Norse Greenland lasted for centuries. L'Anse aux Meadows, by contrast, was brief. The sustained Atlantic expansion that begins with the voyages of 1492, examined in Chapter 38, was not a continuation of Norse settlement. There is no institutional chain linking Vinland to Columbus. Understanding why not is one of the chapter's principal lessons.

Finally, the chapter observes the limits set by the textbook's overall structure. It is not a chapter on the Viking Age. Iceland, Norway, ships, religion, and European politics appear only as far as they explain Greenland and the western voyages. It does not retell the formation of Thule-tradition Inuit society, which belongs to Chapter 35, and it stops before the voyages of Columbus.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Norse episode is best described as the first securely documented European presence in Indigenous North America and not as a "discovery" of the continent.
  2. Describe the earlier Indigenous history of Greenland and explain why it matters for understanding Norse settlement.
  3. Trace the movement of Norse settlers from Iceland to Greenland and explain the organization of the Eastern and Western Settlements.
  4. Analyze the Greenland Norse economy, including farming, marine hunting, and the export of walrus ivory and other Arctic goods.
  5. Evaluate the Vinland sagas as historical evidence, distinguishing what they can and cannot establish.
  6. Explain how archaeology at L'Anse aux Meadows established a Norse presence independently of the sagas.
  7. Describe how the AD 993 radiocarbon event allowed researchers to date Norse-cut wood to the year 1021.
  8. Assess the range of interpretations of L'Anse aux Meadows' function and duration.
  9. Identify the Indigenous archaeological traditions of Newfoundland and Labrador around AD 1000 and explain why archaeological traditions should not be casually equated with later named nations.
  10. Interpret the Norse term skrælingar and explain why it reveals more about Norse categories than about Indigenous identities.
  11. Compare the strength of evidence for Norse contact at different sites and distinguish confirmed, disputed, and discredited claims.
  12. Explain why Norse Greenland ended and why Vinland did not become a permanent colony, using multi-causal reasoning.

Historical Context

The Arctic and subarctic Atlantic worlds that the Norse reached were environments of extreme seasonality, shaped by sea ice, ocean currents, short growing seasons, and migratory animals. Indigenous peoples had developed settled and mobile ways of life suited to them. Paleo-Inuit traditions, which emerged in the Arctic several millennia before the Norse and are discussed in Chapter 11, had spread across the eastern Arctic and into Greenland and the Labrador coast. Later, Thule-tradition communities, whose origins and expansion across the Arctic are the subject of Chapter 35, moved east from the Bering Strait region. By the thirteenth century they had reached Greenland.

On the European side, the Norse were part of a wider medieval Scandinavian expansion into the North Atlantic. Beginning in the ninth century, Scandinavian-speaking settlers, together with people from the Atlantic fringes of the British Isles, colonized the Faroe Islands and Iceland, islands without Indigenous human populations at the time of settlement. Settlement in Greenland in the late tenth century extended the same pattern of farming settlement into a region that was, at the time, thinly peopled in the southwest. The settlers brought a livestock-based farming economy, a Christian church (after the conversion of the Norse world in this period), and legal and social institutions modeled on those of Iceland and Norway.

Both Norse Greenland and the western voyages depended on a small number of ships and a small population. Timber, iron, and many manufactured goods had to be imported across hundreds of kilometres of open sea. These facts of scale underlie most of the chapter's explanations. Chapter 36 has shown how densely connected Indigenous North America was; this chapter shows a European outlier that, by comparison, had a very thin network of its own. The continuing story of Indigenous interaction with sustained European empires begins in Chapter 38.

Section 37.1

The First Atlantic Encounter in Continental Perspective

Older narratives of "exploration" often treat a European arrival as the moment a region enters history. This textbook has argued from Chapter 1 that such framing misleads. The Norse case is a useful test of that argument, because it is the one case before 1492 in which Europeans demonstrably reached North America and stayed, for a time, in more than one place.

Two Norse presences must be kept distinct. The first, in Greenland, was a settled colony of farms, churches, and chieftaincies lasting from about the late tenth century until the fifteenth. Greenland is geographically part of North America, but its Norse communities looked east, toward Iceland and Norway, for church authority, trade, and political connection. The second presence was the series of western voyages from Greenland to lands that the sagas call Helluland, Markland, and Vinland. The only site on that western side of Davis Strait that archaeologists have securely identified as Norse is L'Anse aux Meadows. It was small and brief, and it was never a farming colony.

Treating these as one story of "Vikings in America" flattens important differences of scale, duration, and evidence. It also tempts students to regard the western voyages as a first act in a drama culminating in Columbus. The evidence does not support that reading. The Norse western voyages left no institutional descendant, and the later expansion of Spain, France, England, and the Netherlands drew on different motives, technologies, and political systems. Chapter 38 begins that story.

For the history of Indigenous North America, the Norse episode matters in three ways. It shows Indigenous peoples of the eastern Arctic and Atlantic Canada as already established actors whom outsiders encountered. It provides some of the earliest written descriptions, however biased, of Indigenous people by Europeans. And it illustrates that early European presence did not automatically equal colonization.

Section 37.2

Greenland Before the Norse

Humans reached Greenland several thousand years before Norse settlers did. Archaeologists group the earliest peoples under the label Paleo-Inuit, a modern scholarly convention covering a series of related archaeological traditions that developed in the Arctic after about 2500 BCE. In Greenland these include the Saqqaq tradition in the west and Independence traditions in the far north. Later came the Dorset tradition, which is known from many sites across the eastern Arctic and persisted in parts of Greenland well into the first millennium AD and, in the far northwest, into the early second millennium. Chapter 11 discusses these Arctic adaptations in detail.

These traditions are known primarily from archaeological evidence: dwellings, hearths, stone and bone tools, seal, caribou, and musk-ox remains, and patterns of settlement along coasts and fjords. Archaeologists identify and name them by technology and style. Such names do not tell us what these people called themselves, what languages they spoke, or how they governed themselves, and the terms should be read with the cautions of Chapter 1 in mind.

What matters for the Norse story is that the Greenland to which the Norse came was not a landscape without human history. The fjords of southwestern Greenland where the Norse established farms appear to have been without a resident Indigenous population at the time of settlement, though earlier Paleo-Inuit sites occur in the region. A twelfth-century Icelandic text, Ari Þorgilsson's Íslendingabók, reports that the settlers found traces of earlier inhabitants: remains of dwellings, fragments of skin boats, and stone implements. The same text compares these traces to what the people of Vinland, the skrælingar, used. The Norse therefore knew, from the outset, that Greenland had been peopled before them.

The point has an analytical use. The southwest of Greenland was not empty because it had never been inhabited, but because earlier peoples had moved on or disappeared by processes archaeologists are still working to understand. The later arrival of Thule-tradition communities in the thirteenth century (Section 37.15) was therefore the return of Indigenous settlement to a region the Norse had occupied alone for some generations.

Section 37.3

From Iceland to Greenland: Norse Atlantic Expansion

Iceland was settled from the late ninth century, a process the Icelandic tradition calls landnám, "land-taking." The settlers were mostly Scandinavian-speaking people, many from western Norway, together with people from the Gaelic-speaking zones of the British Isles and Ireland, some of whom were enslaved. Within a few generations Iceland had a population of farmers governed by local chieftains and a general assembly, and a literate culture that produced the sagas.

Icelandic tradition places the settlement of Greenland in the generation about a century after Iceland's. The sagas and Íslendingabók agree that a man named Erik the Red, who had been outlawed from Iceland after violent disputes, explored the southwest coast of Greenland during his years of exile and then returned to recruit settlers. According to Eirik the Red's Saga, a large fleet set out and only part of it arrived. The sagas also say that Erik chose the name "Greenland" to attract settlers. That claim should be treated as a saga tradition, not as demonstrated motive.

The conventional date for the first settlement is about 985, but this date derives from later written tradition and is approximate. Archaeological dating of the earliest farms is broadly consistent with settlement in the late tenth century. Radiocarbon dates cannot, in most cases, decide between 985 and a few years either side.

Why did people move? Sources point to several overlapping pressures and opportunities: competition for land in Iceland as the best pastures were taken, the prestige and leadership opportunities available to a successful colonist, the lure of natural resources such as walrus, seal, and caribou, and the practical knowledge that Greenland's fjord heads offered grass. Relatively mild climatic conditions in the North Atlantic around this time may have helped, but the effect should not be overstated. Greenland's farms were always marginal.

The settlers brought Christianity in this same period. The sagas credit Leif Eriksson with introducing the faith and describe church-building by his mother, Þjóðhild. The details belong to saga tradition, but archaeology confirms that churches and churchyards were a feature of Norse Greenland.

Section 37.4

Eastern Settlement and Western Settlement

Approximately 560 Norse farm sites are recorded in the Eastern and Middle Settlements (the Middle Settlement is a small cluster usually grouped with the Eastern) and around 75 in the Western Settlement. Medieval descriptions counted roughly 90 farms in the Western Settlement, a difference that reflects site survival, identification, and change over time. Not all farms were occupied simultaneously, and estimates of the total population at its peak vary widely, generally falling in the low thousands at most.

Farms were built of stone and turf, with long dwelling houses, byres for livestock, storehouses, and enclosed infields. The settlements were not socially uniform. A small number of large estates had substantial halls, large byres capable of housing many cattle, and churches, while most farms were far smaller. Brattahlíð, associated by tradition with Erik, and Garðar were among the wealthiest. This hierarchy mattered economically, because the largest farms were best placed to organize hay production, hunting expeditions, and trade, and politically, because their owners were the most likely local leaders.

Churches were a prominent feature of settlement. Sixteen Eastern Settlement farms alone have recorded associated churches, mostly small buildings of turf and stone set within churchyards. The pattern, in which churches stand on or beside major farms, suggests that wealthy farmers sponsored and perhaps controlled them, as in Iceland and Norway. Garðar became the seat of a resident bishop in the early twelfth century (traditionally the 1120s), and its estate includes the remains of a substantial church. The well-preserved stone ruin at Hvalsey is the most visited of the surviving church buildings.

Political life combined local assemblies, the authority of chieftain-farmers, and the church. The relationship with Norway was formalized in the thirteenth century. A UNESCO nomination drawing on medieval records reports that news reached Norway in 1261 that Greenlanders had sworn allegiance to the Norwegian crown. Later sources represent the chronology somewhat differently, which is why some accounts give dates as late as 1264. Whatever the exact sequence, Norwegian royal interest in Greenland's trade increased in this period.

The settlements were connected to Iceland and Norway by trade and church ties, but they were also, in practice, largely self-governing for long periods because sailing was difficult. Contact depended on a small number of ships, and the written record for Greenland after about 1300 is sparse. Much of what historians know about the later settlements therefore comes from archaeology, with a few later annal entries and letters.

Section 37.5

Farming at the Edge of the Arctic

The Greenland Norse brought a North Atlantic farming system that depended on livestock: sheep and goats, cattle, and, in the early period, pigs, together with dogs and horses. Hay was the foundation of this economy. Summer fields near the farm were cut to feed animals through the winter, and in Greenland the short growing season made hay yields precarious. Cattle, which require much more winter feed than sheep or goats, were concentrated on larger estates and appear to have been a mark of status.

The Norse economy in Greenland was nevertheless never purely agrarian. Seals, especially migratory harp seals, caribou, fish, and seabirds were important from early on. Stable isotope analysis of Norse skeletons indicates that marine foods formed a growing share of the diet over time. These findings show an adaptive community, not a failed attempt to reproduce Iceland. At the same time, the Norse did not adopt many Inuit hunting technologies, such as the kayak and the toggling harpoon, which Chapter 35 describes and which were highly effective in winter and open-water sealing.

Farming also changed the landscape. Cutting turf and grazing livestock affected soils and vegetation around settlements, and archaeological and environmental records show evidence of such changes. Scholars debate how far this contributed to the later difficulties of the settlements, and they now generally avoid treating environmental damage as a single explanation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in Norse Greenland

Most Greenland Norse lived in farm households that combined family members, dependants, and probably servants or laborers, with a workload governed by the seasons. Summer meant haymaking, tending animals at upland pastures, and long hunting trips. Winter meant indoor work: spinning, weaving, mending, tending stock in the byre, and processing dairy products. Spindle whorls and loom weights found on farm sites testify to the importance of textile work, much of it done by women. Cloth was both a household need and a trade good.

Some of the most evocative evidence comes from the churchyard at Herjolfsnes, where burials in cold soil preserved fragments of clothing. The garments, from the later period of the settlements, show that Greenlanders continued to follow some European fashions even as they adapted to local conditions. Skeletons from cemeteries provide evidence about diet, work, and health, although interpretation of stress or malnutrition in individual remains varies and cannot be generalized to a whole population. Churchyards also mark a community identity tied to Christian burial. Such evidence is richest for farm households and weakest for the poorest members of society, who left fewer traces.

Section 37.6

Walrus Ivory, Hunting, and the Greenland Economy

Greenland could not supply everything its society required. Iron, grain, and many manufactured and church goods had to be imported, and the Greenlanders paid for them with goods that medieval European markets valued. The most important was walrus ivory. Tusks, and apparently also the skulls to which they were attached, were exported in quantity. Other exports included polar bear skins, walrus-hide rope, and live gyrfalcons, which European elites prized.

Timber requires more careful statement than older accounts gave it. Analyses of wood from Norse sites indicate that ordinary households relied primarily on driftwood and native woody vegetation such as willow, birch, and juniper. Imported timber from Europe and North America was more available to elite farms and for needs that local wood could not meet. Greenland was therefore not wholly dependent on imported wood, but larger and higher-quality timbers were a real advantage, and North American timber, discussed in Section 37.7, was one valuable source of them.

Obtaining export goods required organized hunting expeditions to northern grounds that the sources call Norðrsetur, traditionally located around Disko Bay, far north of the settlements. Hunters made long summer voyages and returned with tusks and hides. This was a large undertaking for farmers on small estates and made seasonal specialization part of the economy. Chieftains and the church appear to have controlled much of the trade, and papal records of the later thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries record tithes paid by Greenland largely in walrus ivory.

Recent genomic sourcing has widened this picture considerably. A 2024 study traced later Norse Greenland walrus ivory especially to the North Water Polynya (Pikialasorsuaq) region between northwestern Greenland and Canada, with some material possibly from the Foxe Basin. The study argues that later Norse ivory procurement therefore reached much deeper into the High Arctic than Disko Bay and may have involved both direct hunting and exchange with Thule Inuit, and perhaps with earlier Tuniit populations. The method identifies where the walruses lived, not who killed them, so the balance between hunting and exchange remains an inference. The findings connect the European ivory economy to Indigenous Arctic networks and show that the two were not parallel stories. They feed directly into the discussion of contact in Section 37.15.

This system tied Greenland to a European demand it did not control. Walrus ivory competed with other sources of ivory, and the commercial context in Norway and the wider North Atlantic changed over the fourteenth century. Historians now give increasing weight to the weakening ivory trade in explanations of Norse Greenland's decline (Section 37.17). The point for now is structural: a society that relies on a single high-value export to buy essential imports is exposed when conditions change.

Section 37.7

Voyages Beyond Greenland: Helluland, Markland, and Vinland

The Norse sagas describe voyages west from Greenland to three lands. Helluland ("flat-stone land") is described as a barren country of rock and ice. Markland ("forest land") is low, wooded, and has white sandy beaches. Vinland, usually rendered "Wineland," is a place of abundant resources, where grapes or self-sown wheat are said to grow. Scholars commonly associate Helluland with Baffin Island and Markland with Labrador, but these are interpretations of saga geography, not identifications supported by inscriptions or maps, and they remain approximate. The location and even the meaning of Vinland are debated. Some scholars understand the first element as "wine," others as a word for meadow or pasture, and the saga descriptions of vines may be partly literary.

One practical motive stands out. As Section 37.6 explained, ordinary Greenland households could make do with driftwood and native woody plants, but larger, higher-quality timber was valuable and mostly had to be imported. The forests of Labrador were the nearest source. This helps explain why Markland features prominently in the sources and why the Icelandic annals record, under the year 1347, a small Greenland ship whose crew had sailed to Markland and was blown to Iceland on the return. That entry suggests that voyages to the Labrador coast, probably for timber, continued sporadically long after the Vinland voyages of the sagas. It does not tell us whether the Norse established any settlement there.

Section 37.8

The Vinland Sagas as Historical Evidence

Two Icelandic sagas contain the principal narratives of the western voyages: The Saga of the Greenlanders (Grœnlendinga saga) and Eirik the Red's Saga (Eiríks saga rauða). Both were composed in Iceland, most likely in the thirteenth century, some two centuries after the events they describe, and both survive in manuscripts of the fourteenth century or later. Before being written down they were presumably transmitted orally, perhaps in more than one form.

The two sagas agree on certain features: voyages from Greenland to lands to the west, a place of wood and good pasture, encounters with local people, and eventual abandonment. They disagree on important matters, including who first sighted the western lands, who led the principal expedition, how many voyages occurred, and what happened. In the first saga, a Greenland-bound trader named Bjarni Herjólfsson sights unfamiliar coasts and Leif Eriksson later follows his route. In the second, Leif is blown off course on his way from Norway and discovers Vinland by accident, and a settlement attempt is led by Thorfinn Karlsefni. These differences are one reason historians do not read either as a straightforward report.

Saga evidence has particular strengths and weaknesses. It preserves genealogies, place-names, and episodes that may rest on older oral memory. It reflects Icelandic interest in Greenlandic families and their prestige. It is also shaped by narrative conventions, including set scenes of conflict and the tendency to attribute actions to named heroes. Sagas can also be influenced by the concerns of their own time of composition and by earlier literary models. Historians therefore use them as sources for Norse memory and Norse attitudes, and cautiously as sources for events.

Other written evidence helps in limited ways. The German cleric Adam of Bremen, writing in the 1070s, mentions Vinland, reporting information he attributed to the Danish king; this shows that the name was known outside Iceland in the eleventh century. The Icelandic annals mention later Greenland voyages and events. None of this, however, describes the voyages in detail.

The crucial lesson is that the sagas could not, on their own, prove that Norse people reached North America. The proof came from archaeology. A saga is a historical source, but a historian reading the sagas alone would have to remain uncertain whether Vinland was a real place, a partly legendary one, or a composite of several voyages.

Historical Vocabulary
Saga

Definition: An Old Norse–Icelandic prose narrative, in this context usually a medieval account of the lives of Icelandic families and leaders, with episodes drawn from older oral tradition and written down by Icelandic authors.

Historical context: The Vinland sagas belong to a larger body of saga literature composed mostly in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. They were written in a Christian, literate Iceland, long after the pagan and early Christian world they describe.

Why it matters: Sagas preserve valuable material about medieval Norse society and memory, but they are literary works, not eyewitness reports.

Potential misunderstanding: Students sometimes treat a saga either as pure fiction or as literal history. Historians do neither. They evaluate each episode against other evidence, as they would with a medieval chronicle.

Primary Source Window

Eirik the Red's Saga: Exchange and Fear in Vinland

Creator: Anonymous Icelandic author(s) · Date: Composed probably in the thirteenth century; surviving in medieval manuscripts of later date · Type: Icelandic saga, a literary narrative drawing on oral tradition

Context: The saga recounts the settlement of Greenland by Erik the Red and a later expedition to Vinland led by Thorfinn Karlsefni. It was written in Iceland for an audience interested in the founding families of Greenland and Iceland. It is not a contemporary eyewitness record.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): In one episode, the Norse expedition meets a group of local people who arrive by boat. The two sides begin to exchange goods. The local people are keen to obtain red cloth, and in return they offer furs and grey pelts. The Norse give them strips of cloth, and as the cloth supply dwindles they cut the strips narrower. In another episode, a bull belonging to the Norse suddenly bellows. The visitors are terrified and flee, and the encounter later turns violent.

This is a summary of episodes in the saga in the editor's words. It reproduces no part of any translation.

Analysis: The passage portrays trade as the first form of contact, with both sides interested in goods the other had. It describes the visitors as people with their own boats, goods, and preferences. It also reflects the storyteller's interest in mishap and conflict. The sequence from trade to fear to violence is a literary pattern as well as a possible memory.

Limitations: The saga was written long after the events and from a Norse viewpoint. It does not name the people encountered, does not say what language they spoke, and describes no Indigenous community by its own name. Details such as the cloth-trading and the bull scene cannot be independently confirmed. The saga cannot establish which Indigenous people the Norse met or where.

Perspective: The author represents an Icelandic Christian perspective. Indigenous perspectives are entirely absent from the saga.

Section 37.9

L'Anse aux Meadows: Archaeological Discovery

In 1960, the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad was guided by local residents around the northern tip of Newfoundland, on the Great Northern Peninsula. At the settlement of L'Anse aux Meadows, he was shown a group of low grass-covered mounds. Ingstad had been searching the coast for traces of Norse settlement. Together with his wife, the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad, he led excavations in 1961–1964 and 1966–1968 that uncovered the outlines of turf-walled buildings of a type known from Norse Greenland and Iceland. Parks Canada conducted further excavations from 1973 to 1976. The site was among the first to be inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, in 1978.

The remains consist of eight buildings in three groups, including several large dwelling halls with multiple rooms and hearths, and smaller structures used as workshops. The Norse identification rests on convergence of independent evidence, not on the sagas. The architecture closely resembles Norse buildings in Greenland. A hearth area and slag show that bog iron, naturally occurring iron ore from the surrounding wetlands, was smelted at the site, and a forge was used to produce iron nails and rivets. Iron rivets and nails were also found, along with fragments consistent with boat repair. Other finds include a bronze ring-headed pin of a Norse type used to fasten cloaks, a soapstone spindle whorl used in spinning, and a bone needle. Spinning implements are significant because they show that women, not only men, may have been present at the site, though the evidence is limited.

Equally important is the evidence about people who had been there before and after. Parks Canada notes that Indigenous peoples used the area for thousands of years, and several distinct Indigenous occupations are known at the site, though the evidence indicates that none was present during the Norse occupation itself. The site thus sits within a longer Indigenous history of the area, and archaeologists distinguish carefully between the layers.

The findings also include butternuts and a burl of butternut wood. Butternut does not grow naturally this far north; it grows farther south, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence region and beyond. Their presence suggests that Norse voyagers explored south of L'Anse aux Meadows, a conclusion that supports the sagas' notion of voyages to places farther away. It cannot, however, identify those places.

The excavation shows what archaeology can and cannot achieve. It demonstrated beyond reasonable doubt that Norse people built and used the site. It did not, by itself, say when, for how long, or under what saga name the place appears.

Section 37.10

AD 1021: Dating the Norse Presence

For decades the date of the site depended on radiocarbon dating of charcoal and other organic material, which gave a range of probabilities. Typical ranges clustered around the late tenth to early eleventh century but could not pinpoint a year. They were consistent with the sagas' placement of the voyages near the year 1000 but did not confirm any specific saga account.

In 2021 a team of researchers published a result that dated Norse activity to a specific year. They examined three pieces of wood that bore marks of having been cut by metal tools. Because the tools were metal and no Indigenous people at that time had iron tools of this kind in the area, the cutting is attributed to the Norse. The researchers used the tree rings in each piece, and a distinctive feature in the rings, to determine when the trees were cut.

The feature in question is a short-lived spike in atmospheric radiocarbon in the year AD 993, one of a small number of such events known as Miyake events. They are thought to be caused by bursts of cosmic radiation, and they leave a signal in the carbon in tree rings that grew in the year of the event. Researchers identified the 993 signal in the rings of the three wood samples. Counting the rings from the 993 ring to the outermost ring on each piece gave the year in which the tree was cut. In each case the answer was 1021.

Section 37.11

What Was L'Anse aux Meadows For?

A single dated year raises a question of function. L'Anse aux Meadows has features that argue against a farming colony. There are no byres or evidence of significant livestock, no substantial middens, and no cemetery. The workshops, forge, and boat-repair evidence suggest a place concerned with maintenance and making, not agriculture.

The layout is informative. The site consists of eight buildings in three complexes, each centered on a large multi-roomed hall with hearths and associated smaller structures. Parks Canada's interpretation is that the halls could have housed up to roughly 90 people, though not necessarily all at once. A smithy, a carpentry area, and a boat-repair area show that specialized work was done on site, and the scale suggests an organized expedition, not a single ship's crew. The evidence for the presence of women, in the form of spinning implements, is limited but significant, because it points away from a purely military or trading party.

Parks Canada describes the site as a base camp or gateway from which resources could be gathered, with occupation probably lasting only a few years. Interpretations include a temporary exploration base, a ship repair station, a staging point for voyages farther south, and a place to obtain timber for Greenland. These are not mutually exclusive. Butternuts and a butternut burl found at the site show that either the Norse traveled farther south, or that goods from farther south reached them, which supports the view that L'Anse aux Meadows was a gateway rather than a final destination.

Seasonality is harder to establish. Halls with hearths imply that people were present through cold weather at least in some years, but the evidence does not decide whether occupation was continuous or repeated, and the 1021 date shows presence in that year without ruling out earlier or later visits. Researchers have also noted that relatively few valuable objects were left behind, consistent with an orderly abandonment instead of a destructive attack. Estimates of duration have ranged from a few years to roughly a decade, and the evidence does not permit certainty.

Researchers disagree about whether L'Anse aux Meadows can be identified with the "Leif's Booths" of the sagas or with Vinland. Students should therefore keep apart two questions: whether the Norse were at L'Anse aux Meadows (they were), and which saga place it corresponds to (uncertain). Searches for additional Norse sites, such as one tested at Point Rosee on Newfoundland's southwest coast in the 2010s, produced no confirmed Norse evidence, a reminder that apparent traces can have natural explanations.

Section 37.12

Indigenous Peoples of Newfoundland and Labrador Around AD 1000

The lands the Norse reached were home to Indigenous peoples with long histories. In Newfoundland and Labrador, archaeologists identify a sequence of traditions. The Maritime Archaic tradition, known from burial sites such as Port au Choix on Newfoundland's Great Northern Peninsula, flourished thousands of years earlier. Paleo-Inuit traditions followed, including Groswater and Dorset. Provincial archaeology currently dates Dorset occupation in Labrador to roughly 2,400 to 800 years ago, but on Newfoundland itself to roughly 2,000 to 1,000 years ago. Around AD 1000, therefore, Dorset occupation of Newfoundland was at or near its end, while Dorset communities persisted longer farther north in Labrador.

Other peoples occupied the same region at the same time. Archaeologists have traditionally called their traditions "Recent Indian," a term some now replace with "Recent First Nations," because the traditions are ancestral to First Nations peoples. In Newfoundland the best known is the Little Passage complex, usually dated from about AD 1000 to the sixteenth century, and its continuity with the historic Beothuk is quite strongly supported. In Labrador the Point Revenge complex occupied the central coast over roughly the last two thousand years before the colonial period, according to one provincial summary. Thule-tradition Inuit communities expanded into northern Labrador somewhat later, in the following centuries.

These are archaeological categories, defined by tool types, house forms, and settlement patterns, not by names the people used. They should not be mapped casually onto later nations, and the case of Point Revenge shows why. For decades, many archaeologists, including those who worked with the Innu, understood Point Revenge people as ancestors of the Innu who, around the time of European contact, moved into the Labrador interior. On 17 March 2026, provincial archaeologists published a paper in North American Archaeologist arguing instead that Point Revenge people did not disappear into the interior but returned to Newfoundland in the early sixteenth century, and that they were essentially Beothuk. The Innu Nation rejects that interpretation, calling it an erasure of Innu history, and holds that Point Revenge materials belong to Innu ancestry, a position grounded in Innu knowledge and oral history. Archaeologists at Memorial University publicly supported the Innu Nation in June 2026, and on 4 September 2026 the provincial government committed to an independent audit of the archaeological record concerning the Innu.

This textbook does not treat the question as settled. It is a live dispute, with new evidence and review still to come, and the interpretation of an archaeological tradition is entangled with living communities' claims to history and place. The point for this chapter is that Norse voyagers who reached Labrador and northern Newfoundland around AD 1000 met, or could have met, peoples whose archaeological traditions are interpreted in more than one way, and whose descendants, including Inuit, Innu, Mi'kmaq, and others, hold their own accounts of the region.

Parks Canada's research on L'Anse aux Meadows indicates that Indigenous peoples used the broader area for millennia but that there is no evidence of Indigenous occupation of the site itself during the years the Norse were there. That does not mean no Indigenous people were nearby. The sagas describe meetings with local people, and the archaeological record shows peoples moving and living along the coasts. The Norse outpost sat in an inhabited region, not a vacant one. Evidence for Indigenous history in this period comes mainly from archaeology and, for later centuries, from Indigenous oral traditions and written records of European observers. Recognizing the limits of that evidence is part of treating Indigenous history on its own terms, a principle treated further in Chapter 145 on knowledge sovereignty.

Section 37.13

"Skrælingar": Trade, Conflict, and the Problem of Identification

The Norse sources refer to the Indigenous people they met with a single word, skrælingar (singular skrælingr). It appears in the sagas' accounts of Vinland, in Íslendingabók's reference to the people of Vinland, and in later medieval texts about Greenland. Its origin is uncertain, and some scholars suspect a derogatory sense. What the word means in practice is a Norse category covering people who were not Norse, not Christian, and not familiar. It does not correspond to a single Indigenous nation, language, or culture.

This has a practical consequence. The Norse used the term for Indigenous people in Vinland and, later, for Inuit in Greenland. It is possible that the Norse travelers on the western voyages met different Indigenous populations at different places and times: ancestral First Nations groups (traditionally called "Recent Indian") in Newfoundland and southern Labrador, for instance, and Dorset populations persisting farther north in Labrador. Historians cannot say with confidence which people the sagas have in mind, and efforts to identify the skrælingar of Vinland with a specific modern nation go beyond the evidence.

The saga narratives of the encounters follow a recurring pattern: curiosity and trade at first, then misunderstanding, fear, and violence. The Norse describe the people as arriving in skin boats and as trading furs for cloth. They also describe a battle in which the skrælingar use a weapon the sagas describe in a way that scholars have interpreted in several ways, and in which Norse men die. The sagas portray Freydís, Leif's sister, as unafraid in the face of an attack. These narratives have the marks of storytelling, but violence is plausible. Armed Europeans entering lands where Indigenous people lived and hunted could meet with suspicion, and neither side had a shared language.

Historical Vocabulary
Skrælingar

Definition: The Old Norse word applied by the Norse to the Indigenous peoples they met in North America and, later, in Greenland.

Historical context: The word occurs in the Vinland sagas, in Íslendingabók, and in Icelandic annals. Etymology is uncertain.

Why it matters: The term tells historians how the Norse categorized outsiders. It is an exonym, and it conceals the diversity of the people it names.

Potential misunderstanding: Modern writers sometimes use "Skraelings" as if it named a particular nation. It did not. It reflects Norse perception, and a single saga passage might refer to a group from one place or time and a later source to another.

Section 37.14

Archaeological Evidence for Contact Beyond L'Anse aux Meadows

L'Anse aux Meadows is secure. Other claimed Norse traces in North America vary in quality, and a careful historian ranks them by strength of evidence. At the top, Norse objects found in Indigenous contexts in Greenland, such as iron and other items at Thule-tradition sites in the northwest, are well documented. They show contact, though not its character.

In the Canadian Arctic, scattered reports of small Norse-type objects, such as metal fragments, in Indigenous sites have been published. Some may have arrived through trade networks from Greenland, passing from hand to hand without direct Norse presence. One of the most discussed cases comes from excavations at Tanfield Valley on southeastern Baffin Island, where researchers reported spun yarn, whetstones, and other items interpreted by some as evidence of Norse contact with Dorset-tradition occupants. Others have questioned the dating, the identification, or the interpretation.

At the other end of the evidentiary scale are claims that have been discredited or lack support. The Kensington Runestone, found in Minnesota in the 1890s, is regarded by the great majority of scholars as a nineteenth-century creation. A coin of medieval Norwegian origin found at a Native site in Maine has drawn attention, but how it came to be there is disputed and does not demonstrate a Norse settlement. Searches in other locations, including Point Rosee, have not produced confirmed Norse evidence.

Historical Debate

How Far Did the Norse Travel Beyond L'Anse aux Meadows?

The question: Did Norse voyagers range widely along the northeastern coasts of North America and interact with Indigenous peoples at multiple places, or was their activity restricted to a very small area?

Interpretation A — Wide-ranging contact: Some researchers argue that evidence such as the butternuts at L'Anse aux Meadows, the Icelandic annal of a Markland voyage, and possible Norse objects in Arctic sites indicates that Norse contact extended over a wide area and lasted for centuries, in part through exchange with Indigenous communities.
Interpretation B — A narrow archaeological footprint: Other scholars hold that only L'Anse aux Meadows is securely established, that most reported finds have alternative explanations, and that Indigenous trade networks may have moved small amounts of Norse material without the Norse themselves traveling.
Interpretation C — Graded confidence: A third approach avoids a single verdict and classifies each claim by the quality of evidence. Norse-Indigenous contact in Greenland is documented, L'Anse aux Meadows is confirmed, voyages to Markland are plausible on the strength of the annal entry, and many Arctic claims remain unresolved.

Evidence: The debate involves excavated structures, tree-ring and radiocarbon dating, the identification of materials such as yarn and metal, and the reading of saga and annal references.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the identification of materials, the dating of contexts, the possibility of indirect exchange, and how far later written sources can be applied to earlier periods. One review of claims for Norse-Dorset contact concluded that the Dorset tradition probably owed nothing to Norse contact, while acknowledging some possible exchange.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians describe early contact: as a single outpost, as occasional voyages, or as a sustained set of interactions with Indigenous peoples. It also affects how much agency is attributed to Indigenous trade networks.

Student task: Choose two reported finds and rank them by strength of evidence. Explain what further evidence would raise or lower your confidence in each.

Section 37.15

Norse and Thule-Inuit Encounters in Greenland

The longest period of Norse-Indigenous contact took place not in Vinland but in Greenland. Chapter 35 explains how Thule-tradition communities, ancestors of modern Inuit, spread east across the Arctic and reached northwest Greenland in or around the thirteenth century, bringing a technology of kayaks, umiaks, toggling harpoons, and dog sleds particularly suited to sea-ice and marine hunting. Over the following generations their communities extended to central and southern parts of the island. Norse and Inuit therefore overlapped in Greenland for roughly two centuries.

Evidence for the relationship comes from several sources. Archaeologists have found Norse-made goods, such as iron, in Thule-tradition sites, and a few Inuit-made objects, including carved figures that appear to depict Europeans, have been found as well. Icelandic annals record, for the year 1379, an attack by skrælingar on Norse Greenlanders, in which a number of men were killed and others taken captive. A fourteenth-century account attributed to a Norse visitor, Ívar Bárðarson, reports finding the Western Settlement without people and with the skrælingar in possession of it, but the text survives only in later copies, and its reliability is debated. Nineteenth-century collections of Inuit oral tradition preserve stories of earlier peoples and of the Norse, though their value as evidence for medieval events is difficult to judge.

The sourcing of walrus ivory (Section 37.6) adds an economic dimension. If later Norse ivory came substantially from the North Water Polynya region, as a 2024 genomic study argues, then Norse procurement reached waters where Thule-tradition communities were established, and perhaps where earlier Tuniit populations remained. The Norse may have hunted there themselves, or acquired ivory through exchange with Indigenous hunters, or both; the evidence cannot tell which. Either possibility connects the Norse economy to Indigenous Arctic networks and makes the relationship less a matter of occasional encounters than of structural entanglement. It also raises the possibility that Indigenous peoples were suppliers whose decisions affected the Greenland trade, a reversal of the usual picture of passive local peoples.

Taken together, the evidence suggests both peaceful and hostile contact: exchange of goods and information, along with episodic conflict. Historians disagree about how frequent each was and how much influence the contact had on the end of Norse Greenland. There is little reliable evidence for large-scale warfare that destroyed the settlements. The Norse hunting grounds in the north overlapped with areas of growing Thule presence, and it seems likely that competition for marine resources played some role, but the weight of that factor is uncertain.

Multiple Perspectives

Meeting in Greenland, c. 1250–1400

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing what the evidence suggests about each group's circumstances. They are not quotations from named individuals.

From the perspective of a Norse farming household: Newcomers who hunt seals expertly from skin boats could be competitors for northern grounds, potential trading partners, or a source of danger. Household security depended on hay, livestock, and annual voyages, and farms near the edge of settlement were the most exposed.
From the perspective of a Thule-tradition hunting community: The southern coast of Greenland contained abandoned structures, unfamiliar farms, and people with iron and cloth. Settlements of livestock keepers were an unusual presence in a landscape their own technology was designed to exploit, and their goods, especially iron, were valuable.
From the perspective of church and Norwegian crown authorities: Greenland was a distant tributary province that supplied ivory and furs and had to be kept in the faith. Reports of attacks by non-Christian peoples were mostly of concern as signs of vulnerability.
From the perspective of modern archaeologists: The evidence is uneven. Norse written sources describe Inuit from outside, Inuit oral traditions were collected centuries later, and excavated objects show contact without explaining its terms.

Note on power: The groups did not have equal access to resources or equal knowledge of the environment. Thule communities were well adapted to Arctic conditions, whereas the Norse depended on a distant market and on livestock.

Section 37.16

Why Vinland Did Not Become a Permanent Norse Colony

If the Norse found a land of wood and good pasture, why did they not settle it permanently? Several factors operated together. Distance and cost were the first. The sea crossing was long, and the Greenland population from which colonists could come was small, a few thousand at most. Every voyage depended on a limited number of ships, and replacing losses was difficult. Greenland itself, though marginal, offered pasture and hunting grounds closer to home. For a small society, committing people and ships to an outpost far away had high costs and uncertain benefits.

Relations with Indigenous peoples probably contributed, though not decisively. The sagas portray hostility, and it is plausible that conflict made a small group wary. But the archaeological record at L'Anse aux Meadows shows no sign of a destructive attack, and the saga accounts are literary. The picture of the Norse driven out by Native resistance is too simple. The better statement is that a small, isolated foothold, uncertain of local friendship and distant from reinforcement, was hard to sustain.

Logistics and demography reinforce the point. A Greenland population in the low thousands had to supply farm labor, the northern hunting expeditions on which its trade depended, and any western voyages, all with a limited number of ships and experienced sailors. Each person committed to a western outpost was unavailable for hay, hunting, or the ivory voyages that paid for imports. There is no evidence that a royal or church authority sponsored a colonizing venture in the west, or that Iceland or Norway supplied settlers for one. Expeditions appear to have been organized by Greenland chieftains and their followers, with the resources of private households.

Resources also shaped the decision. If the aim was timber, voyages to Markland could supply it without a permanent settlement, and the 1347 annal entry suggests that is how it was done. L'Anse aux Meadows has no byres or other signs of a farming economy, and no evidence that livestock were brought. A farming colony would have required exactly the investment that a small society could least afford.

Cause and Effect

Why Norse Presence in Northeastern North America Remained Brief

Long-term causes

A small population base in Greenland, a settlement economy dependent on imported iron and timber, and the great distances of North Atlantic sailing.

Short-term causes

High logistical cost of supplying a distant outpost, available resources closer to Greenland, and uncertain relations with local Indigenous peoples.

Immediate context

Expeditions with limited crews, apparently aimed at exploration and obtaining timber, with no evidence of an organized plan for permanent farming settlement.

Event

A short occupation of L'Anse aux Meadows followed by apparently orderly abandonment, with later voyages to Markland possibly continuing.

Immediate effects

No continuing Norse settlement in the Americas outside Greenland and no recorded continuous institutions linking the voyages with later Atlantic crossings.

Long-term consequences

Memory of Vinland survived in Icelandic texts, but the later European colonization of the continent began from different origins, as Chapter 38 explains.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the outcome. Distance, scale, resources, and human relationships all mattered, and historians weigh them differently.

Section 37.17

The End of Norse Greenland and the Limits of the First Atlantic Crossing

The Greenland settlements themselves ended, and how they ended has been a subject of long scholarly discussion. The Western Settlement was abandoned in the fourteenth century, apparently by the middle of the century. The Eastern Settlement lasted longer. The last datable written record of its community is a marriage at Hvalsey church on 16 September 1408, involving Icelanders and mentioned in later Icelandic documents. Archaeological evidence suggests the settlement continued to some point in the fifteenth century. There is no contemporary account of a final event.

Current scholarship treats the end as multi-causal, and recent research has changed the environmental part of the picture. Older accounts emphasized late-medieval cooling. A high-resolution 2022 reconstruction from near the Eastern Settlement found no substantial local temperature decline during the Norse occupation, but instead a persistent drying trend that probably reduced hay production. A 2023 study added another mechanism: local relative sea level rose by as much as roughly 3.3 metres in parts of southwestern Greenland, with modeling estimating about 204 square kilometres of inundation, which put additional pressure on coastal farms. Environmental pressures therefore included drying, changing sea ice and growing conditions, and local sea-level rise, not simple cooling alone.

Economic change was equally important. As Section 37.6 explained, the Greenland economy depended on walrus ivory, and shifts in European markets weakened that trade, a factor now given increasing weight. Demography and isolation mattered too: a small population with declining shipping contact had little margin. Environmental effects of farming, relations with Thule-tradition Inuit, and the effects of epidemic disease in Europe, such as the Black Death, have been proposed as additional pressures, with varying support. Some Greenlanders may also have left, and the evidence cannot settle individual fates.

Historians therefore reject the idea of a single solved cause. Interpretations that blame environmental ruin alone, or Inuit attack alone, or Norse inflexibility alone, each overlook evidence of adaptation and complexity. Norse Greenlanders did change their diet and economy, but ultimately their way of life depended on connections that weakened.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Greenland, c. 985–1500

What Changed

  • A Norse farming population settled the southwest, grew, and eventually disappeared, probably over about four and a half centuries
  • Thule-tradition Inuit communities expanded into the island and became its principal inhabitants
  • Greenland's connections with Europe weakened in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries
  • Diet and economy among the Norse shifted toward more marine resources

What Stayed the Same

  • Greenland remained an Arctic environment shaped by sea ice, seasonal resources, and long distances
  • Indigenous communities, with the knowledge and technology described in Chapter 35, continued to live in and use the island
  • Indigenous societies of Newfoundland, Labrador, and the eastern Arctic continued their own histories after the Norse left the continent

Why the pattern matters: Norse Greenland was a significant episode, but it was not a turning point in the history of Indigenous North America as a whole. The change lay largely within Greenland. Renewed European presence in Greenland in later centuries is a separate development outside this chapter.

The larger significance of Norse Greenland and Vinland lies in what it did not become. Norse contact with North America did not establish continuous European colonization. The voyages left traces in sagas, in archaeological sites, and in the memory of Greenlandic and Icelandic communities, but there is no straight institutional line from Vinland to Columbus. The sustained Atlantic expansion beginning in 1492, with its empires, extractive systems, and demographic effects, arose from different political and economic circumstances, and it is the subject of Chapter 38. Meanwhile, Indigenous peoples, whose territories the Norse had touched at the margins, continued to govern their own lives, as the chapters of Volume III have shown.

Section 37.18

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This section gathers the chapter's review apparatus. Students should use it to test whether they can distinguish firm evidence from tradition, separate Greenland from Vinland, and explain why a brief encounter did not become a colonial transformation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the first securely documented European presence in Indigenous North America and has argued that it was significant but limited. The Norse settled in Greenland, voyaged farther west, and left a small, dated outpost at L'Anse aux Meadows. They did not begin continuous European colonization.

Greenland had an Indigenous history before the Norse. Paleo-Inuit traditions had occupied parts of the island for thousands of years, and the Norse themselves knew that earlier people had lived there. The Norse settled the southwest in the late tenth century, probably around 985 by saga tradition, in two clusters of farms called the Eastern and Western Settlements.

Norse Greenland was a society of farm households with sheep, goats, and cattle, but it was also a society of hunters. Marine foods grew in importance. Its connection with Europe rested on exports, above all walrus ivory, which paid for imported iron, timber, and church goods. This dependence made the settlements vulnerable to changes in distant markets.

The sagas describe voyages to Helluland, Markland, and Vinland. They are valuable but late, literary, and discordant, and they could not by themselves prove that the Norse reached North America. That proof came from archaeology at L'Anse aux Meadows, where Norse-style buildings, iron working, and personal objects were found, and where butternuts suggest travel farther south.

Scientific dating anchored the site. The AD 993 radiocarbon spike, found in the tree rings of Norse-cut wood, shows that trees were felled at the site in 1021. The date is secure, but it does not reveal the length of occupation or the identity of the saga place the site corresponds to. The site looks like a base for exploration, repair, and timber, not a farming colony.

Indigenous peoples of Newfoundland and Labrador had long histories. Archaeologists identify several traditions around AD 1000, but those categories should not be mapped casually onto later nations. The Norse word skrælingar tells us about Norse categories and conceals Indigenous diversity. The sagas describe trade and violence, and the pattern is plausible but literary.

Evidence for Norse contact beyond L'Anse aux Meadows varies in strength. Contact in Greenland is documented, a Markland voyage is recorded in 1347, and some Arctic claims remain unresolved, while others, such as the Kensington Runestone, have been rejected. Historians therefore grade claims by evidence and avoid a simple yes-or-no answer.

In Greenland, Norse and Thule-tradition Inuit communities shared the island for generations. The evidence points to both exchange and conflict, with the 1379 annal entry documenting an attack. The Thule communities were well adapted to Arctic hunting, whereas the Norse relied on a distant market.

Vinland did not become a permanent colony because of distance, small population, high costs, nearer resources, and uncertain relations with Indigenous peoples. No single cause explains the result. Norse Greenland itself ended in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries through a combination of environmental pressures (drying and sea-level rise), weakening trade, isolation, and other pressures.

The chapter's lesson is that encounter is not the same as colonization. There is no institutional chain from Vinland to Columbus, and Indigenous societies continued their own histories. Chapter 38 turns to the voyages beginning in 1492, which initiated a different, sustained transformation of Indigenous America.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Norse
Scandinavian-speaking people and their descendants in the medieval North Atlantic, including the settlers of Iceland and Greenland.
Landnám
The Old Norse term for "land-taking," used for the settlement of Iceland and, by extension, other settlements.
Eastern Settlement
The larger cluster of Norse farms on Greenland's southwest coast; the name probably reflects the sailing route, not compass direction.
Western Settlement
The smaller cluster of Norse farms farther northwest, near modern Nuuk, abandoned in the fourteenth century.
Saga
An Old Norse–Icelandic prose narrative, composed in the medieval period and drawing on oral tradition.
Skrælingar
The Norse word for the Indigenous people they met in North America and Greenland; an exonym of uncertain origin.
Helluland
A saga name for a barren western land, commonly associated with Baffin Island by interpretation.
Markland
A saga name for a wooded western land, commonly associated with Labrador by interpretation.
Vinland
A saga name for a western land of rich resources whose location and meaning are debated.
Walrus ivory
Ivory from walrus tusks, the principal export of Norse Greenland.
Norðrsetur
The northern hunting grounds, traditionally located around Disko Bay but, on recent evidence, reaching farther north, to which the Norse sent seasonal expeditions.
Bog iron
Iron ore formed in wetlands, smelted by the Norse at L'Anse aux Meadows.
Dendrochronology
Dating by counting and matching tree rings.
Radiocarbon dating
A method of dating organic material by measuring the decay of carbon-14.
Miyake event
A sudden spike in atmospheric radiocarbon, recorded in tree rings, thought to result from bursts of cosmic radiation; the AD 993 event was used to date Norse wood.
Paleo-Inuit
A scholarly label for a series of early Arctic archaeological traditions, including Saqqaq, Independence, Groswater, and Dorset.
Dorset
A Paleo-Inuit archaeological tradition of the eastern Arctic.
Thule tradition
The archaeological tradition ancestral to modern Inuit, which spread east across the Arctic and reached Greenland in the thirteenth century.
Archaeological tradition
A grouping of sites by shared material culture; it does not identify a people's name, language, or political identity.
Tithe
A church levy, paid in Greenland largely in goods such as walrus ivory.
Exonym
A name applied to a people by outsiders.
Historiography
The history of historical interpretation, including changing readings of the sagas and the archaeology.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, such as "discovery of America," to an earlier period where it does not fit.

Chapter Reflection

The Norse chapter in North American history is short, well documented in places, and easy to misread. Its value lies partly in the story it tells and partly in the discipline it demands. Students must separate a literary saga from a dated piece of wood, a secure archaeological site from a speculative one, and a Norse category from an Indigenous identity.

The chapter also shows what Indigenous North America looked like from the Atlantic edge. Newfoundland, Labrador, Baffin Island, and Greenland were already places with human histories, and the Norse reached them on terms those histories shaped. The Norse needed timber, and Indigenous peoples knew the coasts. The Norse sought ivory and encountered Thule-tradition hunters expert in marine hunting.

What the chapter does not show is a beginning of colonization. The Norse foothold disappeared, and Indigenous societies went on. The sustained transformation of the continent would come through different channels, and Chapter 38 opens that history.

Taken with Chapter 36, the chapter makes a double point. Indigenous North America was a connected continent, and its first contact with Europeans was marginal, local, and in the end reversible. That makes the later arrival of sustained European empires, with their capacities for conquest and settlement, a break with what came before. Understanding the earlier episode helps students see how different the later one was.
