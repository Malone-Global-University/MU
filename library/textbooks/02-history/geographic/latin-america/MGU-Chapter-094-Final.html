The Return to Democracy

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Winning an Election, Not Yet the Country

On the night of 5 October 1988, a majority of Chileans voted "No" to eight more years of General Augusto Pinochet. Pinochet nonetheless remained commander-in-chief of the army until March 1998. That gap between an electoral victory and the dismantling of authoritarian power is the central problem of this chapter.

Between the late 1970s and the end of the 1990s, most of Latin America's military and authoritarian governments gave way to elected civilian rulers. In 1978 the Dominican Republic saw an opposition candidate take office after a count that soldiers had briefly interrupted. Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia followed between 1979 and 1982. Argentina's generals, discredited by defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War, lost power in 1983. Uruguay and Brazil returned to civilian government in 1985, Chile installed an elected civilian government in 1990, and Paraguay began a military-led transition in 1989 before inaugurating its first freely elected civilian president in 1993. Haiti, Nicaragua, Panama, Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador took their own, violent and externally entangled, routes. By 2000 Mexico's long-dominant party had also lost a presidential election. Historians and political scientists often call this a "wave," and the word is useful so long as it does not suggest a single event.

It was not a single event. Some militaries negotiated their departure, some were defeated or disgraced, some were pushed out by mass mobilization, and one Paraguayan dictator was overthrown by his own relative and fellow general. Each route left a different institutional inheritance. In Chile, the departing regime wrote a constitution, an amnesty, and a timetable that bound its successors. In Argentina, defeat gave civilians more leverage, and they used it to put senior officers on trial, but that leverage was not unlimited. In Brazil the military shaped the pace of its own exit so thoroughly that civilian rule arrived through an indirect election in 1985, and the first direct presidential vote did not take place until 1989.

The chapter therefore separates two achievements that are easily confused. The first is the restoration of elected civilian government: competitive elections, legal parties, a functioning legislature, and a peaceful transfer of office. This was a real and hard-won change, bought in many places at great human cost. The second is democratic accountability: civilian command over armed forces, courts able to judge powerful people, protection of rights for all citizens, and a public reckoning with state violence. That second achievement was longer, less certain, and in some countries remains incomplete.

Citizens were central to the story, and not only presidents and generals. The Mothers and Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo, Chile's Vicariate of Solidarity, Brazil's metalworkers, Uruguay's human-rights groups, and Indigenous and Afro-Latin American organizations all pressed claims that elections alone could not satisfy. They asked what had been done to their relatives, who was responsible, and whether the new institutions would protect people who had never held power. The answers varied, and the answers that governments gave were often shaped by what the armed forces would tolerate.

The evidence for this chapter is unusually varied. It includes constitutions and election returns, court judgments, amnesty statutes, and the reports of truth commissions, most of which are available in full and are examined as Primary Source Windows. It also includes testimony from survivors and relatives, forensic findings, newspapers and broadcasts, archival records of security agencies, and the scholarship that political scientists, historians, and lawyers have built since the 1980s. Official reports and laws reflect the limits of what governments were willing to say, and testimony reflects the position of those who survived. Students should read each source for what it records, what it omits, and who produced it.

The chapter's boundaries are deliberate. Chapter 93 explained the debt crisis and its economic consequences, and this chapter refers to them only to explain political outcomes. The conflicts in Nicaragua, El Salvador, and Guatemala are treated in Chapters 89 through 91, and Chapter 94 revisits them only for their political openings and their truth-seeking. The economic reforms of the 1990s belong to Chapter 95 and the Zapatista rising to Chapter 96. The task here is to explain how civilian rule returned, what it did and did not change, and why the memory of dictatorship became a continuing political struggle.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the variety of authoritarian systems in Latin America in the 1970s and distinguish them from countries with competitive electoral institutions.
  2. Explain why military governments lost legitimacy without treating economic decline as an automatic cause of democratization.
  3. Analyze the role of unions, churches, women's organizations, families of the disappeared, and human-rights networks in political opening.
  4. Distinguish among negotiated transitions, military defeat, electoral withdrawal, palace coup, and regime collapse as routes to civilian government.
  5. Trace the transitions in the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, Chile, and Paraguay, with attention to what each left unresolved.
  6. Compare constitutional reconstruction in Brazil, Paraguay, Chile, Ecuador, and Peru and evaluate the significance of the Brazilian Constitution of 1988.
  7. Assess the persistence of military autonomy and the different civil-military settlements reached in Argentina, Brazil, and Chile.
  8. Evaluate the distance between formal rights for women, Indigenous peoples, and Afro-Latin American communities and effective political inclusion.
  9. Compare the mandates, evidence, and consequences of CONADEP, the Trial of the Juntas, the Rettig Commission, and the Salvadoran and Guatemalan commissions.
  10. Interpret amnesty laws, impunity, and the role of domestic and Inter-American courts in debates over accountability.
  11. Contextualize Central America, the Caribbean, Mexico, Colombia, and Venezuela within, and outside, the regional democratic wave.
  12. Evaluate whether competitive elections amounted to accountable democratic government by 2000.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did authoritarian governments surrender or lose power in the years after 1978, and why through such different mechanisms?
  2. How did the manner of a regime's exit shape what its civilian successors could do about the military, the courts, and the past?
  3. What roles did civil society and international human-rights networks play, and what limits did they face?
  4. Why did civilian governments often accept restrictions on accountability, and were those compromises prudent, unavoidable, or avoidable?
  5. What distinguishes a founding election from the democratic consolidation that later elections might demonstrate?
  6. In what ways did constitutions written after dictatorship expand rights, and why did constitutional recognition not guarantee inclusion?
  7. How did truth commissions differ from criminal trials as ways of confronting state violence, and what could each achieve?
  8. Why did Mexico, Colombia, Costa Rica, and Venezuela not fit the pattern of transition from military dictatorship, and what does that show about the regional wave?
  9. What does it mean to say that memory of dictatorship remained a political struggle after elections returned?

Historical Context

Chapters 84 through 92 described the period this chapter closes. Military governments took power in Brazil in 1964, Uruguay and Chile in 1973, and Argentina in 1976, and they governed alongside long-lived personal regimes such as Alfredo Stroessner's in Paraguay and the Somoza dynasty's in Nicaragua. They justified themselves through national security doctrines that treated political opposition as subversion, and they built security agencies that practiced detention, torture, and disappearance. Chapter 87 described how some of these agencies cooperated across borders in Operation Condor. Chapter 88 showed how churches, relatives, lawyers, and journalists began to resist. This chapter begins where their efforts began to alter the political balance.

Democracy was not a new idea to the region. Most Latin American states had written constitutions with electoral provisions since the nineteenth century, and several had long experience of competitive politics. Uruguay and Chile were regarded as stable democracies before 1973, Costa Rica had abolished its army in 1948, and Colombia and Venezuela had built power-sharing systems in 1958. Earlier experiments had also collapsed, often in the same decades as the dictatorships, and political leaders of the 1980s carried that memory. Many feared that a rapid reckoning with the military would provoke another coup, and some military officers believed that civilian politicians would repeat the failures that had justified intervention.

The international environment changed as well. The United States under President Jimmy Carter publicly linked aid to human rights from 1977, though later administrations returned to Cold War priorities in Central America. Europe, the Organization of American States, and the United Nations developed instruments for monitoring abuses, and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights investigated disappearances. The end of the Cold War in 1989 removed a justification for anti-communist repression. These developments shaped the context, but they did not determine outcomes. Domestic coalitions did the decisive work in each country, and the chapter treats foreign influence as one factor among several.

The economic setting, explained in Chapter 93, matters here only through its political effects. The debt crisis of 1982 and the austerity that followed weakened military governments' claims to competent management and constrained civilian governments' capacity to meet the expectations that elections raised. This chapter therefore asks about institutions, citizenship, and accountability, and leaves the sovereign-debt and stabilization narratives to the chapter that owns them.

The Breakdown of Authoritarian Rule

Why military and authoritarian governments faced growing challenges, and why transitions emerged through different combinations of mobilization, negotiation, institutional crisis, and defeat.

Section 94.1

Latin America under Authoritarian Rule in the 1970s

By the late 1970s a large share of South and Central America was governed by armed forces or by long-entrenched personal rulers. Brazil had been under military presidents since 1964, Bolivia under Hugo Banzer since 1971, Uruguay and Chile since 1973, and Argentina since March 1976. Peru's armed forces had ruled since 1968, and Ecuador's since 1972, with a three-man military council in power from 1976. Paraguay lived under Alfredo Stroessner, in office since 1954, while Nicaragua's Somoza family, Haiti's Duvalier family, and a succession of generals in Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras held power in Central America and the Caribbean. The Dominican Republic was formally electoral but had been governed since 1966 by Joaquín Balaguer under heavy military influence.

These governments were not identical. Political scientists distinguished the institutional military regimes of Brazil, Argentina, and Chile, in which the armed forces as organizations claimed to govern for a defined period, from the personal regimes of Stroessner, the Somozas, and the Duvaliers, in which one family or leader dominated the army. Guillermo O'Donnell's concept of the "bureaucratic-authoritarian state" described the Southern Cone regimes as alliances of officers, technocrats, and business interests that excluded labor and the left in the name of order and economic modernization. The Peruvian military under General Juan Velasco Alvarado, by contrast, had carried out nationalizations and an agrarian reform, and Panama's General Omar Torrijos combined authoritarian control with nationalist reform. Authoritarian rule could be reactionary, populist, or developmentalist, and its legacies differed accordingly.

Many of these governments kept some electoral machinery. Brazil retained a Congress and two state-sanctioned parties and held controlled elections. Paraguay held regular elections that the Colorado Party always won, and Chile's 1980 constitution was approved in a plebiscite held without independent electoral registers or free opposition campaigning. Such procedures gave regimes a legal vocabulary and sometimes a mechanism, later exploited by opponents, through which change could occur. They also make it essential to distinguish the existence of voting from the existence of competitive elections.

Repression was the common instrument. Security agencies such as Brazil's National Information Service, Chile's DINA and later CNI, and Argentina's army- and navy-run task forces operated clandestine detention centers, practiced torture, and in Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, and Paraguay carried out forced disappearances (Chapters 84 through 87). Censorship, purges of universities and unions, the exile of tens of thousands of citizens, and the surveillance of ordinary social life accompanied overt violence. The scale varied: the Argentine disappearances, in the thousands, and Uruguay's extraordinary rate of political imprisonment per capita both exceeded Brazil's, where torture was widespread but killings were fewer.

A group of countries stood outside the pattern. Colombia and Venezuela had maintained competitive, if restricted, party systems since 1958, and Costa Rica had been a stable democracy since 1949. Mexico was a civilian one-party-dominant system that held elections without alternation. These cases matter because later sections show that democratic quality could be questioned in elected systems too, and that the "return" to democracy was a regional story with exceptions rather than a uniform restoration.

Section 94.2

The Crisis of Military Legitimacy

Military governments had come to power promising order, security, and growth. By the early 1980s many could claim the first in only one narrow sense: guerrilla movements in Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, and Chile had been defeated, and the emergency that justified exceptional rule had receded. As the threat faded, so did the argument that the armed forces had to remain in government. Officers began to ask what continued rule was costing their institutions. Governing divided officers by ideology and rank, drew them into corruption and politics, and exposed the armed forces to public hostility. Many came to prefer a controlled return to the barracks to the erosion of professional unity.

Economic conditions weakened legitimacy but did not mechanically end regimes. Chapter 93 described the debt crisis that began in 1982 and the inflation and austerity that followed. Military governments that had boasted of competent management faced falling wages, unemployment, and bankruptcies, and they lost the support of business groups that had welcomed them. Chile's recession of 1982–1983 triggered the largest protests against Pinochet, while Argentina's economic collapse and the discrediting of its policy accompanied the junta's decline. Yet the Chilean regime survived that recession and left office only after an economic recovery, and Stroessner's Paraguay fell during a period of relative stability. Economic distress created openings, and political actors decided whether to use them.

Corruption and criminal entanglement also eroded authority. Bolivia's coup of July 1980, led by General Luis García Meza, was widely called the "cocaine coup" because of its links to trafficking, and it isolated Bolivia internationally. Smuggling and contraband enriched Paraguayan officers, and revelations of personal enrichment damaged regimes that had claimed to be guardians of public morality. Family and succession disputes mattered in personal regimes: Stroessner's advancing age and the rivalry over who would succeed him opened divisions inside the Colorado Party and the army.

Argentina's defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War is the clearest case of military failure as a political catalyst. On 2 April 1982 the junta led by General Leopoldo Galtieri invaded the islands, hoping to restore popular support at a time of economic crisis and rising protest. Britain's task force recaptured the islands, and Argentine forces surrendered on 14 June after losing roughly 650 men. Galtieri resigned within days. The defeat exposed incompetence and unpreparedness, discredited the army in the eyes of its own supporters, and ended the junta's claim to national leadership. A later military inquiry, the Rattenbach commission, was highly critical of the conduct of the war. Within months the armed forces were negotiating the terms of their withdrawal from a position of weakness.

Defeat in war was exceptional. Elsewhere the armed forces left when their own calculations changed or when mobilization raised the costs of staying. Uruguayan voters in a November 1980 plebiscite rejected the military's proposed constitution, an unexpected defeat that began the regime's retreat. The lesson for students is that legitimacy crises created conditions, not outcomes. The form of the transition depended on whether opposition forces were united, whether officers remained cohesive, and whether either side believed it could negotiate better terms than it would get by resisting.

Section 94.3

Civil Society, Labor, Churches, Women, and Human-Rights Movements

Opposition to authoritarian rule was often organized outside political parties, which were banned, suspended, or infiltrated. Unions provided some of the earliest large-scale challenges. In Brazil the metalworkers of São Paulo's industrial ABC region, led by Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, struck in 1978 and 1979 in defiance of the labor laws, and their movement contributed to the founding of the Workers' Party in 1980. In Chile, the copper workers' confederation under Rodolfo Seguel called a national day of protest on 11 May 1983, which opened a cycle of protests lasting through the year. In Argentina the main labor confederation, divided and harassed, called a general strike on 30 March 1982 that was met with repression. Bolivia's labor federation resisted successive coups in 1979 and 1980. Student federations, professional associations of lawyers, doctors, and journalists, and neighborhood groups formed a broad civic opposition.

Churches were crucial, though not uniform. Many bishops and lay leaders in the Catholic Church had initially accepted military governments as barriers against communism. By the mid-1970s significant parts of the Church, shaped by the Medellín conference of 1968 and by liberation theology (Chapter 88), began to protect victims. In Chile the Vicariate of Solidarity, created in 1976 under Cardinal Raúl Silva Henríquez, gave legal aid and documented thousands of cases of detention and disappearance. In Brazil, Cardinal Paulo Evaristo Arns of São Paulo and the Presbyterian minister Jaime Wright helped organize a secret project to photocopy military court records, which became the 1985 book *Brasil: Nunca Mais*. Protestant and ecumenical groups, including Argentina's Ecumenical Movement for Human Rights, and Uruguay's Peace and Justice Service, joined them. Church institutions had resources, international contacts, and a degree of legal protection that other groups lacked, though some bishops, especially in Argentina, remained close to the regime.

Relatives of the disappeared created a new political form. In Argentina, mothers whose children had vanished began meeting in Buenos Aires's Plaza de Mayo on 30 April 1977, walking in silence in front of the presidential palace in a public demonstration that the regime found hard to repress openly. Their numbers grew, and they wore white headscarves that became their symbol. In December 1977 the navy's task force at the Escuela de Mecánica de la Armada abducted Azucena Villaflor, a founder of the group, along with other relatives and two French nuns, and they disappeared. The Mothers continued. A related organization, the Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo, formed in 1977 to find grandchildren born to pregnant women in captivity and given to other families, and in the 1980s it worked with geneticists to develop methods of establishing biological kinship. The Argentine movement received international attention, and in 1980 the Argentine pacifist Adolfo Pérez Esquivel won the Nobel Peace Prize for human-rights work.

Women's participation went well beyond the Mothers. The Brazilian Women's Movement for Amnesty, founded in 1975, campaigned for political prisoners; Chile's Women for Life movement, formed in 1983, brought together women from across the opposition; and neighborhood committees in Lima's poor districts organized communal kitchens and milk programs that were, in practice, political schools. Women organizers drew on their social roles as mothers and caretakers to claim a public voice in settings where political speech was dangerous. Their claims were often framed as moral rather than partisan, a framing that helped them survive but that also sometimes made them invisible in later accounts of the transitions. Feminist networks held regional meetings from 1981, and many of their demands about family law, violence, and political participation emerged in the constitutional debates of the late 1980s.

International networks multiplied the effect of local protest. Amnesty International won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1977, and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights conducted an on-site visit to Argentina in September 1979 and issued a report on disappearances. Exiled activists in Europe, Mexico, and the United States lobbied governments and wrote reports. These networks gave local groups a way to bypass national censorship, but they depended on information produced by local activists at great personal risk. Historians sometimes describe these movements as a "human-rights regime" of norms and organizations, a useful phrase that should not be read to imply that international pressure alone brought down any dictatorship.

Section 94.4

Negotiated Transitions, Opposition Coalitions, and Military Withdrawal

The scholarly literature on transitions, especially the study edited by Guillermo O'Donnell and Philippe Schmitter in 1986, emphasized that the process began with divisions within authoritarian governments, between "hard-liners" who wished to continue and "soft-liners" who saw advantages in limited opening, and that outcomes depended on negotiation between moderates on both sides. Later scholars criticized parts of that model as too focused on elites and underestimating the role of popular mobilization, and the evidence from Latin America supports a more mixed account. Some transitions were largely controlled by the military; others were forced by crisis; and in many, opposition organization changed what the armed forces judged they could safely do.

Five broad routes can be distinguished. In the first, the armed forces set a schedule for withdrawal and retained substantial control over its terms, as in Brazil and, to a considerable degree, Chile. In the second, negotiation between the military and the main opposition parties produced an agreement, as in Uruguay's Naval Club Pact of 1984. In the third, military defeat or collapse of authority left the armed forces unable to dictate terms, as in Argentina in 1982–1983. In the fourth, soldiers organized an electoral withdrawal in response to economic and political pressures, as in Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia. In the fifth, a palace coup replaced a dictator while preserving much of his political system, as in Paraguay in 1989. Nicaragua in 1979 and Haiti in 1986 involved the overthrow of dynasties by other means and are treated later in the chapter.

Opposition coalitions shaped these routes. In Chile seventeen parties, from Christian Democrats to Socialists, formed the coalition that ran the "No" campaign in 1988, overcoming decades of animosity born of the 1973 coup. In Brazil the opposition within the legal party, the Brazilian Democratic Movement, merged moderate opposition with new popular movements. In Argentina the main parties formed a multi-party group that pressed the junta for elections after 1981. Opposition unity did not guarantee success, but its absence made it easier for authoritarian governments to divide their opponents and to bargain from strength.

Incoming civilian governments frequently accepted limits that would have been unthinkable to their supporters a few years earlier. They accepted amnesty provisions or de facto immunity for officers, protected military budgets and promotion systems, and in Chile, a constitution drafted by the departing regime. The reasons were practical. Armed forces retained control of weapons and intelligence services, many officers believed they had been fighting a legitimate war, and the memory of earlier coups encouraged caution. Civilian leaders feared that a reckoning would provoke the very coup that would end the transition. Whether those fears were justified in each case is a matter of continuing historical debate, examined in the debate box in Section 94.15.

Historical Vocabulary
Pacted Transition and Reserved Domains

A pacted transition is a change of regime in which outgoing rulers and opposition leaders negotiate the terms of the handover. A reserved domain (enclave autoritario in the Spanish-language literature) is an area of state power, such as military promotions, intelligence, or the budget, that remains outside the control of elected officials after a transition.

These concepts clarify why the return of elected government did not by itself end authoritarian influence. They are analytical terms devised by scholars, not categories used by participants, and they should not be treated as proof that negotiation was a mistake. The same pacts that preserved military privileges also helped avoid renewed violence in some cases.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Military Governments Relinquished Power by Different Routes

Long-term causes

The weakening of national-security justifications after guerrilla defeats; the costs of governing for military unity; the growth of civil society and international human-rights networks; the example of earlier transitions in Southern Europe.

Short-term causes

Economic crisis after 1982; the Falklands defeat; the Uruguayan plebiscite loss; mass protests in Chile and Brazil; succession crisis in Paraguay.

Event or transformation

Armed forces left government through scheduled withdrawal, negotiated pact, collapse after defeat, electoral opening, or palace coup.

Immediate effects

Elections and legal parties returned; exiles came back; political prisoners were released; security agencies were reorganized or left in place depending on the route.

Long-term consequences

Different degrees of civilian control, different amnesty arrangements, and different capacities to prosecute past abuses.

Historical complexity

No single factor, not economic decline, foreign pressure, or opposition mobilization, explains the whole pattern, and the transitions were not inevitable.
Division II · Sections 94.5–94.8

Democratic Transitions across Latin America

The main country-level narrative of the return to civilian rule: how transitions actually occurred, not merely when elections were held.

Section 94.5

The First Openings: Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia

The Dominican Republic's 1978 election was the first test of whether an incumbent backed by the armed forces would accept defeat. Joaquín Balaguer, in power since 1966, ran for another term against Antonio Guzmán of the Dominican Revolutionary Party. After voting on 16 May, early returns favored Guzmán, and soldiers entered the electoral board and suspended the count. The United States, under President Jimmy Carter, issued a public statement on 19 May, and other governments and domestic groups pressed for the count to resume. The board eventually proclaimed Guzmán the winner, though a ruling on legislative seats gave Balaguer's party control of the Senate. Guzmán took office on 16 August 1978 and moved to retire several senior officers. This was a significant, if limited, case of an opposition candidate taking power through the ballot under pressure from both domestic groups and a foreign government.

In Ecuador the armed forces had governed since 1972 and in 1976 transferred power to a three-member council that organized a return to civilian rule. A commission wrote a new constitution, which voters approved by referendum in January 1978, and which extended the vote to illiterate adults, a change that brought many Quichua-speaking Indigenous citizens into the electorate for the first time. Jaime Roldós of the Concentration of Popular Forces won the presidency in a runoff in April 1979 with about two thirds of the vote and took office on 10 August. The Ecuadorian military retained influence and Roldós died in a plane crash in May 1981, but the civilian succession held under his vice president, Osvaldo Hurtado.

Peru's transition was set in motion when General Francisco Morales Bermúdez replaced the reformist Velasco in 1975 and, facing economic crisis and labor unrest, announced a return to civilian rule. Voters elected a Constituent Assembly in June 1978, presided by the veteran APRA leader Víctor Raúl Haya de la Torre. The 1979 Constitution lowered the voting age to 18 and gave the vote to illiterate citizens, thereby enlarging the electorate in a country with large Quechua- and Aymara-speaking populations. In the presidential election of May 1980, Fernando Belaúnde Terry, the president overthrown by the army in 1968, won and returned to office in July. The day before the election, members of the Maoist Shining Path burned ballot boxes in the Andean village of Chuschi, beginning an insurgency that would shape the next decade. The opening to civilian rule thus coincided with the start of a conflict that gave the armed forces new reasons for autonomy.

Bolivia's transition was the most turbulent. General Banzer announced elections in 1978, but the vote was annulled amid fraud, and a series of coups followed. Elections in 1979 produced no majority, and Congress chose an interim civilian president before Colonel Alberto Natusch Busch seized power for about two weeks in November 1979 and was forced out by popular resistance and congressional opposition. Lidia Gueiler, president of the Chamber of Deputies, then became interim president, Bolivia's first woman head of state. New elections in June 1980 gave a plurality to Hernán Siles Zuazo, but on 17 July 1980 General García Meza seized power with extensive violence and links to the cocaine trade. International isolation, military splits, and economic collapse forced the armed forces to allow the 1980 Congress to reconvene, and Siles Zuazo took office on 10 October 1982. García Meza was later convicted in absentia for human-rights abuses and corruption, and was extradited from Brazil in 1995.

Comparison of these four cases shows how varied the early openings were. The Dominican case turned on an electoral commission and external pressure, the Ecuadorian and Peruvian cases on military-managed constitutional processes, and the Bolivian case on repeated military failure. In all four the armed forces retained significant autonomy, and elected governments faced economic crisis almost immediately.

Section 94.6

Argentina and Uruguay: From Military Rule to Civilian Government

After the Falklands defeat, General Reynaldo Bignone took over the presidency on 1 July 1982 with a mandate to arrange elections. The regime tried to protect itself. In April 1983 it issued a document defending the "war against subversion" and declaring that disappearances had been deaths, and in September it decreed a self-amnesty, the so-called Law of National Pacification. The law was widely denounced, and Congress annulled it after civilian government began. Elections were held on 30 October 1983. Raúl Alfonsín of the Radical Civic Union won about 52 percent of the vote against about 40 percent for Ítalo Luder of the Peronist Party, the first defeat of Peronism in a fully free national election. Alfonsín had campaigned on human rights and constitutional government, and he took office on 10 December 1983.

The government moved quickly. Within days, Alfonsín ordered the prosecution of the members of the first three military juntas and of leaders of left-wing guerrilla organizations, and created the National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons (Section 94.13). The military was reorganized under a civilian defense ministry, and the first civilian defense minister was appointed. The government's policy, however, distinguished among three groups: top commanders who gave orders, officers who carried them out with excess, and those who obeyed. That distinction, later written into law, shaped the limits of accountability and is examined in Sections 94.13 and 94.15.

Uruguay's route was different. The armed forces, which had effectively governed since 1973 although a civilian remained nominal president until 1976, held a plebiscite in November 1980 on a constitution that would have institutionalized military tutelage. About 57 percent voted No. The defeat forced the military to negotiate. Political parties, some of whose leaders were banned or imprisoned, regrouped, and mass demonstrations in 1983 and 1984 showed popular desire for change. The Naval Club Pact of August 1984 was an agreement among the armed forces and the legal parties, with the left Broad Front participating, that set the terms for elections and a transfer. Key opposition figures, notably the Blanco leader Wilson Ferreira Aldunate, who was arrested on returning from exile in June 1984, were excluded from the contest.

Elections on 25 November 1984 gave the Colorado Party about 41 percent, the Blanco Party about 35 percent, and the Broad Front about 21 percent, and Julio María Sanguinetti, a Colorado, took office on 1 March 1985. The new government and Congress promptly freed political prisoners, and the Uruguayan prison system, with a very high per capita rate of political detention, was emptied within weeks. The question of accountability was deferred. Human-rights groups and some legislators demanded investigation of killings, disappearances, and torture, but the armed forces made it clear that they would not accept trials of their officers. The result was the 1986 Expiry Law, discussed in Section 94.15.

The two cases show how the same crisis could yield different settlements. Argentina's military had lost a war and with it the capacity to bargain, which allowed prosecutions that Uruguay's negotiated transition did not. Yet Uruguay's civilian institutions had deeper roots, its parties had survived, and its election was more orderly. Neither route produced complete accountability, and in both countries the memory of the dictatorship became a lasting domestic argument.

Section 94.7

Brazil: Diretas Já, the New Republic, and the Return of Civilian Rule

Brazil's military controlled the pace of its own retreat longer than any other Southern Cone regime. President Ernesto Geisel announced a "slow, gradual, and secure" political opening in 1974, and his successor, General João Figueiredo, continued it. Institutional Act No. 5, the 1968 decree that had given the executive sweeping powers, ceased to apply in January 1979. On 28 August 1979 Congress passed an Amnesty Law, which freed political prisoners and allowed exiles to return, and which the regime also interpreted as protecting its own agents. In December 1979 the two-party system was dissolved, and new parties formed, including the Workers' Party, founded in 1980 from the strikes in São Paulo's industrial belt, and the Democratic Labor Party. Governors were elected directly in 1982 for the first time since the coup.

The movement for direct presidential elections, known as Diretas Já ("Direct Elections Now"), emerged in 1983 and 1984. A constitutional amendment proposed by Congressman Dante de Oliveira would have restored direct presidential voting. Mass rallies, beginning in smaller cities and growing in Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo in the spring of 1984, drew hundreds of thousands and, by common estimates, more than a million people in each of the largest. The campaign united opposition parties, unions, Church groups, artists, and athletes. On 25 April 1984 the Chamber of Deputies voted on the amendment: a majority favored it, but it fell short of the two-thirds required, and the military-created electoral college would choose the next president.

The opposition adapted. A faction of the pro-government party broke away and joined the Brazilian Democratic Movement Party in the Democratic Alliance, nominating Tancredo Neves, the governor of Minas Gerais, with José Sarney, a dissident from the government party, as vice president. On 15 January 1985 the electoral college chose Neves over the government's candidate, Paulo Maluf. The outcome showed that the opposition had won inside the institutions the military had designed. But Neves fell ill on the eve of his inauguration and died on 21 April 1985, and Sarney, a former supporter of the regime, assumed office on 15 March 1985 and was confirmed as president. Brazilians thus had a civilian government with a leader the opposition had not chosen as its first figure.

The New Republic, as it called itself, was therefore a hybrid. The amnesty remained in force, the armed forces kept ministries and considerable influence, and the elected Congress was asked to write a new constitution. In November 1986 voters elected a Congress that would serve as a Constituent Assembly, convened in February 1987 and presided over by Ulysses Guimarães, which promulgated the Constitution on 5 October 1988 (Section 94.9). The 1988 Constitution provided for direct presidential elections, and the first was held in November and December 1989, when Fernando Collor de Mello defeated Lula in a runoff. Brazil's civilian government thus preceded direct presidential election by four years.

Why did civilian government come before direct elections? The military had managed the transition through institutions it controlled, and opposition leaders, concluding that they could not win the amendment vote, chose to defeat the regime within its own system. The decision gave the transition continuity and stability but also left unresolved questions about the legitimacy of the 1985 government and the amnesty. The Brazilian experience shows that democratization could be an extended process with several milestones rather than a single date.

Section 94.8

Chile and Paraguay: Plebiscites, Military Power, and Regime Collapse

Chile's transition was written into the 1980 Constitution that the regime had drafted. Its transitional provisions scheduled a plebiscite before March 1989, in which the commanders of the armed forces would nominate a single candidate for an eight-year term, and voters would accept or reject him. Opponents debated for years whether to participate in a process they regarded as illegitimate. Most concluded that registering voters and campaigning offered the best chance to defeat Pinochet on his own terms. Seventeen parties formed the Concertación of Parties for the No. A television "franja" of fifteen minutes each night gave the opposition its first sustained access to broadcasting in fifteen years, and its campaign emphasized hope and reconciliation rather than recrimination.

The plebiscite was held on 5 October 1988, with the high turnout of about 97 percent of registered voters. The "No" vote won roughly 56 percent against roughly 44 percent for "Yes." On the night itself, the regime delayed announcing results, and some officials reportedly considered resisting, but senior figures among the military leadership, including the air force commander, indicated that the result would be respected. The vote then entered a negotiated phase. In July 1989 a constitutional amendment package, negotiated between the regime and the opposition and approved by plebiscite, eased the rules for future amendments, increased the number of elected senators, and removed a ban on parties described as Marxist. Presidential and legislative elections on 14 December 1989 gave Patricio Aylwin, a Christian Democrat, about 55 percent of the vote. He took office on 11 March 1990.

The transfer of office coexisted with the survival of Pinochet's institutional framework. The 1980 Constitution reserved senate seats for appointed senators, including former commanders, created a National Security Council with military members, protected the commanders from dismissal by the president, and set up an electoral system that favored the second-largest bloc. An amnesty decree issued in 1978 covered crimes committed up to that date. Pinochet declared that he would remain commander-in-chief until 1998, and he did. When he stepped down, he became a senator for life. Chile's transition was therefore both a defeat of the dictator in a vote and a case of authoritarian enclaves surviving in the new order.

Paraguay's transition came through a different mechanism entirely. General Alfredo Stroessner had governed since 1954 through the combination of the armed forces and the Colorado Party, with a pervasive security apparatus. By the late 1980s, factions of the Colorado Party and the army disagreed about the succession, and some officers were reluctant to continue supporting him. In the early hours of 3 February 1989 troops under General Andrés Rodríguez, commander of the First Army Corps and a relative by marriage of Stroessner, attacked the presidential bodyguard and forced him into exile in Brazil. Rodríguez, who promised free elections, won the presidency in May 1989 with about 74 percent of the vote in a vote still dominated by the Colorado machine.

The Paraguayan opening was real but limited. Political parties were legalized, press restrictions loosened, and municipal elections were held in 1991. A Constituent Assembly elected in December 1991, in which the Colorado Party won a plurality, wrote a new constitution in 1992 (Section 94.9). In December 1992, a lawyer and a former political prisoner discovered the police records known as the Archive of Terror, a trove of documents of repression and of the cross-border cooperation of Condor-era security services. Yet the Colorado Party continued to govern until 2008, and the armed forces remained politically influential, as the military challenges of the 1990s show (Section 94.19). Rodríguez remained a serving general at the opening of the transition: 1989 did not mark the installation of a civilian president. The new constitutional framework was followed by the May 1993 election of Juan Carlos Wasmosy, who was inaugurated on 15 August 1993 as Paraguay's first freely elected civilian president in nearly four decades. Paraguay shows how a democratic opening could begin with a coup by the dictator's closest collaborator without immediately establishing civilian presidential government.

Section 94.9

Constitutional Reconstruction and the Rule of Law

New or revised constitutions were among the first tasks of post-authoritarian governments, and the process by which they were written said much about the transition. Ecuador's 1978 referendum and Peru's 1979 charter, noted in Section 94.5, were produced under military supervision but enlarged the electorate. Brazil's 1988 Constitution came from an elected assembly. Paraguay's 1992 Constitution came from an assembly dominated by the party of the old regime but written in a context of open public debate. Chile's 1980 text was imposed by the dictatorship and amended in 1989 and afterward through negotiation. These differences in origin affected the document's legitimacy and the ease with which its provisions could be changed.

Brazil's Constituent Assembly was unusual in method. Members of the Chamber and Senate elected in 1986, 559 in all, met from February 1987 in committees and subcommittees that held public hearings, and citizens and organizations submitted proposals and "popular amendments" supported by signatures. The assembly was fractious, with a conservative bloc known as the Centrão contesting reforms on land, labor, and the military's role, and it wrote one of the longest constitutions in the world. Only 26 women were members, but they organized across party lines, in what the press called the "lipstick lobby," to press for equality provisions in family law, labor, and health. Ulysses Guimarães called the final text the "Citizen Constitution" when he promulgated it on 5 October 1988.

The text expanded rights substantially. It defined sovereignty, citizenship, human dignity, and political pluralism as foundations of the state and declared that all power emanates from the people. It prohibited torture, made racism a crime without bail or statute of limitations, established the right of access to information about oneself, and recognized health as a right of all and a duty of the state, which led to the creation of a national public health system. It made voting compulsory for literate adults aged 18 to 70, optional for illiterate citizens, for those aged 16 and 17, and for those over 70, which enlarged the electorate. It also recognized Indigenous peoples' social organization, languages, and original rights to the lands they traditionally occupy, and it granted property titles to descendants of quilombo communities (Section 94.12). The Constitution also kept an article allowing the armed forces to act in defense of law and order at the request of the constitutional powers, which later generations of scholars and politicians have read in different ways.

Paraguay's 1992 Constitution defined the state as a representative, participatory, and pluralist democracy, limited the president to a single five-year term, prohibited torture and made torture, forced disappearance, and political killing not subject to statutes of limitations, recognized Guaraní alongside Spanish as an official language, and devoted a chapter to Indigenous peoples as groups that preceded the state. It restricted the military's role in politics. Chile's constitutional history was different: the 1989 reforms removed a ban on Marxist parties, increased the number of elected senators, and loosened the rules for amendment, but the military's protected position survived until reforms in 2005 removed appointed senators and restored the president's power to dismiss the commanders. Peru's 1979 Constitution was replaced in 1993 under Fujimori after his self-coup (Section 94.19).

The rule of law was harder to build than constitutions. Judiciaries had been complicit with or intimidated by the military governments, and judges appointed under dictatorship often remained. Police forces continued abusive practices, prisons remained overcrowded, and access to justice was unequal. Constitutional rights existed on paper long before the courts and police could protect them for poor, Indigenous, rural, and Afro-descendant citizens. Students should read each constitution as a statement of aspiration and a political compromise, and use evidence of implementation to judge its effects.

Section 94.10

Elections, Political Parties, and Peaceful Transfers of Power

The first elections after dictatorship are often called "founding elections," and they were extraordinary occasions. Voters who had lived for years without competitive campaigns went to the polls in large numbers, and turnout was high where voting was compulsory and where citizens saw a chance to repudiate the regime. Parties that had been banned were legalized, and many emerged with surprising strength: the Argentine Radicals, the Brazilian PMDB, the Chilean Christian Democrats, and the Uruguayan Colorados and Blancos all drew on organizational memory. Communist parties were legalized in Brazil in 1985 and in Chile in effect after the 1989 reforms.

Elections required institutions to administer them. Electoral commissions and tribunals had to compile voter registers, print ballots, count votes, and adjudicate disputes, and their independence from the executive mattered. Uruguay and Costa Rica had long-established independent electoral bodies. Chile built its register almost from scratch in 1987–1988, with the opposition urging citizens to register. Mexico, which had a ruling party that controlled the electoral machinery, created a federal electoral institute in 1990 and made it autonomous in 1996 (Section 94.18). International observers, including the Organization of American States and regional networks of civic groups, increasingly monitored elections, though sovereignty concerns limited their reach.

A founding election and a democratic order are not the same thing. Political scientists sometimes point to alternation in power as a test: a governing party that loses and leaves office shows that the system is competitive. In Argentina, Alfonsín transferred the presidency to Carlos Menem of the Peronist Party in July 1989, several months early, amid hyperinflation and unrest, the first time in decades that an elected civilian president handed power to an elected rival. In Uruguay, Sanguinetti was followed in 1990 by Luis Alberto Lacalle of the Blanco Party. Bolivia's elected governments alternated beginning in 1985. Brazil's 1989 election brought Collor to office, and Peru's 1990 election brought Fujimori. In Nicaragua in February 1990, the Sandinista government, which had come to power through revolution, held an election that it lost to Violeta Chamorro's opposition coalition and surrendered the presidency in April.

Competition did not guarantee that campaigns were fair or that parties were strong. Governing parties used state resources, media ownership concentrated, and campaign finance rules were weak. Party systems varied: Uruguay and Chile had long-lived parties with ideological identities, while Brazil's and Peru's systems fragmented and personalistic candidates often won. Voters in several countries expressed disillusion with parties after the economic crises of the 1980s, a development that created openings for outsiders such as Collor and Fujimori. The legitimacy of elections rose in the 1980s and then faced pressures from the 1990s, which are examined in Section 94.19.

Everyday Life and Social History

Voting, Speaking, and Assembling Again

For ordinary citizens the return of elections meant changes that were concrete and uneven. Newspapers and magazines that had been closed or censored reappeared, and broadcasting that had been controlled opened to opposition voices. Chile's nightly television "franja" in 1988 gave families the first sight in a generation of opposition leaders on screen. Brazilians who attended the 1984 Diretas Já rallies wore yellow, and street demonstrations became ordinary again. Trade unions and student federations held elections, and neighborhood committees, often led by women, demanded services that dictators had ignored.

Exile ended for hundreds of thousands. The Uruguayan writer Mario Benedetti used the word desexilio for the difficult return, which involved children raised abroad, jobs lost, and family members who had not left. Returning exiles found societies that had changed without them, and they met neighbors who had stayed and sometimes resented those who left. Families of the disappeared and ex-political prisoners faced a different return: freedom did not erase torture's effects or restore lost years. Poor and rural citizens, especially Indigenous communities, experienced elections as an unfamiliar or intermittent presence, and many noticed that political freedom did not lower the price of food or provide land. The sources record public debates more fully than private experiences, and oral histories and memoirs are essential for understanding the second.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Are Competitive Elections Sufficient for Democracy?

The question: Did the return of competitive elections in the 1980s and 1990s establish democracy in Latin America, or did democracy require institutions and protections that elections alone did not supply?

Interpretation A — Elections as the core of democracy: Scholars in a minimalist tradition define democracy by free, fair, and competitive elections with inclusive suffrage, and they argue that this standard identifies a real and demanding threshold. On this view, the post-1978 wave was a major change: governments chosen by citizens replaced rulers who had never faced voters, and later alternations in power showed that losers accepted defeat.
Interpretation B — Democracy requires accountability and rights: Others argue that elections without civilian control of the military, an independent judiciary, protection of rights, and equal access to justice describe "delegative" or "illiberal" systems. On this view, authoritarian enclaves, impunity, and social exclusion meant that many countries had elections without the constraints and protections that make elected power accountable.

Evidence: Both positions rely on comparative election data, constitutional texts, surveys of public opinion, reports of human-rights bodies, and studies of judicial and police practice. They often disagree about where to draw the line and about how to measure institutional quality.

Why it matters: The definition shapes the date at which a country is counted as democratic and the criteria by which later governments are judged. It also affects how the achievements of transitions, and the demands of those who found them inadequate, are understood.

Student task: Choose two countries from this chapter and evaluate each under both definitions. Identify the evidence that most affects your judgment.

Section 94.11

The Military after Dictatorship: Civilian Control and Institutional Power

Civilian control means that elected officials set defense policy, control budgets and appointments, and direct security institutions, and that the armed forces accept this authority. Achieving it after years of military government required changes in law, institutions, and political culture. Elected governments created or strengthened defense ministries, but in many countries those ministries were headed by officers or lacked the expertise to oversee budgets, doctrine, and promotions. Military budgets were often protected by earmarked revenues, such as the share of Chile's copper exports reserved for arms purchases, which remained in place for decades.

Internal security doctrines were a particular problem. Dictatorships had defined internal political opposition as a military matter, and intelligence agencies had been instruments of political control. Dissolving or reforming those agencies proved slow: Brazil's National Information Service was abolished in 1990 under President Collor, Argentina restructured its intelligence bodies in stages, and Chile's army intelligence continued under its own authority. Military courts also remained, in many countries, a jurisdiction for crimes committed by officers, which meant that abuses could be judged by their peers. Questions of conscription, officer education, and the role of the armed forces in domestic policing remained contested.

Argentina's case illustrates confrontation. The Alfonsín government reduced the military budget, changed the command structure, and prosecuted senior officers, and it faced repeated military rebellions between 1987 and 1990 (Section 94.19). Congress passed a national defense law in 1988 that restricted the military's role to external defense, and a domestic security law in 1992 reinforced the separation of defense from internal security. Compulsory conscription was abolished in 1994 after the murder of a conscript sparked public outrage. Over time civilian control strengthened, helped by military discredit after the Falklands and the Dirty War, though through setbacks and the presidential pardons discussed in Section 94.15.

Brazil's case illustrates negotiated continuity. The military left government gradually and kept strong representation in the political system: the three service ministers remained in the cabinet until 1999, when a defense ministry led by a civilian was created, and the armed forces retained informal influence in areas such as nuclear and space programs, intelligence, and territorial policy. The 1988 Constitution defined the armed forces as permanent national institutions responsible for national defense and, at the request of the constitutional powers, for the guarantee of law and order. The military did not threaten a coup after 1985, and scholars have debated whether this reflects institutionalized civilian control or the military's satisfaction with a settlement that protected its prerogatives.

Chile's case illustrates protected autonomy. Under the 1980 Constitution and organizing laws approved just before the transfer of power, the armed forces were guaranteed a measure of institutional independence: the president could not dismiss the commanders, appointed senators included former commanders, and the commanders sat on the National Security Council. Pinochet, as commander, twice showed what this meant: in December 1990 the army carried out a mobilization known as the "liaison exercise" during a dispute over investigations into his family's finances, and in May 1993 troops in combat dress appeared near the capital in an episode called the "boinazo." These actions were interpreted as warnings to the Aylwin government. Reforms in 2005 finally ended most of these prerogatives. In Honduras and Guatemala, comparable autonomy persisted until the late 1990s, when civilian defense ministers were introduced.

What Changed

  • Presidents and legislators were chosen in competitive elections
  • Parties, unions, and newspapers operated legally
  • Political prisoners were released and exiles returned
  • New constitutions or amended texts expanded rights

  • Military budgets, promotion systems, and in some places reserved political roles

Section 94.12

Expanding Citizenship: Women, Indigenous Peoples, and Afro-Latin American Communities

Formal democratic rights and effective political inclusion are different things. The return of elections extended the vote and opened public arenas to groups that military governments had suppressed, but many citizens remained excluded from decision-making by poverty, geography, language, discrimination, and the weakness of institutions. The transitions coincided with the growth of organizations of women, Indigenous peoples, and Afro-descendant communities that demanded recognition as political actors and not only as beneficiaries of elected governments.

Women had voted in most countries since the 1930s through the 1960s, but their representation in office was low. The mobilization of women under dictatorship, in human-rights groups, neighborhood organizations, and feminist circles, translated into demands during constitutional debates. Brazil's 1988 Constitution declared men and women equal in rights and obligations and extended labor protections, including paid maternity leave, and the small group of women in the Constituent Assembly secured provisions on family law and domestic violence. Argentina adopted a national quota law in 1991 requiring that at least 30 percent of candidates on party lists for the national Chamber be women, a pioneering measure that others in the region later copied. Violeta Chamorro's election in Nicaragua in 1990 and Lidia Gueiler's brief presidency in Bolivia show women reaching top office, though their paths were exceptional. Many gains in practice, such as the creation of women's agencies and reform of family codes, came in the 1990s and after.

Indigenous peoples entered constitutional politics through a combination of national reform and international mobilization. The extension of the vote to illiterate citizens in Ecuador, Peru, and later Brazil enlarged the Indigenous electorate. Guatemala's 1985 Constitution and Nicaragua's 1987 Constitution, with its autonomy statute for the Atlantic Coast, recognized Indigenous and ethnic communities. Colombia's Constituent Assembly of 1991 included Indigenous delegates elected through national Indigenous organizations and adopted provisions for Indigenous territories and reserved Senate seats. Brazil's 1988 Constitution, Paraguay's 1992 text, Bolivia's 1994 reform declaring the country multiethnic and pluricultural, and Argentina's 1994 reform recognizing the prior existence of Indigenous peoples followed. The International Labour Organization's Convention 169 of 1989 provided an international framework that several states, including Mexico and Colombia, ratified in the early 1990s. Indigenous mobilization intensified around the 1992 quincentenary of Columbus's voyage, and the Guatemalan K'iche' activist Rigoberta Menchú won the Nobel Peace Prize that year. The rising Indigenous movements, which Chapter 96 treats through the Zapatistas, thus built on this constitutional opening.

Afro-Latin American claims were slower to gain constitutional recognition. Black movements, such as Brazil's Unified Black Movement, founded in 1978, argued that the myth of racial democracy concealed discrimination. Brazil's 1988 Constitution made racism a crime without bail or statute of limitations and granted property rights to descendants of quilombo communities that still occupy their historic lands, and the federal government created the Palmares Cultural Foundation in 1988. Colombia's 1991 Constitution contained a transitional article that led to Law 70 of 1993, which recognized collective land rights for Black communities of the Pacific coast. Honduras's Garífuna and Nicaragua's coastal Creole and Afro-descendant communities pressed for autonomy and land. In most countries racial inequality continued to be poorly measured, and public recognition lagged behind legal recognition.

The gap between recognition and implementation was the central fact of these years. Land demarcation procedures were slow, courts were inaccessible, and private and state interests in land, mining, and logging collided with the rights written into law. Voting rights meant little where polling places were distant or documents were unavailable. Yet the new texts gave organizations tools and arguments, and a generation of litigation and mobilization followed. The narrative should not imply that elected governments automatically represented previously excluded populations: in many cases they did not, and inclusion advanced when excluded groups organized to claim it.

Section 94.13

Argentina: CONADEP, Nunca Más, and the Trial of the Juntas

Argentina's accountability process began with a presidential decision. On 15 December 1983, five days after taking office, Alfonsín created the National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons (CONADEP) by decree. He appointed ten members, including the novelist Ernesto Sabato as chair, and the commission was a presidential body rather than a parliamentary one, a choice that reflected both the government's reluctance to give Peronist legislators a role and its desire to move quickly. Its mandate was to receive testimony and evidence about disappearances and to forward cases to the courts. It had no judicial power, and the armed forces refused to cooperate, so its investigation depended on survivors, relatives, and the documentation compiled by human-rights groups.

The commission worked for nine months. It received thousands of depositions, inspected former detention centers, and identified roughly 340 sites of clandestine detention across the country. It delivered its report, titled Nunca Más ("Never Again"), to the president on 20 September 1984, documenting approximately 8,960 cases of disappearance and stating that the true number was probably higher. Human-rights organizations have long argued for a much larger figure, often given as 30,000, based on estimates of undocumented cases. The report was published as a book that sold in large numbers and a television program broadcast its findings. The original prologue described the violence as coming from extremes of left and right, a framing, later called the "two demons" theory, that survivors' organizations criticized for equating guerrilla violence with state terror; a later edition added a new prologue that rejected that equivalence.

The legal route was parallel. In December 1983, Alfonsín ordered the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces to try the members of the first three juntas, but the military justice system stalled, and in 1984 jurisdiction passed to the federal civilian appeals court in Buenos Aires. The trial began on 22 April 1985, with prosecutor Julio Strassera and deputy prosecutor Luis Moreno Ocampo presenting cases drawn from CONADEP's files and hundreds of new witnesses. The prosecution selected 709 cases, and the court heard testimony from more than 800 witnesses, many of them survivors describing torture and detention in public sessions that were reported, in audio and in print, to the nation. Defense lawyers argued that the actions were acts of war against subversion and that the commanders had acted legitimately.

The court delivered its judgment on 9 December 1985. It found that a system of repression, including clandestine detention, torture, and killing, had been carried out under orders from the commanders. It convicted five of the nine defendants. General Jorge Rafael Videla and Admiral Emilio Massera received life sentences, General Roberto Viola 17 years, Admiral Armando Lambruschini eight years, and Brigadier Orlando Agosti four years and six months. The court acquitted the others. The trial was a first in Latin America: a civilian court, through due process, convicted former heads of a military government for crimes committed in office. It also showed the limits of the courts' reach, since the sentences covered only the top commanders and the cases the prosecution selected.

The trial produced a backlash within the army, as lower-ranking officers feared widespread prosecutions. Hundreds of cases against mid-level officers were opened as victims filed complaints. The government's response, the Full Stop law of December 1986, set a deadline for new complaints and led to a surge of filings before it expired, and the Due Obedience law of June 1987 presumed that most officers below the rank of colonel had acted under orders. Both are examined in Section 94.15. Argentina's early prosecutions gave the country a model of judicial accountability, but their limitation and later reversal made the years that followed a continuing struggle.

Historical Vocabulary
Desaparecido

A desaparecido ("disappeared person") is someone seized by agents of the state or those acting with its consent, whose detention is then denied and whose fate is concealed. The practice, used by the Argentine, Chilean, Uruguayan, Paraguayan, Guatemalan, and other security forces, was designed to remove opponents without legal process and to deny relatives knowledge or burial.

The term became central to law and politics because disappearance is a continuing offense: as long as the fate of the victim is unknown, relatives argued, the crime continues. That reasoning later shaped decisions of the Inter-American Court and the argument that amnesty laws should not cover disappearances. The word describes a victim of a specific crime and should not be applied loosely to missing persons in other contexts.

Section 94.14

Truth Commissions across Latin America

A truth commission is an official, temporary body created to investigate a pattern of past violations and to report on it. Unlike a court, it does not usually punish, and unlike a historian, it works under a legal mandate and a deadline. Latin America produced some of the world's earliest and most influential commissions. Bolivia created a national commission of inquiry into disappearances in 1982, though it was dissolved without issuing a report. Argentina's CONADEP was the first to publish a comprehensive report. Uruguay's parliament investigated disappearances in 1985, and later a presidential commission examined cases in the early 2000s. Commissions differed in their mandates, access to evidence, treatment of victims, and political constraints.

Chile's National Commission on Truth and Reconciliation, known as the Rettig Commission after its chair, the jurist Raúl Rettig, was established by President Aylwin in April 1990 and delivered its report in February 1991. Its mandate was limited to deaths and disappearances, including those committed by agents of the state and by political violence, and it excluded torture and imprisonment that did not end in death. It received more than 3,400 complaints and recognized 2,279 victims under its mandate. The commission classified 1,068 as killed by state agents or people acting for them, 957 as detained and disappeared by state agents, 90 as killed in politically motivated attacks by private actors, and 164 as other victims of political violence. Thus 2,025 fell in the two directly state-agent categories; 2,115 were counted in the broader human-rights-violation grouping that also included the 90 private-actor cases. These categories should not be conflated. It did not name perpetrators, in part because the amnesty and the military's power made naming politically and legally fraught, and it could not itself refer individuals for prosecution. Aylwin presented the report in a televised address on 4 March 1991, and asked forgiveness on behalf of the state. A follow-up body continued to review cases and administer reparations, and the later Valech commissions, beginning in 2003, investigated torture and political imprisonment and recognized tens of thousands of victims.

El Salvador's commission arose from a peace process. Under the 1991 Mexico City agreements and the January 1992 peace accord, the United Nations created a three-member Commission on the Truth, chaired by the former Colombian president Belisario Betancur, which worked from July 1992 and presented its report, From Madness to Hope, on 15 March 1993. It received thousands of complaints, which it said were attributable overwhelmingly to state agents and death squads and in a small share to the guerrilla front, and it named individuals it held responsible for specific cases, including killings of Archbishop Óscar Romero and of six Jesuit priests, their housekeeper, and her daughter. Within days, the Legislative Assembly passed a general amnesty, which prevented prosecution of those named. Chapter 91 covers the war itself.

Guatemala had two parallel inquiries. The Archdiocese of Guatemala's human-rights office conducted the Recovery of Historical Memory project, whose report Guatemala: Never Again appeared on 24 April 1998; two days later its coordinator, Bishop Juan Gerardi, was murdered. The UN-backed Historical Clarification Commission, created under the 1994 peace negotiations, presented Guatemala: Memory of Silence in February 1999. It estimated that some 200,000 people were killed or disappeared, attributed the great majority of documented violations to state forces and allied paramilitary patrols, and concluded that the state had committed acts of genocide against Maya populations in certain regions. The commission was barred from naming individuals or assigning legal responsibility.

Commissions can be compared along several dimensions. Their mandates determined which crimes they could examine, and their access to evidence ranged from voluntary testimony to subpoena powers. Their treatment of victims varied between public hearings and closed testimony. They operated under political limits, such as amnesty laws and military pressure, that shaped whether they named perpetrators. Their reports could shape public memory and educational policy, ground reparations, and provide material for later prosecutions, but only some countries translated findings into trials. The commissions' effects were thus determined less by their texts than by what governments, courts, and civil society did afterward.

Section 94.15

Amnesty, Impunity, Human Rights, and the Courts

Amnesty laws were the principal legal barrier to accountability. Brazil's Amnesty Law of 28 August 1979 covered political crimes and related crimes committed between 1961 and 1979. It freed prisoners and allowed exiles to return, and it was widely understood by the military, though contested by its opponents, to protect state agents as well. Chile's Decree Law 2191 of April 1978 amnestied crimes committed between September 1973 and March 1978, the period of the worst violence. Argentina's military issued a self-amnesty in 1983 that Congress annulled, and Uruguay passed the Expiry Law in 1986. Elsewhere, comparable measures followed peace agreements and transitions.

Argentina's later laws show a government responding to military pressure. The Full Stop law of December 1986 set a 60-day limit for opening new criminal cases for crimes of the dictatorship, and the Due Obedience law of June 1987 established that officers below a certain rank were presumed to have acted under orders. Between them came the Easter 1987 rising, in which Lieutenant Colonel Aldo Rico and other middle-ranking officers occupied an army barracks, and Alfonsín negotiated an end, which critics saw as concession and supporters as defense of a fragile democracy. President Carlos Menem pardoned the convicted junta leaders and others in October 1989 and December 1990. Congress repealed the two laws in 1998 without retroactive effect, declared them null in 2003, and the Supreme Court held them unconstitutional in 2005, after which trials resumed.

Uruguay's Expiry Law, Law No. 15,848 of December 1986, declared that the state's authority to prosecute military and police personnel for crimes committed for political reasons before 1 March 1985 had lapsed. The law was passed after officers indicated they would not appear in court, and Sanguinetti's government and Congress accepted it. Opponents gathered signatures for a referendum, and on 16 April 1989 about 57 percent of voters chose to retain the law. The result showed that a majority, possibly out of fear of military reaction or desire to close the past, accepted the amnesty. Later reinterpretations narrowed its effect, and the Uruguayan Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional in a 2009 case, though a plebiscite that year to annul it fell short.

The Inter-American human-rights system added a new venue. The American Convention on Human Rights entered into force in 1978, creating a Commission and a Court. In its first contentious case, Velásquez Rodríguez v. Honduras, decided in July 1988, the Court found Honduras responsible for a disappearance and held that states have a duty to prevent, investigate, and punish such crimes. In 1992 the Commission concluded that the Argentine and Uruguayan amnesty laws were incompatible with the Convention. Later decisions, including the Court's 2001 ruling on Peruvian amnesty laws in the Barrios Altos case, the Chilean Almonacid Arellano case of 2006, and the Brazilian Gomes Lund case of 2010, extended this reasoning. Spain's judge Baltasar Garzón used universal jurisdiction to seek the extradition of Pinochet, who was arrested in London in October 1998 and eventually returned to Chile in March 2000, after which Chilean courts began stripping his immunity.

The Inter-American system worked slowly, depended on states' willingness to comply, and was not a substitute for domestic courts. Its effect was to supply norms, arguments, and a forum for victims and lawyers when domestic doors were closed, and to influence judges and legislators later. Compliance varied: Argentina's courts adopted its reasoning in 2005, while Brazil's Supreme Court in 2010 upheld the 1979 Amnesty Law as consistent with the Constitution, a decision at odds with the regional court's ruling the same year. The national and regional systems therefore interacted without any single outcome.

Historical Vocabulary
Impunity

Impunity refers to the failure to investigate, prosecute, and punish those responsible for serious crimes, especially violations by agents of the state. It can result from amnesty laws, pardons, judicial inaction, destruction of evidence, intimidation of witnesses, or military control of investigations.

The term describes a condition produced by law and institutions and does not by itself assert the guilt of any named person. Its importance in the chapter lies in explaining why elected governments faced continuing demands from victims, and why human-rights groups tied democracy to accountability.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Negotiated Compromise or Immediate Accountability?

The question: Were amnesties and limits on prosecution a necessary price of stable transitions, or did they unnecessarily weaken democracy and the rule of law?

Interpretation A — Compromise protected fragile democracies: Some scholars and participants argue that armed forces retained the power to overturn elected governments, and that trials could provoke coups, as the Argentine rebellions of 1987–1990 suggested. On this view, limited and gradual accountability, through truth commissions and later trials, allowed civilian institutions to consolidate before confronting the past.
Interpretation B — Impunity damaged democratic accountability: Other scholars and many victims' organizations argue that amnesties rewarded perpetrators, left security institutions intact, and taught citizens that powerful actors were above the law. They point to cases in which later trials went ahead without military revolt, such as Argentina after 2005, as evidence that the risks were overstated, and to the Inter-American rulings that treat disappearance and torture as crimes that cannot be forgiven by amnesty.

Evidence: The two readings rely on the military's behavior in each case, accounts by negotiators, public opinion data, court records, and comparisons between countries that prosecuted early and those that did not. Because a counterfactual cannot be observed, the evidence is contested.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how the 1980s transitions are judged, whether they appear as prudent or as lost opportunities, and what lessons later transitional processes draw.

Student task: Choose Argentina, Chile, or Uruguay, and assess which interpretation the evidence there best supports, noting what additional evidence would change your view.

Section 94.16

Families of the Disappeared, Reparations, Archives, and Historical Memory

Official bodies were not the only agents of accountability. Relatives of the disappeared sustained the demand for truth when governments preferred closure. In Chile the Association of Relatives of the Detained-Disappeared, formed in 1974, continued under democracy and campaigned for investigations. In Argentina the Mothers divided in 1986 over strategy, with one wing continuing as a confrontational movement and another, the Founding Line, accepting a more institutional approach, while the Grandmothers pursued the identification of children taken at birth. By the 1990s, adult children of the disappeared formed their own organization, H.I.J.O.S., which used public denunciation of known perpetrators when courts did not act.

Forensic science became a tool of memory. In 1984 an Argentine team of anthropologists, trained by the American forensic scientist Clyde Snow, began to exhume unmarked graves and identify remains, and it grew into the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team, which later worked in Chile, Guatemala, El Salvador, and elsewhere. Guatemala's Forensic Anthropology Foundation, created in 1992, supported exhumations of massacre victims in Maya communities, with families present. Genetic testing, developed with the Grandmothers, allowed identification of children raised under false identities. Identification of remains gave relatives the possibility of burial, and gave courts evidence, though in many cases bodies were never found.

Reparations took several forms. Chile's 1992 law created a national corporation to administer pensions, health care, and educational benefits for relatives of victims recognized by the Rettig Commission. Argentina approved compensation for the families of the disappeared in the 1990s. Brazil's 1995 law recognized as dead some 136 named disappeared persons and created a commission to examine others. Uruguay, Guatemala, and Peru also developed compensation programs of varying scope. Victims' groups often argued that money without truth and justice was inadequate, and they questioned whether compensation could substitute for accountability.

Archives were central to memory work. Brazil's Nunca Mais project had preserved judicial records at a time when the state would not. In Paraguay the discovery in December 1992 of police files in the Asunción suburb of Lambaré, by the lawyer Martín Almada and the judge José Agustín Fernández, produced a documentary record, the Archive of Terror, of arrests, torture, and cross-border cooperation. The records of security services in other countries were often destroyed, hidden, or classified, and access to them became a long political fight, with declassified foreign diplomatic and intelligence documents sometimes compensating. Archival work gave historians evidence that official narratives had withheld.

Memory itself was contested. Governments, armed forces, victims' groups, and political parties offered competing accounts, from the military's narrative of a necessary war to victims' narratives of state terrorism. Memorials and former detention sites became places of dispute: Chile's Villa Grimaldi, a former detention and torture center that was saved from demolition and opened as a Peace Park in 1997; Argentina's former naval school; and Montevideo's and Asunción's sites of detention. Survivors' accounts differed from official reports in detail and emphasis, and from each other, and historians must weigh testimony as evidence while recognizing that memory is shaped by trauma, politics, and time. The effort of the period was to move such stories from private grief to public record.

Section 94.17

Central America and the Caribbean: Uneven Paths to Democratic Government

In Central America and the Caribbean the return to elected government overlapped with civil wars, revolutions, foreign intervention, and state collapse. The category "transition" fits only loosely. Honduras held a constituent election in 1980 and a presidential election in November 1981, and Roberto Suazo Córdova of the Liberal Party took office in January 1982 under a new constitution. But the armed forces, under General Gustavo Álvarez Martínez until his removal in 1984, remained dominant, an intelligence battalion carried out disappearances, and the country served as a base for U.S.-supported Nicaraguan insurgents. A national human-rights commissioner was created in 1992, and its 1993 report documented 184 disappearances. A 1988 Inter-American Court decision, Velásquez Rodríguez, had already found the state responsible for a case. Constitutional reform in 1998–1999 created a civilian defense ministry and ended the armed forces commander's autonomy.

Guatemala returned to civilian president in January 1986 when Vinicio Cerezo, a Christian Democrat, took office after elections organized by the military government and a new constitution. The army, which had waged a campaign of massacres against Maya communities in the early 1980s, remained the strongest institution and retained its counterinsurgency structures, including the civil defense patrols that organized peasants. Cerezo's room to act was narrow, and military coup attempts occurred in 1988 and 1989. The peace negotiations of the 1990s, concluded in December 1996, produced a political opening and the truth commission described in Section 94.14. In El Salvador, elections in 1982 and 1984 brought civilian presidents, notably José Napoleón Duarte, while the war continued, and the 1992 peace accords reformed the armed forces and created a new civilian police. Chapters 90 and 91 narrate these wars.

Nicaragua's path differed. After the 1979 revolution (Chapter 89) and the Contra war (Chapter 90), the Sandinista government held elections in 1984 and, under a regional peace framework, a second election on 25 February 1990. Violeta Chamorro, the publisher of the opposition newspaper La Prensa and leader of a broad coalition, defeated President Daniel Ortega with about 55 percent of the vote. The Sandinistas accepted the result, a notable peaceful transfer after a decade of war, and the transfer took place on 25 April 1990. The settlement left Humberto Ortega, the former president's brother, in command of the army until 1995 and Sandinistas in control of unions and parts of the state, so that the new government governed under constraints. Chamorro presided over demobilization of the Contras and reductions in the army.

Panama's case was shaped by external force. Manuel Noriega had annulled the May 1989 election in which the opposition candidate, Guillermo Endara, appeared to have won, and the U.S. invasion of 20 December 1989 (Chapter 92) removed him. Endara was sworn in at a U.S. military base on the day of the invasion, so that Panama's civilian government began with a foreign intervention that many Panamanians welcomed and others condemned as a violation of sovereignty. The Panamanian Defense Forces were dissolved, and a 1994 constitutional amendment abolished the standing army, leaving Panama, like Costa Rica, without one. The case illustrates that the restoration of an elected government could come about through means that created their own questions of legitimacy.

Haiti's story was the most turbulent. After the fall of Jean-Claude Duvalier on 7 February 1986, a military-dominated council governed. A 1987 Constitution, approved by referendum, recognized Haitian Creole alongside French, but an election scheduled for November 1987 was abandoned after a massacre of voters, and successive military and civilian-military governments followed. Ertha Pascal-Trouillot, a Supreme Court justice, became interim president in March 1990, Haiti's first woman head of state, and the election of December 1990 was won by the priest Jean-Bertrand Aristide with about two thirds of the vote. He took office in February 1991 and was overthrown by the military in September 1991. A U.S.-led intervention restored him in October 1994. Haiti's experience shows how foreign intervention, military power, and deep social divisions could keep an electoral transition incomplete.

Across these cases, students should distinguish negotiated elections, such as Nicaragua's, from political reform under military tutelage, such as Honduras and Guatemala, foreign intervention, such as Panama and Haiti, and postwar reconstruction, such as El Salvador. Each carried different institutional legacies, and none can be read as a simple return to a previous democratic order, because several of these countries had never had one.

Section 94.18

Mexico, Colombia, Venezuela, and the Limits of a Regional Democratic Wave

Not every Latin American country emerged from military dictatorship, and treating the region as a single wave obscures the different problems of Mexico, Colombia, Costa Rica, and Venezuela. These countries had civilian governments and regular elections throughout the period, yet their democratic quality was questioned for different reasons: dominant-party control in Mexico, restricted competition and violence in Colombia, and growing dissatisfaction with party-dominated politics in Venezuela. Costa Rica, without an army since 1948, stands as the most stable case.

Mexico's Institutional Revolutionary Party had governed since 1929 through control of the presidency, labor and peasant organizations, and the electoral machinery, and its system combined periodic elections with limited competition, repression of dissent (notably the 1968 Tlatelolco massacre and a "dirty war" against rural guerrillas in the 1970s), and a state-managed media. Reforms began in 1977, when a law legalized new parties and created proportional representation seats in the Chamber of Deputies. The presidential election of July 1988 proved a turning point: Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas, a former PRI member who left the party to lead a leftist coalition, appeared to be ahead when the vote-counting system failed, and Carlos Salinas of the PRI was declared the winner amid widespread allegations of fraud. A federal electoral institute was created in 1990 and made autonomous in 1996. In 1989 the National Action Party won a governorship, the first opposition victory in a state election recognized by the PRI, and in 1997 the PRI lost its majority in the Chamber and Cárdenas was elected head of government of Mexico City. On 2 July 2000, Vicente Fox of the National Action Party won the presidency, and he took office on 1 December. The Mexican transition was therefore a gradual electoral process, not a break with military rule, and it left questions about past repression that a special prosecutor, created in 2001, addressed with limited success.

Colombia's National Front, the 1958 agreement under which the Liberal and Conservative parties alternated the presidency and shared offices until 1974, had ended civil war between the parties but restricted competition, and it left rural inequalities and armed groups unaddressed (Chapter 83). By the 1980s guerrilla groups, drug-trafficking organizations, and paramilitary forces added to the violence, and thousands of members of the Patriotic Union, a party formed from the FARC peace talks of 1985, were killed in subsequent years. The 1991 Constitution, written by an assembly elected in December 1990 that included demobilized guerrillas of the M-19 and Indigenous representatives, established a constitutional court, a protective action known as the tutela that allows citizens to seek rapid judicial protection of rights, and provisions for Indigenous territories and Black communities. Colombia thus experienced constitutional reform and electoral continuity amid severe violence, a combination later chapters on the cocaine economy and peace process examine.

Venezuela's Punto Fijo system, established in 1958, had alternated the Democratic Action and COPEI parties in government, financed by oil revenue (Chapter 105). By the late 1980s the system's legitimacy was eroding under economic strain and perceptions of corruption. The riots known as the Caracazo in February 1989 and two coup attempts in 1992, one led by Lieutenant Colonel Hugo Chávez, revealed the depth of the discontent, and President Carlos Andrés Pérez was removed from office in 1993 on corruption charges. Venezuela is a reminder that a country counted as a long-standing democracy could experience a crisis of representation without a military regime having preceded it.

These cases caution against assuming that democracy was uniformly "returning." The regional wave described by scholars primarily concerned South America and Central America. Mexico, Colombia, Costa Rica, and Venezuela had different problems, and in each democratic dissatisfaction was a political fact that shaped later developments.

Section 94.19

Fragile Democracies: Coups, Corruption, Violence, and Political Crisis

Civilian rule did not remove the risk of institutional breakdown. Several elected governments faced military rebellion, presidential seizures of power, scandals that led to removal, and violence that tested constitutional order. These episodes show that electoral legitimacy did not always protect constitutional government and that outcomes depended on the strength of institutions and the choices of political and military actors.

Argentina's military rebellions were the most direct challenge to a government pursuing accountability. In April 1987 officers led by Lieutenant Colonel Aldo Rico occupied a barracks, demanding an end to trials. In January 1988 Rico rose again, and in December 1988 Colonel Mohamed Alí Seineldín led another rising. Their rebels, known as the carapintadas ("painted faces"), claimed to defend the army's honor. A larger rising on 3 December 1990, also led by Seineldín, was put down by loyalist forces, after which the government tried the leaders. The risings influenced the Full Stop and Due Obedience laws and the pardons, and they show why civilian leaders feared military reaction.

Paraguay's army and the Colorado Party remained entangled. In April 1996 General Lino Oviedo, commander of the army, refused an order to retire from President Juan Carlos Wasmosy, and the standoff was resolved under pressure from Paraguayan civil society, Brazil, Argentina, and the United States, backed by the regional trade bloc Mercosur, which warned that a coup would threaten Paraguay's membership. In March 1999 the vice president, Luis María Argaña, an adversary of Oviedo, was assassinated, and the killings of protesters that followed led to the resignation of President Raúl Cubas. Paraguay's institutions survived these crises, but they showed the fragility of civilian control.

Peru's president Alberto Fujimori, elected in 1990 in a runoff, faced a Congress, courts, and a war with the insurgent Shining Path. On 5 April 1992 he suspended the Constitution, dissolved Congress, and intervened in the judiciary with the support of the armed forces, an act commonly called a self-coup (autogolpe). He promised to restore order and had popular support at first, in part because of the insurgency and hyperinflation. International pressure led him to call elections for a constituent congress, which wrote the 1993 Constitution, and he won reelection in 1995 and, in a disputed contest, in 2000. The capture of the Shining Path leader Abimael Guzmán in September 1992 strengthened his position. In 1995 a broad amnesty law shielded military personnel, including those responsible for a 1991 massacre in Barrios Altos, a measure the Inter-American Court later declared incompatible with the Convention. Fujimori fled to Japan and resigned by fax in November 2000, and Peruvian courts later convicted him of human-rights violations and corruption, which belongs to the later history of accountability.

Other crises tested constitutional rules. In Brazil, President Collor, elected in 1989, resigned in December 1992 as the Senate was about to try him after the Chamber of Deputies voted to impeach him on corruption charges, a case that showed constitutional mechanisms working. In Guatemala, President Jorge Serrano attempted a self-coup in May 1993, and it failed within days after opposition from the army leadership, the Constitutional Court, business groups, and the United States. In Venezuela, the coups of 1992 and the impeachment of Pérez in 1993 fit the same broader pattern. In Ecuador, a president was removed by Congress in 1997 and another overthrown in January 2000 in an uprising involving Indigenous leaders and junior army officers.

Together these episodes show that elections did not guarantee that presidents, militaries, or legislatures would accept constitutional limits. They also show institutional resilience, since in many cases the crisis was resolved within a constitutional framework or through combined domestic and regional pressure. Scholars have used terms such as "delegative democracy," coined by Guillermo O'Donnell, to describe elected presidents who governed as if the mandate exempted them from checks. Students should treat such labels as analytical proposals and test them against the evidence of each case.

Section 94.20

The Meaning and Limits of Latin America's Return to Democracy

By 2000 almost every Latin American government was chosen through competitive elections, with the principal exceptions of Cuba and a few contested cases. This was a historic change from the situation of the mid-1970s, and it endured through severe economic crises, which earlier democracies had not survived. Citizens voted in large numbers, parties alternated in power, and open debate was a regular feature of public life. These achievements deserve to be stated plainly before the limits are described.

The limits, however, were substantial and varied. On elections, countries differed in the fairness of competition and the strength of parties. On civilian control, the Argentine, Brazilian, and Chilean militaries had retained different degrees of autonomy, and in Central America and the Andes the armed forces continued to hold large prerogatives. On constitutional guarantees, new texts expanded rights, but courts, police, and prisons often failed to protect them for the poor. On human rights, truth commissions, trials, and reparations advanced unevenly, and amnesties persisted in many places. On participation, women, Indigenous peoples, and Afro-descendant communities gained recognition and organization but remained underrepresented in office and unequal in access to land and services. On stability, coups and self-coups showed continuing vulnerability, and on memory, societies remained divided about what the dictatorships had been.

Comparing the pathways clarifies why outcomes differed. Transitions by military defeat, as in Argentina, gave civilians leverage that negotiated transitions, as in Uruguay and Chile, did not. Where the armed forces left on their own schedule, as in Brazil, they kept institutional continuity. Where a single party outlasted a dictator, as in Paraguay, the party's dominance constrained competition. Where war and foreign intervention shaped the opening, as in Central America and Haiti, democratic institutions were built on contested foundations. The route of exit mattered, but later events, including the choices of presidents, judges, activists, and voters, also changed outcomes, as Argentina's reversal of its amnesty laws in the 2000s shows.

The chapter also leaves a bridge to the next. The same civilian governments that were building democratic institutions in the 1980s were doing so in the midst of the debt crisis and, by the early 1990s, under pressure to adopt market-oriented reforms. Chapter 95 explains the Washington Consensus, privatization, and NAFTA, and the economic policies that shaped the lives of voters who had just regained political rights. Whether democratic governments could address inequality and social exclusion, and whether citizens would continue to support them if they did not, were questions the 1990s and later decades would answer.

The question with which this chapter ends is whether democracy had become both an electoral system and an effective system of accountable government. The honest answer for 2000 is that it had become the first in most of the region and the second only in part, and that the difference mattered most to those whom institutions served least. The struggle over truth, justice, and rights, begun under dictatorship, continued under elected governments, and the subsequent decades would show that the return to democracy was the beginning of a long process and not its completion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Latin America's return to elected civilian government between about 1978 and 2000 and argued that the return was a real achievement whose meaning was settled only gradually. Its central claim is that democratic restoration and democratic accountability were different processes. Elections, legal parties, and peaceful transfers of office came first and came in most countries within a generation. Civilian command of armed forces, independent courts, protection of rights for all citizens, and a public reckoning with state violence took longer and remained incomplete.

The authoritarian governments of the 1970s were varied. Institutional military regimes in Brazil, Argentina, and Chile, personal regimes in Paraguay, Nicaragua, and Haiti, and reformist military governments in Peru and Panama all restricted competition and relied on security agencies that practiced censorship, detention, torture, and, in the Southern Cone, disappearance. A group of countries, including Colombia, Venezuela, Costa Rica, and Mexico, retained electoral systems of differing quality, so that the regional story has exceptions from the outset.

Military legitimacy eroded for several reasons that interacted. Guerrilla threats subsided, which weakened the emergency justification for rule. The costs of governing divided officers, and economic crisis after 1982 discredited regimes that had promised competent management. Corruption and succession disputes weakened some, and Argentina's defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War destroyed its junta's authority. Economic decline did not automatically produce democratization, as Chile's survival of the recession of 1982–1983 shows, and the outcomes depended on how political actors used the openings.

Civil society supplied much of the pressure. Unions, churches, professional associations, student groups, women's organizations, and above all the families of the disappeared, such as the Mothers and Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo, made public claims that regimes found hard to suppress. Women's mobilization in particular, framed as moral rather than partisan, created spaces for political speech. International human-rights networks amplified these voices without replacing them.

Transitions followed several routes: scheduled withdrawal, negotiated pacts, collapse after military defeat, military-managed electoral return, and palace coup. In Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia, the armed forces organized or conceded elections between 1979 and 1982. Argentina's civilians won leverage from defeat in 1983, Uruguay's negotiated transition produced a civilian government in 1985, and Brazil's gradual opening ended in an indirect election the same year, four years before the first direct presidential vote. Chile's opposition used the dictatorship's own plebiscite to defeat Pinochet in 1988, and Paraguay's Stroessner was overthrown by a fellow general in 1989.

The manner of exit shaped what followed. Constitutions were rewritten or amended, with Brazil's 1988 text and Paraguay's 1992 text responding to the experience of authoritarian rule and expanding rights, and Chile's inherited 1980 constitution preserving authoritarian enclaves until 2005. Elections gained legitimacy through alternation in power, as in Argentina, Uruguay, Bolivia, and Nicaragua, though campaign fairness and party strength varied. Civil-military settlements ranged from Argentina's confrontation to Brazil's negotiated continuity and Chile's protected autonomy.

Accountability followed distinct paths. Argentina's CONADEP and the Trial of the Juntas established the scale and method of state terror and convicted its top commanders, but the Full Stop and Due Obedience laws and the pardons curtailed further trials until they were annulled in the 2000s. Chile's Rettig Commission documented deaths and disappearances without naming perpetrators, Uruguay's Expiry Law was upheld in a referendum, and El Salvador's and Guatemala's commissions established responsibility without immediate judicial consequences. The Inter-American system developed norms against amnesties for disappearance and torture, interacting unevenly with national courts.

Citizenship expanded on paper before it did in practice. Women's organizations won equality clauses and quotas, Indigenous movements obtained constitutional recognition in Brazil, Colombia, Paraguay, Bolivia, Argentina, and elsewhere, and Afro-Latin American communities secured collective land rights in Brazil and Colombia. Implementation lagged, and elected governments did not automatically represent previously excluded groups, which advanced where they organized to claim inclusion.

Central America and the Caribbean followed paths shaped by war, revolution, and intervention, with Honduras and Guatemala under military tutelage, Nicaragua's electoral transfer in 1990, Panama's post-invasion government, and Haiti's incomplete transition. Mexico's gradual electoral reform culminated in the 2000 alternation, Colombia's 1991 Constitution coexisted with severe violence, and Venezuela's party system showed strain. The wave was therefore not uniform, and several countries did not emerge from military dictatorship at all.

The chapter's three conclusions are these. First, democratic restoration came through distinct pathways, each with different institutional consequences. Second, civilian rule did not immediately dismantle authoritarian legacies in security institutions, amnesty arrangements, courts, and social exclusion. Third, historical memory and accountability became continuing democratic struggles in which truth commissions, trials, victims' organizations, and constitutional reform helped determine what the return to democracy meant. Chapter 95 turns to the economic reforms that governments pursued in the same years.

Key Terms

Transition
The period between one political regime and another, here the movement from authoritarian rule to elected civilian government.
Democratic consolidation
The process by which democratic rules and institutions become the accepted framework of politics, so that major actors treat them as the only legitimate way to compete for power.
Pacted transition
A change of regime negotiated between outgoing rulers and opposition leaders, as in Uruguay's Naval Club Pact of 1984.
Reserved domain
An area of state power, such as military appointments or budgets, protected from elected officials after a transition.
Bureaucratic-authoritarian state
Guillermo O'Donnell's term for Southern Cone military regimes that allied officers, technocrats, and business interests to exclude labor and the left.
Plebiscite
A direct vote on a single question or proposal, such as Chile's 1988 vote on Pinochet's continuation.
Indirect election
Election of an executive by an assembly or electoral college rather than by citizens, as in Brazil in 1985.
Diretas Já
The 1983–1984 Brazilian campaign for direct presidential elections.
Constituent assembly
A body elected or appointed to write a constitution, such as Brazil's of 1987–1988.
Civilian control
Elected officials' authority over defense policy, budgets, appointments, and security institutions.
Authoritarian enclave
An institution or rule inherited from an authoritarian regime that limits elected government, such as appointed senators in Chile.
Carapintadas
Argentine army rebels of 1987–1990 who demanded an end to trials of officers.
Alternation in power
The transfer of government from one party or coalition to a rival after an election.
Founding election
The first competitive election after authoritarian rule.
Self-coup (autogolpe)
An elected executive's illegal seizure of extra powers, such as Fujimori's 1992 dissolution of Congress.
Delegative democracy
O'Donnell's term for systems in which elected presidents govern as if their mandate freed them from institutional checks.
Desaparecido
A person seized by state agents whose detention is denied and whose fate is concealed.
Truth commission
A temporary official body that investigates past violations and reports on them, usually without judicial power.
Transitional justice
The set of mechanisms, including trials, truth-seeking, reparations, and institutional reform, used to address past abuses.
CONADEP
Argentina's National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons, created in December 1983, which reported in September 1984.
Nunca Más
"Never again": the title of CONADEP's report and a slogan of human-rights movements.
Trial of the Juntas
The 1985 civilian trial of nine former Argentine commanders, ending in five convictions.
Full Stop and Due Obedience laws
Argentine laws of 1986 and 1987 that limited new prosecutions of officers; annulled in 2003 and held unconstitutional in 2005.
Expiry Law (Ley de Caducidad)
Uruguay's Law 15,848 of 1986, declaring that the state's power to prosecute certain past crimes had lapsed.
Amnesty
A legal measure that bars prosecution or punishment for specified acts.
Impunity
The failure to investigate, prosecute, and punish those responsible for serious crimes.
Rettig Commission
Chile's National Commission on Truth and Reconciliation (1990–1991), limited to deaths and disappearances.
Historical Clarification Commission
Guatemala's UN-backed truth commission, which reported in 1999.
Inter-American Court of Human Rights
The regional court established under the American Convention, which ruled on disappearances and amnesty laws.
Universal jurisdiction
The principle that some grave crimes may be prosecuted by courts of states other than where they occurred, invoked in Pinochet's 1998 arrest.
Reparations
Compensation and other measures, such as pensions, health care, and recognition, offered to victims of abuses.
Forensic anthropology
The use of skeletal and genetic evidence to identify remains and document violence.
Historical memory
The ways societies remember, commemorate, and dispute the past, including through memorials, archives, and education.
Tutela
Colombia's constitutional action, created in 1991, for rapid judicial protection of fundamental rights.
Quilombo
A community founded by escaped enslaved people in Brazil, whose descendants gained land claims under the 1988 Constitution.
ILO Convention 169
The 1989 international treaty on Indigenous and tribal peoples, ratified by several Latin American states in the 1990s.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a region that in the mid-1970s was largely governed by armed forces came to be governed, by 2000, through contested elections. That change was won by citizens who organized under danger, by politicians who negotiated with those they distrusted, and in some cases by soldiers who calculated that leaving was better than staying. It also shows that political regimes are not transformed in a single moment: each transition carried forward institutions, laws, and habits of the one before.

The chapter also reveals a broader pattern of Latin American history, in which formal change and social reality advance at different speeds. Constitutions that promised rights to Indigenous peoples, women, and Afro-descendant communities were written before courts and agencies could deliver them, and amnesty laws that ended one conflict opened another about justice. The persistence of such gaps helps explain why later movements, including the Indigenous resurgence of Chapter 96, drew on democratic language to press claims that democratic institutions had not satisfied.

The settlements described here shaped later developments in ways that remain visible. Disputes over amnesty, military prerogatives, and memorials continued into the twenty-first century, and several countries revisited their earlier decisions. The same civilian governments faced economic pressures that Chapter 93 explained and that Chapter 95 follows into the liberalizing reforms of the 1990s. Whether elected governments could address inequality while sustaining popular support became a central question of the following decades.

For the larger history of Latin America, this chapter is a hinge between Volume IV's revolutions and dictatorships and the rest of Volume V. It shows that the Cold War's end, the debt crisis, and domestic movements converged to change political life, while leaving unsettled what democracy would mean in practice. Students should carry forward the distinction between electoral and accountable government, since it organizes much of what follows.
