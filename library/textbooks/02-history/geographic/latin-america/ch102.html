Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, the Gulf Cartel, and Los Zetas

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: One Trade, Many Organizations

When the Guadalajara-centered networks weakened after 1989, Mexican drug trafficking did not end, and it did not pass to a single successor. It passed to several organizations whose bases, methods, and relationships with the state differed, and whose competition would reshape Mexican public life.

On 14 January 1996, Mexican federal agents arrested Juan García Ábrego near Monterrey and expelled him to the United States the same day. García Ábrego, who led the organization based on the Gulf coast in Tamaulipas, was a U.S.-born citizen, and that status gave Mexican authorities a legal route for removing him that was not available for most traffickers. Less than two years later, in July 1997, Amado Carrillo Fuentes, who built the most profitable organization of the Ciudad Juárez region, died in a Mexico City clinic after cosmetic surgery. Between those two events, in February 1997, Mexico's newly appointed anti-drug chief, General Jesús Gutiérrez Rebollo, was himself arrested on suspicion of protecting the Juárez organization. Taken together, these episodes show the chapter's field of study: organizations that moved drugs, laundered money, and bargained with officials, and a state whose institutions both fought and, in places, served them.

This chapter follows Chapter 101, which traced the origins of Mexican trafficking from early prohibition through the Guadalajara networks and the crisis that followed the murder of DEA agent Enrique Camarena. It takes up the story in 1989 and follows five organizational lineages: the Sinaloa networks associated with Joaquín Guzmán Loera and Ismael Zambada García, the Tijuana organization of the Arellano Félix family, the Juárez organization of the Carrillo Fuentes family, the Gulf organization of Tamaulipas, and Los Zetas, which began as the Gulf organization's armed wing and became an independent enterprise in 2010. The chapter explains how these organizations were structured, which corridors and plazas they contested, how they obtained protection, how they formed and broke alliances, and why Mexico's drug trade fragmented as it grew.

The chapter's argument is that these organizations were products of specific markets, geographies, and political arrangements rather than interchangeable "cartels." The word cartel itself is a misleading shorthand, because economists use it for firms that fix prices, and these organizations were competitors as often as partners. They differed in structure. The Tijuana organization was a family enterprise that depended on a small number of brothers. The Sinaloa networks were a flexible alliance of semi-autonomous leaders. The Zetas built a hierarchical, paramilitary organization that preyed on whole populations as well as on rival traffickers. Their histories also depended on the Mexican political system. Under the long rule of the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI), protection tended to be negotiated through a small number of senior officials and regional bosses, and the erosion of that system after 2000 changed incentives and violence.

The chapter also shows the limits of enforcement that targeted individuals. The arrest or death of a leader, a recurring event in this chapter, rarely ended the activity of the organization he led. Sometimes it produced succession, sometimes splitting, and sometimes open war. The same period brought a changing international market. Colombian suppliers increasingly paid Mexican partners in cocaine rather than cash, as discussed in Chapter 101. Mexican organizations thereby gained a larger share of the business, a reason for their growing wealth and ambition. U.S. demand, U.S.-origin firearms, and U.S. banks were integral to the story, but the actors examined here were Mexican, and the chapter treats them as agents with their own interests rather than as extensions of Colombian or American policy.

Evidence requires care. Much of what the public believes about these organizations comes from journalism, memoirs, police intelligence, television, and ballads (narcocorridos) that may exaggerate or romanticize. Court records, indictments, plea agreements, trial testimony, and government sanctions notices are more reliable, though each reflects the prosecution's aims and the incentives of cooperating witnesses. Mexican official statements about arrests, seizures, and deaths have often been contradicted by later investigation. Academic research by scholars such as Luis Astorga, Benjamin T. Smith, Carlos Pérez Ricart, and others, and investigative reporting by Mexican journalists, many of whom paid for their work with their lives, supply a necessary corrective. Throughout the chapter, allegations, indictments, convictions, and established facts are distinguished.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It does not retell the Guadalajara period of Chapter 101. It treats the post-2006 federal offensive, the mass violence of that period, and the disappearances that followed as the subject of Chapter 103, and it treats the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, fentanyl, and the synthetic-drug economy as the subject of Chapter 104. Where events after 2006 appear here, they appear because they conclude a lineage of organizational history begun earlier. The final section carries four of these lineages to 2026, in particular the Sinaloa leadership trials and the 2025 U.S. terrorism designations, and it marks clearly what remains unresolved.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the weakening of the Guadalajara-centered networks after 1989 produced several regional organizations rather than a single successor.
  2. Describe the structure, base of operations, and principal corridors of the Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and Gulf organizations.
  3. Compare family-based, alliance-based, and paramilitary organizational models, using the Arellano Félix, Sinaloa, and Zetas examples.
  4. Analyze how protection by police, military officers, and politicians functioned under PRI rule and how it changed after 2000.
  5. Trace the origins of Los Zetas in military defection and explain their break from the Gulf organization in 2010.
  6. Assess the consequences of arresting or killing organizational leaders, using cases from 1993 to 2010.
  7. Evaluate the roles of money laundering, firearms, prisons, and corruption as shared infrastructure for competing organizations.
  8. Distinguish allegations, indictments, guilty pleas, convictions, and government designations in the sources used to study these organizations.
  9. Interpret competing scholarly explanations of why fragmentation was accompanied by escalating violence.
  10. Connect the organizational history of this chapter to the militarized conflict of Chapter 103 and the transformed markets of Chapter 104.

Historical Context

Chapter 101 explained that Mexican trafficking grew from older systems of border smuggling, rural cultivation of opium poppy and marijuana, and local political protection. By the early 1980s, a group of Sinaloa-born traffickers around Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo had made Guadalajara an important coordinating center and had linked Mexican routes to Colombian cocaine suppliers. The 1985 murder of Enrique Camarena, the arrests of Rafael Caro Quintero and Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo, and the 1989 arrest of Félix Gallardo broke that cooperation. Chapter 101 also warned that the popular image of a single Guadalajara "cartel" is contested, and that stories of a formal division of Mexican territory among successors remain unproven. This chapter inherits that caution.

Three structural conditions shaped what followed. The first was the geography of the border. Mexico shares roughly 3,100 kilometers of frontier with the United States, and the movement of goods across it is among the largest in the world. A handful of crossing points, and the cities that serve them, therefore carry exceptional value. Traffickers and officials alike came to speak of plazas, the urban and regional trafficking corridors whose control, or at least tolerance, determined who could move drugs through them. Tijuana and Mexicali on the Pacific side, Ciudad Juárez opposite El Paso, and Nuevo Laredo, Reynosa, and Matamoros in Tamaulipas were the great plazas of this period.

The second condition was the political system. The PRI governed Mexico continuously at the national level from 1929 to 2000, and for much of that time its control of governorships, police forces, and federal agencies allowed illicit activity to be managed through informal arrangements. Historians disagree about how centrally those arrangements were coordinated, a question taken up in Chapter 101. What is clear is that a hierarchical party-state could usually impose a degree of order on corruption, and that the arrangements were weakened by economic crisis in the 1980s and 1990s, by the 1994 political assassinations, and by the opening of electoral competition. The third condition was the market. The Colombian state's campaigns against the Medellín and Cali organizations, covered in Chapters 97 to 100, reduced the Colombians' control of transport routes through the Caribbean and enlarged the role of Mexican intermediaries, who moved cocaine into the United States and increasingly took a share of it.

A caution about vocabulary applies to the whole chapter. Names such as "Sinaloa Cartel," "Tijuana Cartel," "Juárez Cartel," and "Gulf Cartel" are conventions, partly created by police agencies, journalists, and later by U.S. legal designations. They are useful but imprecise. Each refers to a shifting set of leaders, families, local bosses, enforcers, corrupt officials, and business partners, often with changing alliances. This chapter uses the conventional names while noting their limits, and it avoids describing these organizations as though their membership, rank, and finances were known with certainty. Where estimates of size or revenue appear, the chapter identifies them as estimates.

DIVISION I · 1989–1997

Regional Organizations after Guadalajara

Section 102.1

A Market without a Coordinator, 1989–1993

The arrest of Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo on 8 April 1989 removed a figure whom Mexican and U.S. officials regarded as a principal coordinator of trafficking relationships. Chapter 101 explained why historians dispute how centralized his role was. What is not disputed is that the traffickers who had worked in his orbit did not stop working. Those from Sinaloa, including Joaquín Guzmán Loera, Héctor Luis Palma Salazar, and Ismael Zambada García, continued to operate from Sinaloa and other states. The Arellano Félix family, also from Sinaloa, concentrated on Tijuana. Amado Carrillo Fuentes, whose uncle Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo had been arrested in 1985, built on family connections and on the Ciudad Juárez plaza. In Tamaulipas, the Gulf organization of Juan García Ábrego continued a regional tradition that had a distinct history, as Chapter 101 noted.

Popular accounts claim that Félix Gallardo, from prison, divided the country among his lieutenants at a meeting in Acapulco in 1989, assigning each a plaza and thereby creating the later cartels. This story appears in journalism, memoir, and television, but documentary corroboration is thin, and several scholars treat it as a retrospective simplification. A better-supported account describes a gradual separation. Leaders who had cooperated found it harder to do so after Camarena's murder and Félix Gallardo's arrest, because enforcement pressure rose, trust fell, and the political protection that had supported coordination was less certain. They then built or defended their own territories, sometimes with former partners and sometimes against them. The result was not a plan but an outcome.

Market conditions also favored regional organizations. The Colombian cocaine suppliers, whose Caribbean routes were under pressure, relied more on Mexican land and air routes, and Mexican organizations that could move large loads across the border had bargaining power. Each border plaza had its own geography, local officials, and informal rules. A trafficker who controlled one plaza could not easily control another, and the number of organizations capable of operating at scale grew. The decade that followed was therefore one of competition among regional enterprises, with episodic cooperation, rather than domination by one.

The difference between a successor institution and a series of successors is fundamental. A single command could have enforced collective rules and punished defectors without recurring territorial wars. The actual record points instead toward bargains that depended on personalities, kinship, access to officials, and geography. An agreement to share a shipment did not bind the next shipment. A common birthplace did not prevent enmity. Nor did the term plaza describe an uncontested tract of land. It referred to a shifting set of permissions, demands, risks, and commercial opportunities. The stories that turn this complex transition into a ceremonial division of the country are attractive because they supply a clear founding moment, but they should be compared against the later evidence of overlapping routes and rapidly changing partners.

The shift was also an institutional history of the border. San Diego–Tijuana, El Paso–Ciudad Juárez, and the lower Rio Grande offered different customs facilities, transportation industries, populations, and opportunities for illicit concealment. Legitimate cross-border trade created large volumes of freight and passenger travel; criminal intermediaries exploited part of that infrastructure without controlling the legitimate economy. A route could become valuable because of a friendly official or a reliable supplier, then suddenly become dangerous after an arrest. Historical geography therefore helps explain why the same Colombian demand for transportation produced markedly different Mexican businesses. Competition unfolded inside real cities whose residents and elected officials had interests separate from the criminal entrepreneurs who sought to use them.

Section 102.2

The Sinaloa Networks and the Pacific Corridor

Sinaloa occupies a narrow coastal plain between the Pacific and the Sierra Madre Occidental. Its irrigated valleys produce vegetables, grain, and other export crops, its ports and fishing fleets provide maritime access, and its mountains, part of the Golden Triangle described in Chapter 101, have long been used for poppy and marijuana cultivation. Many traffickers came from small highland communities, such as La Tuna and El Álamo in the municipality of Badiraguato, where legal livelihoods were scarce. That regional origin does not mean the whole state was a criminal territory. Most Sinaloans were farmers, workers, merchants, and professionals, and the state also produced victims of the trade and critics of it. The organizations that bear its name drew on regional networks while operating well beyond them.

Joaquín Guzmán Loera, born in 1954 in La Tuna, rose through the Sinaloa networks of the 1980s, working with the Guadalajara-period organizers and then with Palma and Zambada. By the early 1990s he was among several figures seeking to control routes through Sinaloa, Sonora, and Baja California, and to ship cocaine northward through the Pacific. Ismael Zambada García, from El Álamo, took a different approach. He avoided publicity, cultivated relationships with local communities and officials, and survived for decades without arrest, which earned him a reputation as the organization's strategist. In the guilty plea that preceded his 2026 sentencing, Zambada acknowledged that between 1980 and 2024 his organization had been responsible for transporting at least 1.5 million kilograms of cocaine, most of it bound for the United States. That figure is an admission in a plea agreement, not an independent measurement, but it indicates the order of magnitude that U.S. prosecutors attributed to the Sinaloa networks.

The Sinaloa organization is better understood as an alliance than a corporation. Guzmán, Zambada, and other leaders, including Ignacio "Nacho" Coronel Villarreal, commanded their own people, financed some shipments jointly and others separately, and shared certain relationships with suppliers, corrupt officials, and transport specialists. Authorities and journalists later called this arrangement the Federation, a term examined in the vocabulary box later in this chapter. Its resilience came from decentralization. The loss of one leader removed neither the others nor the underlying business relationships, which helps explain why Sinaloa survived arrests that devastated more centralized organizations. Its weakness was the opposite one: internal rivalry, and the possibility that a dispute between partners could split the alliance, as it did in 2008 and again after 2024.

Historical Vocabulary
Plaza

In Spanish, a plaza is a square or marketplace. In the language of Mexican trafficking and policing, it came to mean a city or corridor through which illicit goods move, and by extension the right, whether purchased from officials or imposed by force, to operate there. "Holding the plaza" meant controlling, or at least being tolerated within, a territory.

The word matters because it captures the economic logic of competition. The most valuable plazas were border crossings, and an organization that controlled one could charge other traffickers for passage, a payment often called a "tax" or cobro de piso. Students should avoid reading the term as proof of exclusive territorial control. Many plazas were contested, shared, or leased, and their boundaries shifted with alliances.

The alliance's economic advantage was not simply that it controlled a particular highway. Partners could combine supplier credit, financing, distribution contacts, and local political access while keeping their own personnel and accounts. This modular pattern made substitution possible after a seizure: one intermediary might be replaced without dismantling all the others. It also made claims about a single supreme commander unreliable. Prosecutors may describe a conspiracy as one organization because legal accountability depends on showing connected conduct, while historians must still ask which decisions were shared, which actors disagreed, and when the relationship existed. The term Sinaloa thus identifies a durable constellation of networks, not necessarily an unbroken corporate chain of command.

Legal agriculture and migration were part of the surrounding society, not evidence of criminal complicity. Export businesses, trucking firms, and emigrant communities made Sinaloa internationally connected before any particular cartel attained notoriety. A trafficker could exploit such mobility, but most travelers and workers had nothing to do with the trade. This distinction matters when studying the region's reputation. Romantic ballads, sensational journalism, and prosecutorial maps can all enlarge an organization's supposed territory until an ordinary community appears to be an extension of a criminal enterprise. A historical explanation should retain both realities: regional social connections made clandestine business easier, and those same communities also contained people who rejected or suffered that business.

Section 102.3

Tijuana: The Arellano Félix Organization and the Western Border

Tijuana lies directly across from San Diego, and together with Mexicali and Tecate it forms Baja California's border system. The region has been a site of commercial smuggling since the early twentieth century, and Chapter 101 noted its regional trafficking networks. The Arellano Félix family, whose members came from Culiacán, became the dominant trafficking family there in the late 1980s and 1990s. The brothers Benjamín, Ramón, Francisco Javier, Eduardo, and others, with their sister Enedina, are described in U.S. indictments and Mexican accounts as running a family-centered enterprise. Benjamín is generally portrayed as the strategist and Ramón as the enforcer. Accounts differ on how much each controlled, and the organization's internal workings are known mainly through court testimony and intelligence reporting.

The organization's methods drew attention. It reportedly relied on corrupt police and officials in Baja California, on armed enforcers recruited from local youth and from border neighborhoods in both countries, and on the use of tunnels and vehicles to cross the border. It was also associated with notable acts of violence, including killings of rivals, officials, and journalists. Tijuana in these years saw high-profile assassinations, among them the March 1994 killing of the presidential candidate Luis Donaldo Colosio, which Mexican investigations attributed to a lone gunman and which has remained a subject of public suspicion, though evidence tying it to trafficking has not been established. The case illustrates how events in border cities fed public perceptions of a connection between crime and politics even when the evidence was inconclusive.

The family's fortunes turned in the early 2000s. Ramón Arellano Félix was killed in a shootout with police in Mazatlán in February 2002, and Benjamín was arrested in Puebla in March 2002. Javier was captured at sea by the U.S. Coast Guard in 2006, and Eduardo was arrested in Tijuana in 2008. Their organization, depleted of leaders, also faced pressure from the Sinaloa networks, which sought the Tijuana plaza. The result was not disappearance but fragmentation. In 2008 a commander named Teodoro García Simental broke away and aligned with Sinaloa, producing a bloody struggle for Tijuana. The episode illustrates a recurring pattern: the removal of top leaders in a family-based organization weakened its capacity for coordination and left it exposed to rivals and to defecting subordinates.

Family organization gave the Arellano Félix enterprise a relatively clear succession pool but also concentrated its vulnerability. Kinship could encourage trust in handling money and sensitive contacts, while the family's name gave enforcers a recognizable brand. Yet close family bonds did not eliminate competition between managers, and the removal of multiple brothers left other participants to decide whether they were employees, successors, or independent entrepreneurs. U.S. prosecution records are particularly useful here because they identify specific pleaded conduct by surviving leaders. They are less useful as complete maps of every neighborhood faction that subsequently claimed part of the Baja California trade.

The border city's political economy cannot be reduced to its violence. Tijuana was an industrial, cultural, and migratory center whose maquiladora employment and service industries drew large numbers of residents. Conflict damaged confidence, threatened witnesses, and created opportunities for official abuses, but citywide changes in homicide cannot be treated as a direct meter of a single cartel's market share. Investigators should distinguish the killing of a trafficker, the killing of a journalist, and a widely contested political assassination; merging them into a single criminal explanation would substitute insinuation for proof. The family's documented criminal record is extensive enough without attributing every extraordinary event of the period to it.

Section 102.4

Juárez: Amado Carrillo Fuentes and the Air Bridge

Ciudad Juárez, across the Rio Grande from El Paso, is an industrial border city that grew rapidly with the assembly plants (maquiladoras) of the late twentieth century and drew migrants from across Mexico. It had an older smuggling tradition, discussed in Chapter 101. In the early 1990s the local trafficking leadership was challenged, and Amado Carrillo Fuentes, a Sinaloa-born trafficker with family ties to the Guadalajara-era networks, took control of the plaza and expanded the organization. Unlike the Arellano Félix organization, which relied on territorial enforcement in one city, Carrillo Fuentes is described in U.S. and Mexican accounts as building a business based on aircraft.

The nickname "Lord of the Skies" referred to reports that his organization used a fleet of planes, including large jets, to move cocaine from Colombia to Mexican landing sites and onward to the United States. The air bridge reflected a shift in the market: Colombian suppliers, under pressure on other routes, used Mexican partners who could receive and distribute large loads. The details of volumes and payments rest largely on prosecution accounts and journalistic investigation, and the extent of the fleet is probably exaggerated in popular retellings. But the general picture, that Mexican organizations increasingly negotiated with Colombians as partners and that Carrillo Fuentes was among the richest, is widely accepted.

Carrillo Fuentes died on 4 July 1997 in a Mexico City clinic after surgery intended to alter his appearance. Rumors that he had faked his death circulated, but Mexican authorities and forensic examinations confirmed it. His death, in the same year as the arrest of the anti-drug chief, shook the Juárez organization, and a succession struggle followed in which his brother Vicente Carrillo Fuentes eventually emerged as leader. The organization remained influential, but it never again matched its peak. Its later conflict with Sinaloa, examined in Section 102.11, turned Ciudad Juárez into one of the most violent cities in the world and made the city an emblem of the human cost of the period.

The Juárez enterprise benefited from a very different location than the Pacific organizations. Ciudad Juárez and El Paso formed an immense paired urban system of factories, trucking routes, rail traffic, and daily crossings. A successful transport organization could plug into that flow while also using air connections farther south. Amado Carrillo Fuentes acquired the nickname Lord of the Skies because air cargo figured prominently in accounts of his operations. It is misleading, however, to picture a business that relied on one airplane or one runway. The decisive asset was a chain of brokers, aircraft, storage sites, drivers, border contacts, and distributors capable of moving and financing shipments over time.

Carrillo Fuentes's death exposed the difference between command of a trafficking enterprise and ownership of a territory. His brother Vicente inherited significant relationships, but other actors could test the family's claims and negotiate independently with local enforcement groups. The city's later conflict cannot be explained by the 1997 death alone: shifting alliances, corruption, the federal security offensive, and the local rivalry with Sinaloa all contributed. Historians should likewise avoid presenting disputed estimates of Carrillo Fuentes's personal fortune as audited financial facts. His importance rests on the documented scale and connections of the organization, not on an exact wealth figure copied from repeated newspaper profiles.

DIVISION II · 1990s–2001

Political Protection and Institutional Vulnerability

Section 102.5

The Gulf Organization: From García Ábrego to Osiel Cárdenas Guillén

Tamaulipas, on the Gulf coast, borders Texas along the Rio Grande, and its cities of Matamoros, Reynosa, and Nuevo Laredo have long been smuggling hubs. Chapter 101 described the regional tradition that grew around Juan Nepomuceno Guerra, whose enterprises included alcohol and commerce during the prohibition era. Juan García Ábrego, a nephew of Guerra born in Texas, expanded that tradition into cocaine trafficking in the 1980s and 1990s. By the mid-1990s U.S. authorities regarded him as one of the most important traffickers in the hemisphere, and in 1995 he became the first drug trafficker placed on the FBI's list of ten most wanted fugitives.

His arrest on 14 January 1996 and immediate expulsion to the United States, where he was convicted in Houston later that year and sentenced to multiple life terms, left the organization without its leader. Control passed after an internal struggle to Osiel Cárdenas Guillén, a former police officer from Matamoros who consolidated authority by force. Cárdenas was reported to have threatened two U.S. agents in Matamoros in 1999, an incident that reinforced U.S. attention on him. In March 2003 he was arrested after a shootout with the Mexican army, and in 2007 Mexico extradited him to the United States, where he was later convicted and sentenced. Even from prison, accounts suggested, he retained influence.

The Gulf organization is a useful contrast with Sinaloa. Its power was tied to a specific region, the northeastern border, and to a lineage of smuggling networks older than the cocaine trade. Chapter 101 stressed that it was not simply an offshoot of the Guadalajara networks, and the historical record supports that view. The Gulf organization's most consequential innovation was not a route but an enforcement arm. Cárdenas recruited former members of Mexico's elite military units to protect him and attack rivals, and that decision, examined in the next section, created Los Zetas.

The Gulf organization's roots lay in the northeastern border economy rather than the Pacific mountains. Matamoros, Reynosa, and Nuevo Laredo supplied access to distinct Texas markets, road connections, and crossing points. Juan García Ábrego's organization had strengthened during the era when cocaine shipments from Colombia increasingly moved through Mexico. His 1996 arrest was therefore a major leadership shock, but it did not eliminate suppliers, purchasers, or local operators. Other participants could compete for his relationships, and the organization ultimately passed into an era associated with Osiel Cárdenas Guillén. Its continuity came from regional markets and established protection practices, not from uninterrupted personal control.

Cárdenas's rise helped alter the enterprise's use of armed force. Protection and enforcement had always been important to trafficking organizations, but the decision to retain a group with significant military experience provided a more permanent coercive instrument. That choice eventually created a principal-agent problem: those hired to defend a business acquired their own commanders, their own loyalties, and the ability to raise revenue without the original patron. The later Gulf–Zetas rupture was not inevitable at the moment of recruitment. It became possible because an armed organization could survive the imprisonment of its benefactor and could discover business opportunities that no longer required his approval.

Section 102.6

The Late PRI Era: Gutiérrez Rebollo, Operation Casablanca, and State Protection

In December 1996, President Ernesto Zedillo appointed Army General Jesús Gutiérrez Rebollo to head the National Institute to Combat Drugs. U.S. officials, including the American drug-policy director, praised the appointment. On 18 February 1997, the Mexican government announced that Gutiérrez Rebollo had been arrested on suspicion of protecting the Juárez organization of Amado Carrillo Fuentes, and he was later prosecuted and sentenced to a long prison term. The episode embarrassed both governments. It showed that the official responsible for fighting trafficking could be compromised, and that the information shared with Mexican counterparts could be vulnerable. It also complicated the annual U.S. certification process, under which Washington judged other countries' cooperation in the drug war and could withhold aid, a practice many Latin American governments resented as an intrusion on their sovereignty.

A second episode, Operation Casablanca, showed the limits of cooperation from the other direction. In May 1998, U.S. Customs announced indictments arising from a long undercover investigation of money laundering, in which agents posed as launderers and caught officials of several Mexican banks. Mexico protested that it had not been told of the operation, which it regarded as a violation of cooperation agreements and sovereignty. The two cases revealed a reciprocal distrust that shaped the period: Americans feared Mexican institutions were penetrated, and Mexicans resented unilateral American methods. Neither belief was baseless, and both complicated joint action.

The historical question is what these cases reveal about state protection. The simplest account holds that the PRI state coordinated the drug trade from above. A more careful reading finds a range of arrangements: local police commanders selling tolerance, governors accepting payments or ignoring activity, federal agents working for traffickers, and honest officials, including prosecutors and soldiers, pursuing cases and sometimes dying for it. The Dirección Federal de Seguridad, discussed in Chapter 101, was dissolved in 1985, and its former members were widely reported to have entered other agencies and private security. This chapter's approach is to distinguish documented cases of corruption by named officials from claims about the whole state. The evidence supports the first better than the second.

The late PRI system produced incentives for bargaining, but there was no guarantee that an agreement with one official would survive a transfer, a scandal, or an election. Federal, state, and municipal agencies possessed overlapping powers; their personnel sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed. Political centralization could make protection more predictable in certain places while also enabling high-level corruption. At the same time, journalists, prosecutors, and honest officers repeatedly exposed abuses. Describing this entire era as a single pact between the PRI and traffickers erases those conflicts and attributes to party leaders a uniform power over criminal markets that the evidence cannot sustain.

General Gutiérrez Rebollo's 1997 arrest was striking because he had been appointed to combat drug trafficking and was accused of connections to Amado Carrillo Fuentes. In that moment, public claims of an effective national crackdown collided with the possibility that enforcement itself could be captured. Operation Casablanca soon showed that the institutional geography extended into banks and the United States. These were different scandals: one concerned a Mexican official and alleged protection; the other concerned covert financial investigations and cross-border diplomatic fallout. Treating them separately permits students to identify what was proved, what prosecutors alleged, and what policy changes each episode actually caused.

Section 102.7

Law-Enforcement Scandal, Operation Casablanca, and Financial Diplomacy

The fight against trafficking depended on investigations that extended far beyond seizures on highways. Government agencies needed access to bank records, international cooperation, reliable intelligence, and witnesses willing to identify officials who provided protection. Those tasks created frequent tension between national sovereignty and the need to follow transnational transactions. Mexico and the United States shared an interest in prosecuting trafficking, but they disagreed about how foreign agents should act on Mexican territory and how information should be shared. An arrest made by one country could disrupt the other's inquiry. A financial investigation intended to expose money laundering might expose diplomatic mistrust as well. For that reason the history of criminal markets is also a history of the legal arrangements built to investigate them.

The 1997 detention of General Jesús Gutiérrez Rebollo was a crisis of credibility for Mexican federal enforcement. He had taken leadership of the national antidrug apparatus shortly before his alleged ties to the Juárez organization became public. His removal raised questions about personnel vetting and about the capacity of an investigative institution to detect corruption at its summit. That scandal should not be interpreted as proof that every officer in the apparatus served traffickers; it demonstrated the severity of the risk when a sensitive position could be compromised. It also increased pressure on Mexican leaders to show that investigations would be independent of political protection and military status.

Operation Casablanca was announced by the U.S. Treasury and Justice Departments on 18 May 1998 after a multiyear undercover investigation. The Treasury release described investigations spanning six countries and, as of its announcement, 112 arrests before the weekend operation and seizures of $35 million in illegal proceeds, over two tons of cocaine, and four tons of marijuana. These were reported operational results at a particular date, not final adjudications for every person identified. The operation drew attention to bank officials and money-transfer mechanisms associated with drug proceeds linked to the Cali and Juárez enterprises. It also provoked objections in Mexico about the conduct of U.S. investigators and questions over notification between governments.

The episode revealed a structural feature of the trade: different countries could interpret the same investigation through different institutional priorities. U.S. agents emphasized an international money trail and the use of undercover techniques. Mexican officials emphasized jurisdiction, foreign activity, and the possibility that their own authorities had not received sufficient information. Both issues could be real at once. A historian should not transform a diplomatic grievance into exoneration of a defendant, or a successful prosecution into proof that every undercover method respected another state's legal order. The primary documents must be read as representations by governments with overlapping but not identical interests.

By the end of the 1990s, prosecutors understood that removing transport leaders was insufficient if illicit profits remained usable through financial institutions. Yet banks processed enormous volumes of lawful payments, and a suspicious movement of funds was not automatically a proven narcotics transaction. Reconstructing any financial prosecution requires identifying the conduct charged, the scope of the monitoring failure, the money shown to be illicit, and the ultimate settlement or judicial outcome. The size of a bank's transaction stream should not be substituted for a measured cartel budget. This distinction will matter again in the Wachovia and HSBC cases discussed later in the chapter.

Operation Casablanca also offers a corrective to narratives that describe the era solely as a sequence of gunfights. Documents, ledgers, money-service businesses, legal assistance requests, and meetings between prosecutors all shaped which organizations were exposed. Corruption could be facilitated by an individual official, but it could also depend on routine administrative blind spots. Financial diplomacy was therefore neither a side issue nor a simple alternative to policing; it was a domain in which two governments attempted to control an industry crossing their shared border while protecting their own institutional authority.

Section 102.8

Arrest, Prison, and Escape: Joaquín Guzmán, 1993–2001

On 24 May 1993, Cardinal Juan Jesús Posadas Ocampo, the Archbishop of Guadalajara, was shot dead outside the city's airport during a gunfight involving armed men. The Mexican government's initial account held that gunmen of the Arellano Félix organization, seeking Guzmán, had mistaken the cardinal's car for his, and that the killing was an accident of a conflict between trafficking groups. That explanation has been disputed, and critics, including some in the Catholic Church, have long questioned whether the official version was complete. What is established is that the killing produced intense public outrage and strong pressure on the government, and that it brought the rivalry between the Sinaloa and Tijuana organizations to national attention.

Guzmán fled and was arrested on 9 June 1993 in Guatemala, then handed over to Mexican authorities and sentenced to a long term. He was held at a high-security prison and later moved to Puente Grande in Jalisco. Evidence from later trials and investigations, including testimony by cooperating witnesses, indicated that prison did not remove him from his business. Officials and guards were reportedly bribed, and he is alleged to have retained influence over his organization. In January 2001, as a political transition was beginning, Guzmán escaped from Puente Grande. The details of the escape, including the extent of official complicity, remain a matter of dispute among accounts, though it is widely accepted that prison employees helped.

The escape is a milestone because it shows how prisons figured in these histories. They were places where leaders could be neutralized, but also where they could be protected, supplied, and, if compromised guards could be found, released. Guzmán's later history illustrates the same pattern. He was recaptured in February 2014 in Mazatlán, escaped again in July 2015 through a tunnel from the Altiplano prison, was arrested in January 2016 in Los Mochis, and was extradited to the United States in January 2017. A federal jury in Brooklyn convicted him on all counts in February 2019, and in July 2019 a judge sentenced him to life in prison plus thirty years. The trial, which relied heavily on cooperating witnesses, produced the most detailed public account to date of the Sinaloa networks, but students should remember that such testimony came from participants with incentives to cooperate, and that the verdict established Guzmán's responsibility under U.S. law, not a complete history of the organization.

The government's legal action against Guzmán had several meanings beyond the imprisonment of one person. To the public, capture suggested that the state could reach a prominent criminal after a highly visible killing. To rival traffickers, it changed the distribution of bargaining power. To Guzmán's associates, it posed a question of succession and trust: who could direct operations, settle debts, and preserve alliances while he was inaccessible? None of these questions was settled by the courtroom sentence. The distinction between a prisoner's physical custody and an organization's economic life is central to explaining why high-profile arrests could coexist with continued trafficking.

Evidence for corruption inside prisons should be evaluated with particular care. A witness who admits bribing officials may have first-hand knowledge, but also a reason to exaggerate access or to obtain a sentencing benefit. A government inquiry may document disciplinary failures without proving that every alleged payment occurred. Conversely, an absence of written orders is unsurprising where conduct was intentionally concealed. The challenge is to triangulate testimony, surveillance, contemporaneous administrative records, and judicial outcomes. The 2001 escape became a political symbol because it occurred so soon after a democratic transition; it does not, on its own, establish that the new president personally authorized or knew of the operation.

DIVISION III · late 1990s–2007

Enforcement, Federation, and Contested Corridors

Section 102.9

Los Zetas: Military Defectors and Paramilitary Enforcement

Los Zetas originated in the late 1990s, when Osiel Cárdenas Guillén recruited deserters from the Mexican army, in particular men from the Grupo Aeromóvil de Fuerzas Especiales (GAFE), an elite unit trained in counterinsurgency and, according to widely reported accounts, in part with U.S. assistance. The first leader, Arturo Guzmán Decena, a former army lieutenant, reportedly recruited about three dozen soldiers and ex-police officers. Their radio call-sign designation, the letter "Z," gave the group its name. The numbers and training details come from Mexican prosecutors, journalistic investigation, and testimony, and the accounts do not agree in every particular, but the central fact, that the Gulf organization hired trained soldiers, is well established.

What distinguished the Zetas was less their weaponry than their organization. They used military-style structure, reconnaissance, communications, and discipline, and they recruited widely, drawing in ex-police, local youths, and others. After Guzmán Decena was killed in Matamoros in November 2002 and Cárdenas was arrested in 2003, leadership passed to figures including Heriberto Lazcano and, later, Miguel Treviño Morales. Their model proved influential because it showed that an organization could prosper by providing armed protection and then, increasingly, by imposing itself on the communities where it operated.

Scholars note a shift in business model. Earlier organizations had earned most of their revenue from moving drugs through a plaza. The Zetas, especially after their break with the Gulf organization, diversified into extortion of businesses and households, kidnapping, human smuggling, theft of fuel, piracy, and control of local markets, using violence not only against rivals but against ordinary people who could be taxed. They also sought public intimidation, leaving bodies, banners, and videos. That strategy of predation, and the competitive pressure it placed on others to respond in kind, helped spread paramilitary tactics across the trade. Students should be careful to avoid treating the Zetas as the sole source of such violence: other organizations also killed, tortured, and extorted. But the Zetas became a model that others imitated or opposed.

Electoral alternation in 2000 introduced new political bargaining without instantly replacing the institutions built over decades. Vicente Fox's victory ended the PRI's continuous hold on the presidency, but governors, municipal officials, police services, and private intermediaries retained their own powers. The transition could disrupt older protection arrangements while allowing other ones to persist. It would be too simple to infer that democracy itself caused criminal violence. A plausible interpretation is that competition among political authorities made some informal bargains less stable, while criminal organizations exploited institutional weaknesses during a period of reform. Other scholars place more weight on enforcement choices and trafficking-market changes.

Nuevo Laredo illustrates how these mechanisms intersected. The city's importance arose from its legal trade corridor and its position opposite Laredo, Texas. Fighting there involved competition among the Gulf–Zetas alliance and Sinaloa-linked interests, as well as confrontations with police and soldiers. The term war should not obscure the uneven tempo: local episodes, assassinations, intimidations, and shifts in officials did not occur at the same intensity every day. Civilian exposure depended on where people lived and worked. A careful chronology separates the early-2000s contest from the much broader militarized security campaign begun in late 2006, which the next chapter treats in detail.

Section 102.10

The Federation and Its Rivals: Sinaloa Expansion, 2001–2007

After his 2001 escape, Guzmán worked to rebuild and expand. Reports indicate that he and Zambada, together with Coronel and other figures, formed an alliance with leaders such as the Beltrán Leyva brothers and Vicente Carrillo Fuentes of Juárez. U.S. and Mexican officials called this arrangement the Federation. Its members, in the standard account, agreed to cooperate against common rivals, notably the Tijuana and Gulf organizations, and to share certain facilities. The alliance was not a merger, and its terms are known imperfectly. It rested on mutual interest and was vulnerable to betrayal.

The strategy brought gains. Sinaloa-linked groups moved against the Arellano Félix organization in Baja California and challenged the Gulf and the Zetas in Tamaulipas. U.S. authorities came to regard the Sinaloa organization as the most powerful of the Mexican groups, and in April 2009 the U.S. Treasury designated it under the Foreign Narcotics Kingpin Designation Act, which allowed the freezing of assets. The organization's success is often attributed to its diversified business, which included not only cocaine but marijuana, methamphetamine, and heroin, and to its skill at corrupting officials and avoiding publicity. Interpretations differ as to whether Sinaloa's expansion reflected unusual strategic skill or simply the weakness of its rivals, who suffered more arrests.

Historical Vocabulary
Kingpin Strategy and Decapitation

The kingpin strategy is a counternarcotics approach that targets the top leaders of trafficking organizations for arrest, extradition, or killing on the theory that removing them will cripple the organization. The related term decapitation refers to the removal of leaders in this way. The name "kingpin" comes from U.S. law and policy, notably the 1999 Foreign Narcotics Kingpin Designation Act.

The strategy matters because this chapter documents many leadership removals, and their consequences were mixed. Some organizations declined, some split, and some went to war. Students should treat the approach as a policy whose effects are debated, not as a proven success or failure. They should also notice that the term assumes a pyramid-shaped organization, which fits some cases better than others.

The Federation did not make Sinaloa a monolith. Different leaders maintained separate resources and could join in one operation while competing in another. Some public descriptions treat the Federation as a formal council with clear membership and fixed voting rules. Surviving court records are generally more convincing about particular agreements and conspiracies than about such a permanent constitution. The label is analytically useful when it describes documented cooperation among leaders; it becomes misleading when it implies that all their associates answered to one person. A flexible network can be both highly effective at transactions and fragile when trust breaks down.

Expansion also created disputes over access to territory that other organizations already regarded as theirs. To move into a border market required more than dispatching a convoy: local brokers, protection intermediaries, suppliers of credit, and enforcers had to switch sides or be displaced. Every such negotiation created risks for officials and residents, who could be threatened for information or suspected of favoring the wrong faction. Federation successes therefore created conditions for later conflict. The organization's history alternated between cooperation and coercive rivalry; neither phase should be treated as its permanent essence.

Section 102.11

Political Alternation and the Nuevo Laredo Conflict, 2000–2007

On 1 December 2000, Vicente Fox of the National Action Party (PAN) was inaugurated as president, ending the PRI's seventy-one years in the presidency. The transition was a democratic achievement and, in the view of many scholars, a turning point for the drug trade. Under PRI rule, protection arrangements had tended to run through a single party that controlled federal and state offices, so a trafficker who bought protection could plausibly rely on its being honored. When governorships, mayoralties, and legislatures were divided among parties, and police and prosecutors answered to different political masters, those arrangements became harder to guarantee. Political competition also meant that a local official's tenure was uncertain, which could make deals shorter-lived and rivals more willing to test them.

The Nuevo Laredo conflict illustrates the new conditions. Nuevo Laredo is the principal commercial crossing on the Texas border, and its plaza was of great value. The Sinaloa networks, led by Guzmán, sought to enter it in the mid-2000s, challenging the Gulf organization and its armed wing the Zetas. The fighting, beginning in 2004 and intensifying in 2005, included killings of police officers. In June 2005, the newly appointed police chief, Alejandro Domínguez, was shot dead hours after taking office, and the federal government sent troops and federal police to the city in a deployment named Operation Safe Mexico. The conflict is often described as a precursor of the post-2006 wars: it involved open battles over a plaza, attacks on police, and a federal deployment that did not settle the matter.

Historians should avoid reading the transition as a simple cause of violence. Violence had occurred under the PRI as well, and the trade was changing for other reasons, including the growth of Mexican shares of the cocaine business and the weakening of the older leaderships by arrests. But the end of one-party rule changed the political environment in which organizations bargained, and the arrangements that had limited open conflict in some regions no longer held. The question of how much weight to place on this factor is examined in the Historical Debate below.

Recruitment categories require precision. Some early Zetas personnel were reportedly former members of elite army units, including soldiers trained in special operations, while other recruits came from the wider military, police, or civilian criminal networks. The number of original defectors differs among journalistic and intelligence accounts, and formal membership in a particular elite unit cannot safely be assigned to every person called a Zeta. The important historical difference is organizational: a force recruited for specialized enforcement developed communications, discipline, and an identity that could be turned toward independent markets. The danger was not a magic military skill but the ability to coordinate threats, surveillance, and force across local jurisdictions.

Under Cárdenas Guillén, armed enforcement could punish rivals and intimidate those whom the Gulf organization wanted to control. As the commanders became stronger, they also learned to collect revenue and negotiate with local intermediaries. The relationship thus challenged a basic assumption about the division of labor: that the transporters retained ultimate authority and the gunmen remained salaried assistants. The subsequent expansion of extortion and kidnapping showed how coercion could become a commodity in its own right. Recognizing this evolution helps explain why later violence was not confined to competition for cocaine and marijuana shipments.

Section 102.12

Regional Alliances, Corridor Competition, and the Limits of Territory Maps

A trafficking route was not an ownership deed. The northern border contained highways, rail crossings, rural tracks, industrial warehouses, and lawful trade channels used by countless people outside the drug trade. Control of an individual transfer point might require a temporary relationship with a local intermediary rather than command of a city. This makes the word territory useful only with a date and an evidentiary qualification. One organization could arrange shipments through a crossing while a rival provided protection at a warehouse nearby. Federal officials might announce that a plaza had been cleared only to discover that its suppliers had moved or replaced personnel. Students should therefore read broad control maps as interpretations, not literal cadastral surveys.

Sinaloa-linked expansion in the first years of the twenty-first century was supported by alliances among figures who had different relationships with Pacific ports, mountain producing regions, and border distributors. The same network could cooperate with a leader in one region and compete against the leader's associates elsewhere. These arrangements were not all visible to the public. Court testimony supplies evidence for particular transactions, while journalists often reconstructed wider alliances from killings, arrests, and interviews. A careful historian asks whether a source documents a negotiated partnership, merely alleges it, or infers it from the fact that two organizations attacked a third.

The Gulf–Zetas association demonstrates a contrasting form of alliance. Gulf traffickers depended on an armed force that originally received resources and status from its patron. But a subordinate unit with its own recruitment and communications could develop independent bargaining power. Its ability to collect payments from local populations made that autonomy greater still. When Gulf and Zeta leaders disputed obligations, they were not simply arguing about the share of one shipment; they were debating who could command violence and claim revenue. The eventual break exposed the instability of delegating organized coercion to a force capable of becoming a rival enterprise.

Juárez posed another model. After Amado Carrillo Fuentes, the organization relied on family continuity and local armed relationships, including the group known as La Línea. Its conflict with Sinaloa-linked interests cannot be reduced to two national headquarters issuing parallel orders. Smaller gangs and neighborhood actors became relevant to local developments, while law-enforcement deployment changed the incentives for everyone involved. A leader who announced a national alliance could not ensure that each local gunman obeyed it. This is one reason the same city sometimes appeared to experience overlapping conflicts rather than a single coherent campaign.

Tijuana likewise illustrated how a fragmented leadership created opportunities for outsiders but did not erase the influence of long-standing local brokers. National journalists could speak of Sinaloa entering Baja California, yet the practical negotiations might involve individual police stations, transport intermediaries, and armed groups changing loyalties. When violence subsequently eased, some observers interpreted the result as monopolization of the plaza. Others pointed to law-enforcement action, civic pressure, and changing tactical arrangements. A chronology needs independent evidence before it attributes changes in homicide to a particular secret agreement.

The organizations also depended on cross-border relationships that cannot be mapped as solid blocks. Distribution networks in the United States did not necessarily share the Mexican organization's hierarchy, and money moved through a range of intermediaries. Two criminal entrepreneurs could supply the same city without coordinating their activities. By distinguishing operational corridors, political protection zones, armed presence, and the headquarters of individual leaders, historians can build maps that reveal uncertainty rather than concealing it. This chapter's three geography studies ask students to do precisely that, for 1989–1996, 2001–2007, and 2008–2012, not to assign eternal colors to entire Mexican states.

DIVISION IV · 2008–2015

Rupture, Civilian Consequences, and Successor Groups

Section 102.13

Rupture and War, 2008–2010: Beltrán Leyva, Juárez, and Tijuana

In January 2008, Mexican authorities arrested Alfredo Beltrán Leyva, a brother of Arturo and Héctor. The brothers, according to accounts that cannot be independently verified, suspected that Guzmán or his associates had betrayed him, and they broke with the Sinaloa alliance. The Beltrán Leyva organization allied with others, including, in some regions, the Zetas, and fought Sinaloa. Arturo Beltrán Leyva was killed by Mexican marines in Cuernavaca in December 2009, and the organization itself fractured, spawning smaller groups. The rupture shows how an alliance of equals could dissolve, and it illustrates how an arrest could set off suspicion among former partners.

Meanwhile, in Chihuahua, the Federation's alliance with Vicente Carrillo Fuentes had collapsed, and from 2008 Sinaloa-linked forces fought the Juárez organization for Ciudad Juárez. The Juárez organization's enforcement arm included a group known as La Línea, drawn partly from police officers, and it allied with the Barrio Azteca street gang, which operated on both sides of the border. Sinaloa allied with other local gangs. The conflict, together with the arrival of federal troops in 2008, made Juárez one of the most violent cities in the world, with homicides in 2010 commonly counted at more than 3,000. Chapter 103 examines the militarized response and its human costs. For this chapter, the conflict demonstrates the destructive potential of a plaza war among organizations whose leaders no longer shared a protective arrangement.

In Tijuana, the breakaway of García Simental and the aftermath of the Arellano Félix decline produced a bloody 2008–2010 struggle, in which Sinaloa gained ground. García Simental was arrested in January 2010. Observers noted that violence declined in Tijuana in subsequent years, a change attributed variously to Sinaloa's dominance, to enforcement, and to local factors. Whatever the cause, the Tijuana case suggests that violence tends to be highest when control is contested and lower when one organization prevails or when contenders reach accommodation. This is an interpretation, not a settled finding, and it is one of the questions for the debate below.

The fall of one political protection relationship could open a corridor to predatory competition. In the first years after 2000, officials at different levels sometimes offered conflicting accounts of who controlled Nuevo Laredo, while federal agencies asserted jurisdiction over investigations that local institutions struggled to conduct. People who worked in transportation, customs, journalism, or retail did not choose to participate in a cartel contest simply because their livelihood placed them in a strategically situated city. When policing became unreliable, residents also had fewer safe ways to report threats. The result was a contest not only for shipments but over the everyday authority to issue orders and punish refusal.

Subsequent descriptions frequently project a later map of cartel zones backward onto 2003 or 2005. Such maps disguise the uncertainty of those years. A neighborhood might contain people linked to different organizations; a police unit might protect one faction while another level of government pursued it; and a transport route could be used by several actors. A responsible historical map must be dated, display contested places, and distinguish documented activities from claimed exclusive control. Nuevo Laredo thus offers a method as well as a case: geography is evidence only when the historian states when and how a particular spatial claim was established.

Section 102.14

Tijuana after the Arellano Félix Brothers: Succession and Accommodation

The removal of several Arellano Félix brothers between 2002 and 2008 did not destroy the commercial reasons that made Tijuana an important border city. The San Diego crossing remained valuable; regional transporters and corrupt intermediaries did not vanish when a prominent name disappeared from the leadership. Rather, the family organization faced a problem familiar to succession histories: who had the authority to allocate revenues and decide how to respond to competitors? The answer did not necessarily come from seniority alone. Enedina Arellano Félix and other relatives were described in reporting as continuing financial and organizational roles, while armed subordinates increasingly exercised power of their own.

The breakaway led by Teodoro García Simental, often called El Teo, intensified conflict during 2008–2010. Accounts portray him as having left an Arellano-linked faction and developed ties to Sinaloa interests. His opponent Fernando Sánchez Arellano was associated with continued family leadership. These labels can imply cleaner alignments than the evidence warrants. Armed groups sometimes adopted a sponsor's name to strengthen their position, while their practical loyalties followed personal relationships, local business opportunities, and the pressure of policing. Investigative reports about killings should therefore be checked against arrest dates, judicial evidence, and changes in local security conditions.

García Simental's arrest in January 2010 was a decisive event in the immediate leadership contest, but it should not be credited automatically with producing every subsequent improvement in public safety. Tijuana's homicide patterns involved the interaction of several mechanisms, among them disputes among remaining factions, policing, public pressure, and adjustments in criminal business. A fall in recorded killings does not prove that intimidation, corruption, or trafficking ended. It also does not prove that a new single organization had an uncontested monopoly. The city could become less visibly violent while clandestine operations continued and while citizens still faced threats that rarely appeared in national statistics.

The sentencing of Eduardo Arellano Félix in the United States illustrates the value and limits of judicial sources. Prosecutors described his financial role and a long history of laundering and organizational support, and he pleaded guilty to specified financial crimes before receiving a 15-year sentence in 2013. The plea documents firmly establish particular wrongdoing; press statements that the family organization had ended, by contrast, were claims about an evolving market made at a political moment. Historians should not treat the closing of one criminal case as proof that every remaining participant in an organization disappeared from the region.

Comparison with the Sinaloa alliance reveals why the outcomes differed. A family-centered organization concentrated authority among related leaders, which created a recognizable leadership target for police. A loosely associated federation could lose one leader yet remain capable of coordinating several others. Neither pattern guaranteed stability. Decentralized networks could suffer internal feuds, while a family business could survive under administrators who were less visible than its founders. The distinctive Tijuana history is thus one of narrowed family control combined with recurring local reconfiguration, not simply a straight line from dominance to extinction.

The civilian significance of that process is visible in the city's divided experiences. Industrial workers continued to cross the border, businesses tried to remain open, and journalists attempted to document killings in an atmosphere of danger. Some victims were directly targeted for perceived connections with rival groups; others were assaulted because ordinary work put them near contested spaces. Families seeking justice confronted investigations that often lacked witnesses or trusted institutions. These histories matter even when the chapter focuses on organizational succession. They prevent the retrospective language of cartel victory from making civilian insecurity seem like an acceptable price of a more stable illicit market.

Section 102.15

The Zetas Break Away: Tamaulipas, Migrants, and Diversified Predation

In early 2010 the long partnership between the Gulf organization and its armed wing ended. Accounts agree on the general outline: a dispute over the handling of a Zeta member, which the Gulf leadership declined to resolve to the Zetas' satisfaction, led the Zetas to declare independence, and fighting broke out across Tamaulipas, beginning in January and February 2010. Gulf leaders, including Ezequiel Cárdenas Guillén, "Tony Tormenta," allied with Sinaloa and others against the Zetas. Tormenta was killed in Matamoros in November 2010. The war devastated the northeast. It also ended the old pairing of a trafficking organization with a specialized enforcement force, and it created a new, independent paramilitary organization with its own revenues.

The Zetas' conduct in this period produced some of the worst atrocities in recent Mexican history. In August 2010, the bodies of 72 migrants, mostly from Central and South America, were found at a ranch in San Fernando, Tamaulipas. In April 2011 authorities discovered 193 bodies in graves in the same municipality, many of them unidentified at the time; their identities must not be assumed. Mexican officials attributed the killings to the Zetas, and the surviving evidence indicates that migrants, who traveled north through Tamaulipas in large numbers, were kidnapped for ransom, forced into the organization, or killed when they refused. In March 2011, in and near the town of Allende, Coahuila, Zetas members were later reported to have killed or disappeared a large number of people; official and human rights estimates differ widely, and the full toll is not established. In August 2011, an arson attack on a casino in Monterrey killed 52 people.

The Zetas also expanded to other states, including Veracruz and Coahuila, and to Guatemala, where in May 2011 twenty-seven farmworkers were killed in the Petén, a massacre Guatemalan authorities attributed to Zetas associates. Prison conflicts reflected the same rivalry: in February 2012, forty-four inmates were killed in a riot at a prison in Apodaca, Nuevo León, in fighting between Zetas and Gulf-aligned inmates. Zeta leaders were eventually killed or captured. Heriberto Lazcano was killed in October 2012 in Coahuila. Miguel Treviño Morales, "Z-40," was arrested in July 2013 and his brother Omar Treviño Morales in March 2015. The organization fragmented, and a remnant, now called the Cártel del Noreste, continues. Students should note that migrants, Indigenous and rural people, women, and journalists were disproportionately among the victims, and that this violence was directed at civilians who had no role in the trade.

The alliance politics of northern Mexico changed repeatedly because partners judged the value of a relationship against the risk of betrayal. A pact could be strengthened by marriage, shared suppliers, or a common rival, but none of these ties ensured compliance. The Gulf organization and Zetas began as partners with interdependent functions. Sinaloa-linked groups allied against some competitors even as Sinaloa members disagreed among themselves. Leadership removals multiplied those uncertainties. A person who had previously enforced cooperation might suddenly be in prison, while younger commanders sought prestige by challenging old agreements.

The important lesson is that violence could be a consequence of fragmented authority rather than evidence of a unified campaign directed from above. In some localities the killing of a senior figure produced a brief scramble; elsewhere it accelerated a split that already existed. The national picture is a collection of local histories, not a synchronized sequence. Historians can test the influence of a leadership removal by comparing developments before and after it and by asking whether rival claims, revenue changes, or police operations were already underway.

Section 102.16

Civilians, the Northeast, and the Emergence of Successor Factions

The Gulf–Zetas split transformed the northeastern border because it divided not only trafficking relationships but armed authority over communities. The two camps and their local associates fought in Tamaulipas and adjacent states, and ordinary residents experienced the conflict through threats, disappearances, interrupted journeys, and the fear of reporting crimes. The consequences differed by locality. A border town used by freight companies might see extortion and coercion of workers, while a rural corridor might become dangerous for migrants or small agricultural settlements. This uneven geography means that national casualty figures, useful as a warning of the scale of violence, cannot substitute for local inquiry into how specific organizations operated.

In August 2010, seventy-two migrants were murdered at San Fernando. Human-rights organizations documented the failures of identification and investigation that compounded the harm to relatives searching across borders. In 2011 investigators discovered 193 bodies in graves in the same municipality; that discovery belongs to a different case, and many identities remained unresolved at the end of that year. The distinction is not just numerical. The people in the graves had individual biographies, places of origin, and relatives, and the inability to determine those identities was itself an institutional failure. Treating every body as a confirmed migrant would turn an incomplete forensic record into an invented demographic fact.

The March 2011 attacks in Allende, Coahuila, are also part of this story, but estimates of the number of people killed or disappeared vary considerably. A credible history describes the assault and subsequent investigations without reporting an unsupported definitive total. Residents of Allende suffered both the immediate violence and the later uncertainty about their missing relatives. In August of that year an attack on the Casino Royale in Monterrey killed fifty-two people. Official victims' institutions later sought public acknowledgment of the state's failures and of the needs of survivors. Such records illuminate the responsibilities of institutions beyond criminal prosecution alone.

Across the southern border, the 2011 massacre of twenty-seven agricultural workers in Petén, Guatemala, showed the reach of armed networks operating in wider regional disputes. United Nations special procedures recorded allegations of Zetas responsibility; the source is a communication of reported claims, not a final judicial determination. Guatemala's national history, including rural inequality and the aftermath of civil war, shaped vulnerabilities that cannot be explained by Mexican cartels in isolation. The example nevertheless demonstrates that the organizational footprint of the Zetas was not limited to an American-facing border corridor.

The original Zetas hierarchy also underwent succession and fragmentation. Heriberto Lazcano died in October 2012, and Miguel Treviño Morales was arrested in July 2013; later developments included the arrest of Omar Treviño Morales in March 2015. The Cártel del Noreste emerged from portions of this broader lineage, but the continuity of names should not be taken to mean uniform membership, territory, or leadership over time. Regional successors differed in their capacity to sustain cross-border trafficking and their reliance on local predatory markets. Rival claims and factional boundaries remained disputed even among law-enforcement sources.

This section has described the organizational implications of violence rather than attempting a comprehensive history of Mexico's post-2006 humanitarian emergency. Chapter 103 addresses militarization, homicide, disappearance, journalists, and state accountability at national scale. The present chapter's contribution is narrower: an armed wing developed autonomy, split from its patron, preyed on civilians, and then itself fragmented after leadership removals. That sequence shows how organizations can outlive their founders while losing the internal arrangements that once held them together. It also shows why the words cartel and control cannot describe the human cost without evidence from those most exposed to coercion.

Section 102.17

Money, Weapons, Prisons, and Corruption: The Shared Infrastructure

Although the organizations fought one another, they relied on shared infrastructure, much of it legal and much of it located in the United States. The first element was money. Trafficking revenue arrived largely as cash in the United States and had to be converted into usable funds. Methods included smuggling bulk cash southward, using currency exchange houses (casas de cambio), trade-based schemes, and, in some documented cases, banks. In 2010, Wachovia settled a U.S. criminal case concerning its handling of transactions from Mexican exchange houses, and in December 2012, HSBC agreed to pay about $1.9 billion in a U.S. settlement that, according to the Justice Department, acknowledged failures in monitoring Mexico-related accounts, including the laundering of drug proceeds from the Sinaloa organization. Such cases show that laundering involved major financial institutions, not only criminals. They also show the limits of prosecutions, which typically ended in corporate penalties rather than individual convictions.

The second element was weapons. Mexican law makes civilian gun ownership highly restrictive, and the country has few gun shops, while the United States has many. U.S. and Mexican investigations traced large numbers of firearms recovered at Mexican crime scenes to U.S. sources, many bought by intermediaries known as straw purchasers who bought on behalf of others. The 2009–2011 ATF operation known as Fast and Furious, in which agents allowed some such purchases to proceed in order to track them, ended in controversy because some of the guns were later found at crime scenes. The case illustrates the entanglement of U.S. policy in the arming of Mexican organizations, an entanglement Mexican officials repeatedly criticized. It does not alter the fact that the buyers and the violence were Mexican.

The third element was institutional: prisons and corruption. Mexican prisons were overcrowded and under-resourced, and, as Guzmán's story showed, they could be penetrated. Organizations also depended on corrupt police, whose role varied from supplying information to acting as armed enforcers, as with La Línea. Corruption was not uniform. Honest officers were threatened or killed, and some Mexican institutions, including parts of the military and federal prosecutors' offices, worked at great risk against trafficking. Historians should therefore avoid describing the Mexican state as simply complicit. The better description is of a state with institutions of varying integrity and capacity, which organizations sought to buy, intimidate, or avoid, with uneven success.

The Beltrán Leyva rupture illustrates the danger of projecting unity backward. The brothers had collaborated with Sinaloa-linked leaders over an extended period, but by 2008 accusations of betrayal and disputes over arrests helped break that relationship. Different participants later supplied incompatible explanations for who informed authorities and why. The historian can establish that fighting intensified and the alliance changed without accepting a single insider's causal story. Likewise, the Juárez rivalry had its own organizational components: relatives, local armed groups, and neighborhood actors did not necessarily follow a central command in the same way.

It is important to separate this organizational history from the extraordinary social catastrophe that followed. More than one group and more than one level of government operated in Ciudad Juárez, and homicide totals cannot be assigned mechanically to a single command. An increase in killings can reflect rival attacks, opportunistic crime, security operations, and failures of investigation. Chapter 103 will address the national security strategy, enforced disappearances, rights violations, and the experiences of residents in depth. Here the 2008 split demonstrates the breakdown of shared business relations and the difficulty of preserving a stable division of markets after a succession shock.

Section 102.18

Comparing Organizational Structures and the Limits of Leader Removal

The five lineages in this chapter offer an unusual comparative opportunity because they participated in related transnational markets while adopting different ways of organizing authority. Tijuana was strongly identified with the Arellano Félix family; Juárez with the Carrillo Fuentes family and local enforcement relationships; Sinaloa with an alliance of partially autonomous entrepreneurs; the Gulf organization with successive regional leaders and an increasingly powerful armed partner; and the Zetas with a coercive structure that developed out of that partnership. These are analytical descriptions, not immutable constitutions. All five could recruit outside the family, transact with independent businesses, and change their internal arrangements in response to arrests.

A family business solves some trust problems through kinship, but it concentrates authority in a small succession group and creates high-profile targets. A federation spreads risk among participants yet must manage collective action without a reliable superior. An armed organization may be able to impose discipline over its members and extract revenue by force; it also risks turning coercion into a self-perpetuating business that no founding patron can restrain. The contrasting structures help explain why identical enforcement tactics did not produce identical results. Arresting one member of a loose network may merely elevate another partner, whereas removing several brothers can destabilize a family hierarchy.

Leadership removal nevertheless had varying effects within each organization. Guzmán's 1993 arrest was followed by survival of the Sinaloa networks and his eventual escape. His 2019 conviction was a substantial legal result, but the enterprise persisted under other actors. By contrast, the deaths and arrests of the early-2000s Arellano Félix leaders precipitated a visible family decline and local rivalries. García Ábrego's 1996 removal did not end the Gulf trade, while the seizure of Cárdenas Guillén created opportunities for his armed associates. Lazcano's death and Treviño's arrest preceded additional Zetas fragmentation. The pattern is not that enforcement always fails; it is that organizational outcomes depend on existing succession options and local alliances.

The word decapitation is rhetorically powerful because it suggests that removing a head disables a body. Criminal organizations are rarely simple organisms. Their operations may be distributed across suppliers, accountants, brokers, drivers, retail sellers, corrupted officials, and gunmen who can continue working without consulting a detained commander. Sometimes removing a leader disrupts coordination and lowers the capacity for violence; sometimes it unleashes rival claimants. An evaluation must specify which outcome is being measured, over what geographical area, and across what period. Counts of arrests alone measure a state's activity, not necessarily a reduction in harms to residents.

Three kinds of evidence can discipline the comparison. The first is a dated sequence of leadership events and known successors. The second is a set of documented organizational ties, such as pleaded conspiracies or judicially established financial relationships. The third is locally collected evidence of market and social outcomes, including whether violence changed or an armed faction fragmented. Scholars who disagree about the effects of leader removal often emphasize different time frames and outcome measures. Students should therefore ask whether a claim concerns short-term competition, long-run institutional replacement, the volume of trafficking, or the safety of communities.

The comparative conclusion is conditional rather than fatalistic. Corruption investigations, fair prosecutions, financial regulation, victim protection, and institutional reform can affect how organizations operate. Yet a policy focused entirely on capturing a small list of leaders leaves the regional markets, financial intermediaries, and institutional incentives described throughout this chapter incompletely addressed. Differences among Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, the Gulf organization, and Los Zetas point toward a historical lesson: understanding an organization's internal structure is essential before predicting what will follow the removal of one of its leaders.

Section 102.19

The 2024–2026 Sinaloa Cases: Pleas, Sentencing, and Sovereignty

The legal history of the Sinaloa organization reached a new turning point on 25 July 2024, when Ismael Zambada García and Joaquín Guzmán López arrived in the United States and entered federal custody. Zambada had evaded capture for decades. The two men's arrival immediately raised questions about how the journey had been arranged and what consent, if any, had been given. Those questions require separate evidentiary treatment from the preexisting drug-trafficking indictments. A criminal court can establish responsibility for trafficking while the circumstances of a cross-border transfer remain the subject of conflicting public accounts and another government's inquiry.

In August 2025 Zambada pleaded guilty in Brooklyn to a continuing criminal enterprise charge and a racketeering-related charge. On 20 July 2026 Judge Brian Cogan sentenced him to life imprisonment, without possibility of parole, and federal prosecutors announced a forfeiture order of fifteen billion dollars. The legal record establishes his admissions and the judgment imposed by the court. The order should not be misread as an independently audited estimate of readily available cash or as the value of every Sinaloa enterprise across decades. Monetary figures in criminal cases can represent legally defined proceeds and liabilities rather than a balance-sheet snapshot.

Joaquín Guzmán López pleaded guilty in Chicago on 1 December 2025 to a drug conspiracy count and to engaging in a continuing criminal enterprise. The Justice Department announced the plea the following day. Its account states that he also stipulated to committing an international kidnapping offense in connection with Zambada's transfer and that prosecutors did not induce or condone that conduct. This was a stipulation reported within the plea arrangement; students should not silently convert it into conviction on a separate kidnapping count when the pleaded counts were different. Such distinctions matter because prosecutors, defendants, and journalists often summarize a case in language broader than the formal disposition.

Mexico's Federal Attorney General's Office, the FGR, issued a statement on 8 July 2026 describing its investigation into the alleged seizure of Zambada and his transfer from Sinaloa to New Mexico. It raised questions about cooperation with U.S. authorities and information available to Mexican investigators. The statement is a primary record of the Mexican state's position and investigative activity, not a neutral resolution of each contested fact. In particular, the possibility of U.S. participation remained disputed and should not be stated as an established finding. Both governments have institutional interests in how the affair is characterized, especially because sovereignty and intelligence cooperation are at stake.

The detention affected a trafficking network whose leaders had long coordinated overlapping operations without identical political loyalties. Press coverage and official statements described violent tensions between groups associated with Zambada and with Guzmán's sons. These developments are historically significant but still close in time to the writing of this chapter. Precise claims about command structures, responsibility for particular incidents, or the duration of new factions require dated evidence. An arrest and plea may settle an individual defendant's liability, yet the subsequent organization can reorganize through alliances that remain hidden until later investigations or trials.

Students can use these cases to practice a three-column evidentiary method. In one column belong admitted conduct and the legal judgments of U.S. courts; in another, Mexican investigative assertions about a disputed transfer; in a third, provisional accounts of changes inside Sinaloa after July 2024. Comparing them prevents the authority of a courtroom verdict from spilling into matters that court did not decide. It also permits a more rigorous account of cross-border relations: the United States and Mexico cooperate against trafficking while retaining separate laws, powers, and claims about what happened on Mexican territory.

Section 102.20

Fragmentation and Unfinished Histories (through 2026)

By 2012 the landscape had changed. The Arellano Félix organization was a shadow of its former self. The Juárez organization had lost its dominance. The Gulf organization and the Zetas had each split into factions. The Beltrán Leyva organization had fragmented. Only the Sinaloa networks remained a large, transnational organization, though even they were not unified. Meanwhile, new groups, including the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (Chapter 104), were rising. The fragmentation of the older organizations did not end the trade. It produced more organizations, many of them smaller and more locally rooted, with more diverse revenues, including extortion and fuel theft, a trend that continued after the period covered here.

The founding leadership of the Sinaloa organization has since been removed. Joaquín Guzmán was convicted in 2019 and is serving a life sentence. Ismael Zambada was arrested on 25 July 2024 at an airfield in the El Paso area. He said he had been abducted in Mexico by associates of Guzmán's son Joaquín Guzmán López and flown to the United States, and Guzmán López pleaded guilty in Chicago on 1 December 2025 to drug conspiracy and continuing criminal enterprise counts; the Justice Department also reported a stipulation concerning international kidnapping, not a separate kidnapping conviction. In August 2025 Zambada pleaded guilty in Brooklyn to charges including engaging in a continuing criminal enterprise, and on 20 July 2026 the judge sentenced him to life in prison, as the plea required. Reporting indicates that Zambada's arrest set off fighting between his loyalists and supporters of Guzmán's sons, the "Chapitos," in Sinaloa. On 8 July 2026, Mexico’s FGR detailed its inquiry into the alleged seizure and transfer; whether U.S. authorities participated remained a disputed question, not an established finding. These events are recent, and their causes and consequences are not fully established.

On 20 February 2025, the U.S. State Department designated the Sinaloa Cartel, the Gulf Cartel, the Cártel del Noreste (identified as Los Zetas), and several other organizations as Foreign Terrorist Organizations and Specially Designated Global Terrorists. A Foreign Terrorist Organization designation is a U.S. legal status under immigration law, with consequences for financial transactions and for criminal liability for material support. It is not a scholarly classification, and scholars debate whether profit-driven criminal enterprises fit the concept of terrorism. Mexican officials expressed concern about the implications for sovereignty. This chapter takes no position on the policy but notes that the designation reflects a U.S. government judgment and that its long-term effects remain to be seen.

The unresolved questions are numerous. Whether the Sinaloa organization will remain a single organization or break into lasting factions has yet to be seen. Whether the designations will change U.S.–Mexican cooperation, or alter the organizations' behavior, is unclear. The extent to which the arrest of aging leaders matters, in an economy whose center of gravity has moved toward synthetic drugs, is a question for Chapter 104. The post-2006 militarized security strategy, which shaped how these organizations fragmented, is the subject of Chapter 103. What this chapter has shown is how the system came to be: not through a single organization or a single decision, but through regional markets, geography, political protection, enforcement pressure, and the choices of ambitious and violent individuals.

One reason the Zetas were feared was that their coercive economy was not limited to those already engaged in trafficking. Migrants moving north could be intercepted in places where they lacked legal status, local contacts, or access to authorities. Storeowners and transport workers could be pressured because their ordinary business made them visible. The same conditions increased the danger of disappearance: families in different countries struggled to learn where missing relatives had last been seen. The historical record must therefore be constructed from shelter reports, forensic evidence, human-rights investigations, and survivor testimony, not only official claims about arrests.

The 2010 and 2011 San Fernando cases are related geographically but should not be conflated. Seventy-two migrants were murdered in the August 2010 massacre. The 193 bodies recovered from clandestine graves in 2011 constituted a separate discovery; many were not identified in the reporting period, so the identities and life histories of all those victims cannot be assumed. Likewise, the number of people killed or disappeared at Allende remains contested, and the state cannot simply replace investigation with a single dramatic estimate. These distinctions do not minimize atrocities. They protect victims from the false certainty that often accompanies retrospective crime narratives.

Historical Vocabulary
Cartel and Federation

In economics, a cartel is a group of producers who agree to restrict competition, as when oil exporters set output. Mexican drug organizations were called "cartels" by journalists and officials from the 1980s onward, partly because early Colombian groups were described that way. The word suggests coordinated price-setting, which seldom fit these organizations, who competed violently as often as they cooperated. "Cartel" is retained here because it is the common name, but students should treat it as a label rather than an analysis.

The term Federation (La Federación) was used by authorities and the press for the alliance of Sinaloa-linked leaders in the 2000s, and it has also appeared in official alias lists. It conveys a loose association of semi-autonomous leaders and not a corporation. Both terms illustrate a larger problem: names created by police and media shape how the public and historians imagine organizations whose real structures were more fluid.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did Fragmentation Come with Escalating Violence?

The question: Between the late 1990s and 2010, Mexican trafficking organizations fragmented and violence rose sharply in several regions. Was the cause primarily political change, primarily enforcement, or something else?

Interpretation A — The breakdown of political protection: Some scholars argue that violence rose when the PRI's centralized arrangements broke down. As electoral competition spread, fewer officials could guarantee protection, local bargains became unstable, and organizations used force to defend or seize plazas. On this view, violence responded to the changing structure of state-criminal relations.
Interpretation B — The effects of enforcement: Others stress that arrests, killings of leaders, and later militarized deployments fragmented organizations, produced succession fights, and gave subordinates incentives to break away. On this view, enforcement pressure, especially against leaders, often increased violence in the short run even when it weakened the targeted organization.

Evidence: Both views draw on homicide statistics, court records, press reports, and case studies of Tijuana, Juárez, Tamaulipas, and Michoacán. Neither can fully control for the many other factors, including shifts in the drug market, the Zetas' paramilitary model, firearms flows, and local political conditions. Homicide data are imperfect, and attribution of killings to organizations is difficult. Many scholars combine the two explanations.

Why it matters: If political change was decisive, democratization created conditions that required new institutional arrangements. If enforcement was decisive, the design of counternarcotics strategy, including U.S. support for it, bears greater weight. If both mattered, policy must address the interaction. The debate also shows how scholars use comparison across regions to test explanations.

Student task: Choose one case from this chapter, such as Tijuana, Juárez, or Nuevo Laredo. Explain how each interpretation would account for it, and identify what additional evidence would help decide between them.

  • From the PRI's single-party protection to competitive, unstable arrangements after 2000

Who experienced change most directly: Residents of contested border cities, migrants, and officials in the path of violence. Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Fragmentation of Mexican Trafficking Organizations

Long-term causes

Border geography, U.S. demand, older smuggling traditions, rural poverty, and decades of informal protection arrangements.

Short-term causes

Camarena fallout and Félix Gallardo's arrest, the rise of Mexican shares in the cocaine trade, leadership arrests and killings, and the erosion of PRI rule.

Event or transformation

Several regional organizations replaced the Guadalajara-centered networks and later themselves split.

Immediate effects

Plaza wars, new enforcement arms such as the Zetas, and the spread of extortion and kidnapping.

Long-term consequences

A more fragmented and violent criminal landscape, a militarized state response (Chapter 103), and new organizations and markets (Chapter 104).

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Politics, enforcement, markets, and individual choices interacted, and the results varied by region.
Everyday Life and Social History

Fields, Factories, Highways, and Households

For most Mexicans, these organizations were not an abstraction. In the Sinaloa and Chihuahua highlands, farming families, including Indigenous communities such as the Rarámuri (Tarahumara), lived amid cultivation, military sweeps, and armed groups, and some were displaced. In the irrigated valleys, seasonal laborers, many of them Indigenous migrants from southern states such as Oaxaca and Guerrero, worked in conditions shaped by low pay and by the presence of armed groups. In border cities, assembly-plant workers, many of them women, traveled through neighborhoods where violence could erupt. In Ciudad Juárez, the killing of women, which attracted attention from 1993 onward, remains a subject of research and activism. Scholars caution that these killings had multiple causes and should not all be attributed to trafficking organizations.

Popular culture also felt the trade. The narcocorrido, a ballad form, and devotion to figures such as the folk saint Jesús Malverde reflected a culture in which some people admired the traffickers' wealth, while others deplored it. Families of the disappeared and of murdered journalists began organizing. Women appeared in these histories in several roles, including as financial managers, as recruits, and as the family members who searched for loved ones. The sources, dominated by police and prosecutors, record the leaders far better than the households they affected, and that silence is itself part of the evidence.

Historical Comparison I: Authority within Five Organizations

Sinaloa: a flexible but unstable alliance of leaders, whose coordination could persist after one capture. Historical Debate

Debate: Are Terrorism Designations Appropriate for Profit-Oriented Criminal Organizations?

Position A — Policy and legal utility: supporters argue that deliberate campaigns of intimidation, attacks on civilians, and coercive territorial control make certain armed organizations appropriate targets for terrorism-related legal and financial tools. They cite the 2025 Federal Register notices as an exercise of U.S. statutory and executive authority.

Position B — Conceptual and diplomatic limits: critics distinguish organizations primarily organized to profit from illicit markets from political or ideological organizations that fit classical terrorism definitions. They caution that designations may alter bilateral relationships, expand legal exposure, and create incentives not captured by the familiar cartel label.

The two perspectives do not change the legal fact that the United States designated the listed groups; the scholarly debate concerns analytical fit, policy outcomes, and Mexico’s sovereign authority. Read the FTO and SDGT notices separately, then compare their authority with research on coercive criminal governance.

Discussion: What additional empirical evidence, beyond the text of a designation notice, would test claims about deterrence, civilian protection, or the effects on intergovernmental cooperation?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the emergence of the principal Mexican trafficking organizations from the weakening of the Guadalajara-centered networks in 1989 to the leadership trials of the 2020s. Its central argument is that these organizations were regional products of specific markets, geographies, and political arrangements, and that their fragmentation changed the organization of an older illicit economy without creating it.

After Félix Gallardo's arrest, no single successor emerged. Leaders who had cooperated in the 1980s built or defended their own bases, and the popular story of a formal division of Mexico among them is poorly documented. Colombian suppliers, pressed on other routes, relied more on Mexican partners and increasingly paid them in cocaine, which gave Mexican organizations a larger share of the business and greater resources.

The Sinaloa networks, led by Guzmán and Zambada with other partners, operated as a flexible alliance of semi-autonomous leaders. Their decentralization helped them survive arrests, while rivalry among partners periodically split them. Guzmán's arrest in 1993, escape in 2001, later recapture and escape, and conviction in 2019 show how prisons could be both instruments of control and sites of corruption. Zambada's arrest in 2024, guilty plea in 2025, and life sentence in 2026 closed the founding leadership era, while an internal conflict followed.

The Arellano Félix family built a family-centered organization in Tijuana, reliant on corrupt police and violent enforcers, and fell victim to its concentration of power when its leaders were killed or captured. Amado Carrillo Fuentes built a wealthy Juárez organization based on air transport and Colombian partnerships, and his death in 1997 began a long decline. The Gulf organization of Tamaulipas, rooted in an older regional smuggling tradition, passed from García Ábrego to Osiel Cárdenas Guillén after the former's expulsion in 1996.

Cárdenas's recruitment of elite military deserters created Los Zetas, whose military-style organization and diversified predation, including extortion, kidnapping, and attacks on migrants, set them apart. Their break with the Gulf organization in 2010 produced a new, independent paramilitary enterprise and some of the worst atrocities of the period, notably in San Fernando, Allende, and Monterrey. The organization fragmented after the killing or capture of its leaders.

Political change mattered. The end of PRI rule in 2000 coincided with a loosening of centralized protection arrangements, though historians debate how coordinated those arrangements had been and how much weight to give political change relative to enforcement, market shifts, and organizational innovation. The late-PRI scandals of 1997, involving the anti-drug chief and the bank-laundering sting, showed the vulnerability of institutions and the distrust between Mexico and the United States.

The organizations also shared infrastructure. They depended on banks and exchange houses to move money, on U.S. firearms purchased through intermediaries, on prisons that could be penetrated, and on corrupt officials. The Mexican state was not uniformly complicit: some institutions and officials worked against the organizations at great cost, and the victims of the violence included migrants, journalists, and ordinary residents, many of whom had no connection to the trade.

Leader-focused enforcement had mixed results. Removal of leaders weakened family-based organizations, produced succession fights in others, and gave subordinates opportunities to break away. The sequence from 1993 to 2013 suggests that organizational form mattered as much as the identity of the person removed, and that fragmentation could increase violence even as it weakened the targeted organization.

The U.S. role was substantial but not controlling. American demand, firearms, banks, indictments, and designations shaped the trade, and the 2025 designation of several organizations as Foreign Terrorist Organizations reflects a U.S. legal judgment that scholars debate. The organizations' leaders, rivals, and victims were largely Mexican. This chapter leaves a foundation for Chapter 103, which examines the militarized state response and its human costs, and Chapter 104, which examines the transformed markets of the twenty-first century.

Money laundering investigations revealed the cross-border infrastructure that allowed organizations to convert illicit cash into usable resources. Operation Casablanca in 1998, Wachovia in 2010, and HSBC in 2012 concerned different cases and legal resolutions. Their figures must not be treated as measurements of one cartel’s total revenues, and a flow that was inadequately monitored is not necessarily proven illicit in its entirety.

Sources must be identified by type. An indictment records accusations; a plea identifies admitted conduct; a conviction or sentence is a legal result; a government sanction records an executive classification; and survivor testimony, investigative reporting, and scholarship reveal questions that courts may not address. None should be made to carry evidentiary weight beyond its purpose.

The 2024 transfer of Ismael Zambada and subsequent prosecutions illustrate those distinctions. Zambada pleaded guilty in August 2025 and received a life sentence in July 2026. Joaquín Guzmán López entered his plea in December 2025, while the circumstances and foreign participation in the July 2024 transfer remained disputed. Legal outcomes and sovereignty disputes must be evaluated in parallel, not collapsed into a single narrative.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the history of Mexican organized crime is the history of changing relationships among markets, geography, and states. The organizations examined here did not spring from a single founding act. They grew from border economies, regional traditions, and political arrangements, and they adapted, split, and fought as those conditions changed. Understanding them as institutions with different structures, and not as a single monolithic enemy, is necessary for understanding why policies aimed at their leaders so often produced unintended results.

The chapter also shows the cost of fragmentation to people who were not part of the trade. Migrants, journalists, residents of contested cities, and honest officials bore violence that no organizational chart explains. The sources are weighted toward leaders and prosecutors, so remembering these people requires deliberate effort and attention to the silences in the record.

Chapter 103 examines the militarized federal offensive that began in 2006 and the mass violence, disappearances, and attacks on journalists that accompanied it, placing the organizations of this chapter within a conflict that transformed Mexican public life.
