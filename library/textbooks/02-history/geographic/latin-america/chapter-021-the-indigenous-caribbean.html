The Indigenous Caribbean

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Sea That Connected

Before any European ship entered the Caribbean Sea, its islands were already joined by canoe routes, kinship ties, shared rituals, exchanged stone and shell, and the movements of people who had been crossing its waters for thousands of years. The Caribbean was not a scattering of remote islands waiting to be found. It was an Indigenous maritime world.

Imagine the view from a beach on the southern coast of Hispaniola, or on the northern shore of Puerto Rico, in the centuries before 1492. The horizon might look empty to a later European observer, but to the people living there it was part of a familiar landscape. Neighboring islands could be reached in days, sometimes in hours. Fishers knew which reefs and currents favored particular seasons. Relatives lived in villages across the water. Goods, stories, marriage partners, and ritual knowledge moved along established routes. Water was not the edge of this world. It was one of its main roads.

Chapter 20 closed the sequence of great Andean states with Tawantinsuyu, an empire that governed millions through roads, labor obligations, and administrative hierarchy. Chapter 21 opens Part V, and its task is deliberately different. Part V exists to prevent Mesoamerica and the Andes from standing in for all of Indigenous Latin America. The Caribbean shows why. Its peoples developed sophisticated political systems, intensive agriculture, elaborate religious life, monumental public spaces, and long-distance networks, but they did so without building territorial empires or stone cities on the model of Tenochtitlan or Cusco. Political complexity in the Caribbean took different forms, and understanding those forms requires setting aside the assumption that civilization must look like an empire.

The societies examined in this chapter include the peoples conventionally called Taíno in the Greater Antilles, the Lucayans of the Bahamas and the Turks and Caicos, and the varied communities of the Lesser Antilles, some of whom were later identified by Europeans as "Caribs" and whose descendants are known today as Kalinago. These labels are not simple. "Taíno" is largely a modern scholarly category applied to peoples who did not use it as a unified self-name. "Kalinago" is a later identity whose projection back to 1492 is debated. Part of this chapter's work is to explain why these names are historically difficult, and why the difficulty is itself a lesson in how colonial classifications shaped later knowledge.

The evidence for this world is unusual in one respect. For the centuries before 1492, historians depend overwhelmingly on archaeology: pottery sequences, stone and shell tools, burials, plant and animal remains, settlement layouts, plazas and ball courts, rock art in caves, and carved objects. Written descriptions exist, but they were produced after Europeans arrived, by Spanish observers such as Christopher Columbus, the friar Ramón Pané, Peter Martyr d'Anghiera, Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, and Bartolomé de las Casas. These authors described societies that were already in contact with, and being violently altered by, Spain. Their accounts are indispensable, but they carry the assumptions, vocabulary, and interests of the colonizers. A central skill of this chapter is learning to read archaeology and colonial texts against each other without allowing either to speak for the whole past.

This chapter is bounded carefully. It concerns the Indigenous Caribbean before sustained European colonization. It does not narrate Columbus's voyages, the founding of the first Spanish settlements, the earliest forced labor systems, or the demographic catastrophe that followed. Those subjects belong to Chapter 27, Chapter 29, and later chapters of Volume II. Here, Columbus appears only as one of the sources of information about a world that existed long before him, and the chapter ends with a clean handoff to that later story.

The chapter also connects to its neighbors in Part V. Caribbean communities maintained ties with northeastern South America, the Orinoco region, and the coasts of what are now Venezuela and Colombia, linking the islands to the Isthmo-Colombian world of Chapter 22 and to the tropical lowland societies of Chapter 23. The Caribbean was not an appendage of the mainland, but neither was it sealed off from it.

Finally, this chapter asks students to keep two facts in view together. The Indigenous Caribbean before 1492 was diverse, dynamic, and historically deep. And its peoples did not disappear from history after 1492. Indigenous ancestry, linguistic legacies in Caribbean vocabulary and place names, foods, technologies, and Indigenous identities persist across the Caribbean today, in communities such as the Kalinago of Dominica and in the resurgent Taíno identity movements of Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, and diaspora communities. Understanding the Indigenous Caribbean on its own terms is therefore both a historical and a contemporary responsibility.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geography of the Caribbean, including the Greater Antilles, Lesser Antilles, Bahamas, Turks and Caicos, and nearby mainland, and explain why the sea functioned as a connecting corridor.
  2. Trace the multiple phases of human settlement of the Caribbean and distinguish Archaic-era from Ceramic Age traditions, recognizing that pottery is not an absolute dividing line.
  3. Explain how the societies conventionally called Taíno developed in the Greater Antilles and why the label conceals regional diversity.
  4. Analyze how political authority worked in Caribbean chiefdoms without territorial bureaucracy, and evaluate the limits of Spanish descriptions of that authority.
  5. Describe the mixed agricultural, fishing, and gathering economy of the islands and explain why cassava alone cannot represent it.
  6. Assess the role of canoes and maritime knowledge in migration, exchange, kinship, diplomacy, and conflict.
  7. Interpret archaeological evidence for exchange networks in stone, shell, pottery, and prestige objects.
  8. Interpret zemís, caves, cohoba ritual, and ancestor relationships as evidence of Indigenous religious and political thought rather than as "idols."
  9. Explain the distinct history of the Lucayans and why they should not be treated as a peripheral offshoot of the Greater Antilles.
  10. Evaluate the historical problem of "Carib" and Kalinago identity and identify how colonial categories shaped later understanding.
  11. Distinguish archaeological evidence from post-1492 colonial testimony and explain the limitations of each.
  12. Connect the Indigenous Caribbean to the wider aims of Part V and to the later treatment of Columbus and the Caribbean encounter in Chapter 27.

Historical Context

Volume I has so far followed the rise of large and intensively studied civilizations: the Olmec, Zapotec, Teotihuacan, Maya, Mexica, and the long sequence of Andean states culminating in Tawantinsuyu. These chapters were shaped by monumental architecture, writing or recording systems, and, in the Mexica and Inca cases, imperial administration. Part V now widens the lens. Its four chapters restore the Caribbean, the Colombian and Venezuelan lands, Amazonia, and southern South America to the narrative, so that "Indigenous Latin America" is not reduced to its two best-known imperial systems.

The Caribbean presents a particular historiographical challenge. Its societies left no writing and no monumental stone cities. Many of the most informative materials, such as wooden objects, cotton textiles, and thatched buildings, decay quickly in tropical conditions. Much of the coastline that earlier peoples used has been altered by sea-level change, erosion, plantation agriculture, and modern development. And the Indigenous populations were devastated so soon after 1492 that Spanish authors described them in the midst of collapse. For these reasons, the Caribbean has sometimes been misrepresented as a simple, small-scale, or "primitive" backdrop to European arrival. Modern archaeology has thoroughly undermined that view, revealing dense settlement, elaborate ceremonial architecture, intensive food production, and extensive exchange.

Geographically, the Caribbean is usually divided into several zones. The Greater Antilles, comprising Cuba, Hispaniola (today Haiti and the Dominican Republic), Jamaica, and Puerto Rico, contains the largest islands, with substantial rivers, fertile valleys, and mountains. The Lesser Antilles form an arc of smaller islands stretching from the Virgin Islands southward to Trinidad and Tobago, near the coast of Venezuela. The Bahamas archipelago and the Turks and Caicos, lying northeast of Cuba and north of Hispaniola, are low limestone islands without large rivers. Around the sea lie mainland coasts, including Central America, Colombia, Venezuela, and the Guianas, from which some island populations originated and with which many maintained contact.

Historians also need to understand a set of conventions used in Caribbean archaeology. Prehistoric sequences are commonly divided into an Archaic age, historically often described as "preceramic," and a Ceramic Age associated with the major expansion of pottery-making, farming village societies. The division is a convenience, not an absolute line: many early Archaic assemblages lack pottery, but pottery is now documented in some contexts that predate the major Saladoid expansion. Ceramic styles are grouped into named "series" such as Saladoid, Ostionoid, Meillacoid, and Chican Ostionoid, originally organized in the twentieth century by the archaeologist Irving Rouse. These labels describe pottery styles, not necessarily peoples, languages, or political units, and contemporary archaeologists use them cautiously. Students should treat them as tools for organizing evidence, not as ethnic identities.

Finally, students should remember the periodization of this textbook. This chapter describes the centuries up to about 1492 and, where needed, uses early colonial testimony as a window onto what preceded it. It does not follow the Caribbean into the colonial period. That transition is the subject of Volume II, beginning with Iberian and African contexts in Chapters 25 and 26 and reaching the Caribbean encounter in Chapter 27.

Section 21.1

A Caribbean World before European Colonization

The first interpretive principle of this chapter is that water connected these societies as much as it separated them. Continental histories often treat coastlines as boundaries. In the Caribbean, the sea was the medium of daily life. Many islands are intervisible, meaning that a traveler leaving one shore can see the next, and the eastern Caribbean in particular forms a chain of stepping stones from the mouth of the Orinoco toward Puerto Rico. Even where the crossings are longer, as between Cuba and Hispaniola or between Hispaniola and the Bahamas, the prevailing winds and currents, combined with skilled paddling and maritime knowledge, made regular contact feasible. The result was an archipelagic world, in which communities on different islands were neighbors, trading partners, in-laws, allies, and rivals.

This chapter therefore rejects the idea of the Caribbean as "isolated islands." Archaeologists working on the Lesser Antilles have shown that exchange and social networks operated at several scales, from neighboring communities to relationships spanning many islands and reaching the mainland. Older pictures of small, self-contained island societies do not fit the evidence. What appears instead is a dense web of relationships that shifted over time but persisted for millennia.

The Caribbean also compels students to rethink what political complexity looks like. In Chapter 14 the Triple Alliance built an imperial tribute system, and in Chapter 20 Tawantinsuyu organized labor and roads across a vast mountain territory. The Caribbean's largest societies did neither. They did not create a bureaucracy that governed distant provinces, and they left no cities of monumental stone. Yet by the late fifteenth century, Greater Antillean societies had ranked social hierarchies, leaders whose authority extended across many villages, organized ceremonial centers, specialized ritual roles, and intensive food production. This is complexity of a different kind, grounded in kinship, alliance, ritual authority, and control of prestige goods more than in territorial administration.

Finally, the Caribbean forces a change of scale. Puerto Rico and Jamaica are not large by continental standards, and even Hispaniola is small beside Tawantinsuyu. Yet these islands supported substantial populations, and their historical significance is out of proportion to their size, since they became the first arena of sustained European colonization. Understanding them first as Indigenous places, with their own long histories, is the necessary corrective to narratives that begin with the arrival of ships.

Section 21.2

Peopling the Islands and the Deep Caribbean Past

Chapter 2 examined the peopling of the Americas as a whole. The peopling of the Caribbean is a separate, later, and more specific story. It was not a single migration but a multiphased process, involving groups from different directions, with different material cultures and, very likely, different languages. Archaeologists debate the details, and new dates continue to shift the picture, but the general outline is broadly accepted.

The oldest securely dated evidence currently known in the West Indies comes from Banwari Trace on Trinidad, with occupation around 7,800–7,900 calibrated years ago. In the Greater Antilles, securely documented human settlement reaches back roughly 6,000 years. The origins of these earliest settlers are debated. Some researchers see movements from Central America or Yucatán into Cuba and Hispaniola, while others emphasize movement from northern South America through Trinidad and up the Lesser Antillean chain. It is quite possible that both occurred. These early populations, called Archaic-era peoples, lived by fishing, gathering shellfish, hunting, collecting wild plants, and managing useful species. They made tools of stone, shell, and bone, and their sites include shell middens, camps, and, in some places, burial areas and rock art. Many early Archaic assemblages lack pottery, which led older archaeologists to call the period "preceramic," but pottery is now documented in some contexts well before the major Saladoid expansion. Pottery therefore cannot serve as an absolute dividing line between all earlier and later Caribbean populations. These communities were not "simple" in any case. They crossed water, exploited diverse island environments, and maintained enduring communities.

A second major phase began around 500 to 400 BCE, when ceramic-making groups from northeastern South America, associated with the Saladoid pottery tradition and with origins in the Orinoco region, moved into the Lesser Antilles and on to Puerto Rico. These arrivals brought decorated pottery, established permanent villages, and practiced agriculture, including root-crop cultivation. They did not enter empty islands in every case. Archaeological evidence increasingly suggests that they interacted with existing Archaic communities, though the nature of that interaction, whether exchange, coexistence, intermarriage, or displacement, varied and remains debated. Later ceramic traditions in the Greater Antilles, such as Ostionoid and Meillacoid, developed through local transformation, continued contact, and further migration rather than simple replacement.

Students should be careful with the language of "waves." Archaeologists often speak of pottery styles as marking arrivals, but styles can also spread through exchange and imitation, and a single site may reflect several processes at once. Linguistic evidence adds a further layer. The Indigenous languages later recorded in the Greater Antilles were of the Arawakan family, which is rooted in the northern South American lowlands, and this is consistent with a South American origin for ceramic-age migrants. But language, pottery, and biological ancestry do not always travel together. Ancient DNA studies of Caribbean remains have begun to show more complex ancestry histories than pottery sequences alone would predict, and this work must be done, as discussed in Chapter 2, in partnership with descendant communities.

Several points follow. First, the Caribbean's Indigenous history is deep and layered. Second, connection with the mainland was not a single event but a continuing relationship. Third, the peoples encountered by Europeans in 1492 were the product of thousands of years of settlement, adaptation, and interaction, not a static population arrested at an earlier stage. And fourth, earlier Archaic-era populations were part of the ancestry and history of later communities, although how much of their heritage persisted, and where, is debated. Spanish chroniclers described culturally or linguistically distinct groups in western Cuba, later labeled Guanahatabey or Ciboney, a reminder that not every island community fit a single label. Whether those groups were surviving descendants of much older Archaic populations is disputed: William Keegan's reassessment argued that the archaeological populations conventionally identified this way did not demonstrably survive to European contact, while some linguistic reconstructions still posit a distinct speech community. The question remains open.

Section 21.3

The Emergence of Taíno Societies in the Greater Antilles

From about AD 600, in the Greater Antilles, ceramic and settlement traditions grouped as Ostionoid developed from earlier Saladoid and related roots. Over the following centuries, populations grew, villages became larger and more numerous, and social hierarchy became more marked. By about AD 1200, in Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, and neighboring regions, archaeologists recognize a distinctive late tradition, Chican Ostionoid, sometimes called "Classic Taíno," characterized by particular pottery styles, more elaborate ceremonial spaces, and greater evidence of ranking. The societies encountered by the Spanish in these areas are the historical descendants of this development.

The societies conventionally called Taíno occupied Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, much of Cuba, and, through Lucayan offshoots, the Bahamas and the Turks and Caicos. Arawakan Taíno varieties were widely spoken across much of the Greater Antilles and probably functioned as an important language of interregional communication, but the region was not linguistically uniform. Colonial testimony identifies distinct speech communities, including Macorís and Ciguayo on Hispaniola, and the exact linguistic geography remains partly reconstructed. Shared elements included types of settlement, agricultural methods, forms of leadership, ritual practices such as those involving zemís and cohoba, and ball games. This is why scholars have found the category useful.

The key caution is that "Taíno" should not suggest a single nation-state or an identical culture across every island. Development was regionally diverse and uneven. The largest and most politically complex chiefdoms appear in Hispaniola and Puerto Rico, while eastern Cuba and Jamaica show important local patterns, and central and western Cuba show different local traditions whose relationship to older Archaic populations is debated. Even within Hispaniola, environments, resources, and histories varied, from the fertile Vega Real and the mountains of the interior to arid coasts and offshore islands. What Europeans described as "Taíno" society was a family of related societies, connected by language, ritual, and interaction, but politically separate, sometimes allied, and sometimes in conflict.

The name itself is a further complication. As discussed in the vocabulary box below, "Taíno" as a general ethnonym is mostly a modern scholarly and later popular usage. The peoples so labeled identified with their local communities, with their leaders, and with kin groups, not with a pan-island nation called Taíno. This does not make the term unusable. But it means students should treat it as an analytical category with a history, not as a name the peoples themselves used for all of themselves.

Historical Vocabulary
Taíno

Taíno is the standard modern name for the Arawakan-speaking Indigenous societies of the Greater Antilles and their Lucayan offshoots at the time of European contact. The word appears in early colonial reports, where it is often interpreted as an expression meaning something like "good" or "noble" used to distinguish the speakers from other peoples, but its adoption as a general name for all these societies is largely a product of later scholarship, especially from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The term matters because it gives students a shorthand for a large and important set of societies, but it also carries risks. It can flatten regional variation, imply a unified political identity that did not exist, and suggest that these peoples vanished. Today, many people in Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, and the Caribbean diaspora claim Taíno ancestry and identity, and the term now also has a living political and cultural meaning. It should not be confused with "Arawak," an older and broader label sometimes applied to Indigenous Caribbean peoples but properly used for a large language family and for certain mainland groups, not as a synonym for every island population.

Historical Debate

How Useful Is "Taíno" as a Single Cultural Label?

The question: Is "Taíno" a justified way of describing the societies of the Greater Antilles, or does it impose a false unity on diverse, changing communities?

Interpretation A — "Taíno" identifies a real, shared cultural tradition: Supporters point to a common Arawakan language family, related pottery and settlement patterns, similar ritual forms such as zemí veneration and cohoba, shared ball-court traditions, comparable ranked leadership, and evidence of ongoing interaction. On this view, the label captures a genuine historical pattern, and abandoning it would leave students without a way to discuss these connections.
Interpretation B — "Taíno" obscures regional diversity and changing identities: Critics argue that the label was projected onto diverse communities by outsiders, that the peoples so named identified locally, that political forms and material culture varied considerably across islands and regions, and that the term can wrongly suggest a homogeneous, static population. Some also note that older categories such as "Ciboney" and "Arawak" were similarly over-extended, and that overly neat schemes reflect colonial classification more than Indigenous reality.

Evidence: Both positions rely on archaeology, linguistic evidence, and Spanish descriptions. They differ in how much weight to give shared features versus local variation, and in how far they trust colonial categories.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether the Greater Antilles appears as one culture area or as a mosaic of related societies, and it affects contemporary questions of Indigenous identity and recognition. This textbook uses "Taíno" with care, as a broad heuristic, while naming particular islands, regions, and leaders whenever evidence permits.

Student task: Construct the best argument for each interpretation. Then identify what additional evidence, such as ancient DNA, new excavations, or additional Indigenous-language material, might shift the balance.

Section 21.4

Caciques, Communities, and Caribbean Political Organization

How did political authority work in the Indigenous Caribbean without the territorial bureaucracy of Tawantinsuyu? The answer lies in kinship, prestige, ritual, redistribution, and alliance. The basic unit was the village, a community of households, often numbering from a few dozen to several hundred people, and in the larger chiefdoms of Hispaniola sometimes more. Spanish sources describe villages as clusters of round thatched houses called bohíos arranged around a central plaza, with a larger rectangular house, the caney, for the leader. Archaeology confirms settlement patterns of this kind, along with variations in village size and layout.

Each community was led by a cacique, a Taíno word that Spaniards later applied to Indigenous rulers across the Americas. Sources indicate that caciques could be men or women, and that succession was often reckoned through the maternal line, with a cacique succeeded by a sister's son or other relative. Caciques held high rank, enjoyed privileges, received deference and tribute in food and labor, sponsored feasts and ceremonies, and mediated with spiritual beings. Alongside them stood a hereditary elite, described by the Spanish as nitaínos, and a mass of commoners, often called naboría. Ritual specialists, the behiques, healed, conducted cohoba rituals, and interpreted the will of the zemís. Such vocabulary comes from Spanish sources and must be read with caution, since it may reflect Spanish attempts to fit Indigenous institutions into European social categories.

Larger political formations existed above the village. Spanish accounts describe several major regional polities on Hispaniola in the late fifteenth century, usually identified as Marién in the northwest, Maguá in the Vega Real, Maguana in the central and southern interior, Jaragua in the southwest, and Higüey in the east, each led by a principal cacique to whom lesser caciques owed allegiance. Names associated with these polities, such as Guacanagarí, Guarionex, Caonabo, Behechio, and Cayacoa, and the prominent female leader Anacaona, appear in the early colonial record. In Puerto Rico, leaders such as Agüeybaná appear, and Spanish sources also mention female caciques. These accounts describe real political alignments, but their scheme of five neat kingdoms probably simplifies a more fluid landscape of alliances, marriages, rivalries, and shifting loyalties.

Differences among islands were significant. The larger chiefdoms of Hispaniola and Puerto Rico had greater population, more evident ranking, and more monumental ceremonial spaces. Smaller communities elsewhere, including on Jamaica, in eastern Cuba, and on smaller islands, appear to have had leaders whose reach was more local. Some peoples, such as those described in western Cuba, appear to have been organized differently again, though their nature is debated. Students should therefore avoid assuming a single four-tier chiefdom model applied everywhere, a temptation created by the tidiness of later chroniclers.

The central point is that authority was relational and ritual as much as administrative. A cacique's power rested on genealogy, the loyalty of kin and followers, the ability to organize feasts and ceremonies, control of prestige items such as guanín, and the perceived favor of ancestral and supernatural beings. Chiefly authority was reinforced through marriage alliances, gift exchange, and public performance, and it could be contested. This was a political world in which persuasion, generosity, and legitimacy mattered profoundly. It is not a "lesser" form of politics than empire, but a different one, adapted to island environments and to the possibilities of movement by sea.

Historical Vocabulary
Cacique and Cacicazgo

Cacique is a Taíno word for a community or regional leader that Spanish colonizers carried across the Americas and applied broadly to Indigenous authorities, including in Mexico and the Andes. Cacicazgo refers to the polity, following, and territory associated with a cacique. In this chapter, the terms describe Caribbean political forms, but students should remember that the later, wider colonial use of the word often erased important local differences.

"Chiefdom" is a modern anthropological category often applied to cacicazgos. It is helpful for signaling ranked, kinship-based leadership without a bureaucratic state, but it should not be used to suggest that these societies were "less developed" versions of states. Different island communities practiced different forms of leadership.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Political Complexity Develop Differently across Caribbean Islands?

Long-term causes

Island size and agricultural potential, access to river valleys and fertile soils, the timing and pattern of settlement, existing Archaic populations, and the position of each island within maritime routes to the mainland and to neighboring islands.

Short-term causes

Population growth in the centuries after AD 600, intensifying competition for prestige and resources, marriage alliances among elite families, and the growth of ceremonial centers that concentrated ritual authority.

Effect

Large, regionally organized cacicazgos in Hispaniola and Puerto Rico, smaller and more local leadership elsewhere, and enduring variety in political form across the archipelago.

Long-term consequences

Different islands entered the colonial period with different political structures, which shaped early Spanish strategies, Indigenous responses, and the timing of both accommodation and resistance, examined in Volume II.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains why some islands developed larger polities. Geography, demography, contingency, warfare, alliance, and exchange all interacted, and archaeologists continue to debate their relative weight.

Section 21.5

Farming the Islands: Cassava, Maize, Conucos, and Food Production

The Indigenous Caribbean fed itself through a mixed economy that combined cultivation with fishing, shellfish collecting, hunting, and gathering. The most famous crop is manioc, also called cassava or yuca. Bitter manioc is highly productive and stores well in the ground, but it contains toxic compounds and must be processed. Caribbean communities grated the roots, pressed the pulp in a woven tubular press, the cibucán, to extract the poisonous liquid, and baked the flour into flat bread on a clay griddle, the budare. Sweet varieties could be eaten with less processing. Cassava bread kept well and could be stored and carried on voyages, which mattered to a maritime society.

But cassava alone did not feed the Caribbean. Cultivators also grew sweet potatoes, yautía (tannia), maize, beans, squash, peppers, peanuts, and other plants, along with cotton, tobacco, gourds, and dye plants such as achiote. Fruit trees and palms, including guava and other useful species, were part of managed landscapes. The proportion of each crop varied by island and time. Archaeobotanical work, including starch-grain and phytolith analysis, shows that maize and peppers, and possibly manioc, were present in the Bahamas by around AD 800, and that broader crop repertoires characterized many island communities. Students should treat any single-crop summary with suspicion.

Cultivation typically took place in conucos, a term used for plots or gardens. Spanish accounts describe mounded planting, sometimes called montones, in which soil was heaped to improve drainage, aeration, and root development, and to reduce erosion in tropical rain. Multiple crops could be planted together, and plots were managed through shifting fallow and rotation. This is a sophisticated, ecologically adapted form of agriculture, not an undeveloped one. Its productivity supported growing populations in the Greater Antilles, and the ability of leaders to organize labor and feasts from surplus helped underpin political authority.

Agriculture was matched by rich use of the sea, rivers, and land. Communities caught fish with hooks, nets, weirs, and traps, and communities collected mollusks such as conch, gathered crabs, hunted sea turtles and manatees, and took birds, iguanas, and the hutía, a large rodent. Some coastal sites show heavy reliance on marine resources, while inland communities depended more on cultivation and hunting. In low, dry islands such as the Bahamas, marine foods played an especially large part. The overall pattern was a mixed terrestrial and marine economy, flexible and responsive to local conditions.

Food production was also a social and gendered system. Spanish sources suggest that men and women contributed differently to clearing, planting, harvesting, fishing, and processing, and that leaders' households organized labor for communal fields and feasting. Because these sources reflect outsiders' assumptions, historians should be careful about reading them as complete descriptions. But the general point, that food, labor, and ritual were tightly linked, is well supported.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Coastal Village Household

Consider, as a composite reconstruction rather than a documented family, a household in a coastal Greater Antillean village in the fifteenth century. Its members live in a round thatched bohío near the village plaza. Some tend mounded plots of manioc, sweet potatoes, and maize, clearing weeds and harvesting according to the seasons. Others grate and press manioc, bake bread on a clay griddle, spin and weave cotton, and shape and fire pottery. Men and women fish from shore and canoe, gather shellfish from the reef, and carve and repair dugout canoes. Children learn to swim, paddle, and recognize plants, and infants may have their foreheads gently shaped, a practice documented archaeologically, though its precise social meaning, whether identity, affiliation, or status, is not always recoverable.

Household members take part in village feasts and the ball game, and they travel by canoe to visit relatives on other coasts and islands, carrying bread, cotton goods, shell ornaments, and news. The household owes deference and some labor to the cacique, and joins ceremonies led by ritual specialists. It keeps small zemís in the house, honoring ancestors. Written sources tell us little about such ordinary lives, because Spanish authors focused on leaders and on features they found striking. Archaeology, through house floors, food remains, tools, and burials, is our main window on commoner households, which is why it matters that students learn to read material evidence.

Section 21.6

Canoes, Seafaring, and Maritime Knowledge

The canoe is one of the signature technologies of the Indigenous Caribbean. The very word "canoe" entered European languages from a Caribbean Indigenous language through early Spanish contact. Caribbean communities made dugout canoes from the trunks of large trees, notably ceiba and other timber species, hollowing them with fire and stone or shell tools, and shaping them for stability and speed. Spanish observers reported canoes of many sizes, from small craft for fishing and short passages to large vessels capable of carrying several dozen people; early descriptions of Lucayan craft, for example, report roughly forty or more passengers. Paddling was the principal documented means of propulsion; some later or secondary descriptions also report sail use in parts of the Caribbean, and how far sails were a precontact technology is uncertain. Because wooden craft rarely survive, most of what we know derives from these descriptions, occasional archaeological finds, and the distribution of goods moved across water.

Successful seafaring required more than boats. It demanded detailed knowledge of winds, currents, tides, reefs, coastlines, weather, and the behavior of the sea in different seasons. Navigators likely drew on cues including the sun, stars, swells, birds, cloud formations, and the visible outlines of neighboring islands to find their way. Such knowledge was transmitted across generations, presumably through practical training (how it was transmitted is an inference, since no direct evidence survives), and it represented an intellectual achievement comparable to the astronomical and mathematical traditions of Mesoamerica or the administrative knowledge of the Andes, even though it left no written record.

Boats served many purposes. They supported fishing and gathering of marine resources, and enabled the collection of salt in areas where it was available. They carried people for visiting, marriage alliance, diplomacy, ritual gatherings, and trade. They enabled migration, including the movement of ceramic-age groups from the mainland into the Antilles and of Greater Antillean settlers to the Bahamas. And they made raiding and warfare possible. The same technology that linked communities in friendship could also project conflict, and Spanish sources mention canoe-borne fighting among Indigenous peoples. Students should note, however, that raiding and warfare were not uniquely characteristic of any one group, and that later European stereotypes assigned aggression selectively.

Seen in this light, the Caribbean appears as a maritime landscape rather than as continental societies inconveniently separated by water. Coastal villages faced outward as well as inward. Beaches were landing places, reefs were resource zones, and sea lanes were routes of relationship. The ability of a community to build, maintain, and use canoes, and to know the sea, was central to its wealth, security, and reputation. It was also a foundation for the extensive networks discussed in the next section.

Section 21.7

Exchange and Island Networks

Archaeologists trace the movement of materials across the Caribbean by identifying where they came from and where they were found. Distinctive stones, such as certain cherts and volcanic rocks, can be sourced to particular islands. Shell, especially the large conch, was made into ornaments, tools, and ritual objects. Pottery styles, and sometimes the clays themselves, show contact and copying. Ornaments of stone and shell, carved beads, and pendants moved between communities. Foodstuffs, cotton goods, and perishable items likely circulated too, though they rarely survive.

Students should be careful with the word "trade." Modern commercial language suggests markets, prices, and profit-seeking merchants, whereas the evidence in the Caribbean points to a more varied set of practices, including gift exchange between allied leaders, kin-based reciprocity, ritual exchange, and perhaps more commercial forms of barter. Where evidence does not justify commercial terminology, it is more accurate to speak of exchange, circulation, and interaction.

Exchange in the Lesser Antilles offers especially clear evidence. Research on the period from roughly 400 BCE to AD 1492 shows overlapping networks at different scales, with utilitarian objects circulating locally, identity-marking goods moving among linked communities, and ceremonial or prestige items traveling farther. A particularly well-known case is the distribution of a distinctive flint from Antigua across nearby islands. Some greenstone and other exotic stones found in the islands appear to derive from distant sources, including, in some interpretations, the Motagua valley of Guatemala, though the pathways by which such items reached the islands remain unclear and debated.

Connections with the mainland also mattered. Prestige metal objects, notably ornaments of guanín, a gold-copper alloy with mainland analogues in the Isthmo-Colombian world explored in Chapter 22, appear in island contexts, and they were prized by elites as markers of status and spiritual power. Their presence suggests contacts along routes connecting the islands to northern South America, perhaps mediated through intermediate communities. Such objects also help show that Caribbean elites participated in wider systems of prestige and cosmology.

The overall picture is that the Caribbean was interconnected long before Europeans created colonial shipping networks. Spanish ships and later plantation trade did not first knit the islands together. They entered, redirected, and violently reorganized a web of relationships that already existed. This is one of the most important corrections that Indigenous archaeology makes to older accounts of the region.

Section 21.8

Religion, Zemís, Ancestors, Caves, and Sacred Landscapes

Religion in the Indigenous Caribbean was inseparable from politics, kinship, agriculture, and the landscape. Our evidence comes from two kinds of sources. Archaeology yields carved objects, ceremonial spaces, cave sites, and burials. And the brief account by Ramón Pané, written in the 1490s at Columbus's request, records the beliefs he was able to learn among the Indigenous people of parts of Hispaniola, filtered through translation, his own religious framework, and the disruption already underway. Later authors add details. Neither type of evidence is sufficient alone.

Central to Taíno religion were zemís (also spelled cemís). The term denotes both spiritual beings and the objects, carved from stone, wood, shell, bone, or made of cotton, that embodied or mediated their presence. Zemís could be associated with ancestors, natural forces, fertility, and the well-being of the community and its crops. A cacique's authority was tied to his or her relationship with powerful zemís, and elite families kept prominent ones. A distinctive class of stone objects with three points, often connected with the fertility of manioc, is also linked to zemí practice. Spanish observers called these objects "idols," a label that misrepresents them, since they were not simply statues to be worshipped but active presences in relationships with the living.

Ancestors were vital. Communities honored their dead, and some evidence, including Pané's account and archaeological burial contexts, suggests that ancestral bones and remains could be kept and treated with reverence. Ancestor relationships anchored claims to land and to leadership. Origins were also located in the landscape. Pané reports origin traditions in which the first people emerged from caves in a mountain on Hispaniola, and the Caribbean has numerous caves with pictographs and petroglyphs, including in the Dominican Republic and on Mona Island, showing that caves were treated as sacred, liminal places connected with emergence, ancestors, and the underworld.

Ritual specialists, the behiques, mediated with spirit beings. One ritual, cohoba, involved inhaling a hallucinogenic snuff prepared from the seeds of a tree, using tubes and, in some cases, elaborately carved inhalers. Participants, especially leaders and ritual specialists, sought communication with zemís and ancestors. Ritual also involved fasting and purging with vomit spatulas. Carved duhos, ceremonial seats, elevated leaders and ritual specialists in ceremonies. Such practices were not private superstition. They were public political theater, legitimating authority, guiding decisions, and reinforcing community ties.

Gendered dimensions appear in these sources too. Women could be leaders, and female figures appear in origin traditions and in carved representations, though the details of women's ritual roles are hard to reconstruct from male-authored colonial texts. Students should be alert to this evidentiary gap and avoid assuming either that women were marginal or that we know the full range of their religious authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Zemí (cemí)

Zemí refers both to a sacred being and to the physical object, whether carved wood, stone, shell, bone, or cotton, that embodied or mediated the presence of that being. Zemís could represent ancestors, natural powers, and agricultural or protective forces, and they were kept by households, caciques, and communities.

The term should not be translated simply as "idol." Spanish observers used that word because it fit European categories of false worship, and it distorted the relational, ancestral, and political role of zemís. Understanding zemís as participants in social and political relationships is essential to reading Caribbean religion as a coherent intellectual and ceremonial tradition.

Primary Source Window

The Zemí and the Duho: Reading Indigenous Objects through Colonial Texts

Creators: Indigenous artists and ritual specialists of the Greater Antilles (objects); Ramón Pané, Catalan friar (written account, 1490s) · Dates: Objects range across the centuries before and around 1492; Pané wrote on Hispaniola in the mid-to-late 1490s · Type: Material culture (carved wooden and stone zemís, duhos, three-pointed stones, cohoba inhalers) read alongside an early colonial ethnographic account

Context: Museums and archaeological collections hold carved wooden and stone objects from the Greater Antilles, including seats, figures with expressive faces and elaborate bodies, and inhalers. Some wooden objects survived because they were deposited in caves, where dry conditions preserved them. In the 1490s, Pané, who had been sent to Hispaniola with Columbus's expedition, learned the local language and recorded beliefs about zemís, ancestors, origins, and ritual specialists. His original manuscript is lost. The fullest surviving text comes through its incorporation into the history attributed to Ferdinand (Hernando) Columbus, which itself survives chiefly through Alfonso de Ulloa's Italian translation published in Venice in 1571, so modern editions rest on a chain of transcription, translation, and reconstruction.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): Pané explains that leaders and communities kept zemís of various kinds, that ritual specialists consulted them in ceremonies involving inhaled snuff, that zemís were connected to ancestors and to the fertility of crops, and that origin traditions placed the first people in caves.

Analysis: The objects and the text illuminate one another. The objects show the skill, symbolism, and value of Caribbean art and the association of certain seats and images with elite ritual. The text supplies concepts, names, and stories that help interpret them. But each has limits. Objects do not explain themselves, and their original contexts are often lost. Pané's account reflects a Christian missionary's categories, the demands of writing for a European readership, evangelizing aims, the disruption already affecting Indigenous communities, the constraints of translation, the political situation of the 1490s, and the limits of what he was told or allowed to see. It also records the beliefs of particular communities in one part of Hispaniola, and cannot be assumed to represent all Caribbean peoples. Absent from both are the voices of commoners, women in most ritual roles, and the many communities Pané did not meet.

Questions for students:

  1. What kinds of information does a carved duho or zemí provide that a written account cannot?
  2. Who created Pané's account, for whom, and under what circumstances?
  3. What can historians learn from combining objects and text, and what dangers arise from doing so?
  4. What can historians not establish from these sources alone, and whose perspectives are missing?
Section 21.9

Ballcourts, Plazas, Feasting, and Public Life

Where Mesoamerica built pyramids and the Andes built terraced ceremonial centers, the Greater Antilles built a different kind of public architecture: open plazas, often called bateyes, and stone-bordered ball courts. These spaces served as arenas for ball games, dances, feasts, negotiation, ritual, and political display. Their significance lies in their social use rather than in monumental height or mass, and they show that community space and ritual practice can express complexity as powerfully as stone cities.

Puerto Rico offers some of the best evidence. Sites such as Tibes near Ponce and Caguana near Utuado include bordered plazas and courts with upright stones, some carved with petroglyph images. These centers were occupied and modified over centuries, beginning in the first millennium CE, and they show growing formality of ceremonial space over time. Similar features occur in Hispaniola, eastern Cuba, and Jamaica. The distribution of ball courts is uneven, and not every plaza was used in the same way. Some may have been principally for ball games, others for dances and assemblies, and some served as burial or ritual spaces. Students should avoid assuming uniformity.

Spanish sources describe a ball game, called batú in some accounts, played with a rubber ball by teams of many players, with the ball kept in the air by the body, though not the hands. They report that the game could involve wagering and could serve as ritual and diplomacy, and in some accounts that women also played. Whether the Caribbean game derives from, is related to, or developed independently of the Mesoamerican ballgame examined in earlier chapters is debated. Rubber and ball games had a long history in the Americas, and contact across the Gulf and Caribbean cannot be excluded, but evidence for a direct link is uncertain.

Public life was also marked by feasting and performance. Colonial authors describe areítos, communal ceremonies with song, dance, and instruments, in which stories of ancestors, leaders, and community history were recounted and performed. These would have been a form of collective memory, an oral archive of the kind discussed in Chapter 4. Feasts, sponsored by caciques and drawing on surplus food, cemented obligations and displayed generosity. Cemeteries and burial areas, sometimes near plazas, integrated the dead into community space, reinforcing the ancestral basis of authority.

These public spaces demonstrate that the Indigenous Caribbean had a rich civic life. They provided settings where leaders and communities negotiated alliances, resolved disputes, honored the dead, and celebrated. They also provide a way for archaeologists to study social organization, since the scale, formality, and elaboration of plazas and courts track changes in population, hierarchy, and ritual practice across time and place.

Section 21.10

The Lucayans of the Bahamas and Turks and Caicos

The Lucayans deserve fuller treatment than a paragraph attached to the Taíno. The Bahamas and the Turks and Caicos form a large archipelago of low limestone islands and cays, with limited fresh water, thin soils, and rich marine environments. Archaeological evidence indicates that these islands were settled by ancestors of the Lucayans expanding from the Greater Antilles, with the strongest evidence pointing to Hispaniola and continuing debate over Cuba and other routes. Current syntheses generally place colonization around AD 700/800 to 1100, varying by island; a Turks and Caicos site provides evidence by the early eighth century. Research continues to refine the chronology. The name "Lucayan" is often linked to an Indigenous expression meaning something like "island people."

Settlers moved by canoe, most clearly from the north coast of Hispaniola and possibly from Cuba, crossing open water of considerable distance. They established villages on many islands, adapted to conditions very different from those of the large islands, and developed a distinctive material culture. Their pottery, often called Palmetto Ware, is a local style related to Greater Antillean traditions but recognizably Lucayan. Communities relied on farming, using crops such as maize, sweet potato, peppers, and probably manioc where soils allowed, and on rich marine resources, including fish, conch, turtles, and other reef species, along with land animals such as the hutía and iguanas. Salt and shell were also important resources.

The Lucayans were not isolated. Archaeological evidence shows continuing connections with Hispaniola and Cuba, including imported goods and shared pottery traditions, and this is consistent with regular canoe travel. Some ritual objects and ceremonial practices resemble those of the Greater Antilles. At the same time, Lucayan society had its own patterns of settlement, with smaller communities spread across many islands and, as far as we can tell, less developed large-scale hierarchy than in the biggest Greater Antillean chiefdoms. Estimates of Lucayan population at the end of the fifteenth century vary widely, from several thousand to tens of thousands, and remain uncertain.

The crucial point is that the Lucayans were not geographically isolated peripheral Taíno. They developed a distinctive island society within wider Caribbean networks, adapted to comparatively low, dry environments and to a sea-centered way of life. Their historical importance is sometimes overshadowed by the fact that theirs were among the first Indigenous communities Europeans met in 1492, and among the first to suffer from enslavement and removal, subjects treated in Volume II. In this chapter, the Lucayans should be seen first as an accomplished, self-sufficient, and connected people in their own right.

Section 21.11

The Lesser Antilles and the Kalinago Problem

The Lesser Antilles are the chain of islands running from the Virgin Islands southward to Trinidad and Tobago. They were among the first islands settled by ceramic-age migrants from the mainland, and they remained closely tied to northeastern South America through the following millennia. Archaeological research shows communities with varied ceramic traditions, seafaring economies, local and long-distance exchange, and complex social relationships across islands. The pre-1492 eastern Caribbean was neither an empty frontier nor a simple corridor. It was a densely interwoven region whose peoples participated in the wider Caribbean and mainland worlds.

The terminology for these peoples is historically difficult. From the second voyage of Columbus, Spaniards began to call some island peoples "Caribs" or "Caribes," and to distinguish them from the "Taíno" or "Arawaks" of the Greater Antilles. Those labels were quickly entangled with Spanish claims about cannibalism and hostility, and with legal categories that permitted the enslavement of people classed as Caribs. Later, in the seventeenth century, European observers recorded communities in the Lesser Antilles, such as those in Dominica, Saint Vincent, and Guadeloupe, that identified themselves in terms such as Kalinago, and that were called "Island Caribs" by outsiders. Today, the Kalinago Territory in Dominica and communities elsewhere preserve this heritage and identity.

Historians and archaeologists now regard the older story as far less secure than earlier textbooks assumed. The old narrative held that a warlike "Carib" people invaded the Lesser Antilles from South America shortly before 1492, displacing or exterminating a peaceful Arawak population. Contemporary scholarship rejects the older model of a single, simple "Carib invasion" that replaced a peaceful and homogeneous Arawak population. Archaeological continuity in the Lesser Antilles is considerable, and Indigenous communities show complex mixtures of traditions. Linguistic evidence suggests that the Island Carib language belonged to the Arawakan family, with some Carib-derived vocabulary, complicating simple ethnic divisions. Debate nevertheless continues over late mainland-to-island movements, and some recent archaeological comparisons between the Guianas and the Lesser Antilles have renewed arguments for the historical movement of Galibi/Kalinago-related peoples into the islands; the timing, scale, and relationship of such movements to existing island communities remain disputed. Debate also continues about how directly the later Kalinago identity can be projected back to the peoples of the Lesser Antilles in 1492, and about how mainland connections shaped island identities.

Students should avoid two errors. The first is repeating the colonial binary of "peaceful Arawak" and "savage cannibal Carib." Spanish claims about cannibalism became bound up with justifications for enslavement, and historians treat them as products of a colonial discourse, not as established ethnography. Some Indigenous peoples did practice forms of ritual treatment of enemy bodies, and evidence is disputed and varied, but "cannibal" as a blanket description of a whole people was a European projection. The second error is treating raiding and warfare as uniquely "Carib." Communities throughout the Caribbean, including in the Greater Antilles, engaged in conflict and defense, as they did everywhere.

A better approach is to treat the Lesser Antilles as home to a range of communities with varied histories and identities, connected by seafaring, exchange, kinship, and sometimes conflict, and to see the Kalinago identity as a historically developed one whose relation to earlier populations is a matter of ongoing research. After 1492, colonization, disease, warfare, and displacement reshaped these communities, and in Saint Vincent, later interactions with Africans produced the Garifuna, a story that belongs to later chapters, especially Chapters 44 and 47.

Historical Debate

Who Were the Peoples Europeans Called "Caribs"?

The question: To what extent do European labels of "Carib" and later Kalinago identity describe distinct Indigenous peoples of the Lesser Antilles in 1492?

Interpretation A — There was a real cultural and political distinction that later identity reflects: Some scholars argue that eastern Caribbean communities did have distinctive practices, alliances, and self-understandings, connected with mainland groups, and that the later Kalinago identity descends from these. They point to persistent island–mainland ties, distinctive material culture, and early colonial reports of separate peoples, and caution against dismissing Indigenous identity as merely a colonial invention.
Interpretation B — The category was largely shaped by colonial classification: Other scholars emphasize that "Carib" was a European label with legal and political uses, that it was applied broadly and inconsistently, that archaeological evidence shows continuity and blending rather than a sharp division, and that linguistic evidence complicates neat ethnic boundaries. On this view, Kalinago identity emerged through colonial-era history and cannot be assumed for all Lesser Antillean peoples of 1492.

Evidence: The debate draws on archaeological continuity and change in pottery and settlement, linguistic analysis, early Spanish reports, later seventeenth-century French and English observations, and Indigenous testimony. Scholarship rejects the older single-invasion narrative but treats late mainland-to-island movement, and its relation to existing island communities, as an unresolved question.

Why it matters: The answer affects how students understand Indigenous diversity in the eastern Caribbean, how colonial categories shaped later knowledge and policy, and how contemporary Kalinago communities claim and interpret their history.

Student task: Explain why a European label with legal consequences might distort historical evidence. Then identify types of evidence, from archaeology, linguistics, and Indigenous oral tradition, that could help distinguish earlier identities from later ones.

Section 21.12

The Caribbean on the Eve of Sustained European Colonization

By the late fifteenth century, the Indigenous Caribbean was a populous, differentiated, and active world. Population estimates for the late precontact Caribbean remain among the most disputed problems in the region's history. Colonial-era figures, including the large numbers offered by Las Casas, and later demographic reconstructions have produced estimates ranging from tens or hundreds of thousands to more than a million for Hispaniola, while recent ancient-DNA research has argued for substantially smaller populations. Genetic effective population size is not identical to census population, however, so these findings have not eliminated the debate. What is not in dispute is that settlement was substantial, particularly in the fertile valleys and coasts of the Greater Antilles, and that it supported large villages, ceremonial centers, and regional political alliances.

Political differentiation was pronounced. Large, ranked cacicazgos in Hispaniola and Puerto Rico coexisted with smaller polities elsewhere, with Lucayan island communities, with the distinct communities described in western Cuba, and with the varied communities of the Lesser Antilles. Alliances and rivalries existed among caciques, and marriage and gift exchange linked leaders across distances. Conflicts were real, and so was cooperation. It is misleading to imagine either a uniformly peaceful Indigenous Caribbean or a landscape of perpetual war.

Maritime networks were active. Canoes carried goods, people, and news among islands and to the mainland. Ritual life was rich, with zemís, cohoba ceremonies, ancestor veneration, and communal areítos. Agricultural landscapes of conucos, gardens, fishing grounds, and managed trees surrounded villages. This was a world in motion, shaped by long histories of migration and interaction, and still changing, as the growth of larger chiefdoms in the Greater Antilles shows.

Students should also recognize what is uncertain. The detailed political map described by Spanish sources may oversimplify. Estimates of population, the reach of particular caciques, and the internal workings of religious institutions rest on a combination of archaeology and post-1492 testimony, each with limits. Some of what appears in colonial writing may reflect changes already underway in the years immediately before and after Columbus.

A note on the boundary of this chapter. Chapter 27 will return to this same Indigenous Caribbean when Columbus enters it in 1492, and will narrate his voyages, the first encounters, the founding of settlements on Hispaniola, the imposition of coercive labor, and early violence. Chapter 29 will examine the epidemics and demographic catastrophe that followed. This chapter has intentionally stopped short of those events, so that the Indigenous Caribbean can be understood first on its own terms. When students read about the arrival of Europeans, they should carry with them the picture of a connected, historically deep, and diverse world, not of isolated communities awaiting discovery.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Networked Caribbean

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from any named person, and the groups did not possess equal power or status.

From the perspective of a Greater Antillean cacique or cacica: Authority depends on genealogy, alliances, control of prestige items such as guanín, and the favor of ancestral and spiritual beings. Feasts, ball games, and ceremonies are opportunities to display generosity and legitimacy, while maritime and inland connections supply goods and marriage partners.
From the perspective of a commoner farming household: Daily life centers on conucos, fishing, cassava processing, cotton work, and community obligations. The cacique receives labor and food, but also sponsors feasts and offers protection. Kin ties across the water matter for visiting, ritual, and mutual aid.
From the perspective of a Lucayan island community: Life is oriented to the sea, with fishing, salt, shell, and modest gardens on thin soils. Canoe links with Hispaniola and Cuba bring goods, ideas, and relatives. The community is part of a wider Caribbean while maintaining its own identity and practices.
From the perspective of a Lesser Antillean seafaring community: Sea routes link the community to neighboring islands and to the mainland. Exchange, kinship, alliance, and sometimes conflict shape relationships. Identity is local and relational, not defined by the later European categories of "Arawak" and "Carib."
From the perspective of a later Spanish chronicler describing these societies: Observations are filtered through European religious and political assumptions, and often through the interests of colonization. Details of ritual, leadership, and warfare are recorded selectively, and Indigenous concepts are translated into Spanish categories such as "king," "idol," or "Carib."
Continuity and Change Over Time

Maritime Mobility and the Rise of Hierarchy

What Changed?

  • Archaic-era communities interacted with incoming and expanding Ceramic Age populations; over time, pottery-making village traditions and more intensive agriculture became increasingly widespread
  • Larger, more hierarchical polities emerged in parts of the Greater Antilles, especially after about AD 600 and again from about AD 1200
  • Ceremonial architecture, including plazas and ball courts, became more formal and elaborate
  • Island populations grew and settlement expanded to the Bahamas and Turks and Caicos

What Remained Continuous?

  • Reliance on the sea for food, movement, and connection
  • Ties with the South American mainland and among islands
  • Mixed economies combining cultivation, fishing, and gathering
  • The central role of ancestors, ritual specialists, and sacred places

Who experienced change most directly? Communities in the fertile Greater Antilles saw the most striking growth in hierarchy and ceremonial architecture. Who experienced greater continuity? Some smaller-island communities, including many Lucayan and Lesser Antillean villages, retained more locally organized lifeways within wider networks, although the rate and form of change varied considerably by island and period.

Why the pattern matters: Deep continuities of mobility and connection coexisted with regional transformation. Recognizing both prevents the picture of a static Indigenous Caribbean and shows that the societies Europeans met after 1492 were at a moment in an ongoing history.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Caribbean before sustained European colonization was an interconnected Indigenous maritime world, not a scatter of isolated islands and not a single homogeneous culture. Its peoples, whose histories reached back thousands of years, connected the Greater Antilles, Lesser Antilles, Bahamas, Turks and Caicos, and neighboring mainland through migration, exchange, kinship, diplomacy, ritual, and conflict. Water was the medium of these connections.

The chapter began with the settlement of the islands, a multiphased process that included Archaic-era peoples arriving many thousands of years ago (securely by roughly 7,900 years ago in Trinidad and 6,000 years ago in the Greater Antilles) and later ceramic-making groups from northeastern South America, beginning around 500 to 400 BCE. These groups interacted with earlier populations and developed diverse local traditions. Archaeological pottery series such as Saladoid, Ostionoid, and Chican Ostionoid help organize the evidence but should not be mistaken for peoples or identities.

From about AD 600, and especially after about AD 1200, societies in the Greater Antilles developed larger villages, ranked leadership, and elaborate ceremonial spaces. These are the societies conventionally called Taíno. The chapter emphasized that the label captures shared language, ritual, and cultural forms but conceals substantial regional diversity, and that it is largely a modern category. Political authority rested on caciques, male and female, whose power derived from kinship, prestige goods, feasting, ritual legitimacy, and alliance, not from a territorial bureaucracy like that of Tawantinsuyu.

Economic life combined cultivation with fishing, shellfish collection, hunting, and gathering. Cassava was important, but Caribbean food production also included maize, sweet potatoes, peppers, other root crops, fruits, and marine resources, organized through conucos and mixed economies. Canoes and detailed maritime knowledge made possible movement, exchange, marriage alliance, and conflict, and they underlay networks in stone, shell, pottery, ornaments, and prestige goods that connected the islands and reached the mainland.

Religious life was inseparable from politics and landscape. Zemís, ancestors, caves, cohoba ritual, duhos, and ritual specialists formed an intellectual and ceremonial tradition, known through archaeology and the mediated testimony of Ramón Pané and other colonial authors. Plazas, ball courts, feasts, and areítos structured public life. The Lucayans developed a distinctive society in the low, dry islands of the Bahamas and Turks and Caicos, adapting to marine environments while staying connected to Hispaniola and Cuba.

The Lesser Antilles required special care. The older story of a violent "Carib" invasion replacing peaceful "Arawaks" is no longer secure, and the applicability of Kalinago identity to all island peoples of 1492 remains debated. The colonial binary of peaceful Taíno and cannibal Caribs was bound up with justifications for enslavement, and it should not be repeated as ethnography. Instead, students should see a varied set of communities within wider networks.

The chapter also modeled an approach to evidence. Archaeology, linguistics, and colonial texts each illuminate part of the picture and each has limits. Colonial writers described societies already in contact with Spain, using categories that shaped, and sometimes distorted, what later generations believed. Reading these sources critically, and recognizing the voices they omit, including commoners and many women, is a central skill.

Finally, the chapter has positioned the Caribbean within Part V's larger purpose of restoring the diversity of Indigenous Latin America. It corrected the assumption that complexity means empire, showing instead that island societies achieved complexity through kinship, ritual, and maritime networks. It ended at the threshold of 1492, leaving Columbus's voyages and their consequences to Chapter 27, and pointing forward to Chapter 22, which turns to the Isthmo-Colombian world that was connected to the Caribbean by trade and shared traditions.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Archipelago
A chain or cluster of islands; in Caribbean history, a connected social and economic world rather than a set of isolated units.
Greater Antilles
The large western Caribbean islands of Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico, where the largest chiefdoms developed.
Lesser Antilles
The arc of smaller eastern islands connecting the Virgin Islands to Trinidad and the mainland.
Archaic era ("preceramic")
The long early period of Caribbean settlement, based on fishing, gathering, hunting, and plant management; "preceramic" is a historical label, since pottery is now documented in some pre-Saladoid contexts.
Ceramic Age
The period beginning c. 500–400 BCE marked by the major expansion of pottery-making, permanent villages, and cultivation.
Saladoid
An early Ceramic Age pottery tradition associated with migrants from the Orinoco region into the Lesser Antilles and Puerto Rico.
Ostionoid
A pottery tradition from about AD 600 in the Greater Antilles associated with the development of Taíno societies.
Chican Ostionoid
The late tradition, roughly AD 1200–1500, often called "Classic Taíno," linked to larger villages and stronger ranking.
Taíno
Modern name for the Arawakan-speaking societies of the Greater Antilles and their Lucayan offshoots; a broad category that conceals regional diversity.
Arawakan
A large language family of northern South America and the Caribbean; not a synonym for every Indigenous Caribbean people.
Cacique
A Taíno term for a community or regional leader, male or female, whose authority rested on kinship, prestige, ritual, and alliance.
Cacicazgo
The following and territory of a cacique; a chiefdom-like polity of variable size.
Nitaíno and naboría
Terms in Spanish sources for elite and commoner ranks; possibly influenced by European social categories.
Behique
A ritual specialist and healer who mediated with zemís and ancestors.
Zemí (cemí)
A sacred being and the object embodying it; a relational presence linked to ancestors, fertility, and authority.
Duho
A carved ceremonial seat used by leaders and ritual specialists.
Cohoba
A ritual involving inhaled hallucinogenic snuff used to seek communication with spirit beings.
Conuco
A cultivated garden plot, often with mounded soil, used for root crops and other plants.
Cibucán and budare
The woven press used to remove toxins from bitter manioc and the clay griddle on which cassava bread was baked.
Batey
A plaza or ball court used for games, ceremonies, and public gatherings.
Areíto
A communal ritual of song and dance reported in colonial sources as a vehicle for collective memory.
Guanín
A prestige alloy of gold, copper, and sometimes silver, associated with elite status and mainland connections.
Lucayan
The Indigenous people of the Bahamas and Turks and Caicos, descended from Greater Antillean settlers.
Palmetto Ware
The characteristic Lucayan pottery, a local style related to Greater Antillean traditions.
Kalinago
The later self-identification of Lesser Antillean communities, historically linked to the European label "Island Carib"; its application to all island peoples of 1492 is debated.
"Carib" (colonial label)
A European category applied to some Indigenous peoples, entangled with claims of cannibalism and rules permitting enslavement.
Ethnohistory
The critical use of early colonial texts alongside archaeology and Indigenous knowledge to study Indigenous societies.
Chiefdom
An anthropological term for ranked, kinship-based political organization without a bureaucratic state, useful but potentially misleading.
Exchange network
A pattern of circulation of goods, ideas, and people through gift, kinship, alliance, and barter, more precise here than "trade."
Anachronism
Applying later concepts or labels, such as "Kalinago" or modern national names, to earlier periods where they may not fit.

Chapter Reflection

The Indigenous Caribbean matters because it shows that history in the Americas cannot be reduced to its largest empires. Islands of modest size produced ranked societies, elaborate ritual life, intensive food production, and far-reaching networks, and did so through kinship, ceremony, and maritime knowledge rather than through bureaucracy and monumental cities. That lesson is central to Part V, which restores the diversity of Indigenous Latin America to a narrative that might otherwise be dominated by Mexico and the Andes.

The chapter also reveals how much the sources shape the story. Because the Caribbean's earliest detailed written descriptions were produced by colonizers, and because colonial categories such as "Taíno," "Arawak," and "Carib" hardened into common usage, students must learn to read those categories critically. The debates over these terms are not academic quibbles. They influenced legal rules about enslavement, shaped later stereotypes, and continue to affect contemporary Indigenous identities and claims.

The world described here did not end in 1492, but it was about to be violently transformed. Chapter 27 will return to this connected, historically deep Caribbean when Columbus enters it, and Chapter 29 will trace the demographic and ecological upheaval that followed. Later chapters will show how Indigenous, African, and European peoples remade the Caribbean, producing plantation societies, maroon communities, and, eventually, the Haitian Revolution. Indigenous ancestry, foods, technologies, words, and identities persisted through all of this, and they remain visible today.

For now, the next step is to move from the islands to the mainland lands that lay along the same sea. Chapter 22 turns to the Isthmo-Colombian world of Panama, Costa Rica, Colombia, and Venezuela, where metallurgy, exchange, and regional political systems formed another Indigenous world beyond the great empires, and one closely tied to the Caribbean.
