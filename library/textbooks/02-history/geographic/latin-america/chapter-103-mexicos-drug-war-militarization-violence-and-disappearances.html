Mexico's Drug War: Militarization, Violence, and Disappearances

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Security Campaign Becomes a Social Crisis

On 11 December 2006, shortly after taking office, President Felipe Calderón announced a major federal security operation in Michoacán. The operation became an early marker of a sustained national reliance on soldiers, sailors, and federal police for internal security. Two decades later, arguments over security policy, accountability, and the fate of the missing remained central to Mexican public life.

Chapter 102 explained how Mexico's major trafficking organizations took shape, competed, allied, and fragmented. This chapter asks a different question. It asks what happened when the Mexican state turned to a sustained, militarized offensive against those organizations, and how that decision, together with criminal violence, institutional weakness, and the responses of ordinary citizens, transformed Mexican society between 2006 and 2026. The subject is not the biography of cartels but the history of a conflict and its consequences: for public security, for the authority and accountability of the state, for human rights and criminal justice, and for the daily lives of millions of people.

The chapter's central argument is that Mexico's drug war was never simply a confrontation between government forces and trafficking organizations. It became a prolonged crisis of public security, state authority, human rights, criminal justice, and democratic accountability. Soldiers were asked to perform police work in a country whose civilian police and prosecutors were often poorly trained, poorly paid, and vulnerable to corruption. Criminal organizations fought one another and the state, but they also extorted, kidnapped, and killed civilians. State agents sometimes committed grave abuses themselves. And in many cases, when a crime occurred, the machinery of investigation did not work. The word that Mexicans most often used for that failure was impunidad, impunity.

The crisis also produced citizens' movements of unusual moral and political force. Poet Javier Sicilia, mothers searching for sons and daughters in remote fields, forensic scientists, lawyers, and journalists who continued to publish under threat all insisted that victims were persons with rights rather than anonymous statistics. Their work reshaped Mexican law, produced new institutions, and altered how Mexicans discuss the state's obligations. It also demonstrated the persistent gap between legal entitlement and practical implementation.

Three episodes anchor the chapter's narrative. The first is the militarization of public security that began in 2006 and continued, with changes of name and legal form, under four presidents. The second is the crisis of disappearances and the failure of investigations, symbolized by the 2014 disappearance of 43 students from Ayotzinapa and by the thousands of clandestine graves discovered by search collectives. The third is the work of victims' families, journalists, researchers, and civil society in demanding truth and accountability. Around these episodes the chapter traces regional variation among Ciudad Juárez, Tijuana, Michoacán, Tamaulipas, Guerrero, and other regions, and the continuities and changes among the strategies of Calderón, Enrique Peña Nieto, Andrés Manuel López Obrador, and Claudia Sheinbaum.

Evidence requires special care. Homicide counts come from two different systems, civil-registry death records compiled by the national statistics institute (INEGI) and prosecutors' crime reports compiled by the national public-security secretariat (SESNSP), and the two do not agree. Neither can distinguish deaths caused by criminal organizations from other homicides. The national register of disappeared and unlocated persons has been revised repeatedly and is contested. Official investigative narratives, such as the "historical truth" announced about Ayotzinapa in 2014, have been challenged by independent experts. Government statements about declining violence are political claims as well as statistical ones. This chapter therefore distinguishes confirmed facts, judicial findings, official claims, independent investigation, and allegations, and it marks as unresolved what remains so as of its evidence cutoff of 15 November 2026.

The chapter belongs to Volume V and Part XXIII, "Mexico and the Cartel Wars." It stands between Chapter 102, which described the organizations, and Chapter 104, which examines the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG), fentanyl, methamphetamine, and the synthetic-drug economy. Material on leadership successions and organizational histories is therefore summarized only as context, and the synthetic-drug economy is introduced only where it changed security policy.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and security circumstances in which Felipe Calderón launched a militarized offensive in December 2006, without treating its outcomes as inevitable.
  2. Describe the legal, institutional, and practical problems of using the armed forces for domestic law enforcement.
  3. Assess the Mérida Initiative as a framework of U.S.-Mexican cooperation, distinguishing appropriated from delivered assistance and noting debates over sovereignty and human-rights conditions.
  4. Compare violence and policing in Ciudad Juárez, Tijuana, Michoacán, and Tamaulipas, and explain why national totals conceal regional variation.
  5. Distinguish registered homicides from drug-war deaths, and homicides from disappearances, as different measures of different phenomena.
  6. Analyze the mass atrocities of 2010-2012, the Sicilia movement, and the cases of Tlatlaya and Ayotzinapa as problems of evidence, accountability, and institutional response.
  7. Distinguish disappearance by private actors, enforced disappearance, and cases of unknown responsibility, and explain the limits of the national disappearance register.
  8. Evaluate the role of families, search collectives, forensic scientists, and journalists in transforming Mexican law and public discourse.
  9. Compare the security strategies of Calderón, Peña Nieto, López Obrador, and Sheinbaum, identifying continuities as well as changes.
  10. Interpret official claims of declining violence alongside independent data, and identify what remains unresolved as of November 2026.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a newly inaugurated president with a narrow and contested mandate make organized crime the center of national policy, and what alternatives were available?
  2. What are the consequences of asking armed forces trained for external defense to perform policing, and why did that arrangement persist across four administrations of different parties?
  3. Why did violence rise unevenly across Mexican regions, and what can national statistics not tell us?
  4. How should historians treat the claim that removing leaders of criminal organizations intensified violence, given contested evidence?
  5. What distinguishes a homicide, an extrajudicial killing, a disappearance, and an enforced disappearance, and why do the distinctions matter legally and historically?
  6. How did victims' families change the law, the institutions, and the political language of the conflict?
  7. In what ways did Mexico's conflict depend on international markets, weapons flows, and U.S. policy, and in what ways was it driven by domestic institutions and politics?
  8. How can historians evaluate government claims about declining violence when agencies and independent bodies produce different figures?

Historical Context

By the early 2000s, Mexico had completed one of Latin America's most notable democratic transitions. In 2000, Vicente Fox of the National Action Party (PAN) ended seven decades of rule by the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI), which Chapter 74 traced. The end of one-party dominance changed the political setting in which trafficking operated. Chapter 101 described how, under the old system, a centralized political order had regulated, taxed, and sometimes protected illicit trade; Chapter 102 described how competitive politics, federalism, and fragmented policing altered the bargains between criminal organizations and public officials, and how organizations such as the Sinaloa Cartel, the Tijuana organization, the Juárez organization, the Gulf Cartel, and Los Zetas competed for corridors to the United States.

Mexican public security institutions were poorly equipped for this environment. Policing was divided among federal, state, and municipal forces of very unequal quality. Municipal police, often the first line of public security, were frequently underpaid and exposed to bribery or intimidation. Prosecutors' offices (ministerios públicos) were overloaded, and criminal procedure relied heavily on written files and confessions. In 2008 Mexico enacted a constitutional reform to move toward an adversarial, oral trial system, to be implemented nationwide by 2016, but implementation was gradual. The result was a justice system in which most crimes were never investigated to conclusion, a condition that scholars and civil-society organizations described as pervasive impunity.

The armed forces had long been involved in drug-control tasks, including manual eradication of illicit crops in the countryside, and the army and navy were widely regarded as among the country's most trusted institutions, partly because they had been less visibly corrupt than civilian police. That reputation shaped the choice to rely on them. It also meant that, once soldiers and sailors were exposed to the demands of domestic policing, the institutions' reputation and discipline came under pressure. The army's legal framework also gave military courts jurisdiction over many crimes committed by soldiers, a feature that later became central to disputes over accountability.

The international setting mattered. Demand for illegal drugs in the United States sustained the market, and firearms purchased in the United States flowed south. After the terrorist attacks of 2001, Washington framed border security and transnational crime in security terms, and U.S. officials increasingly pressed for cooperation. At the same time Mexican governments guarded national sovereignty, a principle with deep roots in Chapters 63 and 70, and regarded foreign security involvement with sensitivity. Chapter 103 therefore treats U.S. policy as one important factor in a conflict whose principal actors, victims, and institutions were Mexican.

Finally, readers should bear in mind a methodological point. The label "drug war" (guerra contra el narco) was itself contested. Calderón and his officials used war language, and critics adopted it; later presidents, notably López Obrador, rejected it. This chapter uses "drug war" as a conventional name for the period of militarized confrontation beginning in 2006, not as an endorsement of the premise that the conflict was only about drugs. Much of the violence involved extortion, kidnapping, control of territory and local economies, fuel theft, human smuggling, and other illicit markets in addition to narcotics.

The Militarization of Mexican Public Security, 2006-2009

How a new government turned to the armed forces, and how the first escalation unfolded unevenly across regions.

Section 103.1

Felipe Calderón and the Decision to Confront Organized Crime, 2006

Felipe Calderón of the National Action Party was inaugurated on 1 December 2006 under unusual conditions. The presidential election of 2 July had been decided by a margin of well under one percentage point, and the electoral tribunal's September confirmation of his victory was rejected by the runner-up, Andrés Manuel López Obrador, whose supporters occupied central Mexico City for weeks. Calderón took the oath in a congressional chamber disrupted by opposition protest. Historians and political scientists have long debated whether this narrow and contested mandate encouraged him to seek legitimacy through a visible, forceful policy on security, though the sources do not allow certainty about his motives, and Calderón and his advisers consistently presented the decision as a response to real and growing violence.

That violence was real. Trafficking-related killings had risen in several regions during the Fox administration, as Chapter 102 described, particularly along the northern border and in parts of the Pacific coast. In September 2006, members of the criminal group that took the name La Familia Michoacana rolled severed heads onto the floor of a nightclub in Uruapan, Michoacán, an act clearly meant to announce a new organization to the public. Local police were frequently outgunned, outpaid, or compromised. Governors and mayors in affected states asked Washington and Mexico City for help. To Calderón's government, the situation seemed to demand a response that civilian institutions could not yet provide.

The administration's stated objectives were to restore state control over territory, weaken trafficking organizations, reduce violence, and, over time, rebuild police and justice institutions. Officials spoke of a long struggle, but early rhetoric also suggested that decisive action could restore order within a defined period, and critics later argued that this created expectations the state could not meet. Calderón's Secretary of Public Security, Genaro García Luna, led the federal policing effort and became one of the strategy's most visible figures. In 2023 a U.S. federal court in New York convicted García Luna of drug-trafficking conspiracy and related charges, and in 2024 he was sentenced to 38 years in prison; he has denied wrongdoing and has challenged the outcome. The conviction concerned acts attributed to him personally, and historians debate how far it illuminates the policies of the period as a whole, though it complicated the claim that the federal police structure was insulated from the organizations it fought.

It is important not to read later outcomes backward into 2006. The decision to deploy the armed forces was not inevitable, and contemporaries proposed other approaches, including investing first in police reform, concentrating enforcement on the most violent networks, or pursuing negotiation and regulation of illicit markets, though few such proposals had political backing. The choice to use the military on a national scale, announced without a detailed plan for its exit, would shape a decade and a half of Mexican politics.

Domestic politics and institutions also framed the choice. Calderón governed without a congressional majority, which limited his ability to pass sweeping police or judicial reforms quickly, and the federal system placed most policing and prosecution in the hands of thirty-two state governments and more than two thousand municipalities with very different resources and political loyalties. A national force deployed from Mexico City was therefore the instrument the president could mobilize directly. This institutional fact, more than any single ideological commitment, helps explain why the armed forces, which answer to the president as commander in chief, became the central tool of the strategy.

Creator: Presidency of Mexico (Felipe Calderón) · Date: December 2006 · Type: Government declaration and public communications

Context: In his first days in office, Calderón announced a federal operation in Michoacán at the request of that state's governor and framed the fight against organized crime as a national priority tied to public safety and the authority of the state.

Read the identified document: Presidencia de la República, “Anuncio sobre la Operación Conjunta Michoacán,” 11 December 2006 (archived copy)

Paraphrase of the central claims: the government presents organized crime as a threat to families, communities, and the state's authority; it states that federal forces will assist a state facing criminal violence; and it describes the effort as the beginning of a sustained campaign rather than a single operation.

Analysis: The declaration shows how the government defined the problem, as one of public order and state authority, and its early commitment to a federal, force-based response. It does not show what officials expected the long-term costs to be, nor what internal alternatives were weighed. Because it is a political statement, it should be read as the government's framing, which later critics and supporters contested. Voices of residents in Michoacán, local police, and the criminal groups themselves are absent.

  2. Who issued the statement, and what audiences was it addressing?
  3. What can historians learn about the administration's intentions and expectations?
  4. What can they not establish without other evidence, such as internal planning documents?
Section 103.2

Operation Michoacán and the Deployment of Federal Forces

On 11 December 2006 the federal government launched Operation Michoacán, deploying several thousand soldiers, sailors, and federal police to a state where La Familia Michoacana and other groups had challenged local authority. It was soon followed by comparable joint operations: in Tijuana and Baja California and in Guerrero in January 2007, in Nuevo León and Tamaulipas in 2007, and in Chihuahua, including Ciudad Juárez, in March 2008. Estimates of the number of troops engaged in public-security tasks across the country by around 2011 range into the tens of thousands, and figures differ by source and definition.

The army (SEDENA) and navy (SEMAR) played different roles. The army provided the largest numbers and was deployed to patrol, man checkpoints, and carry out raids. The navy, a smaller institution with a reputation for relative professionalism and for close cooperation with U.S. agencies, increasingly carried out high-profile arrests and operations against leaders, a pattern that continued through later administrations. The Federal Police, expanded and reorganized under García Luna, took on investigative and tactical roles, though it too suffered from corruption problems and uneven training. Joint commands blurred lines of responsibility among the forces.

The practical problems of this model were considerable. Soldiers are trained to defeat enemies, not to investigate crimes, protect evidence, or deal with citizens under rules of criminal procedure. They often lacked authority to hold suspects beyond brief periods, so detainees passed to civilian prosecutors who might themselves be compromised or overwhelmed. Military units rotated, which limited their knowledge of local communities, and sustained deployments strained the forces. Arrests of mid-level operators, which were frequent, did not necessarily dismantle the networks they served. These limits were widely recognized by Mexican and foreign analysts, and they raised questions that the government answered by promising police reform that would take years to deliver.

The deployment also raised constitutional and human-rights concerns. The Constitution authorized the armed forces to assist civilian authorities, but critics argued that routine policing by the military exceeded that role, a dispute that would return in 2017 when Congress passed an Internal Security Law, which the Supreme Court declared unconstitutional in 2018. Complaints to the National Human Rights Commission about the army rose from fewer than two hundred in 2006 to well over a thousand in some later years, alleging arbitrary detention, torture, and killings. Because military courts retained jurisdiction over many cases involving soldiers, civilian victims often faced obstacles in seeking redress; Mexico's 2011 constitutional human-rights reform and decisions of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, in cases such as those of Rosendo Radilla and the women of Guerrero, gradually narrowed that jurisdiction.

Command and coordination arrangements evolved through trial and error. Joint operations initially placed army, navy, and federal police units in regional task forces, but rivalries between institutions, differences in training and intelligence practices, and the absence of a unified legal framework produced uneven results. The navy and the army rarely shared intelligence fully, and civilian prosecutors depended on military detentions that were sometimes poorly documented, weakening cases in court. Over time the government created specialized units and expanded cooperation with U.S. agencies, but the underlying problem, that the forces best able to confront armed groups were least equipped to build criminal cases, persisted.

Historical Vocabulary
Militarization

Militarization, in this chapter, means the assignment of public-security functions, such as patrol, investigation, detention, and control of ports, to military institutions, or the transformation of civilian police into forces that are military in training, command, or legal control. It is a matter of degree rather than a single switch.

Mexican law distinguishes seguridad pública (public security, ordinarily a civilian police and prosecutorial responsibility) from seguridad nacional (national security, a broader category involving the armed forces and intelligence). Much of the debate over the drug war concerns whether organized-crime violence should be classified in the first category or the second. Students should not equate militarization with military rule: Mexico remained a civilian-led constitutional democracy throughout the period, and the armed forces acted under presidential authority.

Section 103.3

The Mérida Initiative and U.S.-Mexican Security Cooperation

Calderón's offensive coincided with a new phase of U.S.-Mexican cooperation. Presidents George W. Bush and Calderón discussed expanded collaboration in 2007, and in October of that year the two governments announced what became known as the Mérida Initiative, named for the Yucatán city where Bush and Calderón had met. The U.S. Congress began appropriating funds in 2008 through supplemental legislation. The proposal originally contemplated roughly 1.4 billion dollars over several years for Mexico, plus aid for Central America, concentrated at first on equipment such as aircraft, helicopters, and inspection technology, and on training, intelligence cooperation, and support for justice institutions.

Cooperation must be measured through appropriations, obligations, and actual delivery, which are not identical. The U.S. Congress initially provided $400 million in supplemental funds for Mexico under Public Law 110-252, apportioned across FY2008 and FY2009 accounts; 15 percent of specified assistance was subject to human-rights reporting conditions. A Congressional Research Service overview reports approximately $3.6 billion for broader U.S.–Mexico security cooperation across fiscal years 2008–2023, including successor frameworks; that cumulative number must not be described as Mérida-only spending. Over time, priorities shifted from aircraft and equipment toward investigative capacity, institutional reform, border security, and other rule-of-law programs. Funding commitments did not guarantee prompt delivery or institutional results.

Cooperation extended beyond funding. Mexico expanded extraditions of people accused or convicted of trafficking to the United States, intelligence sharing increased, and U.S. agencies, including the Drug Enforcement Administration, deepened their engagement. U.S. firearms policy also became an issue: Mexican officials pointed to firearms bought in the United States and trafficked south. The U.S. law-enforcement operation known as Fast and Furious, in which agents failed to interdict illegal firearm purchases, became a bilateral controversy after it was exposed in 2011. Neither an aid appropriation nor a weapons seizure, standing alone, measures the initiative's impact on violence or justice.

Debates over sovereignty and human rights accompanied the program. Some Mexicans criticized the deepening of U.S. involvement as a risk to sovereignty, while others saw it as a necessary partnership. The U.S. Congress attached human-rights conditions, requiring the State Department to report on Mexican compliance before part of certain funds could be released, and human-rights organizations argued that the conditions were weakly enforced given the documented abuses by security forces. Defenders of the program stressed capacity-building and the strengthening of the justice reform; critics argued that it supported a strategy that was producing violence and abuse. As with many aspects of this history, both claims drew on real evidence, and the effects of the program on outcomes are difficult to isolate from other factors.

Section 103.4

The First Escalation: Violence, Enforcement, and Regional Variation, 2007-2009

Homicide registers show a sharp national rise after 2007. According to INEGI's civil-registry data, registered homicide deaths fell to a post-1990s low of about 8,900 in 2007 before rising to roughly 14,000 in 2008 and nearly 20,000 in 2009, continuing upward to a peak above 27,000 in 2011. These numbers require interpretation. They count registered homicide deaths of every kind, including domestic, street, and personal violence, and they do not identify the perpetrator or motive. Some analysts, using prosecutors' and media-based tallies, have estimated that a substantial share of the increase involved organized-crime-related violence, but the figures for "drug-war deaths" depend on definitions that governments and researchers have not agreed upon, and no national total of such deaths can be treated as established.

Equally important is the geography of violence. It did not rise evenly across Mexico, and much of the country remained comparatively peaceful. The increase concentrated in particular states and municipalities where criminal organizations were fighting for corridors, where enforcement operations disrupted existing arrangements, or where local institutions had collapsed. Three contrasting cases illustrate the range.

In Ciudad Juárez, across from El Paso, a long-standing organization was challenged by Sinaloa-linked groups beginning around 2008, and the city became the epicenter of urban violence, a case developed in the next section. In Tijuana, the Arellano Félix organization had fractured by the mid-2000s, and a 2008-2009 period of intense killing, kidnapping, and attacks on police gave way, after leadership arrests and military deployments, to a marked decline in violence in the following years, a trajectory that scholars use to illustrate how violence can fall as suddenly as it rises when contested arrangements stabilize. In Michoacán, the emergence of La Familia Michoacana and its later successors involved not only trafficking but also extortion of businesses and agriculture and a rhetoric of religious and social mission, and the 2008 grenade attack on a crowd in Morelia on Independence Day, which killed civilians, showed organized crime directing violence at the public.

These cases show that violence resulted from the interaction of several factors: competition among organizations, enforcement operations that sometimes disrupted existing arrangements, corruption and collusion by local officials, and the weakness of municipal police. Scholars continue to debate the relative weight of these factors, and no single explanation fits every place. What can be said is that the deployment of federal forces coincided with rising violence in many regions and that its effect varied by place, a point developed in Section 103.6.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Escalation of Violence, 2007-2011

Long-term causes

Large illicit markets serving U.S. demand; weak, fragmented policing; a justice system with low rates of conviction; and the end of the PRI-era political arrangements that had regulated trafficking.

Short-term causes

Competition among organizations over corridors; the expansion of extortion and kidnapping; contested succession and fragmentation; and a government decision to deploy force on a national scale.

Event or transformation

A militarized offensive beginning in December 2006, coinciding with sharp regional escalations of violence.

Immediate effects

Rising homicide, attacks on civilians, growing complaints of state abuse, and an expanded U.S.-Mexican security partnership.

Long-term consequences

Entrenched military roles in public security, a disappearance crisis, new victims' movements, and a durable debate over strategy.

Historical complexity

The deployment did not alone cause violence, and violence was not uniform; scholars attribute outcomes to interacting factors that vary by place.

Section 103.5

Ciudad Juárez: Urban Violence and the Crisis of Everyday Security

Ciudad Juárez, a border industrial city of well over a million people whose economy depended on assembly plants (maquiladoras) serving U.S. markets, became the symbol of the first phase of the drug war. The city had long histories of migration, poverty on its periphery, and, since the 1990s, the unsolved murders of hundreds of women, a case that had already exposed failures of local investigation. From about 2008, as competing organizations fought for the corridor into El Paso, killings rose sharply. The federal government responded in March 2008 with a joint military and police operation in Chihuahua, and by 2009 thousands of soldiers were stationed in the city. State prosecutors' tallies placed homicides in Juárez at roughly 3,000 in 2010, a rate that by some calculations exceeded 200 per 100,000 residents and was among the highest recorded for any city outside a declared war zone; such figures vary by source and should be treated as approximate.

The statistics only partly capture how residents lived. Families reported threats and extortion of small businesses, and many shops, restaurants, and clinics closed or paid protection money. Schools lost teachers and students to flight or fear, and parents altered routines. Large numbers of homes were abandoned, as residents moved to other parts of Mexico or across the border, an exodus that affected neighborhoods unevenly. The 2008-2009 economic recession, which cut employment in the maquiladora sector, deepened the vulnerability of young people from poor neighborhoods, some of whom were recruited into criminal groups while others became victims. The army patrols reassured some residents but drew complaints from others of abuse, and the troops' presence did not stop the killing.

A massacre in January 2010 at Villas de Salvárcar, in which gunmen killed fifteen people, most of them teenagers and students attending a party, became a turning point. Initial official statements suggested the victims were gang members, a characterization that families and neighbors rejected, and Calderón later acknowledged the error during a visit to the city. The episode illustrates how official narratives that criminalized victims, a recurring pattern in the war, deepened public distrust. In response the federal government launched the Todos Somos Juárez program in 2010, combining policing changes, social programs, and investment in education, health, and neighborhood infrastructure. In April 2010 the Federal Police replaced the army in the city's patrol functions, and violence began to decline after 2011, falling below 1,000 homicides a year by 2012 and far lower in following years.

Scholars and local observers disagree about why violence declined, and the question matters for policy. Explanations offered include the consolidation of one organization's control, changes in policing and community engagement, the effects of social programs, and fatigue and displacement. Todos Somos Juárez is often cited as an example of combining enforcement with social investment, though evaluations of its effects differ, and the decline cannot be attributed to one factor. What is clear is that for years residents of Juárez experienced insecurity not as a statistic but as a transformation of daily life: where to walk, whether to open a business, whether to send children to school.

Section 103.6

Fragmentation, Civilian Intimidation, and the Expansion of Violence

As enforcement pressure and territorial competition continued, the organizations described in Chapter 102 splintered, and the character of violence changed. Large organizations that had managed relationships with officials and mediated disputes broke into smaller groups, which fought one another and diversified into activities that depended on controlling local populations: kidnapping for ransom, extortion of businesses and farmers, theft of fuel, and the control of local markets. Los Zetas, which had begun as an armed enforcement wing of the Gulf Cartel, became notable for extreme violence directed at civilians, migrants, and officials, and for imitation of its methods by others. Violence against the public was therefore not an accident of competition but, for some groups, a method of governing territory.

Intimidation reached local politics. Mayors, municipal officials, and candidates were threatened, coerced, or killed in several states, and in some municipalities police forces were dismissed or disarmed because of suspected infiltration. In June 2010, Rodolfo Torre Cantú, the leading candidate for governor of Tamaulipas, was assassinated days before the election, an unusual attack on a major political figure. Business owners, farmers, and ordinary citizens paid cobro de piso, a fee for the right to operate. Kidnapping, whose recorded numbers are widely believed to understate reality because victims often did not report it, affected middle-class and poor families alike. In the countryside, entire communities were displaced.

Violence also reached the state's own agents. Soldiers, police, and prosecutors were killed, and public officials sometimes collaborated with criminal groups. The fusion of criminal power with local government in some municipalities, what analysts called captured or criminally penetrated local states, complicated any attempt to separate "the state" from "criminals." This is a reason for avoiding the simple picture of a unified government fighting unified criminal enemies.

The evidence also counsels against treating all violence as the result of a single federal decision. Rivalries in Sinaloa, the Gulf corridor, and the Pacific coast followed different local trajectories. Some municipalities faced competition among several armed groups while others experienced entrenched protection arrangements or relatively lower homicide levels. Researchers distinguish shootings aimed at rivals, violence used to discipline communities, violence against public authorities, and intimidation intended to suppress witnesses; those mechanisms may coexist but should not be counted as identical events. This distinction matters to causal inference. A national time series shows when recorded homicide increased, but without disaggregated regional evidence it cannot establish which murders were caused by a military deployment, a leadership removal, new criminal financing, or changes in local political protection.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Removing Leaders Increase Violence?

The question: A central strategy of the period, often called the kingpin or decapitation strategy, targeted the leaders of criminal organizations through arrest, extradition, or killing. Did these removals reduce violence by weakening organizations, or did they increase it by triggering fragmentation and succession disputes?

Interpretation A: Leadership removal often intensified violence. Several political scientists, using municipal-level data on arrests and killings, have argued that removing leaders was associated with increases in violence in the affected areas, because it disrupted informal arrangements, set off succession struggles, and encouraged rivals to move in. Related research on the contagion of violence suggests that disruption in one place could spread to adjacent territories along trafficking routes.
Interpretation B: Effects depend on conditions and context. Other scholars find that the effects vary with the type of organization, the capacity of rivals, the manner of removal, and local policing, and that some removals produced little change or were followed by declines. They also note that violence rose in places without such removals, and that other factors, including changes in drug markets, local political alignment, and the opportunities for criminal diversification, shaped outcomes.

Evidence: Both views rely on imperfect data: municipal homicide counts that do not identify perpetrators, arrest records of uneven reliability, and models whose results depend on how organizations are classified. Studies by Calderón, Robles, Díaz-Cayeros, and Magaloni, by Phillips, by Osorio, and by Dell are among those students can consult.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether the strategy was a cause of violence or a response to it, and on policy choices thereafter: both López Obrador and Sheinbaum criticized the kingpin approach even as operations against leaders continued, as the events of February 2026 showed (Section 103.19). The most defensible conclusion is that leadership removal sometimes intensified violence in specific contexts, not that it did so universally.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation and identify what kind of data, such as organizational records or interviews, would help resolve the disagreement.

Section 103.7

Mass Atrocities: San Fernando, Allende, and Other Cases, 2010-2012

Three episodes in northeastern Mexico between 2010 and 2011 shocked Mexican and international opinion. They are treated here from the perspective of victims, investigations, and institutional response, with the organizational background of Chapter 102 taken as given.

In late August 2010, the Mexican navy found the bodies of 72 migrants, 58 men and 14 women, from Central and South America at a ranch near San Fernando, Tamaulipas. A survivor from Ecuador, who had been shot and left for dead, alerted the marines. Migrants traveling north through Mexico were exceptionally vulnerable: they carried cash or contacts in the United States, they were often undocumented and afraid of authorities, and criminal groups, sometimes with the collusion of officials, kidnapped them for ransom or forced them into labor. Mexico's national human-rights commission documented thousands of migrant kidnappings in its reports of 2009 and 2011, with its 2011 report counting more than eleven thousand victims over six months of 2010. The San Fernando massacre was widely attributed to Los Zetas, and subsequent prosecutions resulted in some convictions, but families of the victims and human-rights groups criticized delays in identifying bodies and returning remains.

In April 2011, authorities discovered clandestine graves near San Fernando. Officials reported 193 bodies in 47 graves, an extraordinary number, and subsequent forensic work revealed that many victims were passengers pulled from buses traveling through the region. Identification proved difficult. Many bodies were in poor condition; some of the dead appear to have been migrants, others Mexican travelers; and the investigation faced problems of coordination between state and federal authorities and of forensic capacity. The case introduced to a broad public a problem that would grow: the discovery of clandestine graves and the difficulty of identifying their occupants. Students should distinguish between the confirmed discovery of bodies, the smaller number identified, and the unknown number of persons reported missing along the route.

In March 2011, in Allende, Coahuila, and nearby towns, members of Los Zetas carried out several days of attacks on families linked to individuals who had allegedly betrayed the organization. Homes were burned, people were abducted, and many have never been found. Estimates of the number of victims range from dozens to more than three hundred, a divergence that itself reflects the evidentiary problem: official investigations were slow, state and local officials had been warned and, according to later investigations including a report by U.S. academic researchers and human-rights organizations, did little, and families struggled to obtain information. The Allende case thus became an important example of disappearance on a large scale, of delayed investigation, and of the later role of families and forensic work.

In August 2011, an arson attack on the Casino Royale in Monterrey killed 52 people, including employees and patrons, after attackers set fire to the building. The attack was attributed to Los Zetas over an extortion dispute, and prosecutions followed. It displayed the effects of extortion and of the criminal penetration of the nightlife economy, and it shocked a city that had regarded itself as relatively insulated from violence.

Section 103.8

Javier Sicilia and the Movement for Peace with Justice and Dignity, 2011-2012

On 28 March 2011, Juan Francisco Sicilia, a young man of 24, was found dead in Cuernavaca, Morelos, along with six others. They had been abducted and killed. His father, the poet and writer Javier Sicilia, published an open letter in the magazine Proceso in early April, addressed to politicians and to criminals, whose refrain, "estamos hasta la madre" ("we have had it up to here"), became a rallying cry. Sicilia announced that he would stop writing poetry and called for protest. Within days, marches in Cuernavaca drew thousands of people.

The Movimiento por la Paz con Justicia y Dignidad that emerged was not a political party but a coalition of victims' families, civil-society organizations, students, and religious and human-rights groups. Its marches went from Cuernavaca to Mexico City in May 2011, where a large rally gathered in the central square, and in June a caravan traveled to Ciudad Juárez. Participants carried photographs of the dead and missing and told their stories in public. The movement's demands included truth and justice for victims, an end to the militarized strategy, reform of the police and judicial system, attention to the roots of crime, and an approach centered on human rights and what it called citizen security.

The movement's most distinctive achievement was to change who counted in public debate. Government officials had often spoken of victims as criminals who had died in conflicts among criminals, and early official accounts of events like Villas de Salvárcar had repeated that language. Sicilia and the families insisted on naming victims, telling their histories, and demanding that the state investigate their deaths. In June 2011 Calderón met movement representatives at Chapultepec Castle in a televised dialogue in which victims confronted him directly, an unprecedented encounter. The movement did not win agreement to end the military strategy, but it pressed legislation for the protection of victims and sparked the drafting of a victims' law. After a dispute between Calderón's administration and Congress over its text, a revised General Victims Law was promulgated in January 2013, creating a national system and an executive commission to assist victims.

The movement also had limits and internal tensions. It was criticized by some for naivety and by others for being used politically; disagreements over strategy and relations with the government led to fragmentation by 2012. Yet its legacy was durable. It established the idea of the victim as a political subject with enforceable rights, and it connected the experiences of families across regions, a network that later fed the search collectives discussed in Division IV.

Human Rights, Institutional Failure, and the Search for Accountability, 2012-2018

Section 103.9

Enrique Peña Nieto and the Reorganization of Security Policy

Enrique Peña Nieto of the PRI, who returned his party to the presidency in December 2012, promised a new approach. His government sought to change the national conversation from the confrontation that had dominated Calderón's term toward a message of reducing violence and restoring normality, and it limited the visibility of security in official communication. Organizationally, it dissolved the Secretariat of Public Security and moved the federal police and coordination functions into the Interior Ministry, and it announced a national crime-prevention program and, in 2014, a new gendarmerie (Gendarmería Nacional) intended to provide a specialized force for rural and strategic zones. These measures were presented as civilian-centered, but the new gendarmerie was small, and the armed forces remained deeply involved in public security.

The record of the period shows both continuity and change. Operations against leaders continued, including the 2014 arrest of Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán, his escape in 2015 from a maximum-security prison, his recapture in 2016, and his extradition to the United States in January 2017, episodes narrated in Chapter 102. Registered homicides fell from the 2011 peak to a low point near 20,000 in 2014 and then rose again, exceeding 32,000 in 2017 and reaching a record near 36,700 in 2018, according to INEGI. Observers attributed the later increase to a combination of fragmentation, expanding criminal economies, and the weakness of local institutions, though explanations remain contested. In Michoacán, the government in 2013-2014 confronted the rise of community self-defense groups (autodefensas), which had formed to resist extortion by the Knights Templar; it eventually tried to incorporate some into an official rural force, a policy that critics argued blurred the line between state and non-state armed actors.

Why did a change in rhetoric not transform outcomes? The principal reason is that the underlying institutions changed slowly. Municipal police and state prosecutors remained, in many places, under-resourced and vulnerable to infiltration. The federal government could announce national programs, but crime is investigated and prosecuted largely at the state level. The justice reform, fully in force by 2016, introduced oral adversarial trials but struggled with training and resources. Meanwhile, the armed forces continued to deploy, and in December 2017 Congress approved an Internal Security Law to give the military's role a statutory framework, over objections from human-rights organizations and the National Human Rights Commission. The Supreme Court declared the law unconstitutional in November 2018, though the practical presence of the military remained.

Finally, the period was marked by cases that undermined public confidence in the state's willingness to investigate itself. Two of them, Tlatlaya and Ayotzinapa, both in 2014, occupy the following sections. Together with allegations of corruption involving officials and governors, they turned human-rights accountability into a central political issue by the end of Peña Nieto's term.

Social programs and prevention were part of the Peña Nieto government's stated approach, which promised to address the causes of crime through programs for youth and for high-violence municipalities. Evaluations of these programs were mixed, and their budgets were often reduced or redirected. The contrast with the continued use of force illustrates a recurring pattern in Mexican security policy: announcements of integrated strategies in which prevention and enforcement are combined, followed by implementation that favors the more visible and immediate tools of enforcement.

Tlatlaya and Allegations of Unlawful State Violence, 2014

On 30 June 2014, soldiers killed 22 people in a warehouse in San Pedro Limón, in the municipality of Tlatlaya, State of Mexico. The Defense Ministry and state authorities initially reported that the dead were members of a criminal group killed in a shootout with soldiers who had come under fire, a version that appeared in the press and was echoed in official statements. The incident was initially treated as an example of effective operations, and it might have remained so had it not been for later reporting.

In September 2014, the Associated Press and the magazine Esquire Mexico published accounts based on the testimony of a witness, a woman who had been inside the warehouse, and on examination of the scene, indicating that many of the victims had been killed after surrendering. The reports contradicted the official account. The National Human-Rights Commission investigated and, in October 2014, issued a recommendation concluding that at least a dozen of the 22 victims had been arbitrarily executed, that the scene had been altered, and that two surviving women had been mistreated by officials. The federal prosecutor's office opened its own investigation, and several soldiers were detained and charged with a mix of civilian and military offenses.

Tlatlaya illustrates several features of the conflict. First, it shows that the initial official narrative and the evidence could diverge sharply, and that independent journalism and a human-rights body forced a reconsideration. Second, it shows the difficulty of establishing individual criminal responsibility: a finding by a human-rights body that rights were violated differs from a criminal conviction of particular persons, which requires proof beyond reasonable doubt in a court. Prosecutions produced limited and contested results; most of the soldiers initially detained were later freed, and accountability for the killings remained partial. Third, the case demonstrates the importance of the evidentiary role of Mexican human-rights institutions, which, while lacking prosecutorial power, produced detailed reconstructions that were later cited by international bodies.

The case also raises questions that historians cannot fully answer. The victims were described by officials as members of a criminal group, and some may have been, but legal and moral standards of the use of force do not permit execution of persons who have surrendered. The case's significance lies less in the identity of the dead than in the evidence that state agents may have killed in violation of the law and that the state's first account concealed it.

Section 103.11

Ayotzinapa: The Disappearance of 43 Students, September 2014

On the evening of 26 September 2014, students from the Raúl Isidro Burgos rural teachers' college in Ayotzinapa, Guerrero, traveled to the city of Iguala to commandeer buses, a long-standing practice of the school's students, who needed transportation to attend a commemoration of the 1968 Tlatelolco massacre in Mexico City. During the night, municipal police from Iguala and the neighboring town of Cocula and other armed men attacked the buses. Three students were killed at the scene; the body of another, Julio César Mondragón, was found the next day showing signs of severe mutilation; three bystanders were also killed, and dozens were wounded. Forty-three students were taken away and never seen again by their families.

The college itself is part of a historical tradition. Rural normal schools developed in the decades after the Mexican Revolution to train teachers for the countryside (see Chapters 73–74). They educated generations of poor rural youth, including students from Indigenous and peasant families, in settings shaped by political activism and educational reform. Guerrero is one of Mexico's poorest states, with a history of rural guerrilla conflict and state repression in the 1960s and 1970s; that history gave the case particular resonance. The region also saw poppy cultivation and competition among criminal organizations, but these circumstances do not establish a motive for the students' disappearance.

On 7 November 2014 the federal attorney general, Jesús Murillo Karam, presented what became known as the "historical truth": that the students had been handed by police to a criminal group called Guerreros Unidos, who killed them and burned their bodies at a garbage dump in Cocula, throwing the remains into a river. Mayor José Luis Abarca of Iguala and his wife were arrested and implicated. The official account relied heavily on confessions and on the testimony of detainees. Almost immediately it was challenged by the families, who insisted that the students be considered disappeared until proven otherwise, and by independent investigators.

Under an agreement among the Mexican government, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, and the families, an Interdisciplinary Group of Independent Experts (GIEI) began work in 2015. Its September 2015 report concluded, on the basis of an independent fire expert and forensic analysis, that the burning of 43 bodies at the Cocula dump as described was not supported by the physical evidence. The Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team had reached similar doubts about the dump. The GIEI also documented irregularities in the investigation, raised questions about additional lines such as the movement of buses, and reported evidence of mistreatment of detainees. The GIEI's mandate ended in April 2016 after the government declined to extend it, amid disputes over its role. In 2018 the United Nations human-rights office reported evidence that a significant number of detainees had been tortured, and a Mexican collegiate tribunal ordered the creation of a truth commission, citing serious irregularities in the investigation.

Under President López Obrador, a presidential commission for truth and access to justice was established in December 2018. In August 2022 it reported that the disappearance was a "state crime" in which authorities at several levels participated, and that the earlier "historical truth" was a construction of the prior government. Days later Murillo Karam was arrested on charges that included enforced disappearance, torture, and obstruction. Arrest warrants against other officials, including military personnel, were issued, though a number were later canceled or contested in court, and in September 2022 the special prosecutor in the case, Omar Gómez Trejo, resigned amid disputes over the prosecution's direction. The case thus involved not only the original crime but also contested efforts to investigate it.

What is established as of the chapter's cutoff? The identity of the 43 students, the attack of 26-27 September, the involvement of local police, and the participation of members of a criminal organization are supported by multiple investigations. The remains of three of the 43 have been identified by independent forensic laboratories. The whereabouts of the others are unknown. The role of higher-level officials and of military personnel, the possible motives, and the fate of the students after they were taken remain disputed or unresolved. In 2025 a new special prosecutor, Mauricio Pazarán, took over the case under the Sheinbaum government. In 2026 the government presented new lines of investigation focused on telephone data and on funeral and forensic services in Iguala, announced the arrest of a former Iguala police officer, and met repeatedly with the families, who acknowledged some advances but stated that the whereabouts of the students remained unknown. Allegations should not be read as judicial findings, and several prosecutions remain unresolved.

Section 103.12

Journalists under Threat: Murder, Disappearance, and Self-Censorship

Mexico has been among the most dangerous countries in the world for journalists outside of declared war zones, and the violence has been concentrated in local and regional reporting. Reporters who covered crime, corruption, and the links between officials and criminal groups were threatened, attacked, kidnapped, or killed. Press-freedom organizations count well over a hundred journalists killed since 2000, with totals differing by methodology, and many more cases of disappearance, threats, and exile. Violence has been most severe in states such as Veracruz, Tamaulipas, Guerrero, Chihuahua, and Sinaloa, and in small cities where a few reporters were the only source of local information.

The Committee to Protect Journalists distinguishes among journalists killed in direct retaliation for their work, in crossfire or while on a dangerous assignment, and cases in which the motive is not confirmed. Using such definitions matters: not every murder of a journalist is proven to be work-related, but in Mexico the combination of impunity and the lack of effective investigations means that many cases remain unresolved and motives unconfirmed. Examples include the 2012 killing of Regina Martínez, a correspondent for the magazine Proceso in Veracruz, and the 2017 killing of Miroslava Breach, a reporter in Chihuahua who had covered links between politicians and organized crime. In May 2017, Javier Valdez, a Sinaloa journalist and co-founder of the weekly Ríodoce who had written extensively about trafficking and its human costs, was shot dead in Culiacán near his newspaper's offices. His killing drew national and international condemnation; prosecutors attributed it to a criminal faction and some individuals were prosecuted, though press-freedom groups have continued to demand clarity about who ordered it.

The state's responses were limited. A federal special prosecutor's office for crimes against freedom of expression existed from 2006 in an earlier form and was reorganized in 2010, and in 2013 Congress amended the law so federal authorities could take jurisdiction over such crimes. In 2012 the government created a federal protection mechanism for human-rights defenders and journalists, which provides measures such as bodyguards, panic buttons, and relocation. The mechanism helped many individuals but was widely criticized for limited funding, delays, and its inability to address the root causes of impunity, and some protected persons were nonetheless killed. The persistence of near-total impunity, as measured in the very small share of cases that reach a conviction, remains the central problem.

Violence produces self-censorship, a reaction in which journalists and editors avoid topics, omit names, or stop publishing. In September 2010 the newspaper El Diario de Juárez, after the murder of a young photographer, published an editorial addressed to the criminal groups asking what they wanted the newspaper to publish, a striking public admission of the limits that violence imposed. Some outlets adopted formal guidelines for covering violence; others shut down or moved coverage online or abroad. The result was that citizens in some areas lost access to information about the groups and officials that governed their lives, a harm not captured in homicide statistics.

Journalists were not only victims but also historians of the conflict in a practical sense. Much of what is known about the early years of the war, the identities of victims, the structure of local criminal governance, and the failures of investigations, comes from regional reporters and from collaborative investigations by Mexican media, some of which continued after the murder of colleagues. Their work is cited throughout this chapter, and students should treat press reporting as evidence whose reliability depends on sourcing, corroboration, and the conditions under which it was produced, including the risks that shaped what could be published.

Section 103.13

The Crisis of Disappearances and Clandestine Graves

Disappearance is not new in Mexican history. During the "dirty war" of the 1960s and 1970s, security forces in states such as Guerrero detained and disappeared suspected guerrillas and political dissidents. What changed after 2006 was scale, perpetrators, and victims. Disappearances now affected men and women of every age and social background, including migrants, workers, students, and people with no known link to crime, and responsibility was diffuse: criminal organizations, sometimes in collusion with officials, and, in some documented cases, state agents. Because most disappearances were not investigated, the identity of perpetrators is unknown in the great majority of cases.

Three categories must be distinguished, and the distinctions have legal significance. Enforced disappearance (desaparición forzada) involves the participation of public servants, or of private persons acting with their authorization, support, or acquiescence. Disappearance committed by private persons (desaparición cometida por particulares) is the deprivation of liberty of a person followed by concealment of their fate or whereabouts, without state participation. A person who is not located (no localizada) is someone whose whereabouts are unknown for any reason, which may include voluntary absence, accident, or crime. Official registers combine these categories, and in most reported cases the facts needed to classify the case are not known.

The Registro Nacional de Personas Desaparecidas y No Localizadas (RNPDNO) is a central instrument for counting disappearances, but it is not a complete census. Reports can come through multiple institutions and routes, including families, search commissions, and authorities; fear, distrust, incomplete reporting, delayed entries, and retrospective corrections limit comparisons. The register passed 100,000 persons reported missing in May 2022. On 27 March 2026 Mexico's government reported 132,534 historical entries from 1952 onward, of which 130,178 related to people recorded since 2006 as still disappeared. It classified those 130,178 entries into 46,742 with insufficient identifying data, 40,308 with later administrative activity, and 43,128 with adequate data but no later matching administrative activity. These are administrative review categories, not verified findings that the 40,308 people had been located alive or that the others' fates were determined. Search collectives and human-rights researchers questioned the review's methodology and cautioned against using categories to discount family testimony. Counts depend on scope, date, and the status categories selected.

Clandestine graves (fosas clandestinas) became central to the crisis. Civil-society collectives and government agencies located burial sites in states including Tamaulipas, Veracruz, Jalisco, Guerrero, Sonora, Zacatecas, and Colima. At Colinas de Santa Fe near Veracruz, searches beginning in 2016 led to the recovery of remains attributed to large numbers of victims. In March 2025, searchers publicized findings at a ranch in Teuchitlán, Jalisco, including personal belongings and evidence investigated by prosecutors; descriptions of the site's precise function, the interpretation of the objects, and claims about the number or fate of victims remained contested pending forensic investigation. Separate UN procedural steps must not be collapsed: in March 2025 the UN Committee on Enforced Disappearances initiated an Article 34 review; on 17 April 2026 it issued decision CED/C/MEX/A.34/D/1 to bring the situation to the General Assembly's attention. The decision is an international treaty-body procedure, not a judicial finding of criminal responsibility in every disappearance case. Mexico challenged aspects of the process. Mexican legislation in 2025 also introduced measures concerning identification and search, whose implementation remained disputed.

Parallel to disappearances is a forensic crisis. Tens of thousands of unidentified human remains were held by state forensic services, in morgues, in common graves, and in storage, with estimates circulating in the 2020s of upward of 70,000, a figure that governments have disputed and that varies with definitions. Identification requires DNA databases, cooperation among states, and the participation of families, which has been uneven. The history of disappearance is therefore also a history of bodies waiting for names.

Historical Vocabulary
Enforced disappearance and Disappearance by private persons

Under international law and Mexico's General Law of 2017, enforced disappearance requires the involvement of state agents or their authorization, support, or acquiescence, and is treated as a grave human-rights violation and, in some circumstances, a crime against humanity. Disappearance by private persons refers to abduction and concealment by non-state actors. Both are crimes in Mexican law.

The distinction determines which legal regime and which institutions apply, and it affects claims about state responsibility. In reporting, the term "disappeared" is often used loosely for any missing person, and students should not assume that a disappearance recorded in the register involved state participation, nor that it did not. Where the evidence does not establish responsibility, the accurate description is that responsibility is undetermined.

Section 103.14

Families Searching for the Missing: Collectives and Forensic Justice

When institutions failed to search, families did. Beginning in the early 2010s, and accelerating after 2014, groups of relatives, most of them mothers, organized as collectives to look for their missing children, spouses, and siblings. They became known as madres buscadoras (searching mothers), though fathers, siblings, and other relatives also joined. Collectives such as those in Coahuila, Veracruz, Sinaloa, Sonora, Jalisco, and Guerrero developed distinctive practices. Members collected information from witnesses and anonymous tips, walked fields and hillsides with metal probes and shovels, recognized signs of disturbed earth, and recorded their findings. Some collectives worked with forensic anthropologists and with non-governmental groups that trained them in procedures to avoid damaging evidence.

The work was emotionally and financially costly. Searchers paid for transportation, tools, and lodging from their own funds, took time away from jobs, and endured years without answers. Searching was dangerous. Searchers have faced threats, and a number have been killed, though counts differ; in some regions, groups reported being warned away from sites or followed. Some state officials cooperated and others obstructed, and collectives sometimes worked in the absence of police protection. Distrust of authorities was deepened by cases in which remains were mishandled or families were given incorrect information.

Forensic identification became a central arena. Matching remains to families requires DNA samples from relatives, genetic databases, and cross-state coordination. Families and collectives pressed for identification, sometimes by providing DNA samples themselves and by monitoring forensic services. International experts, including teams that had worked in other Latin American countries, contributed to training and to specific cases. Institutions created after the 2017 law, including a national human-identification center and an extraordinary mechanism for forensic identification, were intended to address the backlog, but their effects and capacity remain disputed.

The collectives transformed public discussion. They made visible a category of victim that had no corpse and thus no easily recognized crime scene, they insisted that the state had a duty to search, and they brought a moral authority grounded in motherhood and in persistence. They also made gendered claims: many searchers were women who combined care roles with unpaid public labor, and the women among the disappeared were often not counted or investigated. The collectives' political influence is reflected in legislation, in the participation of families in national and state search systems, and in international attention. They also operate in tension with the state, accepting its institutions where they work and criticizing them where they do not.

Section 103.15

Displacement, Migration, Gender, and the Transformation of Everyday Life

The consequences of violence extended far beyond those killed or disappeared. Researchers have estimated that violence displaced several hundred thousand people within Mexico since 2006, with wide uncertainty because there is no complete official count. Displacement took different forms. In some cases, whole communities fled after armed groups took over or after massacres, as in parts of Guerrero, Michoacán, Chiapas, and Sinaloa. In others, individual families left cities after threats or extortion. Some displaced people crossed the border, and some sought asylum abroad.

Livelihoods changed. Farmers in regions such as Michoacán's avocado and lime districts and in Guerrero's highlands faced extortion and kidnapping; small shopkeepers paid protection; and transportation workers and public employees such as teachers were threatened. Schools closed or reduced hours in violent periods, and children's routines were restricted. Young people, particularly young men in poor neighborhoods, were recruited as lookouts, couriers, and gunmen, in some cases voluntarily for income and status, in others under coercion, and the distinction is difficult to draw in practice. This chapter therefore distinguishes criminal recruitment, economic coercion, forced migration, and direct victimization, which overlap but are not identical.

Experiences were gendered. Most homicide victims were men, but women experienced violence in distinctive ways, including femicide, sexual violence, forced recruitment, and trafficking, and as relatives of the disappeared they bore the burden of searching. Mexico enacted laws on femicide and on women's access to a life free of violence, and feminist movements pressed for accountability, though implementation has been uneven. Migrants, many of them women and children from Central America, faced kidnapping and abuse as they crossed the country, as shown at San Fernando.

Indigenous communities responded in varied ways. In 2011 the Purépecha community of Cherán in Michoacán, after repeated killings associated with illegal logging and criminal extortion, expelled armed groups and local political parties and established a system of community self-government and a community police force, later recognized by Mexican courts under rules protecting Indigenous self-determination. In other regions, communities formed community police under older traditions of local justice, as in parts of Guerrero, or armed self-defense groups, and some of these groups were themselves criticized for abuses or co-opted by criminal interests. Indigenous communities in Chiapas and elsewhere also experienced displacement due to armed competition and land disputes.

Experiences were not uniform. Much of Mexico experienced comparatively little organized-crime violence, and violence concentrated in specific states, municipalities, and neighborhoods. Wealthier families sometimes bought private security or relocated; poorer families often had fewer options. The social history of the drug war is thus a history of unequal exposure and unequal protection.

Everyday Life and Social History

Searching, Waiting, and Adapting

For a searcher, a day might begin before dawn with a drive to a remote site, carrying probes, picks, and water bought with household funds. Searchers learn to read the ground, to notice differences in vegetation or soil, and to stop when they encounter remains so that forensic personnel can recover them properly. Afterward, families may wait months or years for identification, in some cases visiting forensic offices to review photographs or belongings.

In neighborhoods affected by violence, ordinary adaptations multiplied: families changed routes to school, businesses closed early, and communities developed informal alert networks. For the families of the disappeared, a particular legal and emotional limbo arose: without a body, they could not hold a funeral, yet property, parental authority, and employment benefits required legal solutions, which later legislation on a special declaration of absence attempted to provide. The silences in the record about these experiences are themselves evidence of whose voices public institutions recorded.

Section 103.16

Victims' Rights, Criminal Justice, and the Institutions of Search

Mexico's legal response to the crisis took shape through a sequence of reforms. The 2011 constitutional reform on human rights gave international treaties constitutional standing and strengthened the legal basis for victims' claims. The General Victims Law of 2013 recognized victims' rights to truth, justice, and reparation and created a national victims' system and an executive commission to provide assistance. The criminal-justice reform, fully implemented by 2016, moved toward oral adversarial trials with greater rights for defendants and victims, while retaining controversial measures such as extended pretrial detention.

The most important legal instrument for the disappeared is the General Law on Forced Disappearance, Disappearance Committed by Private Persons, and the National System for the Search for Persons, published in November 2017 and in force from January 2018. The law defines the crimes, establishes the National Search System, creates a National Search Commission and state commissions, requires specialized prosecutors' offices, creates the national register and a national forensic data bank, and recognizes families' right to participate in search. It provides for immediate search upon a report, without waiting periods, and it established penalties for officials who fail to act.

Yet the gap between entitlement and implementation has been persistent. Searching is the responsibility of search commissions, which locate persons, while prosecutors conduct criminal investigation and forensic services identify remains; coordination among these institutions, and among federal and state levels, has often been weak. Commissions and prosecutors' offices have been under-resourced, and by some civil-society analyses only a small share of recorded disappearances have an associated criminal investigation, with percentages that vary by source and period. Few perpetrators have been convicted. Reforms in 2025 sought to strengthen identification, including a biometric identity element and a unified identity platform, measures welcomed by some and criticized by others on privacy and effectiveness grounds, and their results are still being assessed.

Human-rights bodies such as the National Human-Rights Commission and state commissions, and international bodies such as the Inter-American Commission and the United Nations, serve as monitors, issuing recommendations and reports. These bodies lack coercive power, and their influence depends on political will and on civil-society pressure. The history of Mexican victims' law is therefore a history of legal progress and institutional strain, in which the law reflects the demands of families but has often outpaced the capacity of the state to carry it out.

Judicial responses to the crisis also emerged. Mexico's Supreme Court and federal tribunals issued decisions on the right to search, the obligations of prosecutors, and the participation of victims in proceedings, and international bodies, including the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, issued judgments on cases of disappearance and military jurisdiction. These decisions provided families with legal arguments and precedents, even when authorities were slow to comply. The tension between a robust body of legal doctrine and weak enforcement is one of the defining features of the period.

To assess implementation, historians must consult budgets, commission reports, prosecutorial data, and testimony from families. The perspectives of those who negotiated the law appear in its structure, but the voices of families appear only indirectly.

Historical Vocabulary
Impunity (impunidad)

Impunity means the failure to investigate, prosecute, and punish crimes, so that perpetrators face no consequences. In Mexico the term is used for the proportion of crimes, especially homicides and disappearances, that do not result in conviction, and for the immunity from accountability enjoyed by powerful actors.

Estimates of impunity vary because they depend on what is counted: reported crimes, unreported crimes, investigations opened, or convictions. Because most crimes in Mexico are never reported and most reported crimes are not resolved, impunity is a structural feature of the justice system rather than a feature only of the drug war, though violence intensified its effects.

Section 103.17

Andrés Manuel López Obrador: Security, the National Guard, and Militarization, 2018-2024

Andrés Manuel López Obrador, who won the presidency in 2018 at the head of the Morena movement, had criticized the earlier strategy for years, arguing that confrontation had produced violence and that crime must be addressed through its social causes. His slogan, abrazos, no balazos ("hugs, not bullets"), expressed a rejection of the kingpin strategy and a commitment to social programs for young people, such as job-training and scholarship schemes, as a way of reducing recruitment. He stated that the war on drugs had ended. Critics, including opponents who said he was soft on crime, and supporters of dialogue, argued over what the slogan meant in practice.

In practice, the government relied heavily on military institutions. After negotiating a constitutional reform in 2019, it created the National Guard, which began operating in June 2019 with personnel drawn largely from military and federal police units. Although conceived as a civilian force, it was in practice commanded by military officers, and in 2022 and 2024 the government moved it under the authority of the Defense Ministry, formalized by a constitutional reform in September 2024. The Supreme Court had in 2023 struck down the 2022 legal route to that transfer, so the government pursued the constitutional change. A 2022 constitutional provision extended the armed forces' participation in public-security tasks until 2028. The military also took on civilian functions such as the administration of ports, customs, airports, and large infrastructure projects. The army and navy thus acquired roles that went well beyond those of 2006.

Events illustrated the tensions in the strategy. In October 2019 security forces detained Ovidio Guzmán in Culiacán but released him after armed groups launched widespread attacks in the city; the episode, known as the Culiacanazo, illustrated the limits of state force. In 2020 U.S. authorities arrested former Mexican defense secretary Salvador Cienfuegos; the U.S. Justice Department subsequently dismissed its case and Mexico declined to prosecute after his return, amid a bitter controversy over intelligence sharing. Mexico then restricted activities of foreign agents. Ovidio Guzmán was arrested again in 2023 and extradited that year. Across the six calendar years 2019–2024, INEGI registered 208,223 homicides: the annual count reached 36,773 in 2020, declined to 32,252 in 2023, and rose to 33,550 in 2024. These are all registered homicide deaths, not adjudicated drug-war casualties, and the six-year interval does not coincide precisely with the presidential term.

Disappearances also continued, and the López Obrador government's handling of the issue was mixed: it expanded forensic and search institutions, held repeated meetings with families of the Ayotzinapa students, and created the presidential commission whose 2022 report is discussed above, but families and civil-society groups criticized the slow pace of identification, disputes about statistics, and the president's reluctance to acknowledge the scale of the problem. His government's critics argued that militarization had deepened without commensurate accountability for abuses, while its defenders pointed to declining homicide totals after 2020 and to social programs. As with earlier administrations, the evidence does not permit a simple verdict, and homicide trends were affected by factors beyond federal policy.

Section 103.18

International Cooperation, Sovereignty, and Competing Security Priorities

The security relationship between Mexico and the United States evolved from the Mérida Initiative's equipment-oriented program toward a broader partnership, and then toward periodic tension. In October 2021, the two governments replaced Mérida with the Bicentennial Framework, which shifted emphasis toward public health, preventing the flow of weapons, reducing violence, and addressing the root causes of crime, a reflection of Mexican complaints about the earlier model and of the Biden administration's emphasis on drug use as a public-health question.

Disagreements persisted across several issues. Mexico argued that U.S. gun markets supplied weapons to criminal groups and brought lawsuits against U.S. firearms manufacturers, while U.S. officials emphasized Mexican producers and the flow of fentanyl and other drugs. Mexico limited the activities of U.S. agents in the country, as with the 2020 law on foreign agents following the Cienfuegos affair, and U.S. officials expressed frustration about intelligence sharing and extradition. Migration became intertwined with security, as U.S. administrations pressed Mexico to reduce migrant flows to the border and Mexican forces, including the National Guard, were deployed for that purpose. The rise of synthetic drugs, treated in Chapter 104, changed the political framing in Washington by making the drug problem a matter of mass overdose deaths.

After 2025, tensions increased over sovereignty and counter-cartel action. The United States designated six Mexican criminal organizations as foreign terrorist organizations in February 2025 and two additional Mexican organizations in July 2026, according to the Congressional Research Service's 23 September 2026 report R48859. These are U.S. legal designations, not a universal scholarly category. That report also records Mexico's transfer of 92 alleged or convicted traffickers into U.S. custody by January 2026. The word transfer matters: such movements should not automatically be described as extraditions pursuant to ordinary judicial procedure, and they do not establish guilt in every individual case. The two governments developed new security coordination while disagreeing over tariffs, firearms flows, migration, intelligence access, and possible unilateral action. Mexican authorities conducted the operation in Jalisco on 22 February 2026 that killed CJNG leader Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes; U.S. authorities acknowledged intelligence assistance, which should not be confused with direct U.S. execution of the operation. The broader criminal market and CJNG's organizational history belong to Chapter 104.

Historians should avoid describing these developments only as U.S. pressure on Mexico. Mexican governments pursued their own interests, bargaining with Washington over trade, migration, and security, and Mexican officials and institutions were active partners and sometimes initiators. The relationship was asymmetrical, but it was a negotiation among governments with distinct priorities.

The September 2026 CRS assessment provides a dated snapshot of the relationship, not a judgment on every operation: it records expanded intelligence sharing and public security cooperation alongside disputes over Mexican sovereignty, U.S. tariffs, and border policy. A historian must therefore separate four evidentiary categories. First are the dates and legal effects of U.S. designations. Second are the administrative mechanisms by which people enter U.S. custody. Third are claims by governments about coordination and operational outcomes. Fourth are unresolved questions about what intelligence was shared, what Mexican officials authorized, and how citizens were affected. Collapsing these categories turns contested diplomacy into an unjustified account of secret command.

Section 103.19

Claudia Sheinbaum and Mexico's Security Challenges, 2024-2026

Claudia Sheinbaum, a scientist and former mayor of Mexico City who took office on 1 October 2024 as Mexico's first woman president, kept most of López Obrador's institutional architecture but changed emphasis. She appointed Omar García Harfuch, a former head of Mexico City's police who had survived a 2020 assassination attempt, as security secretary. Her government described a strategy with four pillars: attention to causes, strengthening of the National Guard, strengthening of intelligence and investigation, and coordination among federal, state, and local authorities. It expanded the National Guard, which officials said reached about 120,000 members, concentrated operations in priority municipalities, and enacted legislation to strengthen intelligence and investigative systems. It also launched a national strategy against extortion in July 2025.

Official figures report a substantial decline in one prosecutorial indicator. Mexico's public-security secretariat (SESNSP) reported average daily intentional-homicide victims declining from 86.9 in September 2024 to 40.8 in August 2026; its newer report, released 10 November 2026, gave 43.4 per day for October 2026, approximately 50 percent below the September 2024 comparison month. These are government-reported monthly averages, not an INEGI civil-registry series or a causal evaluation of the strategy. INEGI's final November 2026 report recorded 29,488 homicide deaths registered in 2025; an analysis separating the year of occurrence identified 28,089 occurring in 2025. Comparison with prosecutors' figures requires consistent definitions and accounting for when a death occurred versus when it was registered. Homicide indicators do not count disappearances, extortion, unreported violence, or displacement, and violence remained geographically concentrated in particular states. The government said it would review the agencies' statistical differences state by state.

Events illustrated both operational capacity and persistent risk. On 22 February 2026, Mexican army forces in Tapalpa, Jalisco, killed Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, "El Mencho," the leader of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, in an operation for which the United States acknowledged providing intelligence support. Blockades with burning vehicles followed in numerous states, and published tallies counted dozens of deaths, including more than two dozen National Guard members, though counts varied by source. The episode showed that operations against leaders retained their capacity to provoke violence, even under a government that criticized the kingpin strategy. The consequences for criminal organizations are examined in Chapter 104. Elsewhere, violence in Sinaloa, tied to an internal conflict within the Sinaloa Cartel after the 2024 arrest in the United States of Ismael Zambada, continued to affect residents.

Disappearances, journalist security, and extortion remained serious. The register of disappeared persons, under revision, remained above 130,000 records, and families criticized changes to the count as potentially minimizing the crisis. Journalists continued to be killed and threatened, and search collectives continued to report threats. The Ayotzinapa case continued without resolution of the students' fate. Opposition leaders alleged that the government concealed crime statistics or maintained improper relations with criminal groups, claims that were not supported by public evidence in the sources available to this chapter and that the government rejected, while independent analysts noted that arrests are not convictions and that the state's capacity to prosecute and to protect remained limited. Whether the decline in homicide reflects durable institutional change, shifts in criminal markets, or changes in recording and disappearance is unresolved.

The political uses of statistics deserve attention. Governments of every party have emphasized favorable indicators and criticized unfavorable ones, and opposition parties have done the reverse. Independent researchers, university groups, and civil-society organizations such as Data Cívica and México Evalúa have compiled alternative analyses, and journalists have built databases from press reports and public-records requests. Because no single source captures the whole phenomenon, historians working on the recent past cross-check official statistics, independent databases, and testimony, and they state the date on which each figure was consulted.

Section 103.20

Twenty Years of Conflict: Consequences, Explanations, and Unresolved Questions

Two decades after Operation Michoacán, what can be said with confidence? The armed forces became permanent participants in domestic security, institutionalized through the National Guard and constitutional change. Registered homicides rose to unprecedented levels in the 2010s and 2020s and have fallen since 2020 by most measures, though they remain very high by international standards. Disappearances became a mass phenomenon, the clandestine grave a recurring discovery, and unidentified remains a national burden. The justice system remained weak, with high impunity for homicide, disappearance, and attacks on journalists. And Mexican civil society, from the Sicilia movement to search collectives, achieved legal and institutional changes that the state has only partly implemented.

What is disputed? Historians and social scientists disagree about the effect of militarization on violence, about whether fragmentation was caused by enforcement or by market and political factors, about how to count the dead and missing, and about how far the state's agents bear responsibility for abuses and complicity. The comparison of presidents' strategies illustrates the difficulty: Calderón's confrontation, Peña Nieto's lower profile, López Obrador's emphasis on causes, and Sheinbaum's focus on intelligence and coordination each coincided with different homicide trends, but each also faced different criminal markets, institutions, and international pressures. No single policy can be declared solely responsible for Mexico's trajectory.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Militarization a Cause of the Crisis or a Response to It?

The question: Did deploying the armed forces to public security worsen violence and abuses, or did it respond to a collapse of civilian institutions that would have produced violence anyway?

Interpretation A: Militarization intensified violence and abuse. Critics argue that deploying soldiers disrupted criminal arrangements, increased confrontation, raised the number of civilian deaths and complaints of abuse, discouraged investment in civilian police and prosecutors, and entrenched military power with limited accountability. They point to rising homicide and complaints after the deployments and to the continued weakness of civilian institutions.
Interpretation B: Militarization responded to institutional collapse. Defenders and some analysts argue that local and state police were too compromised to confront armed groups, that violence was already rising before 2006, and that the armed forces prevented loss of territorial control. They note that the military remained among the most trusted institutions and that withdrawal without alternatives could have worsened conditions.

Evidence: Studies compare municipalities with and without deployments, examine complaints and homicide trends, and analyze police capacity. Results depend on how deployments are measured, on the timing of violence before and after, and on unobserved local factors. Scholars of criminal markets also emphasize factors independent of deployments, including shifts in drug routes and fragmentation.

Why it matters: The answer shapes judgments about whether the problem was the use of the military or the failure to build civilian institutions alongside it. Many scholars hold a combined view: deployments may have increased violence in certain contexts while also responding to genuine institutional failure, and the persistence of military roles across four governments reflects the absence of a viable alternative as much as political preference.

Student task: Evaluate each interpretation, identify what evidence would favor one over the other, and explain why the two need not be mutually exclusive.

The social and institutional consequences carry beyond security policy. Mexican democracy, achieved through the electoral reforms and alternation of power discussed in earlier chapters, coexists with large areas where citizens' rights are inadequately protected, where local governments are penetrated by criminal interests, and where citizens rely on informal networks. Families of the disappeared became a moral force in national politics. The relations between the armed forces and civilian leadership, between the federal government and states, and between Mexico and the United States have been redefined.

Finally, the unresolved questions: the fate of the 43 students and the responsibility of higher-level officials; the true number of disappeared and the identity of the unidentified dead; whether the 2024-2026 decline in registered homicides will endure; how organizations will reorganize after the removal of their leaders; and how the justice system will convert arrests into convictions. The next chapter turns to the organizations and markets that changed during these years. It examines the rise of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, the fentanyl and methamphetamine economy, and the synthetic-drug supply chains that reshaped both the criminal landscape and the security debate that this chapter has traced.

These analytical limits also shape comparisons of security policy. A reduction in recorded homicide victims does not establish that search institutions have identified the missing or that criminal investigations have resulted in convictions. Conversely, evidence of abuse does not establish that every military operation produced abuse. Better comparisons use contemporaneous official records, independent victim and forensic evidence, and definitions stable enough to permit like-for-like comparison. They also examine whether improved public security was accompanied by independent oversight, access to justice, and safer conditions for journalists and searchers. That is the wider measure by which the consequences of twenty years of conflict should be assessed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of Mexico's drug war from December 2006 to November 2026, arguing that it was not simply a confrontation between government forces and trafficking organizations but a prolonged crisis of public security, state authority, human rights, criminal justice, and democratic accountability. Chapter 102 explained the organizations; this chapter has explained the conflict and its consequences.

Felipe Calderón took office in 2006 after a contested election and made organized crime a central national priority. Operation Michoacán in December 2006 began a deployment of soldiers, sailors, and federal police that spread to other states. The decision was not inevitable, and alternatives existed, but it rested on a judgment that civilian police and prosecutors could not meet the challenge. The deployment raised practical problems, because soldiers are trained for combat rather than investigation, and constitutional and human-rights concerns, because military courts had jurisdiction over many cases involving soldiers.

U.S.-Mexican cooperation under the Mérida Initiative, begun in 2007-2008, provided equipment, training, and institutional support, though appropriated funds differed from delivered assistance. It prompted debates over sovereignty and human-rights conditions. The United States contributed to the conflict through demand for drugs and the supply of firearms, but the principal actors and victims were Mexican.

Violence rose sharply after 2007 and unevenly across regions. National homicide totals rose from about 8,900 in 2007 to above 27,000 in 2011, but these registered homicides cannot be equated with drug-war deaths. Ciudad Juárez, Tijuana, Michoacán, and Tamaulipas illustrated contrasting security environments, and the experience of Juárez, with its urban siege and its later decline in violence, became a major case study. The fragmentation of criminal organizations and enforcement pressure interacted in ways scholars continue to debate, and the claim that leadership removal intensified violence is supported in some contexts but is not a universal law.

Between 2010 and 2012 mass atrocities revealed institutional failure: the killing of 72 migrants at San Fernando, the discovery of mass graves there, the disappearances at Allende, and the Casino Royale arson. These cases showed the vulnerability of migrants and the difficulty of identifying victims. In response, the poet Javier Sicilia and the Movement for Peace with Justice and Dignity brought victims into national politics as rights-bearing citizens, leading to the General Victims Law of 2013.

Under Enrique Peña Nieto, rhetoric changed more than practice. The military remained engaged and homicide fell to about 20,000 in 2014 before rising to record levels in 2018. The Tlatlaya killings of June 2014 showed how an initial official account could be contradicted by journalism and by a human-rights recommendation, while accountability remained limited. The disappearance of 43 students at Ayotzinapa in September 2014 became the emblematic case of disappearance and failed investigation. The "historical truth" announced by the government was challenged by independent experts, a later presidential commission described the case as a state crime, and as of November 2026 the fate of most of the students remained unknown.

Journalists who reported on crime and corruption were threatened and killed, particularly in local news environments, and impunity and self-censorship limited citizens' access to information. Protection mechanisms and special prosecutors offered partial responses, and the killing of Javier Valdez in 2017 became a symbol of the danger.

Disappearances became a mass phenomenon. The chapter distinguished enforced disappearance, disappearance by private persons, and cases of undetermined responsibility, and noted the limits of the national register, which surpassed 100,000 records in 2022 and was revised in 2026 amid disputes. Clandestine graves, unidentified remains, and a forensic crisis became defining features, and the series of disappearances and of homicides measure different things. Families and search collectives, especially madres buscadoras, became central actors who searched for remains and demanded truth, and they transformed public debate and law, including the General Law on Disappearances of 2017, though implementation lagged.

The violence also transformed everyday life through displacement, extortion, school and business disruption, recruitment of young people, and gendered violence. Indigenous communities, as at Cherán, responded with self-government and community police. Experience varied widely across regions and social groups.

Under López Obrador, rhetoric of "hugs, not bullets" accompanied the creation and military consolidation of the National Guard and the expansion of the armed forces' civilian roles, with high homicide totals. U.S.-Mexican cooperation moved from Mérida to the Bicentennial Framework and then to renewed tension after 2025. Under Claudia Sheinbaum, the government emphasized intelligence, investigation, and coordination, reported substantial declines in homicide, and carried out the operation that killed the CJNG leader in February 2026, which produced widespread violence. INEGI's final 2025 figure of 29,488 registered homicides differed from the prosecutors' series, and disappearances, extortion, journalist insecurity, and concentrated violence persisted.

The chapter's conclusion is that no single policy can be held solely responsible for Mexico's security trajectory. Militarization responded to institutional weakness and may have intensified violence in some contexts; civilian institutions were slow to improve; and civil society achieved legal changes the state only partly implemented. The unresolved questions include the fate of the 43 students, the number and identity of the disappeared and unidentified, the durability of recent declines in homicide, and the conversion of arrests into convictions. Chapter 104 turns to the organizations and synthetic-drug markets that changed alongside these developments.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a national security campaign, launched with defined goals, evolved into a long-term transformation of institutions and daily life. The drug war cannot be understood only through the history of organizations. It was also a history of what soldiers and police could and could not do, of what courts and prosecutors failed to do, and of what families, journalists, and researchers insisted that the state must do.

The chapter reveals larger processes that extend beyond Mexico. Fragmented policing, weak courts, and illicit markets created the conditions for violence, and the response, a reliance on armed forces, echoes patterns seen in Colombia in Chapters 98-100 and in Central America in Chapter 111. At the same time Mexico's experience is distinctive: its federal structure, its democratic politics, and its relationship with the United States shaped both the conflict and the responses to it.

Several continuities survived the chapter's boundaries. Military roles in public security remain, impunity remains high, and families continue to search. The tension between the claims of governments about declining violence and the experiences of communities is unresolved, as are the fates of the missing. The achievements of civil society, including victims' law, search institutions, and a public language of rights, are lasting, though incompletely implemented.

Chapter 104 takes up a changing criminal economy. It examines the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, fentanyl, methamphetamine, and the supply chains for synthetic drugs, and it asks how those markets altered the organizations and the security debates traced here. Students should carry forward the view that the institutional and social consequences of the conflict shaped, and were shaped by, the changing economy of drugs.
