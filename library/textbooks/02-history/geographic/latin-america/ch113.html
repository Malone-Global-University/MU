The Political Pendulum: Latin American Democracy in the 2010s and 2020s

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations
Historical cutoff: 16 November 2026 · First-edition textual chapter

Chapter Opening: A Continent Without a Single Political Direction

Elections kept changing governments. Their results did not establish one shared ideological direction for Latin America.

On 25 October 2026, Brazil's electoral tribunal announced that Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva had won another presidential election. Just months earlier, Peru had inaugurated Keiko Fujimori and Colombia had sworn in Abelardo de la Espriella. Chile had begun a conservative administration after the left-wing presidency of Gabriel Boric; Uruguay had returned a left-of-center governing coalition; Mexico had continued under Morena. These outcomes are difficult to place on a single ideological arc. They reveal a hemisphere in which elections remained politically powerful even while citizens argued about courts, corruption, public security, economic hardship, and the legitimacy of governments.

This chapter examines the metaphor of a political pendulum and its limits. At first glance a succession of left- and right-leaning presidents suggests voters repeatedly reversed direction. But an electoral result is more than a label attached to its winner. It emerges from economic circumstances, local coalitions, constitutional rules, party organizations, and diverse citizens whose reasons for voting differ. A party can remain in power while adopting new policies; a party can lose nationally yet retain provincial power; an outsider can win an election and then depend on established legislators to govern.

The narrative begins with the exhaustion of the commodity-boom era, corruption investigations, and dissatisfaction with traditional parties. It then studies Brazil's impeachment and electoral confrontations, Argentina's economic and partisan alternations, and Mexico's formation of a new governing coalition under López Obrador and Claudia Sheinbaum. Further comparisons extend to Chile, Colombia, Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Uruguay, and Costa Rica. The goal is not to force every election into a uniform cycle, but to explain why contrasting institutional histories generated different outcomes during the same decades.

The chapter distinguishes legal findings from political accusations. A court judgment has a different evidentiary status from a campaign speech; an electoral tribunal's final count differs from an election-night forecast; and a survey of support for democracy does not directly measure judicial independence. These distinctions matter especially when contemporary politicians accuse opponents of corruption or electoral fraud. The historian must state what the record establishes, identify where interpretations remain contested, and avoid giving unsupported allegations the authority of verified facts.

The chapter also distinguishes institutional continuity from political agreement. A constitution may regulate peaceful transfer of office while the parties dispute the legitimacy of particular court decisions. Citizens may support elections and simultaneously distrust Congress. A president may receive a substantial mandate without gaining the right to suspend civil liberties or prevent future competition. These tensions are neither confined to one ideology nor solved by assuming that a strong leader represents a uniform national public.

Quantitative exercises use certified election returns, economic statistical series, and comparative democracy research. Each source requires its own denominator and method. Brazilian runoff percentages are shares of valid votes; Argentina's inflation measures price change over a year; Latinobarómetro reports surveyed attitudes rather than a judicial or constitutional determination. Students should learn not only what numbers say but what they do not prove.

The chapter covers the 2010s through 16 November 2026, with earlier background where necessary. It builds on the Pink Tide treated in Chapter 108 and the social movements of Chapter 110 without retelling them. Chapters 111–112 address gangs and organized crime, while Chapter 114 considers the pandemic, migration, Amazonia, lithium, China, and changing resource geopolitics. Contemporary electoral outcomes are presented as completed facts when certified; the longer consequences of the governments formed in 2026 remain open.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the political-pendulum metaphor and explain its analytical limits.
  2. Differentiate electoral alternation, policy change, and democratic consolidation.
  3. Explain how commodity prices and economic insecurity affected political legitimacy.
  4. Evaluate corruption investigations without confusing allegations, convictions, and annulments.
  5. Reconstruct Brazil's impeachment, elections, and 2023 constitutional crisis.
  6. Interpret Brazil's 2026 result using its valid-vote denominator.
  7. Compare Argentine Peronism, Macri, and the Milei governing program.
  8. Explain the rise of Morena and Mexico's 2024–2025 judicial changes.
  9. Assess the 2025–2026 transitions in Chile, Colombia, and Peru.
  10. Compare Bolivia, Ecuador, Uruguay, and Costa Rica without imposing one ideological cycle.
  11. Distinguish popular support for democracy from expert-coded institutional measures.
  12. Analyze how electoral mandates interact with checks, balances, and rights.

Historical Context

Late twentieth-century democratization established or restored competitive elections in many Latin American countries, although at unequal rates and under different constitutional settlements. Mexico's 2000 change of governing party, the end of South American military dictatorships, and peace processes in Central America left new possibilities for electoral choice. The early twenty-first-century Pink Tide then produced a succession of left and center-left administrations. Chapter 113 begins when those coalitions faced altered economic conditions and newly mobilized opponents.

Political succession operated through varied electoral systems. Brazil and Peru used presidential runoffs; Mexico awarded the presidency without a runoff; Argentina combined first-round thresholds with a second round; Uruguay combined stable party coalitions with runoffs. Institutional differences affect the meaning of majority, the ability of parties to form alliances, and the way results become formal authority. A valid-vote share should not be compared to turnout or to a party's share of all registered voters.

Political legitimacy is not exhausted by an election. Economic stabilization, trustworthy public services, functioning courts, constitutional safeguards, legislative compromise, and acceptance of defeat also affect citizens' views. Contemporary evidence often arrives through politicians and media with strong institutional interests. Primary legal documents, national statistical agencies, election authorities, surveys, and specialized democracy research are therefore used together but not conflated.

The narrative remains within the authoritative allocation: the 2019 Chilean uprising belongs to Chapter 110; the long history of the Colombian armed conflict and drug economy belongs elsewhere; and the January 2026 Venezuela crisis is reserved for Chapter 115. This chapter follows electoral realignment, constitutional conflict, anti-establishment leadership, polarization, and party systems across the region.

The Political Pendulum and the Erosion of Established Party Systems

Section 113.1

Understanding Latin America's Political Pendulum

Latin America's political pendulum is a metaphor, not a law of history. During the 2010s and 2020s electorates repeatedly replaced presidents and governing parties, sometimes turning toward market liberalization or conservative social agendas, at other times restoring social-democratic, nationalist, or left-wing coalitions. The image of a pendulum suggests orderly movement between two fixed positions. Actual elections produced more complicated alignments: a left-of-center incumbent could lose to a centrist or conservative challenger while another left-of-center government won reelection in a neighboring country. Presidential competition took place within constitutions, courts, party systems, and economic conditions inherited from earlier decades. A president's ideological label rarely predicts the institutional behavior of every ministry, legislator, judge, governor, or supporter. Historians should therefore begin by asking what changed—policy, leader, governing coalition, party organization, constitutional rules, or popular expectations—rather than simply announcing a leftward or rightward swing.

Political labels are useful only when their meanings are specified. On economic questions, the left commonly emphasizes redistribution, public provision, labor protection, and some measure of state direction, while the right often prioritizes markets, private property, business confidence, and fiscal restraint. But these tendencies overlap, and Latin American conservatism may also embrace state subsidies, tariffs, national industries, or an active security apparatus. A nationalist can be left or right; a candidate may advocate deregulation in economic affairs and greater coercive authority in policing. Liberalism itself denotes different traditions: constitutional civil liberties, social liberalism, and market-oriented liberalization should not be collapsed into one. Populism describes a way of presenting politics as a struggle between an authentic people and a corrupt elite; its economic program can take sharply different forms. These concepts require historical comparison rather than universal labels.

The region's earlier democratic transitions made such competition possible, although unevenly. Military regimes relinquished authority in much of South America during the 1980s, Central American peace settlements reshaped electoral politics, and Mexico ended the Institutional Revolutionary Party's long presidential monopoly in 2000. Competitive elections did not automatically create equal access to public services, effective courts, or parties trusted by citizens. Campaigns asked voters not only to choose policies but to decide whether the institutions that had mediated their grievances were worth preserving. Established parties sometimes survived by renewing their coalitions; in other settings, organizations collapsed and political entrepreneurs occupied the space. Democratic turnover could therefore express both vitality and alienation. A strong opposition capable of winning office is a democratic asset, while widespread distrust that makes any electoral loser reject institutions can be a danger.

For analysis, separate four levels. The first is electoral alternation: a change in governing party or coalition. The second is policy direction, which can move without an electoral turnover when ministers or legislative partners change. The third is institutional quality: whether courts, legislatures, electoral authorities, journalists, and citizens can constrain power. The fourth is democratic legitimacy: whether people regard procedures and outcomes as acceptable even when their preferred party loses. None can be inferred reliably from the other three. Mexico in 2024 continued the governing Morena project while electing its first woman president; Brazil's elections in 2022 and 2026 produced intense competition despite continuity in constitutional electoral machinery. Peru changed presidents repeatedly without the predictability of regular party alternation. These examples expose the metaphor's limits and establish the questions pursued throughout the chapter.

Finally, geography and social differences matter. The same national vote combines metropolitan regions, export provinces, Indigenous territories, industrial centers, rural municipalities, and migrant households with contrasting interests. Women and men, younger and older voters, and formal and informal workers are not unified blocs. Electoral maps indicate territorial patterns but rarely reveal a single motivation. A district may vote against a government because of crime, inflation, a local patronage dispute, or opposition to its cultural agenda. A serious history must connect national transformations to these varied experiences, preserve the distinction between certified results and interpretation, and resist assuming that a single political story explains an entire continent. The pendulum is therefore a starting question, not the chapter's conclusion.

Historical Vocabulary

Electoral alternation and democratic consolidation

Electoral alternation means a governing coalition is replaced through an election. Democratic consolidation adds acceptance of results, constitutional restraint, opposition rights, and institutions that endure beyond one leader. A political pendulum is an interpretive metaphor, not an indicator of either condition.

Section 113.2

The End of the Commodity Boom and the Crisis of Governing Legitimacy

The political realignments of the 2010s followed a long period when high prices for oil, metals, soy, and other exports had increased foreign-exchange earnings and government revenues. The early twenty-first-century commodity expansion helped several governments finance transfers, infrastructure, wage gains, and broader access to education and consumption. Its benefits were unevenly distributed and depended on export structure, institutions, and tax policy, but it shaped expectations across much of South America. Citizens had experienced improvements that could begin to feel permanent. When commodity prices fell after their early-2010s peaks and regional growth slowed, governments confronted a difficult distributional question: how to maintain commitments built under favorable external conditions. The slowdown did not affect every country identically; Mexico's manufacturing integration with North America and Central America's remittance economies followed different paths from Andean mineral exporters.

Brazil's mid-decade recession is central to the story. Falling confidence, fiscal pressures, investment contraction, and political conflict coincided with a severe downturn in 2015–2016. Citizens associated hardship with Dilma Rousseff's government, but disagreement over causation was intense. Critics of the government emphasized fiscal interventions, public-bank lending, administered prices, and managerial errors. Others emphasized the international shock, anti-inflation policies, legislative obstruction, and the destabilizing effects of investigation and impeachment. A historical chapter should consider the combination rather than present recession as a verdict on one ideology. In Argentina repeated exchange-rate crises, capital flight, inflation, and debt renegotiation crossed the administrations of different parties. Governments alternated between heterodox controls and market-oriented stabilization without escaping the underlying problems of currency credibility and fiscal constraint.

Bolivia and Ecuador faced distinctive dilemmas. Bolivia's gas revenues and redistribution had underwritten the political longevity of the Movement toward Socialism; falling reserves, foreign-exchange shortages, and domestic leadership disputes eventually weakened its position. Ecuador's dollarized economy limited monetary options, while oil receipts, borrowing, public employment, and fiscal adjustment generated their own conflicts. In Chile, a relatively strong macroeconomic record coexisted with discontent over pensions, education, medical expenses, and unequal access to opportunity. That combination mattered to the protests examined in Chapter 110 but also to electoral realignment. Voters do not judge government only by GDP. They judge whether employment is secure, rents are payable, public transport functions, savings retain value, and children can find credible opportunities.

The distinction between objective indicators and political perceptions is essential. Economic growth measures total output, not the distribution of its gains or the affordability of household necessities. Falling annual inflation still means prices usually continue rising, only more slowly. A recovery in investment may precede improvements in wages or poverty. Governments may claim successful stabilization before households feel relief; critics may emphasize real losses even after aggregate indicators turn upward. Historical analysis should specify whether a series measures annual consumer-price change, calendar-year output, employment, real earnings, or poverty as defined by a national statistical office. It should also distinguish outcomes observed after an election from factors shaping choices before the election. Otherwise the historian risks attributing votes to information voters could not yet know.

Commodity cycles were therefore important but not sufficient explanations. Corruption investigations, crime, changing cultural politics, digital campaigns, and disputes over constitutional authority altered how material grievances were interpreted. A household struggling with prices might favor stronger social assistance, or it might favor a candidate promising spending cuts and monetary discipline. Even voters facing similar economic conditions can disagree about causes and remedies. Political leaders, parties, unions, evangelical churches, business associations, and online networks supplied rival explanations for hardship. The political pendulum was not pushed mechanically by export prices: it moved through institutions and narratives that converted economic uncertainty into competing electoral choices.

A comparative classroom exercise can make these distinctions concrete. If one country experiences two percent output growth while the prices of rent, food, and transport rise faster than wages, its national accounts and household survey will tell different but compatible stories. Another country may have falling unemployment alongside rising informal work, a change that alters economic security without necessarily appearing in the headline employment rate. Students should ask which group benefits from a commodity rebound, which level of government receives the revenue, and whether spending obligations remain when exports weaken. Such questions translate abstract economic cycles into the electoral geography of industries, provinces, and neighborhoods.

Section 113.3

Corruption Scandals, Mass Protest, and Distrust of Political Elites

Corruption became one of the most powerful languages of electoral competition in Latin America. In Brazil the investigations commonly called Operation Lava Jato, beginning in 2014, exposed schemes involving state enterprises, construction companies, campaign finance, and political officials. Investigators pursued complex financial records and plea agreements, generating genuine public knowledge about illicit arrangements and the vulnerabilities of government procurement. The spectacle of prominent business leaders and politicians under investigation convinced many citizens that parties presented as ideological opponents had participated in mutually beneficial exchanges. Yet evidence of wrongdoing should not be converted into the assumption that every accused person was guilty or that all investigative methods were lawful. Different courts later overturned convictions and questioned jurisdictional and impartiality decisions in important cases.

Lula's case illustrates the distinction. Convictions connected to Lava Jato initially barred him from the 2018 election, altering the political field. In 2021 Brazil's Supreme Federal Court annulled the convictions because the Curitiba court lacked jurisdiction; it also made findings concerning the former judge's partiality in a central case. Annulment for procedural reasons is not identical to a jury declaration that every underlying factual allegation was disproven. Equally, an accusation or previously entered conviction cannot be narrated as a currently valid judgment after it has been annulled. Historians should state the sequence and the procedural basis, then examine how political actors used it. Some saw Lula's return to eligibility as restoration of legal protections; others continued to interpret Lava Jato as an important anti-corruption movement betrayed by judicial reversal.

Street politics amplified these battles. Brazil's demonstrations in 2013 began around bus fares and public services before incorporating wider resentment of political elites. Later gatherings included demands for and against impeachment, the defense of anti-corruption prosecutors, labor protections, and a range of ideological positions. In Guatemala citizen mobilization in 2015 challenged corruption in government; in Peru revelations surrounding major construction contracts contributed to successive investigations and instability. Corruption allegations also figured in Mexican, Colombian, and Argentine campaigns. But national contexts varied, and the scale of protest should not be confused with a representative national vote. A million demonstrators are historically important, yet the social composition of a crowd need not match that of the electorate.

Investigations themselves became objects of political struggle. Prosecutors may strengthen democratic accountability by bringing evidence against powerful figures; they may also overreach, leak selectively, or enter disputes about judicial competence. Media outlets can reveal wrongdoing and simultaneously intensify polarization by presenting unproven claims as settled. Courts can defend rights against politically motivated prosecutions but may lose confidence when judgments appear opaque or inconsistent. The underlying problem is not solved by assuming either that all corruption prosecutions are objective or that every prosecution of a popular leader is persecution. The historical method is to identify particular documents, dates, offenses, procedural rulings, and the evidence that did or did not survive appellate scrutiny.

Distrust of elites created openings for insurgent candidacies. Candidates promised that corruption could be ended by replacing the governing class, reordering public institutions, or giving executives greater freedom from obstructive legislatures. Such promises could attract voters from different socioeconomic and ideological groups, but they did not automatically create cleaner governance. Administrative competence, transparency, independent courts, procurement rules, party finance, and local oversight determine whether corruption opportunities persist after a government changes. The recurring paradox of the period is that voters sometimes demanded a stronger executive to defeat corrupt institutions while constitutional democracy depends on institutions capable of scrutinizing that executive. Corruption is therefore both a policy problem and a conflict over who has authority to define wrongdoing.

Historical Debate

Did corruption investigations renew accountability or destabilize democracy?

Interpretation A: prosecution exposed entrenched illegal exchanges and demonstrated that the powerful were not above the law. Interpretation B: politicized investigative practices and procedural violations altered electoral competition and damaged confidence. Both can be partly true in different cases. Compare an original charge, a later court judgment, and the sequence of elections before evaluating the general claim.

Student task: identify which evidence would establish a lawful conviction and which evidence would demonstrate improper prosecutorial conduct.

Section 113.4

Anti-Establishment Movements and the Transformation of Electoral Coalitions

Anti-establishment politics entered Latin American elections through distinct ideological doors. Jair Bolsonaro portrayed Brazil's established political class as corrupt and promised order, conservative values, and confrontation with the left. Andrés Manuel López Obrador framed Mexico's political establishment as an alliance of privilege and corruption and organized Morena around a nationalist program of social support and political renovation. Nayib Bukele challenged the postwar ARENA–FMLN division in El Salvador with a personalistic, security-centered message. Javier Milei attacked Argentina's political 'caste,' promised a smaller state, and made monetary disorder central to his explanation of national decline. Each used the language of rupture, yet their programs differed in economic policy, social values, institutional strategy, and attitudes toward international alliances. Calling all four 'outsiders' therefore identifies an electoral style, not one governing tradition.

Parties had already changed before these leaders arrived. Brazil's coalition presidentialism combined a powerful presidency with fragmented congressional representation, compelling bargaining across multiple parties. Mexico's PRI, PAN, and PRD had once organized most presidential competition; Morena aggregated former members, activists, regional brokers, and voters disillusioned with all three. Argentina's Peronism retained unions, local machines, and several factions even when it lost the presidency; Milei's La Libertad Avanza began as a comparatively thin organization that needed legislative allies. El Salvador's Nuevas Ideas grew rapidly around a leader and won sweeping legislative control. These contrasts matter because electoral victory is not identical to organizational capacity. A president with a congressional majority can legislate more easily than one dependent on ad hoc agreements; a president without local party networks must often rely on national visibility or allied governors.

Digital communications accelerated political reorganization without replacing older institutions. Bolsonaro supporters used WhatsApp networks and social media; Milei's provocative television and online performances gave his economic message a memorable form; López Obrador's daily presidential communications shaped the news cycle; Bukele used direct online messaging to cultivate an image of decisive control. But networks alone cannot supply volunteers at polling places, implement budgets, or create court judgments. They amplify existing social conflicts, reward messages that travel rapidly, and help leaders bypass gatekeepers. Their political effectiveness depends on local organization, electoral law, financing, and the credibility of the messenger. Critics also raised concerns about misinformation and intimidation, but every contentious message should be examined on its own evidence rather than labeled false because of its political origin.

Coalitions brought together voters whose motives were not identical. Evangelical voters could favor conservative positions on family policy; business owners could prioritize regulation and taxation; working-class voters might focus on crime or inflation; younger voters could be drawn to criticism of older elites. Some supporters of a candidate endorsed a broad ideological project, while others sought to punish the incumbent or prevent an opposing coalition from winning. Electoral scholars describe this as negative partisanship: voting against a feared alternative can matter as much as enthusiasm for one's own party. Campaign promises cannot therefore be read as a complete account of the preferences of everyone who cast a ballot. Electoral data tell who won, while surveys, interviews, and local histories are needed to explain why.

The chapter's first division thus establishes a cautious framework. Market cycles altered expectations; corruption investigations destabilized trusted parties; digital campaigns made new leadership styles possible; and constitutional rules shaped which strategies could turn popularity into governmental authority. But the outcomes were not predetermined. Some newer movements became governing institutions, some older parties recovered, and some presidents attempted to concentrate power in the name of reform. Subsequent divisions examine these choices in Brazil, Argentina, Mexico, and several other democracies, continually distinguishing an electoral mandate from permission to suspend constitutional constraints.

Multiple Perspectives

Three views of a new political outsider

A disaffected voter may see an outsider as a route to responsiveness. A party organizer may fear the loss of stable representation. A public administrator may welcome policy direction but need predictable budgets and legally defined authority. These are reconstructed analytical standpoints, not quotations from named people.

Brazil: Impeachment, Bolsonaro, Lula, and Democratic Confrontation

Section 113.5

Brazil's Political Crisis: Rousseff, Impeachment, and the Collapse of Consensus

Dilma Rousseff entered her second presidential term in 2015 under conditions very different from those that had helped sustain earlier Workers' Party administrations. The economy was contracting, the coalition in Congress was unstable, and corruption investigations were implicating business and political elites. The protests that began in 2013 over urban transport had already revealed dissatisfaction with public services and representative institutions. By 2015 street demonstrations were increasingly polarized between those demanding Rousseff's removal and those defending the electoral mandate against what they considered an opportunistic congressional campaign. The dispute took place within Brazil's constitutional framework of presidential impeachment, but it was intensely political because opposition politicians and former coalition partners stood to benefit from the president's departure. Separating the legality of impeachment from its political motivation is central to evaluating the period.

The formal accusations concerned budgetary maneuvers and decrees, including delayed Treasury transfers associated with agricultural credit programs, rather than a criminal conviction for personal enrichment. The Chamber of Deputies authorized the proceeding; the Senate suspended Rousseff in May 2016 and removed her on August 31 by 61 votes to 20. Vice President Michel Temer, a former ally whose party had broken with the government, succeeded her. Supporters of impeachment argued that constitutionally specified fiscal offenses required accountability and that Congress and the Senate followed prescribed procedures. Critics argued that the charges were disproportionate to practices tolerated under other administrations and that the process amounted to a parliamentary displacement of an elected president. A historical account can state that the removal occurred under constitutional procedures while acknowledging the serious scholarly dispute about its fairness and political meaning.

Lava Jato deepened the legitimacy crisis, exposing corrupt contracting and political financing while creating controversies about prosecutorial conduct and judicial neutrality. The case against former president Lula was particularly consequential because his imprisonment and ineligibility in 2018 excluded a leading opposition figure from that election. Years later the Supreme Federal Court annulled the Curitiba convictions, restoring his political eligibility. These facts must be arranged chronologically rather than interpreted backward as though the 2021 judicial decisions were already known to 2018 voters. The same investigations raised questions about how independent judicial institutions should operate when they can alter the electoral field. Some citizens believed prosecutors had courageously challenged an entrenched order; others saw legal procedure being used to settle political conflicts.

Temer's government pursued fiscal restraint and labor-policy changes, but struggled with legitimacy and corruption allegations of its own. The recession and polarization weakened older party attachments and made a candidate outside the established governing coalitions more attractive. Yet the Workers' Party still retained a substantial electorate, and the Brazilian Democratic Movement, center-right parties, governors, and local networks remained influential. The crisis did not erase Brazil's complex political system; it reorganized the alliances through which presidents needed to govern. It also showed why the outcome of impeachment cannot be explained through a single ideological axis. Institutional conflict, economic deterioration, allegations of corruption, party defection, and public protest operated simultaneously.

The events of 2013–2016 changed the terms of later debate. An argument about fiscal rules became a contest over the legitimacy of presidential mandates; investigations of bribery became a wider judgment on the political class; and citizens increasingly encountered sharply different descriptions of the same constitutional events. The historical challenge is to hold separate the Senate's documented vote, the accusations presented in legal proceedings, the economic circumstances that weakened the government, and subsequent evaluations of the process. The lessons carry into 2018, when Bolsonaro turned anti-establishment anger into an electoral coalition, and into the later confrontations between presidents, courts, and Congress. Democratic institutions acted, but whether their actions strengthened trust remained contested.

The impeachment also revealed competing notions of political accountability. In parliamentary systems a government may fall because it loses legislative confidence; Brazil's presidential system requires specific constitutional procedures for removal. Advocates of removal argued that the fiscal offenses justified the exceptional mechanism, while critics held that using such a mechanism during political collapse transformed policy disputes into a removal campaign. The conceptual difference matters for later comparisons with Peru, where institutional rules gave Congress repeated opportunities to challenge presidents. Students should not use 'impeachment,' 'resignation,' 'coup,' and 'vote of no confidence' interchangeably: each carries different legal predicates and political consequences.

Section 113.6

Jair Bolsonaro, Conservative Mobilization, and the Presidency, 2018–2022

Jair Bolsonaro's 2018 presidential victory emerged from the breakdown of confidence in established governing parties. A longtime legislator and former army captain, he portrayed himself as an opponent of corruption, socialism, crime, and political correctness. He promised moral renewal and stronger public security while his economic team advocated market-oriented reforms. His coalition incorporated evangelical and conservative religious constituencies, supporters of tougher policing, parts of the business community, anti-Workers' Party voters, and people dissatisfied with existing elites. That coalition was heterogeneous. Many voters strongly supported his rhetoric; others chose him because they distrusted his opponent or wanted a decisive break with the period of recession and investigation. A serious electoral history must distinguish coalition breadth from ideological unanimity.

The presidency combined institutional continuity with recurrent confrontation. Bolsonaro governed under the 1988 Constitution, required congressional support for legislation, and faced an active Supreme Federal Court. But his public remarks and political allies frequently challenged journalists, judicial authorities, civil-society groups, and opponents. Tensions rose over environmental enforcement, Indigenous rights, the use of security forces, and the political role of military officers. The administration's relationship with Congress evolved from confrontational outsider politics toward reliance on established centrist parties to enact measures and protect the presidency. This shift demonstrates how an anti-establishment candidate can become dependent on the institutions and bargaining practices he once condemned. Public speech is important evidence, but the historian must also examine budgets, appointments, enacted laws, and decisions actually implemented.

Public security supplied both an electoral promise and a point of debate. Supporters credited Bolsonaro with endorsing police and confronting criminal violence; critics warned that permissive firearms policy and rhetoric approving coercion weakened accountability. Environmental disputes similarly involved competing claims about agricultural production, territorial development, enforcement against illegal deforestation, and protection of Indigenous communities. Neither subject can be understood only through presidential slogans. Governors, prosecutors, federal agencies, local economic actors, and courts influenced practical outcomes. Chapter 114 addresses Amazonian political economy in depth; here the question is how such disputes reorganized political coalitions and contributed to perceptions of executive authority.

The COVID-19 pandemic transformed the government's political environment. Bolsonaro criticized some restrictions and contested the public-health policies of governors and medical authorities, while Brazil suffered a large death toll. Congressional investigators examined procurement and administrative decisions, and opponents accused the president of minimizing the danger. Supporters argued that lockdowns damaged livelihoods and that different branches of government had independent responsibility for public health. This chapter does not duplicate the pandemic history assigned to Chapter 114. It asks how the crisis affected confidence in presidential leadership, the boundaries of federal authority, and the capacity of opposing political camps to agree about evidence. Health statistics, like crime statistics, became symbols in arguments over responsibility.

Bolsonaro's relationship with voting institutions increasingly concerned the reliability of electronic ballots, even though election administrators repeatedly defended the system and no substantiated evidence demonstrated the widespread fraud alleged by his political movement. Criticism of electoral rules is a legitimate part of democratic debate; unsupported claims that defeat can only arise from fraud have a different effect, undermining the possibility of accepting a certified result. By the 2022 campaign the contest was not simply about economic policy or cultural conservatism. It also tested public confidence in courts, election management, and peaceful transfer of authority. Bolsonaro's years thus joined an innovative electoral coalition to an increasingly adversarial relationship with constitutional referees.

The federal character of Brazil's politics deserves special emphasis. State governors, municipal authorities, the Senate, lower house, Supreme Court, public prosecutors, and federal audit institutions could pursue different approaches to the same problem. A national president could be celebrated or blamed for events over which another level of government exercised important practical power. This was visible during the pandemic and in public security, and it remains relevant when comparing presidential approval across states. Electoral historians therefore need subnational evidence rather than treating the presidency as the sole center of Brazilian political authority.

Everyday Life

A municipal health worker, a rural producer, and an urban household might encounter different policies even during the same presidency. State budgets, local policing, national economic decisions, and court rulings combine in daily experience. Section 113.7

Lula's Return, the 2022 Election, and the January 2023 Institutional Crisis

Lula's return to national politics followed judicial reversals of the convictions that had kept him from the 2018 election. In 2021 the Supreme Federal Court annulled the Curitiba judgments because the court had lacked jurisdiction; a separate ruling addressed the former judge's partiality. These decisions restored Lula's eligibility without requiring historians to assume that every political accusation surrounding Lava Jato was either proven or entirely fictitious. The former president assembled a broad alliance, selecting Geraldo Alckmin, a onetime political rival, as running mate. The coalition signaled a strategy of defending democratic institutions while promising social inclusion and a more predictable style of government. It also drew support from voters whose principal concern was defeating Bolsonaro, not necessarily returning to every policy of the first Lula administrations.

Brazil's October 30, 2022 runoff was exceptionally close. According to the Superior Electoral Court's completed count, Lula received 60,345,999 valid votes, or 50.90%, against Bolsonaro's 58,206,354, or 49.10%. The result demonstrated continuing electoral competition and a highly divided political landscape. Election administration, observers, and the courts rejected allegations that the national outcome had been invalidated by systemic electronic-voting fraud. Bolsonaro did not deliver a conventional concession speech, and some of his supporters established encampments and demanded military intervention. Other supporters accepted the outcome, and political parties continued to participate in constitutional institutions. The historical record must avoid assigning the behavior of the most radical faction to every person who voted for the defeated candidate.

On January 8, 2023, crowds invaded and damaged the presidential palace, Congress, and Supreme Federal Court buildings in Brasília. The attacks shocked the country because they targeted the material symbols of all three federal branches shortly after Lula's inauguration. Investigators pursued participants, alleged organizers, and possible officials who had failed to prevent the assault. The government, legislature, and judiciary coordinated a response while debating security accountability and legal process. Legal responsibility for particular individuals had to be established in court; affiliation with a political movement alone was not evidence of participation in the assault. The event nonetheless raised a fundamental question: could elections remain authoritative if groups sought to reverse them through intimidation or force?

Investigations extended to allegations of a broader attempted-coup project after the 2022 election. On September 11, 2025, a panel of Brazil's Supreme Federal Court convicted Bolsonaro and imposed a 27-year-and-three-month sentence in the attempted-coup proceedings. The ruling constituted a judicial finding, not merely an accusation; defense objections, appeals, and procedural history remain part of any fair account. Bolsonaro and allies rejected the prosecution as political, while the court and its supporters characterized it as the defense of constitutional order. The consequences extended beyond Bolsonaro's personal legal position, shaping the organization of the Brazilian right, candidacy choices, and disagreements over judicial power. The Supreme Court's authority was strengthened in some citizens' eyes and contested in others'.

The institutional confrontation should not obscure Lula's ordinary governing challenges. He faced a divided Congress, difficult fiscal choices, social expectations, diplomatic disagreements, and demands from allied movements. Restoring a previous president to power did not restore the political conditions of an earlier commodity boom. Nor did judicial prosecution of opponents eliminate the electorate that had supported them. The years 2022–2025 reveal a central theme: democratic institutions can successfully certify an election and prosecute attacks on constitutional government while public trust in those institutions remains sharply polarized. This tension set the stage for the 2026 election, which provided another test of alternation, continuity, and institutional acceptance.

Section 113.8

Brazil's 2026 Election and the Persistence of Political Polarization

Lula's third presidential administration combined social-policy continuity with efforts to rebuild relations with Congress, state governments, international partners, and investors. His government faced familiar tensions: the desire to finance public services alongside pressure for fiscal credibility, support for environmental enforcement alongside development interests, and diplomatic autonomy alongside trading relationships. A coalition presidency required negotiation among parties whose legislative priorities did not precisely match the Workers' Party's. Economic performance mattered, but it did not determine electoral outcomes in isolation. Brazil's statistical office reported real GDP growth of 2.3% in 2025; this national figure says little by itself about household perceptions of prices, security, or opportunity. Opponents questioned the government's priorities, while supporters emphasized social commitments and state capacity.

The political right did not disappear after Bolsonaro's defeat or conviction. His movement remained embedded in religious organizations, social networks, regional parties, and electoral coalitions. The question of succession within the movement became especially important while the former president faced legal restrictions. His son Flávio Bolsonaro emerged as the principal presidential challenger in 2026, allowing the movement to retain part of its political identity while offering a different candidate. The election therefore compared both governing performance and competing conceptions of institutional authority. Voters had to assess economic management, public safety, social policy, relations with courts, and the legacy of the previous electoral conflict. Describing the race simply as a referendum on Lula would leave out the organized political strength of his opponent.

The October 25, 2026 runoff ended with Lula reelected. The Superior Electoral Court reported 64,256,232 votes for Lula, 51.79% of valid votes, and 59,826,041 for Flávio Bolsonaro, 48.21%. The margin of 3.58 percentage points was wider than Lula's 2022 runoff margin but still indicated an electorate divided into large, competitive political camps. These are official valid-vote shares, not percentages of all registered voters. Students comparing results across years should identify turnout, blank and spoiled ballots, and changes in population before drawing conclusions about shifts in support. Lula was reelected to a fourth nonconsecutive presidential term; as of the chapter's November 16 historical cutoff the new term had not yet begun, so his 2027 program could not be evaluated as an accomplished policy record.

The result also highlighted a distinction between winning an election and resolving an institutional dispute. Supporters of the defeated candidate could remain deeply dissatisfied with the judiciary and electoral administration. Some political figures continued challenging the fairness of proceedings against Bolsonaro, while defenders of the court argued that enforcing constitutional law was necessary precisely when the accused possessed a large public following. The electoral court's count resolved who had won the presidency; it did not settle whether all voters trusted the process. Nor did it produce a single governing coalition in a fragmented Congress. Lula's victory did not transform Brazil into a unified left-wing polity, just as Bolsonaro's 2018 victory had not made Brazilian politics uniformly conservative.

For historical comparison, 2016 brought Rousseff's impeachment, 2018 brought Bolsonaro's rise, 2022 restored Lula through a close election, and 2026 renewed his mandate against a successor candidate rooted in the Bolsonaro political family. Each transition was shaped by changing circumstances and organized institutions. The series does not resemble a pendulum swinging predictably between two endpoints. The left returned with alliances different from its earlier ones; the right adapted to leadership restrictions; and both operated through electoral institutions that could be fiercely contested yet remain operative. The 2026 vote is best read as further evidence of competitive political polarization rather than a final resolution of the debates opened during Brazil's crisis a decade earlier.

A second source of interpretive caution concerns the political calendar. The election was held in October 2026, but the incumbent's next term was scheduled to start in January 2027. Headlines announcing reelection are therefore records of a completed vote, not evidence that a new four-year governing program had already taken effect. This may appear a minor distinction, yet textbook histories frequently compress election, certification, inauguration, and implementation into a single event. A chronology that separates them teaches readers how institutions turn popular votes into executive authority.

Argentina and Mexico: Two Contrasting Political Transformations

Section 113.9

Argentina's Political Alternation: Kirchnerism, Macri, and the Fernández Government

Argentina's political realignment took place inside the unusually durable but internally diverse tradition of Peronism. Néstor Kirchner and Cristina Fernández de Kirchner, in office during much of 2003–2015, combined social policy, state intervention, national industrial priorities, and conflicts with agricultural producers, creditors, and some media organizations. Their supporters emphasized recovery from the 2001 crisis and programs that expanded social protections; critics emphasized inflation, currency controls, concentrated executive decision-making, and corruption allegations. Peronism cannot be reduced to either one economic program or a single party faction. Its unions, provincial governors, neighborhood networks, and ideological currents enabled it to govern and remain competitive even when national leadership changed. The opposition gradually assembled coalitions around demands for market normalization and stronger institutional checks.

Mauricio Macri won the presidency in 2015, signaling an important alternation away from Kirchnerism. His administration relaxed some currency controls, sought financing abroad, and pursued a more market-oriented economic strategy. Yet efforts to reduce inflation and restore confidence collided with external shocks, capital flight, rising debt, and the political costs of austerity. In 2018 Argentina negotiated a large stand-by arrangement with the International Monetary Fund. Supporters viewed borrowing as emergency stabilization amid lost market access; critics argued that the arrangement compounded vulnerability and shifted costs onto households. The episode showed the difficulty of addressing macroeconomic imbalances in a society where distrust of government financial promises had deep historical roots. Macri's defeat in 2019 did not end those pressures.

Alberto Fernández, with Cristina Fernández de Kirchner as vice president, won the 2019 election as the candidate of a Peronist coalition. His presidency confronted a deep recession and the COVID-19 pandemic, followed by continuing monetary instability, price increases, and internal coalition disputes. Decisions over lockdowns, social assistance, debt restructuring, and monetary finance generated competing judgments. Inflation remained a central feature of everyday life: workers could receive nominal wage increases while losing purchasing power; businesses struggled with input prices and exchange restrictions; and household saving increasingly depended on access to stable currency or hard assets. Political conflict inside Peronism reduced the coherence of economic policy and weakened confidence that established leaders could provide a durable remedy.

The 2023 election therefore arose from cumulative disappointment with both a market-oriented and a Peronist administration. That does not prove the two governments had identical policies, nor does it imply that every economic setback originated in the same way. It indicates that neither coalition succeeded in persuading enough voters that stabilization and social protection could be reconciled. Javier Milei entered the contest promising a much sharper break. His success depended not simply on ideology but on the social experience of repeated disappointment and on a party system in which the conventional center-right opposition also failed to monopolize the anti-government vote. The context matters because electoral rebellion gains meaning from the alternatives available at the time.

Argentina also illustrates the role of federal geography. Provinces depend on diverse combinations of agricultural exports, manufacturing, public employment, energy, tourism, and transfers from the national state. Governors and local Peronist machines may remain powerful while their national presidential candidate loses. Buenos Aires Province has a different political and socioeconomic profile from parts of the agricultural interior or Patagonia. The country's political shifts are therefore not only the story of presidential speeches in the capital. They are contests over fiscal transfers, currency, social assistance, public contracts, and the relationship between national and provincial authority. These institutional arrangements help explain how Peronism could lose to an anti-establishment presidential candidate without disappearing as a major political movement.

A further continuity was the political importance of organized labor. Argentine unions retain considerable capacity to negotiate wages, bargain over employment rules, and mobilize against austerity. Employers, informal workers, pensioners, and public employees experience inflation and regulation differently, and collective bargaining can shield some groups better than others. The social coalition behind a president is therefore not simply the group of voters who favored an abstract monetary theory. Organized interests shape how reform is implemented and how the political costs of adjustment are distributed long after the campaign has ended.

Historical Vocabulary

Peronism and Kirchnerism

Peronism is a broad Argentine political tradition associated with Juan and Eva Perón, organized labor, social citizenship, and competing internal policy currents. Kirchnerism is a more specific current identified with Néstor and Cristina Kirchner. Neither should be treated as a timeless uniform doctrine.

Section 113.10

Javier Milei: Libertarian Politics, Economic Reform, and Institutional Conflict

Javier Milei's rise fused economic argument with an insurgent political style. An economist and media personality, he presented inflation as evidence that the Argentine political establishment had exhausted its credibility. He criticized central-bank financing, state regulation, public spending, and entrenched political privileges, and promoted a libertarian program more radical than earlier center-right initiatives. His repeated attacks on the political 'caste' appealed to voters who distrusted both Peronism and established opposition parties. In the November 2023 runoff he defeated Sergio Massa with approximately 55.65% of the vote and took office December 10. The result was a major institutional achievement for an organization with a comparatively limited history and local party structure. Winning the presidency, however, was only the beginning of a struggle to pass legislation and sustain a governing coalition.

The new administration announced fiscal austerity, reductions in subsidies and public employment, deregulation, and attempts to stabilize the currency and prices. These policies generated a recognizable distributional conflict. Fiscal retrenchment could restore confidence in public accounts, supporters argued, while price adjustments and cuts to public services transferred immediate costs to retirees, low-income households, and public-sector workers, critics responded. A devaluation may improve some relative prices while increasing import costs; a reduced fiscal deficit is not equivalent to an immediate rise in real wages. Students must separate the nominal fiscal balance, inflation, household purchasing power, output, unemployment, and poverty. Different measures changed at different speeds, allowing government and opposition to emphasize facts that appeared contradictory but referred to different dimensions of economic life.

Argentina's national statistics office reported annual consumer-price inflation of 31.5% for calendar 2025. That represented a considerable decline from the triple-digit annual inflation recorded earlier in the stabilization period. Yet it did not mean that prices returned to their earlier levels or that all households recovered losses incurred during the adjustment. A percentage decline in inflation measures a slower rate of price growth, not deflation. Claims about poverty also depend on survey reference periods, methodology, and whether temporary losses preceded recoveries. A rigorous chapter should therefore cite the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Censos rather than rely only on executive announcements, and should avoid projecting a short-term trend into a settled judgment about long-term prosperity.

Milei faced a Congress in which La Libertad Avanza initially lacked a secure majority. Decrees, omnibus reform bills, presidential vetoes, and negotiations with legislators and provincial governors therefore became central political instruments. Unions, university communities, pensioners, and public-sector organizations protested aspects of the program. Supporters considered conflict evidence that necessary reforms threatened entrenched interests; opponents argued that the executive marginalized deliberation and imposed unfair social burdens. These competing arguments must be measured against actual laws, judicial decisions, and budgetary outcomes rather than summarized as a struggle between courageous reform and obstruction. Constitutional authority remained divided among the presidency, Congress, federal courts, and provinces, even when the president's political rhetoric suggested a direct confrontation between a mandate and a discredited establishment.

The October 26, 2025 legislative elections strengthened La Libertad Avanza's position and provided an important test of the durability of its support. Provisional official electoral returns put the alliance at approximately 40.9% in the relevant national lower-house aggregate, although comparability depends on which races and provinces are included. The result improved the president's leverage in Congress but did not eliminate the need for coalition-building. Nor should it be read as a full referendum on every part of the economic program. Voters could approve stabilization while opposing specific cuts, prefer the president to the alternatives, or seek to constrain rather than remove him. As of November 2026, the historic question was whether a movement founded on denunciation of the political system could develop the party organization, administrative capacity, and institutional restraint needed for durable government.

Section 113.11

Mexico's Political Transformation: López Obrador and the Rise of Morena

Mexico's national realignment developed from a very different historical tradition. For decades the Institutional Revolutionary Party dominated the presidency and much of the country's organized political life. Alternation in 2000 brought the National Action Party to the presidency, and subsequent competition involved the PRI, PAN, and Party of the Democratic Revolution. Andrés Manuel López Obrador emerged from this system, served as head of government of Mexico City, and became a recurring presidential contender. After organizing Morena, he won the 2018 election by a decisive margin and presented the result as the beginning of a Fourth Transformation, invoking Mexico's independence, nineteenth-century liberal reform, and revolution. The historical analogy provided a language of national renewal and an argument that earlier party alternations had failed to dismantle entrenched privileges.

López Obrador's political style was personalistic and direct, but his program was not equivalent to the market libertarianism of Milei. He emphasized public transfers, minimum wages, state energy enterprises, infrastructure, and an anti-corruption promise directed against an establishment he characterized as neoliberal. His government expanded pension benefits and scholarship programs while defending major infrastructure projects and the fiscal and political role of the state. Supporters credited him with bringing previously marginalized voters into national politics and making government more attentive to inequality. Critics questioned environmental effects, administrative centralization, civilian oversight, public-security strategy, and the removal or weakening of autonomous agencies. Assessing those claims requires attention to the content of policies, their implementation, and the institutions empowered or weakened by reform.

Morena became more than a presidential vehicle. It assembled left-wing activists, former politicians from other parties, regional organizations, and interest groups seeking access to governing coalitions. Its electoral success in congressional and state contests gave it considerable leverage over institutional reform. But heterogeneity also created tensions among career politicians, ideological movements, and state-level actors. National majorities did not eliminate gubernatorial competition or the need to implement policy across a federal republic. The decline of the PRD, changes within the PRI, and PAN's opposition alliances show how traditional party brands could weaken even as their local networks or personnel migrated into new formations. The Mexican case is thus both a story of charismatic leadership and one of party reassembly.

Relations with electoral authorities and the judiciary were central. Mexico's National Electoral Institute had developed out of a long struggle to establish independent electoral administration after one-party predominance. López Obrador and supporters argued that established institutions had become expensive, unaccountable, and in some instances politically biased. Opponents viewed the electoral institute and autonomous courts as safeguards against renewed concentration of power. These arguments were not purely rhetorical: proposals affected appointments, budgets, legal procedures, and the balance among elected institutions. Courts sometimes blocked initiatives, demonstrating their capacity to constrain an elected executive; governing-party supporters responded that unelected judges were frustrating a democratic mandate. Both sides invoked democracy but emphasized different institutions and risks.

The transition to Claudia Sheinbaum in 2024 tested whether Morena could extend its program beyond its founder. López Obrador remained influential, yet the party had to manage candidate selection, local alliances, public expectations, and competing policy priorities. The continuity of the governing organization is a reminder that not every major election reflects a swing to the opposite ideological pole. Mexico's transformation involved the creation of a new dominant coalition amid competitive elections and ongoing institutional conflict. The historian should therefore examine both the popular legitimacy of Morena's victories and whether constitutional checks and independent oversight remained effective. These questions became sharper when Mexico changed the method for selecting judges and held judicial elections in 2025.

Section 113.12

Claudia Sheinbaum, Mexico's 2024 Election, and the Judiciary through 2026

Claudia Sheinbaum's victory in June 2024 marked a historic change in representation and a continuation of Morena's governing coalition. A scientist by training and former head of Mexico City's government, she campaigned with support for the Fourth Transformation while promising her own administrative priorities. Mexico's federal electoral tribunal certified 35,924,519 votes for Sheinbaum, 59.76% of votes cast as described in its final count. She became the first woman elected to the Mexican presidency and entered office on October 1, 2024. Her result showed the governing party's capacity to transfer power to a new leader within competitive electoral procedures. But the breadth of a presidential majority cannot be treated as blanket endorsement of every later measure, particularly constitutional revisions whose specific consequences many voters may not have anticipated.

The September 15, 2024 constitutional reform of the federal judiciary transformed arrangements for the selection of judges, including the Supreme Court. Supporters argued that popular election would break entrenched judicial privileges, reduce nepotism, and increase democratic accountability. Critics argued that judges who campaign for votes may become dependent on political organizations or financing networks, potentially compromising judicial independence and technical competence. These are substantive constitutional disagreements, not merely differences in campaign style. The reform also altered disciplinary and administrative structures, raising questions about who can investigate judges and how legal competence is evaluated. The official text in the Diario Oficial de la Federación establishes the enacted rules; public commentary cannot substitute for reading it.

Mexico held extraordinary federal judicial elections on June 1, 2025. Reported participation was low compared with presidential contests: the electoral authority initially estimated turnout in a range of 12.57%–13.32%, with later calculations varying depending on the offices counted. A historian should not substitute an estimate from election night for a single definitive rate without stating the denominator and stage of reporting. Low turnout can indicate limited public familiarity, election complexity, weak mobilization, or public disagreement; the number alone cannot establish a single cause. Reform advocates emphasized that judges now had electoral authorization; critics questioned the independence and representativeness of candidates selected under the new system. The institutional effects required observation over time, not just counting ballots on one day.

Sheinbaum also faced the ordinary challenges of government: public security, fiscal priorities, energy policy, social provision, relations with the United States, and pressure from different factions within Morena. Her own leadership style differed from López Obrador's in expertise and administrative emphasis, but constitutional reform and party continuity tied their administrations together. Opposition parties faced the strategic dilemma of challenging particular policy decisions while rebuilding their credibility after repeated national defeats. Journalists and civil-society organizations raised questions about judicial independence and access to public information; government supporters argued that reforms ended an era of unaccountable privilege. Historical analysis should present the legal provisions, the evidence of implementation, and the actual institutional results that were observable by November 2026.

The Mexican case complicates the political-pendulum metaphor in two ways. First, a party could retain the presidency by winning a larger popular mandate without an ideological reversal. Second, major institutional change could take place through constitutionally specified amendment procedures and elections rather than through an obvious interruption of electoral competition. The harder evaluative question is whether those changes preserved meaningful independence for institutions that are supposed to scrutinize elected officials. Mexico thus provides a different comparison with Brazil's court-centered battles, Argentina's decree-and-congress struggles, and Peru's confrontation between legislature and executive. The same language of democracy can describe popular authorization, accountability, and constitutional limits, yet these values may come into tension when one institution claims a unique mandate to represent the public.

A useful distinction is between a judicial election and the selection of executive or legislative officials. Judges ordinarily decide individual disputes according to evidence and law, including cases involving unpopular people or policies. If elected judges must campaign on policy promises, voters may expect positions that conflict with the judicial obligation to remain impartial. Advocates of direct election argue that existing appointments already permit political bargaining outside public scrutiny. The central institutional inquiry is therefore how either system screens qualifications, prevents conflicts of interest, and enables lawful review of erroneous judgments.

Section 113.13

Chile: From Gabriel Boric to José Antonio Kast

Chile's political transition from Gabriel Boric to José Antonio Kast brought into view the distance between mobilization for institutional change and durable electoral support for a particular reform program. Boric rose from student activism into parliamentary politics, then won the December 2021 presidential election as a candidate of a broad left coalition. His campaign emphasized social rights, redistribution, and a generational break from traditional politics. But the presidency inherited a divided legislature, persistent demands for public safety, migration pressures, an uncertain economic environment, and disagreements within the coalition. The two constitutional projects examined in Chapter 110 further complicated the governing agenda. The rejection of the convention's draft in September 2022 and the second proposal in December 2023 showed that majority support for changing the constitution did not translate into consensus over either text.

Boric's government had to adjust from movement leadership to executive bargaining. Negotiated reforms, including changes to pension arrangements, illustrated the compromises required when public demands exceeded the political coalition able to enact legislation. Some supporters believed the government had retreated from ambitious promises; opponents argued that the original program had not adequately answered concerns about fiscal discipline and security. The administration faced the problem common to post-protest governments: electoral legitimacy creates responsibility for decisions affecting citizens who did not belong to the movement that helped bring leaders to power. Ministers had to work with institutions, regional governments, and business and labor interests whose priorities diverged. The balance between transformation and practical governing consequently became a campaign issue in its own right.

Kast emerged as a conservative contender emphasizing public order, border enforcement, economic confidence, and a distinct social outlook. In the December 14, 2025 runoff, Chilean electoral results gave him approximately 58.17% of valid votes against Jeannette Jara's 41.83%. The Electoral Qualification Tribunal proclaimed him president-elect on January 5, 2026, and the new term began March 11. These dates matter: a proclaimed president-elect did not yet exercise the office before inauguration. The result represented a clear victory over the governing political camp, but did not necessarily mean voters had collectively renounced demands for fair pensions, accessible healthcare, or greater dignity. As with Chile's earlier constitutional votes, different issues could lead the same citizens to different choices across elections.

The Kast administration's initial period in 2026 became a test of converting a security- and stability-centered campaign into policy. A president can control appointments and propose legislation but must operate within Chile's constitutional, judicial, and parliamentary institutions. The possibility of student protest, labor opposition, or human-rights litigation does not by itself establish that democracy has broken down; it reveals plural interests and checks that a government must manage. Conversely, winning a strong majority does not remove responsibilities to minorities or permit arbitrary treatment of dissent. The chapter should distinguish policies enacted in the months after March from campaign proposals and from the expectations expressed by supporters and critics. Its cutoff in November 2026 is too early to assign a settled historical outcome to the presidency.

Chile offers a revealing comparison with Brazil and Mexico. Its democratic institutions managed multiple referendums and a peaceful presidential transition, but repeated elections exposed disagreement about the content of economic and constitutional change. Student leaders helped create a president; a conservative coalition later defeated his political camp. The pattern is not simply left, then right: it includes independent voters, changing participation rules, regional differences, constitutional fatigue, and conflict over everyday security. By treating the electoral result as one point in a longer institutional history, students can avoid confusing government replacement with the disappearance of the social grievances that originally motivated protest.

Section 113.14

Colombia: Gustavo Petro, Reform Politics, and the 2026 Rightward Turn

Gustavo Petro's election in 2022 brought a former leftist opposition politician to Colombia's presidency after decades in which mainstream national governing coalitions had generally been positioned to the center or right. His coalition, the Pacto Histórico, drew support from social movements, younger urban voters, Afro-Colombian constituencies, and people seeking a different response to inequality and the continuing effects of armed conflict. Francia Márquez's vice presidency carried particular symbolic and political importance. The government proposed reforms in health care, pensions, labor relations, taxation, and land policy while developing an ambitious peace and security agenda. The central political issue was not only what Petro hoped to change but whether a divided Congress, regional power brokers, and public institutions would allow a coherent program to emerge.

Governing exposed tensions between coalition rhetoric and administrative practice. Legislators opposed parts of the proposed social reforms, and cabinet turnover created uncertainty. Petro defended ambitious restructuring as overdue redistribution and democratization; critics emphasized fiscal costs, implementation difficulties, and the danger of executive confrontation with courts or Congress. Security remained a major concern in regions affected by armed organizations, illegal economies, and incomplete implementation of earlier peace commitments. Chapter 100 studies the peace process and Chapter 112 treats contemporary criminal networks; the task here is to explain how concerns about security affected perceptions of presidential leadership and the viability of reform coalitions. Public frustration could coexist with support for social goals.

Colombia's electoral rules, party alliances, and regional political geography structured the 2026 campaign. The contest brought opposing interpretations of Petro's legacy into confrontation. Abelardo de la Espriella, a right-wing political outsider and lawyer, advocated a sharp security and political break with the outgoing administration. Iván Cepeda emerged as a leading candidate of the left, emphasizing continuity with social reform and peace priorities. The first-round election placed these candidates ahead of the wider field; the June 21 runoff was closely contested. Electoral authorities confirmed de la Espriella's victory, and Colombia's Congress administered his presidential oath on August 7, 2026. The inauguration is an established event, not a forecast. The new president's subsequent legislative success or failure, by contrast, required careful observation beyond the initial months.

The transition illustrates both alternation and continuity. Voters could replace a governing political camp through competitive elections while the judiciary, legislature, constitutional framework, and local governments persisted. The result also reflected the diversity of Colombia's electorate: cities and rural departments experienced different combinations of public insecurity, economic expectation, historical memory, and party organization. A national majority should not be represented as agreement on one causal story. Some voters might have rejected particular reforms, others prioritized security, while still others supported a candidate because of identity, coalition alliances, or opposition to Petro's political style. To explain the result, historians need certified totals, regional electoral maps, contemporary polling, and evidence about campaign issues—not merely retrospective narratives constructed by the winners.

As of November 16, 2026, de la Espriella had been in office only a few months. His inauguration established a rightward transfer of executive power but did not settle the future of health reform, regional security, peace negotiations, or Colombia's party system. The case is valuable because it complicates the assumption that electing a historically significant left-wing president necessarily establishes a durable left-wing governing era. Yet it also warns against describing electoral defeat as the disappearance of constituencies that supported reform. Colombia's democratic institutions had accommodated a major turn in 2022 and another in 2026; whether they could sustain negotiated governing compromises remained the central institutional challenge.

Colombia's peace-and-security debate further illustrates the danger of assigning national elections to one issue. In areas previously affected by armed conflict, voters could weigh the prospects of negotiation, territorial protection, roads, schools, and illicit-economy pressures together. In larger cities employment prospects, political rhetoric, taxation, and transport services could predominate. Campaign polling may rank these concerns differently depending on timing and wording. To explain de la Espriella's victory, the historian should place electoral statistics beside regional social history and avoid treating each constituency as an ideological bloc with identical motives.

Section 113.15

Peru: Presidential Instability, Congressional Power, and the 2026 Election

Peru's political instability differed fundamentally from routine left-right alternation. Its constitution, fragmented party landscape, and recurring executive–legislative conflicts produced a series of presidential removals and short administrations. The formula of 'permanent moral incapacity' used by Congress to vacate the presidency became a central source of conflict because its interpretation was politically controversial. Presidents without reliable legislative blocs confronted congressional coalitions that could block ministers, reject confidence votes, and seek removal. At the same time, allegations of corruption and poor administration repeatedly weakened public confidence. The result was neither a stable two-party system nor a simple pendulum of competing platforms, but a struggle over the distribution of authority among institutions whose legitimacy was already under question.

Pedro Castillo's narrow 2021 election was historically notable because a rural schoolteacher and union organizer defeated Keiko Fujimori, who represented a long-standing conservative electoral movement associated with her father, former president Alberto Fujimori. Castillo promised to address social and regional inequalities but lacked a stable coalition in Congress and experienced rapid cabinet turnover. In December 2022 he announced a move to dissolve Congress and establish an exceptional governing arrangement; the institutions of government rejected the announcement, and Congress removed him. Dina Boluarte, the vice president, succeeded under the constitutional line of succession. Major protests followed, especially in southern regions, and deaths during the state response became the subject of human-rights investigations. This sequence cannot be understood solely as either a normal impeachment or a straightforward coup without examining the specific constitutional acts and the protests that followed.

Boluarte's administration faced sustained legitimacy concerns, corruption allegations, and conflict between Lima-centered political institutions and communities that regarded their interests as excluded. Subsequent changes in office and legislative maneuvering did not create a dependable governing mandate. The broader pattern demonstrated the consequences of weak parties: presidents could win national votes without developing enduring legislative support, and legislators could exercise removal powers without producing coherent alternatives for governing. For everyday citizens, the effects were concrete. Policy could change as ministers rotated; regional services and investment could suffer delays; and businesses and households had difficulty predicting the state's direction. Public protest reflected both opposition to particular leaders and deeper distrust of the institutions that selected their successors.

The 2026 election provided a formally certified presidential result after an unusually contested runoff. On June 7, Keiko Fujimori faced Roberto Sánchez, a candidate associated with the left. The Peruvian electoral authority's final proclamation gave Fujimori 9,223,396 valid votes, or 50.135%, with an advantage of 49,641 votes over Sánchez according to the full count published after contested ballots were reviewed. The JNE proclaimed her election July 3; she entered office July 28. These figures must be accompanied by their denominator: the percentage refers to valid votes, not the entire population or all registered voters. Allegations of irregularity deserve examination, but the existence of controversy does not itself invalidate the official result. Students should read the relevant JNE resolution and separate disputes that were formally adjudicated from claims that remained in public debate.

Fujimori's inauguration was a change of political leadership, yet her ability to govern depended on relations with Congress, regional authorities, the judiciary, and a public still marked by protest and distrust. Her family name carried incompatible historical meanings: supporters associated the Fujimori period with security and stability, while opponents emphasized authoritarian abuses and the human-rights record of Alberto Fujimori's government. Neither historical memory was uniform. The 2026 victory did not by itself resolve Peru's structural problems of party fragmentation, the rules of presidential removal, corruption, or uneven regional representation. Peru's experience illustrates why simply tabulating successive presidents can be misleading: institutional instability may persist even when a new electoral mandate has been properly certified.

Section 113.16

Bolivia, Ecuador, Uruguay, and Costa Rica: Why No Single Pendulum Explains the Region

Bolivia's 2025 election marked the weakening of a political order long dominated by the Movement toward Socialism. The MAS had combined redistribution, Indigenous representation, state involvement in natural resources, and electoral organization, while also experiencing internal conflicts over leadership and presidential succession. Growing economic pressures, foreign-exchange scarcity, fuel shortages, and disputes among factions weakened its position. Rodrigo Paz Pereira, running with the Christian Democratic Party, defeated Jorge 'Tuto' Quiroga in the October 19 runoff. Bolivia's electoral authorities reported 54.96% of valid votes for Paz and 45.04% for Quiroga. The outcome ended a lengthy period of MAS presidential rule, but Paz's centrist positioning and pragmatic appeals resist easy classification as a simple conservative restoration. Coalition-building, economic stabilization, and relations with regional movements defined the new challenges.

Ecuador followed a different trajectory. Daniel Noboa emerged as a young center-right security-focused president after the 2023 extraordinary election and secured reelection in 2025 against Luisa González. His appeal drew on public anxiety over organized crime, disruption of prisons, ports, and neighborhoods, and dissatisfaction with earlier leaders. The National Electoral Council certified his victory after legal challenges. Chapter 112 analyzes Ecuador's criminal-security landscape, while Chapter 113 focuses on electoral legitimacy, executive authority, and the role of institutional opposition. Supporters defended extraordinary security measures as necessary for civilian safety; critics raised questions about rights, constitutional procedures, and the efficacy of militarized responses. Noboa's reelection indicated support for continued leadership under difficult circumstances, not an uncontested verdict on every security policy.

Uruguay demonstrated that a left-of-center victory could occur during a period when several other countries elected candidates from the right. Yamandú Orsi, representing the Frente Amplio, defeated the governing center-right coalition's candidate in the November 2024 runoff and assumed office March 1, 2025. Uruguay's Electoral Court recorded the official result, and the presidency changed hands through longstanding institutions. The case suggests that party systems with relatively stable organizations and established bargaining practices can accommodate alternation without the same degree of constitutional crisis seen elsewhere. Yet Uruguayan voters also disagreed on taxation, public safety, labor, social policy, and growth. Institutional stability should not be confused with an absence of political conflict.

Costa Rica's February 2026 election produced the victory of Laura Fernández Delgado, who was formally declared elected by the Supreme Electoral Tribunal and began her term May 8. Her election illustrated the rise of new alliances associated with the outgoing political project and debates over government authority, security, and institutional checks. Costa Rica's constitutional order, stronger record of civilian government, and electoral administration differed markedly from Peru's or Ecuador's. Its election therefore should not be forced into the same causal framework as an Andean security emergency. The winning coalition's appeal can be studied through its campaign proposals, legislative support, and electoral results while leaving its longer-term governing outcomes unresolved as of the historical cutoff.

Taken together these four cases defeat the idea of one simultaneous hemispheric movement. Bolivia elected a centrist challenger to a long-dominant left party; Ecuador renewed a security-centered administration; Uruguay returned a left coalition through ordinary alternation; and Costa Rica selected a new leader in a distinct institutional setting. In a single broad period, voters pursued economic stabilization, personal security, social provision, partisan renewal, and constitutional continuity in different combinations. Presidential biographies do not supply a comprehensive explanation. The comparative historian must consider party organization, electoral rules, local geography, executive-legislative relations, the economy, and public trust. The region was politically interconnected, but it was never electorally uniform.

Democracy, Institutions, Polarization, and the Political Horizon of 2026

Section 113.17

Changing Party Systems, Electoral Coalitions, and Political Representation

Parties connect voters to the state by recruiting candidates, organizing legislative agendas, negotiating interests, and taking responsibility for policy. Their apparent decline was one of the defining developments of Latin America's political realignment, but the story varied sharply across countries. In Mexico, Morena transformed from an insurgent movement into a broad organization able to transfer presidential leadership; in Argentina, La Libertad Avanza gained control of the presidency with a relatively thin early organization and then sought congressional expansion; in Brazil, the Workers' Party survived major scandals and returned to government through alliances, while Bolsonaro's electorate was organized through different parties and networks. In El Salvador, Nuevas Ideas became dominant while parties associated with the postwar settlement contracted. The same term 'anti-establishment' thus described organizations at very different stages of institutional development.

Party fragmentation changes government even when elections are competitive. A president facing dozens of legislative factions must bargain over committee chairs, ministerial appointments, budgets, and legislation. Those bargains can be understood as normal democratic negotiation, but they may also appear to citizens as opaque exchanges of favors. Strongly disciplined parties can simplify responsibility for outcomes, yet they can also concentrate power in leadership groups and reduce room for internal dissent. The institutional design of presidential systems heightens these tensions because executives and legislatures often possess separately elected mandates. When their majorities differ, constitutional checks may look like obstruction; when the same movement controls both, oversight may weaken. Neither outcome follows automatically from ideology alone.

Representation also became more diverse in visible ways. Sheinbaum's election as Mexico's first woman president, Francia Márquez's role in Colombia, Indigenous participation in Bolivian politics, and younger political leaders in Chile signaled changes in who could plausibly occupy national office. But symbolic breakthroughs should not be equated with the elimination of social inequality or the full inclusion of marginalized groups in decision-making. Candidate selection, access to campaign financing, party lists, legislative representation, and protection from political violence are different dimensions of participation. Women may win national executive office while facing harassment in local politics; Indigenous electoral participation may coexist with disputes over land, consultation, and autonomy. Students should ask whether changing the identities of leaders alters institutions or merely broadens the range of contenders operating within existing constraints.

Geographic organization remains fundamental. A party's national headquarters may depend on local mayors, provincial governors, trade unions, community associations, churches, cooperatives, and neighborhood organizers. These networks supply volunteers, distribute information, and articulate grievances that presidential campaigns cannot simply invent. They also create patronage risks, particularly when access to public employment or benefits depends on political loyalty. Reforms designed to open party competition may weaken long-standing patronage networks but can also reduce institutional continuity when personalistic organizations displace trained local cadres. The meaning of renewal is therefore ambiguous. A new party can expand representation while lacking internal mechanisms to supervise leaders, discipline corrupt officials, or replace a failed president.

Electoral coalitions are not identical to governing coalitions. Voters may unite to defeat an incumbent and then disagree on pension rules, public spending, crime, cultural policy, or relations with the judiciary. Coalition dissolution after an election may reflect ordinary programmatic disagreement rather than betrayal. The historical study of party systems must therefore examine not just vote shares but also legislative seats, alliances, regional representation, internal democracy, campaign resources, and the ability to govern beyond a charismatic figure. The political pendulum becomes more intelligible when we see that each swing transforms organizations and rules, not simply the identity of the person at the top.

Section 113.18

Constitutional Conflict, Judicial Independence, and Presidential Power

Constitutions define the rules through which presidents, legislatures, judges, and citizens exercise authority. During the 2010s and 2020s these rules became central objects of political conflict. Brazil's impeachment process removed a president through congressional and judicially supervised procedures; later courts reviewed corruption convictions and prosecuted attacks on constitutional government. Peru's congressional removal procedures and executive–legislative rivalry contributed to repeated crises. Mexico's elected majority changed the judicial appointment system through constitutional amendment. El Salvador's dominant governing coalition altered presidential reelection rules. These events are not equivalent, even though all involve questions about the limits of elected leadership. A sound comparative history begins by identifying the constitutional power used, the sequence of decisions, and the procedural safeguards that remained available.

Executive aggrandizement occurs when a president or governing coalition incrementally reduces independent constraints without necessarily cancelling elections. Judicial packing, manipulation of appointment rules, intimidation of electoral officials, restrictions on journalism, and erosion of legislative oversight may be indicators, depending on evidence and context. But reform of a court, appointment of new judges, or a decisive electoral majority is not sufficient by itself to establish democratic breakdown. Institutional design always changes, and elected legislatures legitimately amend laws. The evaluative question is whether reforms preserve meaningful competition, due process, plural representation, and the ability of future majorities and minorities to exercise their constitutional rights. Historians should avoid applying the word 'authoritarian' as a substitute for tracing precise institutional changes.

Judicial independence does not mean courts are beyond criticism. Judges can make errors, misapply law, delay proceedings, or be influenced by improper political considerations. Investigations of misconduct and lawful procedural reform are legitimate democratic activities. Yet independence requires that a judge be able to decide cases against powerful officials without facing arbitrary retaliation. Brazil's courts were praised by some citizens for protecting elections and criticized by others for excessive intervention. Mexico's elected-judge system sought a new form of accountability but raised the possibility of partisan dependence. Peru demonstrated how disputes over constitutional interpretation and executive removal can make institutions unstable even without a wholesale abandonment of elections. These comparisons show that the practical operation of checks and balances matters more than a constitutional slogan.

Presidential reelection rules provide another comparison. Some systems permit a single reelection; others bar consecutive terms; still others have altered term limits through judicial interpretation or constitutional amendment. Whether such change is legally enacted is one question. Whether it creates structural advantages for the incumbent through control over public resources, media exposure, or electoral institutions is another. The two questions should be analyzed separately. El Salvador's 2025 changes and Brazil's established reelection framework illustrate different constitutional settings, while Latin America's broader past contains both legitimate peaceful continuities and examples in which leaders used amendments to weaken competition. Chapter 111 discusses Salvadoran security powers in detail; here those arrangements serve as a comparator in the politics of institutional design.

Legitimacy ultimately depends on both outcomes and procedures. Citizens want public safety, stable prices, employment, and effective services; a democracy that cannot deliver them faces pressure. But institutional rules are designed to protect citizens precisely when political majorities are impatient. A legislature that obstructs every proposal can damage confidence; a president who treats every disagreement as illegitimate can do the same. The period's central constitutional tension was therefore not simply 'people versus courts' or 'order versus liberty.' It was the question of how elected officials can exercise a mandate while remaining constrained by rules that allow opponents to organize, courts to judge, and future voters to replace those who currently govern. The answer cannot be supplied by the popularity of one president alone.

Historical Vocabulary

Democratic backsliding and executive aggrandizement

Democratic backsliding denotes erosion of institutions that sustain free competition, accountability, or rights. Executive aggrandizement describes incremental expansion of executive authority at the expense of checks. Neither concept should be inferred from a leader's popularity or partisan label alone; the evidence must identify specific practices and changes.

Section 113.19

Populism, Digital Media, Polarization, and the Struggle for Democratic Legitimacy

Populism became a widely used term in political debate, often as an accusation. Analytically, it can describe a political style that divides society into a virtuous people and an illegitimate elite, presenting a leader or movement as the authentic voice of the whole. This style does not prescribe a particular economic policy. López Obrador invoked social justice and opposition to privileged interests, Milei attacked the political caste in the name of economic freedom, and Bolsonaro mobilized cultural conservatism and distrust of established institutions. Their supporters and opponents differed, as did their relations with legislatures, courts, and parties. Historians should apply the concept through observable discourse and institutional practice, not as a synonym for any popular politician or policy they dislike.

Political communication changed as leaders gained the capacity to address audiences directly through social platforms, broadcast events, and personal media channels. Daily presidential communications in Mexico could define the news agenda; provocative social-media language in Argentina and Brazil circulated rapidly beyond formal press conferences. These formats accelerated mobilization and helped voters encounter political arguments without party intermediaries. They also encouraged conflict framed in personal terms and made misinformation easier to spread at scale. Traditional media were not passive: television debates, investigative reporting, newspaper commentary, and radio remained influential. Digital communication should therefore be studied as part of a larger information ecosystem, connected to campaign finance, partisan organizations, algorithms, journalistic practice, and state regulation.

Claims of electoral fraud require disciplined treatment. Genuine irregularities may occur and must be investigated, but a losing candidate's accusation is not itself evidence that a certified election was invalid. Responsible reporting identifies the alleged act, the election authority's procedures, independent monitoring, relevant judicial decisions, and any demonstrated effect on the result. Brazil's electoral disputes after 2022 and Peru's contested 2026 runoff provide contrasting cases. In both, public debate became heated; in neither should the historian erase the legal record by substituting an unsupported claim for the official count. Equally, certification should not silence scrutiny of specific irregularities if credible evidence emerges. Democratic legitimacy depends partly on the capacity to investigate disputes and reach publicly explainable decisions.

Survey research offers one way to understand public confidence, but it cannot be treated as a simple verdict. Latinobarómetro's 2024 regional study asked respondents about preferences for democracy and evaluations of institutions. A respondent may support democracy in principle and distrust the current legislature; another may approve a president's economic program and oppose changes to judicial independence. The answers depend on question wording, timing, sampling, and national context. V-Dem and International IDEA use other approaches, including expert assessments and multiple institutional indicators. These measures are valuable for comparison, but they do not measure precisely the same thing. Students should not combine a percentage of survey respondents with a democracy-index score as if both were points on one numerical scale.

Polarization can be democratic when it means strong, peaceful disagreement among organized alternatives. It becomes more dangerous when opponents are cast as inherently illegitimate, journalists are threatened, electoral results are rejected without evidence, or institutions are disabled to prevent future competition. Yet the costs of polarization should not become an excuse for demanding that all parties abandon substantive differences. Democracy requires genuine contest over taxes, inequality, cultural norms, security, climate policy, and sovereignty. The question is how those conflicts are conducted. Across Latin America, constitutional institutions managed numerous close elections and peaceful transfers even while public language grew harsher. The history of the political pendulum is consequently a history of contested legitimacy: who speaks for the public, who counts the votes, and which rules everyone must continue to obey after a defeat.

The period also generated new forms of civic fact-checking and electoral observation. Independent journalists compared campaign statements with budgets and court records, while election observers assessed voting procedures and political parties scrutinized tallies. These institutions can help correct misinformation, but they too require transparency about funding, methods, and limits. An observation mission may certify that procedures met a standard without endorsing a government's entire policy program. Likewise, a fact-check concerning one false statement does not settle whether an opposition movement's broader economic complaints are justified. Democratic debate improves when claims are evaluated at their proper scale.

Section 113.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Latin American Democracy through 2026

The political changes of Latin America from 2010 through November 2026 were neither a uniform conservative reaction nor a steady advance of the left. They arose through different combinations of economic stress, scandals, institutional design, party fragmentation, changing social representation, and the appeal of leaders promising a break with the past. The earlier commodity boom had helped governments finance expectations they could not always sustain under slower growth. Corruption investigations exposed important abuses while raising difficult questions about judicial procedures. Social movements widened the public agenda, but electoral coalitions formed around more than movement demands. The political metaphor of a pendulum captures some alternations, yet conceals the distinct mechanisms by which parties survived, collapsed, or reinvented themselves.

Brazil provides the longest sequence in this chapter. Rousseff's removal in 2016, Bolsonaro's victory in 2018, Lula's return in 2022, judicial proceedings after the January 2023 attacks, and Lula's reelection in October 2026 demonstrate competitive electoral politics intertwined with profound constitutional conflict. Argentina moved from Peronism to Macri, back to Peronism, and then to Milei's libertarian coalition, making inflation and fiscal credibility central political tests. Mexico selected a new dominant governing movement, Morena, and preserved that continuity under Sheinbaum while changing the rules of judicial selection. These cases cannot be placed along one identical ideological scale. Electoral institutions, legislative support, and the relationship between executives and courts shaped every outcome.

Elsewhere the diversity was equally striking. Chile elected Boric and later Kast after two rejected constitutional proposals. Colombia moved from Petro's historic left-wing victory to de la Espriella's rightward electoral transition. Peru's repeated institutional crises culminated in a closely contested 2026 election that brought Keiko Fujimori to office without resolving congressional fragmentation. Bolivia displaced the long-dominant MAS with the centrist Rodrigo Paz. Ecuador reelected Noboa's security-centered government, Uruguay restored the Frente Amplio, and Costa Rica elected Laura Fernández. Each result reflected domestic institutions, political geography, and voter expectations. Simultaneous elections did not amount to a single regional mandate.

The chapter has repeatedly separated electoral authorization from democratic consolidation. An election determines who is entitled to govern under constitutional rules; it does not establish that every proposed policy is wise or that courts and journalists lose their independent roles. Likewise, a difficult economy or unpopular government does not automatically prove democracy is failing. Durable democratic government requires the ability to replace leaders, protect civil rights, adjudicate disputes, represent minorities, and implement public policy. It also requires acceptance that opponents may lawfully win future elections. Parties are necessary institutions of this process because they organize compromise and responsibility beyond the popularity of one personality. When they weaken, personalistic leaders may fill a representational vacuum without rebuilding institutional trust.

Several lessons remain open as of the November 16, 2026 cutoff. Brazil's certified election could be described, but Lula's new term had not yet begun. The long-run effects of Argentina's stabilization reforms, Mexico's judicial elections, and recent presidential transitions elsewhere could not be settled. Changes in democratic indices required methodological care and source dates. Future movements, economic shocks, and electoral coalitions would reshape the landscape again. These limits are not weaknesses of history; they are reminders that contemporary events must be distinguished from the later outcomes historians will eventually assess with fuller evidence. The chapter closes by turning to the structural pressures explored in Chapter 114: the pandemic's legacy, migration, environmental demands, critical minerals, trade, and geopolitical competition. The political pendulum moves within those enduring material conditions, but it does not move by them alone.

Chapter Summary

The metaphor of a political pendulum captures some shifts between governing parties but conceals the variety of institutional settings, coalition structures, and social interests shaping elections across Latin America. A sequence of presidents does not reveal a single uniform regional direction.

The slowdown after the commodity boom weakened governments that had financed social commitments under favorable export conditions. Economic stress mattered, yet its electoral effects depended on political narratives, local organizations, and differing ideas about stabilization and redistribution.

Corruption investigations exposed serious institutional vulnerabilities but also raised questions about due process and prosecutorial impartiality. The distinction between allegations, convictions, and annulled convictions is essential to writing accurate political history.

Anti-establishment leaders differed radically in programs. Milei advocated libertarian reform; López Obrador emphasized national transformation and social provision; Bolsonaro built a conservative coalition; Bukele prioritized security and personal political renewal. Their common rhetoric cannot erase their differences.

Brazil underwent an impeachment, Bolsonaro's election, Lula's return, attempted disruption of constitutional succession, judicial proceedings, and a renewed Lula victory in 2026. Electoral machinery endured even as public trust in the courts and political opponents remained polarized.

Argentina moved repeatedly between governing coalitions while facing monetary instability. Milei's victory and later legislative gains showed the appeal of fiscal and political rupture, but the longer distributional and institutional effects of reform remained under debate.

Mexico transformed its national party system with Morena and continued that project under Sheinbaum. The 2024 constitutional judicial reform and 2025 elections raised a distinctive debate about democratic accountability and judicial independence.

Chile moved from Boric to Kast after contested constitutional projects. Colombia moved from Petro to de la Espriella, while Peru's narrow 2026 result took place against a history of repeated presidential crises. Electoral events alone do not settle their institutional difficulties.

Bolivia, Ecuador, Uruguay, and Costa Rica displayed different combinations of party continuity, security concerns, center-right victory, centrist adjustment, and left-of-center renewal. Geographic proximity did not ensure the same ideological outcome.

Public-opinion surveys and institutional democracy indices answer different questions. Latinobarómetro records respondents' stated attitudes; V-Dem and International IDEA use other methods to assess institutional arrangements. Their measures should not be merged as interchangeable numbers.

The enduring lesson through November 2026 is that democratic consolidation requires more than winning a presidency. Elected governments must retain effective opposition, constitutional safeguards, reliable electoral institutions, civil rights, and mechanisms for future peaceful replacement.

Chapter Reflection

A political pendulum is an image of orderly movement, but elections are never as simple as a swinging weight. They bring together memories of earlier government, immediate economic experience, local political organizations, cultural commitments, institutional rules, and hopes about what leaders might do differently. Latin America's recent history demonstrates the capacity of voters to remove governments and the persistence of dissatisfaction that no one victory can resolve.

Constitutional procedures are not obstacles external to democracy; they are part of the arrangement through which the public authorizes government without surrendering the ability to challenge it. A presidential mandate, even a large one, remains limited by law and by the rights of citizens who voted for someone else. Conversely, a court or legislature that uses its powers without credibility or transparency can undermine trust. The period raises hard questions about maintaining that balance.

Students should emerge from the chapter able to read an election result carefully and to decline simplistic explanations. They should ask what the vote measured, whether it was certified, how the governing coalition operates, and how institutions can correct error. The transition to Chapter 114 broadens those questions to the economic, environmental, demographic, and geopolitical pressures within which Latin American democracies must continue to govern.
