Chapter Opening: A Dynasty, a Symbol, and an Insurrection

On 19 July 1979 Sandinista columns entered Managua, and the Somoza family's forty-three years of control over Nicaragua ended. The victory was not the work of one guerrilla organization alone. It was produced by a broad and unstable coalition of peasants, urban youth, Indigenous neighborhoods, priests, businessmen, women's organizers, and neighboring governments, united by a shared refusal to continue living under one family and its army.

When the Sandinistas took power, Nicaragua had roughly two and a half million people, a cotton-, coffee-, sugar-, and cattle-based export economy, an army built under United States tutelage, and a capital still marked by an earthquake that had destroyed its center seven years earlier. It also had a national hero whose name the victors had adopted as their own. Augusto César Sandino had fought the United States Marines in the northern mountains from 1927 to 1933 and had been murdered in 1934 by officers of the National Guard, whose commander, Anastasio Somoza García, then took the presidency. The movement that overthrew Somoza's son and namesake forty-five years later called itself the Sandinista National Liberation Front. The name was a claim about history: that the dictatorship was the heir of foreign intervention and that the revolution was the unfinished business of Sandino's war.

This chapter asks how a family regime that looked secure in the 1960s collapsed in the late 1970s, and what kind of government replaced it. The first question requires attention to several processes at once: the structure of the Somoza system, its dependence on the National Guard and on a long relationship with Washington, the economic grievances of peasants displaced by export agriculture, the moral and organizational work of parts of the Catholic Church, the long clandestine labor of the Sandinista Front, and the decision of much of Nicaragua's propertied class to abandon a ruler who had come to treat the national economy as a family business. The second question requires an equally careful look at the revolutionary government of 1979–1980, which inherited a shattered economy, a divided coalition, and a hostile regional environment.

The chapter also insists on a distinction that students will need throughout Part XX. A revolution that has won power is not yet a settled political order. The Sandinista victory of July 1979 was a historical turning point, but what Nicaragua's new rulers would do with it, and how their opponents inside and outside the country would respond, were open questions. The chapter stops while those questions are still open. The Contra war, Iran-Contra, the elections of the 1980s, and the end of Sandinista rule belong to Chapter 90, and this chapter foreshadows them only where needed to explain the strains already visible by 1980–1981.

Women, Indigenous and Afro-descended Nicaraguans, and the people of the Caribbean coast belong in this story as participants and not as a footnote. Women served as combatants, commanders, organizers, and negotiators; the Indigenous neighborhood of Monimbó in Masaya became a symbol of popular insurrection; and the Atlantic coast, with its Miskitu, Creole, Mayangna, Rama, and Garifuna communities, experienced both the Somoza era and the revolution differently from the Spanish-speaking Pacific lowlands. The Catholic Church, often cast as an institution of conservative order, appears here in several guises, from Archbishop Miguel Obando y Bravo's criticism of the regime to the priests who joined the revolutionary government.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. The history of Somoza Nicaragua draws on United States diplomatic and military records, Guardia documents, newspapers such as La Prensa, Inter-American Commission on Human Rights investigations, Sandinista communiqués and programs, church documents, television footage, and a rich literature of memoirs and testimonies by participants, including Omar Cabezas, Tomás Borge, Gioconda Belli, and Sergio Ramírez. Each of these sources has a purpose. Sandinista memoirs were often written within a revolutionary state or after its fall; Somocista and United States records reflect the vantage of those holding power; and oral testimony is shaped by decades of memory and political change. The chapter is organized so that the Sandino story, the Somoza system, the revolutionary movement, and the new government appear as parts of one explanation, with the limits of the evidence noted along the way.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Nicaragua's regional, ethnic, and political geography and explain why the Pacific lowlands, the northern highlands, and the Caribbean coast experienced the Somoza era differently.
  2. Explain the origins of the Somoza system in the United States occupation, the creation of the National Guard, and the murder of Sandino in 1934.
  3. Analyze how the Somoza family combined control of the Guardia, manipulation of the Liberal and Conservative parties, economic holdings, and Cold War alignment to sustain a dynasty.
  4. Trace the founding and development of the FSLN from 1961 through its division into three tendencies and its reunification in 1979.
  5. Assess the roles of the Catholic Church, liberation theology, and Christian base communities in opposition to the regime.
  6. Evaluate the effects of the 1972 Managua earthquake on elite support for Somoza and on the regime's legitimacy.
  7. Distinguish the contributions of armed insurrection, mass urban rebellion, business opposition, international diplomacy, and the Carter administration's policy to Somoza's fall.
  8. Interpret the Historical Program of the FSLN as a primary source and identify what it can and cannot reveal about the movement's later conduct in power.
  9. Describe the institutions and social programs of the revolutionary government in 1979–1980, including the Junta, the National Directorate, the Council of State, and the Literacy Crusade.
  10. Explain the early strains within the revolutionary coalition and between the new government and the United States, the Church, the business sector, and Caribbean-coast communities.

Historical Context

Earlier chapters supply the background. Chapters 57 and 62 traced Nicaragua's independence from Spain, its brief membership in the Central American federation, and the rivalry between the Liberal city of León and the Conservative city of Granada that dominated nineteenth-century politics. Chapter 70 explained how the United States, after 1898, extended its influence across the Caribbean basin through the Panama Canal, the Roosevelt Corollary, and a series of military interventions. Chapters 79 and 81 described two earlier Cold War upheavals: the overthrow of Guatemala's government in 1954 and the Cuban Revolution. Chapter 88 examined human-rights activism, liberation theology, and resistance to dictatorship in South America. All of these are necessary background, but the present chapter summarizes only what a reader needs.

Nicaragua is the largest country in Central America and is divided by geography into three regions. The Pacific lowlands, with the lakes of Managua and Nicaragua and the cities of León, Managua, Masaya, and Granada, contain most of the population and the fertile volcanic soils that supported cotton, sugar, and coffee. The central and northern highlands, including Matagalpa, Jinotega, Estelí, and the Segovias along the Honduran border, were home to coffee growers, small farmers, and poor rural laborers. The Caribbean coast, known historically as the Mosquitia and later as Zelaya, was long oriented toward the Caribbean and Britain more than toward Managua. It held Miskitu, Creole, Mayangna, Rama, and Garifuna communities, English-speaking and Moravian Protestant traditions, and a different relation to the Nicaraguan state. The government of José Santos Zelaya had incorporated the coast militarily in 1894, but its integration remained partial.

The Nicaraguan state before the twentieth century was weak and its politics were turbulent. Conservatives and Liberals, essentially factions of the landed and merchant elite, fought civil wars, and in 1855 an American adventurer, William Walker, took advantage of a Liberal-Conservative conflict to seize power and proclaim himself president before being driven out by a Central American coalition in 1857. The memory of Walker, and the persistent idea that Nicaragua's geography made it a natural site for an interoceanic canal, gave Nicaraguans reasons to view the United States with suspicion. After Zelaya, a nationalist Liberal, was forced from office in 1909 with American encouragement, the country entered a period of intermittent United States military occupation that lasted, with an interruption, from 1912 to 1933.

The Cold War overlaid these older conflicts. After 1945 the United States viewed Central America mainly as a zone to be kept free of communist influence, and Washington valued stable, friendly governments, whatever their domestic character. Nicaraguan rulers understood this and offered cooperation. Somoza García supported the 1954 operation against Guatemala's Árbenz government, and in 1961 the Bay of Pigs invasion force of Cuban exiles departed from Nicaragua's Caribbean coast. Yet the Cold War did not simply produce the regime, nor did it produce its opponents. Both grew out of Nicaraguan social conflict, and external alignment shaped the terms on which they fought.

Historians have also revised older accounts. Early writing on the revolution often portrayed it either as a straightforward communist takeover or as a spontaneous uprising of an entire united people. Recent scholarship emphasizes a more complex picture: a long-developing movement with diverse social roots, divided in its strategy, drawing support from many classes, and supported by regional and international actors for varied reasons. The chapter follows that complexity.

Division I — Sandino, Sovereignty, and the Formation of Somocismo

Section 89.1

Nicaragua before the Somozas: Regions, Rivalries, and the United States

Nicaragua's twentieth-century history opens with a question about sovereignty. After Zelaya's fall in 1909, successive Conservative governments depended on American financial supervision and on American troops. The Bryan–Chamorro Treaty of 1914 gave the United States exclusive rights to build a canal across Nicaragua and to establish naval bases, in exchange for a payment of three million dollars, a transaction that Nicaraguan nationalists denounced and that neighboring states, notably Costa Rica and El Salvador, challenged. Marines landed in 1912 to suppress a Liberal revolt, and a legation guard remained until 1925. They returned in 1926 when civil war again broke out between Liberal and Conservative factions. Chapter 70 placed these interventions within the wider hemispheric order; here they matter because they shaped Nicaragua's army, its politics, and the nationalist memory on which the Sandinistas later drew.

The elite that supported these arrangements was narrow. Landowners, coffee exporters, merchants, and professionals in León and Granada dominated party politics, while most Nicaraguans were small farmers, rural laborers, artisans, and market sellers whose political voice was limited. On the Caribbean coast, American-owned banana, lumber, and mining enterprises gave the region a distinct economy; Miskitu and Creole communities experienced the central government as distant and sometimes predatory. Indigenous communities in the Pacific zone, including those of Monimbó in Masaya and Subtiava in León, retained communal institutions and strong local identities even as land pressure increased. These regional and social differences meant that no single national identity united Nicaraguans against foreign intervention, although opposition to the Marines found support in many communities.

American policy in this period sought stability through institution-building. After the 1927 settlement mediated by Henry Stimson, Washington supervised elections and created a nonpartisan National Guard to replace the party armies that had fought the civil wars. The idea was to end Nicaraguan militarized factionalism and permit an American withdrawal. The result was the opposite of what its designers intended: the Guardia, trained and equipped by the Marines, became a political force in its own right, and its first Nicaraguan commander used it to take over the state. The history of the Somoza regime begins in this unintended consequence of an attempt to engineer order from outside.

The Bryan–Chamorro concession had more than symbolic implications. The canal privilege did not mean a canal would be built, but it limited Nicaragua's ability to negotiate independently with another potential builder; Washington's control over strategic options was the immediate result. Even an agreement presented by the two governments as a benefit could be contested from the standpoint of other Central American states. The Central American Court of Justice ruled against elements of the treaty after objections by Costa Rica and El Salvador, although the ruling did not reverse United States policy. That disagreement reveals the distance between treaty-making power and consent across the region.

Nicaragua's Caribbean region complicates a narrative written only from Managua. Trade with the English-speaking Caribbean, Moravian missions, and the survival of Indigenous forms of community authority shaped social life there. Integration under Zelaya did not erase distinctive languages, land claims, or customary practices. A map of the Pacific cities and Atlantic waterways therefore becomes a tool for historical interpretation: state institutions reached some districts through railroads and plantations, and others through military expeditions, commercial concessions, and local intermediaries. Sandinista leaders later inherited this uneven geography and the tensions it sustained.

Section 89.2

Sandino and the Army in Defense of National Sovereignty, 1927–1934

Augusto César Sandino was born in 1895 in Niquinohomo, the son of a small landowner and a domestic worker, and he spent years working abroad, including in the oil fields near Tampico, Mexico, where he encountered labor organizing and the political currents of the Mexican Revolution. He returned to Nicaragua in 1926 and worked at a gold mine in the northern department of Nueva Segovia. When General José María Moncada, the Liberal leader, accepted the 1927 settlement arranged by Stimson, Sandino refused to lay down arms. He organized his own force, which he called the Army in Defense of the National Sovereignty of Nicaragua, and began a guerrilla war against the Marines and the Guardia in the northern mountains and along the Honduran border.

The war lasted until 1933. In July 1927 Sandino's forces attacked the Marine and Guardia garrison at Ocotal, and the response included aerial bombing by Marine aircraft, an early use of air power against a Latin American town. The Marines, who numbered several thousand at their peak, struggled to catch a mobile enemy operating in difficult terrain with the support of local peasants. Sandino's force was never large, and estimates of its size vary, but it was widely supported and it drew volunteers from across Central America and beyond. For a time Farabundo Martí, the Salvadoran revolutionary, served as his secretary, and Sandino spent time in Mexico in 1929 and 1930 seeking support. His writings and manifestos mixed anti-imperialism, Latin Americanist solidarity, a distinctive spiritual language, and a defense of peasants and workers. Scholars debate how to classify these ideas, and the label "Marxist" fits poorly; they are better understood as an eclectic nationalism with a populist and social dimension.

The war ended for reasons that were as much political as military. The Great Depression and growing domestic opposition to occupation in the United States made the intervention costly. The Marines departed in January 1933 after the inauguration of the Liberal President Juan Bautista Sacasa, and Sandino agreed to a peace settlement the following month under which his fighters would receive land in the northern Río Coco region. The settlement left the Guardia, now under Anastasio Somoza García, in the position of an armed force unreconciled to Sandino's continued existence. On 21 February 1934, after dining at the presidential palace, Sandino was seized and killed together with his brother Sócrates and two of his generals by Guardia officers. Somoza later claimed that he had received the approval of the American ambassador, who denied it, and the exact distribution of responsibility remains discussed. That Somoza ordered or authorized the killing is not seriously disputed among historians.

Sandino's death transformed him from a guerrilla leader into a martyr. For decades the Somoza regime suppressed his memory while claiming to represent the stability that Sandino had threatened. Opponents of the regime treated him as the embodiment of resistance. When Carlos Fonseca and his companions founded their organization in 1961, adopting Sandino's name was the central act of political self-definition, claiming a nationalist lineage that no Marxist label alone could supply. Women appeared in Sandino's story as supporters, couriers, and fighters, and in the later Sandinista movement the memory of women combatants became an important theme. The man himself became a symbol that different groups could interpret differently, which was part of his power.

Historical Vocabulary
Guardia Nacional

The Guardia Nacional (National Guard) was Nicaragua's combined army and national police, established under the 1927 settlement and organized, trained, and at first officered by the United States Marines. It was intended to be nonpartisan and professional.

In practice the Guardia became the personal instrument of the Somoza family. Its officers received land, commercial opportunities, and control of local government; many were bound to the dynasty by patronage and kinship. Students should avoid equating it with a modern professional army. It combined police functions, rural counterinsurgency, local administration, and in many places the collection of illegal income, and its record of abuse became central to the opposition's case. Its disintegration in July 1979 was the practical end of the regime.

Section 89.3

The Making of the Somoza System, 1934–1956

Anastasio Somoza García was an English-speaking former salesman and Liberal politician who had married into the Sacasa family and who impressed American officials with his fluency and cooperative manner. After Sandino's killing he consolidated his control over the Guardia, and in June 1936 he forced President Sacasa to resign. This was a coup, in the specific sense that a military commander removed a constitutional president by force. Somoza arranged his own election in December 1936 and took office in January 1937. He ruled directly until 1947 and, after a brief interlude in which he installed and then removed a compliant successor, returned to the presidency in 1951. Throughout these years he maintained power not through a single mechanism but through a combination of them.

The first was military. Somoza kept the Guardia loyal by placing relatives and trusted associates in key commands and by allowing officers to profit. He built an intelligence apparatus and used the army to monitor, intimidate, and when necessary eliminate rivals. The second was political. He controlled the Nationalist Liberal Party and, through a 1950 pact with the Conservative leader Emiliano Chamorro, guaranteed the Conservatives a share of seats and offices in return for their acceptance of his rule. This arrangement gave the regime a facade of two-party competition and made part of the traditional elite complicit. Opposition parties that refused such deals, as well as independent unions and students, faced censorship, prison, and exile.

The third mechanism was economic. Somoza and his associates acquired land, cattle ranches, sugar mills, a shipping line, an airline, and urban real estate, taking advantage of wartime expropriations of German-owned coffee properties, of state contracts, and of insider knowledge. Estimates of the family's wealth at the time of the revolution varied widely, running to several hundred million dollars, and the family and its associates were often said to control a large share of the country's best agricultural land, although exact figures are difficult to verify. The post-1950 cotton boom, financed by credit and encouraged by international demand, expanded the Pacific plantation economy, while the Central American Common Market, created in 1960, supported modest industrialization. Growth was real, but its distribution was uneven, and the expansion of export agriculture pushed peasants off land and into seasonal labor.

The fourth mechanism was alignment with Washington. Somoza offered the United States political support, bases, and anti-communist credentials, and in return received recognition, military training, and arms. President Franklin Roosevelt is often credited with a remark, of uncertain wording, to the effect that Somoza was a scoundrel but "our" scoundrel; historians doubt the authenticity of the phrase, but it captures a perception widely held by Nicaraguans and Americans alike. The label "U.S.-backed" is used here in a limited and defined sense: Washington provided recognition, training, equipment, and economic ties to the regime. It did not manage Nicaraguan politics, and the dynasty acted with considerable independence and pursued its own interests. This distinction matters for explaining both the regime's resilience and its eventual vulnerability when Washington's policy shifted in the late 1970s.

Somoza García's rule ended on 21 September 1956, when Rigoberto López Pérez, a young poet and printer, shot him at a party in León. López Pérez was killed on the spot, and Somoza died days later in a hospital in the Panama Canal Zone. The assassination showed that opposition existed, but it did not end the regime. Within days the Guardia and the family secured power. His elder son, Luis Somoza Debayle, took the presidency, and the younger son, Anastasio Somoza Debayle, already head of the Guardia, held the institutional foundation of the regime. The dynasty was now a reality.

Section 89.4

The Dynasty in Its Prime, 1956–1972

Luis Somoza, who had studied agriculture in the United States, adopted a more flexible style than his father. He presided over a limited liberalization, accepted a constitutional ban on immediate presidential reelection, and allowed a puppet president, René Schick, to serve in the mid-1960s. He also managed an expanding economy and the relations with Washington that the Alliance for Progress made profitable, receiving large inflows of aid and credit. At the same time the family's control of the Guardia and the Liberal Party was never in question. Luis died of a heart attack in 1967, removing a figure who had sometimes restrained his brother.

Anastasio Somoza Debayle, known as "Tachito," was a graduate of the United States Military Academy at West Point, a fluent English speaker, and a more openly authoritarian ruler. In January 1967, as he prepared to stand for president, the opposition organized a large march in Managua; the Guardia fired on it, killing dozens of people, and Somoza won the election the following month and took office in May. His government continued the pattern of the dynasty: sustained economic growth, concentration of wealth in his family and allied business groups, a political system managed through the Liberal Party, and periodic use of force. Because the constitution prohibited his immediate reelection, in 1971 he negotiated a pact with the Conservative leader Fernando Agüero that created a ruling triumvirate beginning in 1972, a device to keep power until a new term could begin.

The Nicaragua of the 1960s was a society of sharp divisions. Urbanization increased, especially in Managua, and a small middle class of professionals, merchants, and civil servants emerged. In the countryside, the expansion of cotton and cattle ranching displaced tenant farmers, while migrants moved to the capital's growing informal neighborhoods. Literacy was limited, health services were scarce, and the reach of the state in many rural and Caribbean-coast areas was thin. Foreign investment increased, and the Central American Common Market linked Nicaragua's economy to its neighbors. These changes created new social groups, among them wage laborers, students, and urban poor, who were available for organizing but also vulnerable to repression. The regime met demands for reform with a combination of limited concessions and force, a strategy that worked while the elite remained united behind it and the economy continued to expand.

Women's roles in this society were constrained by law and custom but were not static. Women worked as market vendors, domestic servants, seasonal agricultural workers, teachers, and, increasingly, in offices and factories. They headed a large share of urban and rural households, a fact that later made women's organizing a significant political force. The Catholic Church, the family, and the state expected women to occupy a domestic position, and political participation was limited, although women's suffrage had been introduced in 1955. These constraints help explain why involvement in clandestine politics, and later armed struggle, represented for many women both a political and a personal break with convention.

Economic growth in this period should be measured against who owned land, who received loans, and who bore the costs of export dependence. International demand expanded cotton cultivation, but mechanized farming and pressure to sell communal or small holdings altered rural employment and migration. Nicaragua's participation in the Central American Common Market generated manufacturing jobs and strengthened commercial networks; it did not produce equal regional development. Workers could experience an expanding economy and deteriorating security at the same time. These changes help explain why labor unions, student associations, and urban neighborhood organizations increasingly mattered to the politics of opposition.

The Pacific-centered political class did not speak for the Miskitu, Mayangna, Rama, or Creole communities of the Caribbean. Some coastal people depended on wage labor in mines and forestry; others relied on fishing, small farming, and regional exchange. Government claims to national integration frequently treated linguistic difference and communal landholding as obstacles rather than institutions deserving recognition. The revolution of 1979 would therefore encounter older problems not created by the Somozas alone.

Division II — The FSLN, Religious Opposition, and the Deepening Crisis

Section 89.5

Founding the FSLN and the Church of the Poor

The Sandinista National Liberation Front emerged from the radicalization of a generation of university students and young activists in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Carlos Fonseca Amador, born in Matagalpa in 1936 as the illegitimate son of a Somoza administrator and a domestic worker, had belonged to the Nicaraguan Socialist Party, the country's Moscow-aligned communist organization, and had visited the Soviet Union. He became convinced that peaceful opposition and the old party's cautious tactics could not defeat the dynasty. Tomás Borge, Silvio Mayorga, and others around him were influenced by the Cuban Revolution of 1959, which showed that a small armed movement could topple a dictator, and by the nationalist tradition of Sandino. They founded their organization in 1961, originally under the name National Liberation Front, adding "Sandinista" in 1963.

The early years were failures. A 1963 incursion from Honduras along the Río Coco and Bocay region was defeated, as was an attempt in 1967 at Pancasán in the northern mountains, in which Mayorga and other fighters were killed. Guerrilla war on a Cuban model, which assumed that a small armed band could create the conditions for a revolution, proved difficult in Nicaragua's rural terrain and in the face of a Guardia trained for counterinsurgency. In the late 1960s the Front shifted toward patient organizing: building networks among students, urban neighborhoods, and rural communities, and avoiding major clashes. A famous 1969 urban shootout in Managua, in which the Sandinista leader Julio Buitrago died fighting the Guardia, symbolized both the movement's heroism and its small size. In 1969 the Front published its Historical Program, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, which set out its aims.

Meanwhile, a different transformation was under way within Nicaragua's Catholic Church. The 1968 Latin American bishops’ conference at Medellín, Colombia, which Chapter 88 discussed, sharpened the Church’s commitment to justice for the poor, later widely framed as a "preferential option for the poor," and some priests, nuns, and lay catechists began working with peasant and urban poor communities in new ways. In Nicaragua this produced Christian base communities (comunidades eclesiales de base), small groups that read the Gospels in light of their own circumstances and began to question the justice of existing arrangements. The poet and priest Ernesto Cardenal founded a community on the Solentiname archipelago in Lake Nicaragua in 1966, where peasants and artists discussed scripture and produced painting and poetry. Foreign missionaries, including Capuchin priests on the Caribbean coast, documented abuses by the Guardia in the countryside. The Church was not unified: the hierarchy under Archbishop Obando y Bravo criticized the regime on human-rights grounds but resisted revolutionary violence, while many priests and lay workers moved much closer to the Sandinistas.

For the revolution, the importance of these developments was that they linked clandestine armed opposition to a mass social base. A peasant who joined a base community might become a supporter of the guerrillas without reading any Marxist text, and a young Catholic activist might move from catechism to the underground. The Sandinista Front's leadership included Marxists and others who defined themselves more by nationalism and Christian social commitment, and the relationship between these strands shaped the movement throughout its history. Historians continue to discuss how far the revolutionary coalition's religious dimension was a tactical alliance and how far it reflected conviction; the evidence suggests both existed.

Historical Vocabulary
Sandinismo

Sandinismo refers to the political tradition that claims inspiration from Sandino and, more specifically, to the ideology and practice of the FSLN. It is not a fixed doctrine.

The term has meant different things at different times: Sandino's own eclectic nationalism, the FSLN's blend of anti-imperialism, Marxist analysis, Cuban-influenced guerrilla strategy, and Christian social commitment, and later the governing ideology of the revolutionary state. Students should avoid assuming that "Sandinista" automatically meant "communist" or, conversely, that it meant only nationalism. The movement contained several tendencies, and its leaders argued openly about doctrine. The word also became a broad label for popular opposition to Somoza among people who were not formal members of the Front.

Section 89.6

Earthquake and the Erosion of Elite Consent, 1972–1974

On 23 December 1972, shortly after midnight, a series of earthquakes struck Managua, destroying much of the city's center. Estimates of the dead range from roughly five thousand to ten thousand or more, and several hundred thousand people lost their homes; the old commercial center, government buildings, hospitals, and much of the working-class housing were wrecked. The Guardia, which was meant to maintain order, was reported to have looted in some districts, and many officers failed to report for duty. International aid poured in from the United States and other countries, and the Puerto Rican baseball star Roberto Clemente, who had organized relief shipments, died on 31 December when the plane carrying supplies crashed, reportedly after he became concerned that aid was being diverted by officials.

Somoza took personal charge of the emergency, heading a National Emergency Committee and dissolving the triumvirate to resume the presidency in all but name. Reports of corruption in the distribution of aid, of land speculation, and of reconstruction contracts awarded to businesses linked to the family circulated widely in Nicaragua and abroad. The regime's rebuilding plan favored areas in which Somoza interests held property, and the heart of the old city was left in ruins for years. The earthquake did not itself cause the revolution, but it changed the political situation in a decisive way. It displaced and radicalized thousands of urban poor, it exposed the regime's priorities to the public, and, perhaps most importantly, it alienated parts of the business elite. Some entrepreneurs, who had tolerated the dynasty as a guarantor of order, now found themselves competing against a ruler who used state power to enrich his own enterprises.

The elite's reaction is crucial to understanding the regime's collapse. In 1974 Somoza won another presidential term in an election the opposition boycotted. At the same time, sectors of the business community formed new organizations, including the Nicaraguan Development Institute and, in 1974, Pedro Joaquín Chamorro's Democratic Liberation Union (UDEL), which sought to build a broad civic opposition. The private-sector umbrella group COSEP (Superior Council of Private Enterprise), though cautious, increasingly distanced itself from the regime. These groups did not want a social revolution, and many of their leaders feared the Sandinistas, but they wanted an end to dynastic control. The result was a growing convergence, at first uneasy and later more active, between a revolutionary underground and a conservative civic opposition whose goals were quite different.

Disaster relief exposed the underlying distribution of power. After the earthquake, rebuilding contracts, access to imported supplies, and decisions about who might return to the center of Managua became political questions. Informal commerce moved to scattered markets, while displaced families endured long journeys between temporary housing and workplaces. Criticism of Somoza was not only a complaint about stolen aid; it also concerned the administration's preference for businesses connected to the ruler and his associates. The government's emergency powers permitted rapid decisions without public oversight, creating opportunities for favored firms while neighborhoods struggled to rebuild. Independent relief groups and religious workers observed these differences, contributing to an expanding record of discontent.

Section 89.7

Clandestinity, Repression, and Division, 1974–1977

On the night of 27 December 1974, a Sandinista commando unit entered a Managua house where former agriculture minister José María Castillo had hosted a reception for U.S. Ambassador Turner B. Shelton and other prominent guests. The ambassador and his wife had left shortly before the seizure, according to his December 28 telegram. The attackers took a number of leading members of the regime's circle hostage. After negotiations mediated by Archbishop Obando y Bravo, the government agreed to release fourteen imprisoned Sandinistas, including Daniel Ortega, to pay a ransom of one million dollars, to broadcast and publish an FSLN communiqué, and to fly the attackers and freed prisoners to Cuba. The operation transformed the Front's standing. It showed that a small group could humiliate the government, and it won attention among Nicaraguans who had previously regarded the guerrillas as marginal.

Contemporary diplomatic check: U.S. Ambassador Shelton’s telegram of 28 December 1974 (FRUS doc. 248)

The regime's response was a state of siege, in force from December 1974 until September 1977, with press censorship and a counterinsurgency campaign in the countryside. In the northern mountains and in the Caribbean-coast department of Zelaya, the Guardia burned villages, executed peasants suspected of aiding guerrillas, and forced people from their homes. Missionaries, church workers, and later the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights documented killings, torture, and disappearances. The repression devastated rural support networks, killed several Sandinista leaders, and pushed some peasant communities toward the movement even as it crippled its capacity to operate. It also produced the testimonies on which much of the later human-rights case against the regime rests, though in many rural areas documentation remains incomplete.

Under this pressure the Front divided. By 1975–1976 three tendencies had emerged, differing about strategy rather than about the goal of overthrowing Somoza. The Prolonged People's War tendency, associated with Tomás Borge and Henry Ruiz, favored patient rural organizing and slow accumulation of strength in the northern mountains. The Proletarian tendency, led by Jaime Wheelock and Luis Carrión, emphasized organizing urban workers and a Marxist class analysis. The Tercerista, or Insurrectional, tendency, led by Daniel and Humberto Ortega and Víctor Tirado López, argued that the regime was weak enough to be toppled by a combination of armed action and broad political alliances, including with sections of the business elite and the Church. In November 1976 Carlos Fonseca, the Front's founding theorist, was killed by the Guardia in the mountains, removing the figure best placed to arbitrate among them.

The Terceristas' strategy, which looked pragmatic, proved the most consequential. In 1977 they helped organize the Group of Twelve, prominent professionals, businesspeople, and priests, including the writer Sergio Ramírez and the priest Miguel d'Escoto, who declared in an October 1977 statement that the FSLN had to be part of any solution to Nicaragua's crisis. This gave the Front a respectable civilian interlocutor and made it possible for non-Marxists to support it. The Terceristas also cultivated relations with sympathetic governments in the region. Women participated throughout, as fighters, couriers, hosts of safe houses, and organizers; the best-known example is Nora Astorga, a lawyer who in March 1978 helped lure a senior Guardia general to a trap in which he was killed. In 1977 women activists founded the Association of Women Confronting the National Problem (AMPRONAC), which brought middle-class and poor women into anti-regime organizing, and which became the Luisa Amanda Espinoza Association of Nicaraguan Women (AMNLAE) after the revolution.

Section 89.8

Carter, Human Rights, and the Murder of Pedro Joaquín Chamorro, 1977–1978

The election of Jimmy Carter in 1976 changed the external environment. Carter's administration made human rights a declared element of foreign policy, and in 1977 it announced reductions in military aid to several Latin American governments, including Somoza's, citing abuses. The measure was limited and applied unevenly, and economic ties remained, but it signaled that Washington's tolerance for the regime was no longer unconditional. The regime's own vulnerability also increased: Somoza suffered a heart attack in 1977 and spent weeks recovering in the United States, raising questions about succession. In September 1977, under foreign pressure, he lifted the state of siege and relaxed press censorship, and the political temperature rose almost at once.

In October 1977 Tercerista units attacked Guardia posts at San Carlos, at Masaya, and at Ocotal. The attacks were militarily modest, and the Guardia retaliated heavily, but they showed that the movement had revived and that the regime could be challenged openly. The base community on Solentiname, some of whose members took part in the San Carlos attack, was destroyed by the Guardia. At the same time, the opposition press resumed publication, and La Prensa, edited by Pedro Joaquín Chamorro, documented abuses and denounced the family's business dealings. Chamorro, a Conservative from a prominent Granada family who had spent years in prison and exile, was the most visible figure of civic opposition and one whom the Sandinistas themselves respected.

On 10 January 1978, Chamorro was shot dead in his car in Managua. Investigations at the time and afterward implicated people close to the regime, including businessmen associated with Somoza, and Somoza denied responsibility; a complete public accounting of who ordered the killing did not result from the Somoza-era legal process. The reaction was immediate and enormous. Crowds took to the streets, burned businesses linked to the regime, among them a plasma-export company reportedly co-owned by Somoza associates and accused of exploiting poor donors, and attacked Somoza properties. COSEP and other business groups called a general strike that lasted for days, one of the first times Nicaragua's propertied class had joined popular protest against the regime. A new coalition, the Broad Opposition Front, and a more radical popular organization, the United People's Movement, formed in the following months.

The assassination thus produced two results at once. It consolidated an alliance between the civic opposition and the Sandinista-aligned left, and it destroyed the possibility that a constitutional, nonviolent transition led by figures such as Chamorro could succeed. With the most prominent moderate gone, the argument of those who said that only force would remove Somoza gained strength. At the end of February 1978 the Indigenous neighborhood of Monimbó, in Masaya, rose against the Guardia, fighting with homemade weapons, including mortars made from pipes, and holding the neighborhood for several days before being retaken. Monimbó showed the form that popular rebellion would take: neighborhood-based, young, improvised, and supported by entire communities rather than by a small armed vanguard.

Responsibility for Chamorro's assassination remains a point where precision matters. Opposition leaders suspected the regime or figures associated with it; widespread suspicion helped mobilize demonstrators and business organizations, but suspicion alone is not proof of the person who ordered the crime. A historian should compare contemporaneous police accounts, the newspaper's own reporting, family testimony, and subsequent investigations rather than replace uncertainty with a single assertion. The political effect of the murder, by contrast, is established: strikes, demonstrations, and a much broader anti-Somoza coalition followed rapidly.

The Carter administration's criticism did not produce an abrupt, unified anti-Somoza policy. Some officials feared that weakening a longtime ally would benefit revolutionary organizations aligned with Cuba; others argued that continued association with repression threatened U.S. credibility. Washington's military and economic instruments were unequal in effect, and American diplomats sought possible transitions that would preserve a professional armed force and avoid giving the FSLN exclusive control. These tensions persisted through the final weeks of the regime.

Division III — Nicaragua’s National Uprising, 1978

Section 89.9

Monimbó, Masaya, and the Expansion of Civic Rebellion, 1978

In Masaya, the Indigenous neighborhood of Monimbó became a center of confrontation in February and March 1978. Its residents occupied a place in Nicaraguan history distinct from the better-known urban professional opposition: communal identity, networks of artisans, local markets, and neighborhood solidarities made collective action possible. Barricades and improvised defenses challenged the Guardia's capacity to patrol familiar streets. The ensuing repression killed inhabitants and deepened hostility to the regime. To describe Monimbó simply as a Sandinista unit would erase the initiatives of residents, some of whom supported the Front without accepting direction from its clandestine leadership. To describe it merely as spontaneous would overlook years of political awareness and local organization.

Protest moved between cities and constituencies rather than following a single chain of command. Church statements condemned abuses and called for political change; university students mobilized through their own institutions; merchants participated in stoppages that damaged the regime economically. Employees who joined strikes risked losing wages or positions, while informal vendors faced lost income when streets became unsafe. For families, political participation could involve sheltering a wounded neighbor, delivering food, hiding a relative, or identifying a missing person. These activities were not separate from the revolution's military history: they shaped how long a barricaded district could withstand a stronger armed force.

The FSLN benefited politically from Monimbó's resistance, but the sequence complicates retrospective narratives portraying all developments as planned by one center. Popular organizations had their own demands, including limits on the Guardia and an end to rule by one family. As Somoza responded with force, moderation became harder to sustain even for opponents who initially favored negotiation. Regional news coverage gave the conflict a broader audience, and the names of local martyrs acquired symbolic force beyond Masaya. This is why accounts of 1978 need social history as well as the chronology of commandos and raids.

Monimbó also highlights the danger of using a unified term such as 'the people' without asking which populations were actually represented. Pacific Indigenous communities, Managua professionals, plantation workers, and Caribbean-coast peoples did not have identical political experiences. Their interests overlapped in opposition to the dictatorship but were not identical, a distinction that would matter after victory.

Section 89.10

August 1978: The National Palace Seizure and the Revolutionary Public Sphere

On 22 August 1978, a Sandinista commando of about two dozen fighters, disguised as Guardia soldiers, entered the National Palace in Managua while the Chamber of Deputies was in session. Commanded by Edén Pastora, the famed "Comandante Cero," with Dora María Téllez, a young medical student known as "Comandante Dos," and Hugo Torres, the group seized the building and took more than a thousand people hostage, including deputies and relatives of the president. Over two days of negotiations, with Archbishop Obando y Bravo and diplomats from several countries mediating, the regime agreed to pay five hundred thousand dollars, to release fifty-nine political prisoners, among them Tomás Borge, to publish Sandinista communiqués, and to provide a plane to fly the commando and freed prisoners abroad. Crowds lined the road to the airport. The operation was a stunning success and a profound humiliation for the government.

The palace operation forced the regime to respond to an act with extraordinary symbolic value. Nicaragua's legislature represented the public face of a constitutional system whose coercive foundation was the Guardia. A small force gaining control of that building contradicted the government's claim that the opposition was isolated, and the images and reports traveled quickly through regional newspapers and radio. The unit's success also gave the Tercerista leadership an opportunity to appeal to Nicaraguans who did not identify with Marxist politics but wanted to see Somoza removed. Its demand for the release of prisoners connected an armed spectacle with relatives' longer search for detained family members.

The participants' accounts distinguish preparation, surprise, negotiation, and exit. Sandinista commanders were not simply battlefield leaders; they needed to negotiate with authorities, convince guards not to attack, communicate demands, and prevent a prolonged siege that would endanger hostages. The settlement involved released prisoners, a cash payment, public distribution of a communiqué, and safe passage to Panama. Exact descriptions of the number of hostages and payments vary among retrospective accounts and should be attributed rather than standardized without evidence. The operation made leaders such as Edén Pastora and Dora María Téllez nationally recognizable, while also inviting controversy about the use of hostage-taking against civilians.

Nicaraguans interpreted the event differently. Regime officials called it terrorism and stressed the vulnerability of civilians in the palace. Some business and professional opponents regarded the operation as evidence that Somoza could not guarantee order, even as they feared a revolutionary government. FSLN sympathizers celebrated a dramatic demonstration of initiative. Hostages and their relatives experienced the same event as an immediate personal threat. A textbook must sustain these distinct vantage points while placing responsibility for state violence and the use of hostages in their separate institutional settings.

A final consequence was strategic. The raid brought publicity and resources but did not defeat the National Guard. The movement still needed a broader popular uprising, coordinated military fronts, and political support beyond its own cadres. Later descriptions calling the palace seizure the moment of victory mistake an inflection point for the conclusion of a revolution. The difficult confrontations of September and the following year had not yet occurred.

Section 89.11

The September 1978 Urban Insurrections and the Cost of Repression

In the following weeks, popular uprisings broke out in Matagalpa, León, Masaya, Estelí, Chinandega, and other towns, in some cases led by Sandinista units and in others largely by local young people who organized themselves. The Guardia responded with armored vehicles, artillery, and air attacks on civilian neighborhoods, killing a large number of people; estimates of the dead run from about two thousand to five thousand, the majority civilians, and the figures remain uncertain because no complete count was made. By late September the Guardia had retaken the towns, but the cost to the regime was severe. Foreign journalists and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, which visited in October, documented indiscriminate killing, and the regime's international standing, already damaged, collapsed. The insurrection had failed militarily and succeeded politically.

The September uprisings brought fighting into urban spaces on a scale Nicaragua had not previously experienced under the Somoza dynasty. Barricades, improvised weapons, and attacks on Guardia posts were combined with strikes and local efforts to control streets. In some places Sandinista militants helped prepare the outbreaks; in others neighborhood participation outstripped the fighters' ability to direct events. Military counterattacks and aerial fire devastated residential districts. The consequences were intensely local: markets closed, buses stopped, people hid in schools or churches, and homes became first-aid stations until they were destroyed or abandoned.

The National Guard possessed armored vehicles, aircraft, and much greater firepower, but its use of those resources made the political crisis worse. Indiscriminate firing in populated areas and harsh detention practices were documented by witnesses and by human-rights observers. At the same time armed opposition could not hold many towns once the Guardia concentrated its forces. This asymmetry explains why the September rebellions could be defeated militarily yet contribute to the regime's eventual loss of legitimacy. Military success that left whole neighborhoods destroyed was not a restoration of ordinary political authority.

Contemporary evidence must be read carefully. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights visited in October 1978 and examined complaints arising from the repression. The Commission described emergency decrees, legal restrictions, detention, and reports concerning the conduct of security forces. It could not investigate every death or identify the responsible officer for every reported abuse. Newspaper accounts were affected by censorship and access; later revolutionary memoirs sometimes presented uncoordinated neighborhood actions as if they were a unified FSLN plan. Comparing such sources is a way to reconstruct both organization and spontaneity.

The failed urban revolts forced the FSLN to assess its timetable, replenish losses, and find channels for arms and diplomacy. They also made clear that successful insurrection would require a breakdown of the Guardia's ability to concentrate forces across the country. Families who fled or lost relatives had immediate needs irrespective of which political leaders were negotiating abroad. This broader social cost was part of the strategic situation that all sides confronted in early 1979.

Section 89.12

Mediation, Regional Governments, and the Humanitarian Crisis, Late 1978–Early 1979

The Carter administration and several Latin American governments then attempted a diplomatic solution. A mediation team led by the American diplomat William Bowdler, with envoys from the Dominican Republic and Guatemala under the aegis of the Organization of American States, sought a transition that would remove Somoza while preserving the Guardia and excluding the Sandinistas, a formula sometimes summarized as "Somocismo without Somoza." The moderate opposition demanded a plebiscite on Somoza's rule; Somoza refused, and the effort broke down in early 1979. The failure had large consequences. It discredited the moderate opposition's claim that negotiation could succeed, and it strengthened the position of the armed groups. It also left the United States without a plan, since Washington neither wished to use force to keep Somoza in power nor was prepared to accept a Sandinista government.

Regional governments, meanwhile, were not neutral. Costa Rica's president Rodrigo Carazo allowed Sandinista forces to use his country's territory for a southern front, while Panama's leader Omar Torrijos and Venezuela's president Carlos Andrés Pérez provided political support, and, in some accounts, arms and funds; Cuba supplied weapons and encouraged the Sandinista tendencies to unify. Somoza, for his part, obtained arms from Israel and other sources after American supplies ended. These details show that the conflict had a regional dimension, but they do not make Nicaraguans instruments of outside powers; the actions of these governments reflected their own interests, including hostility to a dictator and fear of regional instability, and the main burden of fighting and dying was borne by Nicaraguans.

After the September battles, diplomatic efforts became inseparable from the humanitarian emergency. The International Commission of Friendly Cooperation and Conciliation, with representatives of the United States, Dominican Republic, and Guatemala, entered the crisis as an effort to broker a political route out of dictatorship. Its mandate and proposals reflected the interests of governments anxious about revolutionary change as well as genuine concern about bloodshed. Somoza resisted an internationally supervised plebiscite that might dislodge him. Civic opposition leaders could not accept a settlement that simply restored the ruler's power. The FSLN, meanwhile, was preparing for another round of fighting.

Civilian displacement affected the entire calculus. Families left the worst-hit urban neighborhoods and crossed borders; rural areas experienced military sweeps and disruptions of harvests; humanitarian organizations faced obstacles in delivering food and medicine. A later UN economic assessment estimated approximately 35,000 deaths from the intensive conflict of September 1978–July 1979, most of them civilians, while subsequent scholarship offers a range of estimates. These are reconstructions, not a final individually identified victim register. Their importance lies both in the scale of loss and in the warning against turning uncertain totals into precise claims.

Costa Rica's role combined asylum for Nicaraguan refugees, diplomacy, and tolerance for some anti-Somoza operations on its territory. Panama under Omar Torrijos and Venezuela under Carlos Andrés Pérez were sympathetic to removing the dictatorship, although the types and extent of material assistance differed. Cuba's relationship with revolutionary organizers became more important as coordination accelerated. These governments were not interchangeable members of one alliance: regional support developed from concern for security, political ideology, opposition to Somoza, and assessment of U.S. policy.

A map of refugee flows and political offices illustrates why Nicaragua's revolution cannot be analyzed as an entirely domestic contest. Yet regional assistance did not determine the outcome on its own. Nicaraguan organizers still had to win support in towns and countryside, while the Guardia's officers decided whether to fight, desert, or seek a protected exit. External diplomacy was one strand of a larger crisis. The following division traces how military initiative, alliances, and foreign policy converged during the final offensive.

Section 89.13

FSLN Reunification, the Group of Twelve, and Revolutionary Political Strategy, 1979

In March 1979, under strong encouragement from Cuba and other supporters, the three Sandinista tendencies announced their reunification and formed a nine-member National Directorate: Daniel and Humberto Ortega, Víctor Tirado, Tomás Borge, Henry Ruiz, Bayardo Arce, Jaime Wheelock, Luis Carrión, and Carlos Núñez. The reunification allowed the Front to coordinate its efforts, to receive arms more effectively, and to present a single political face. It also embedded within the national leadership an arrangement, parity among tendencies, that would shape how the revolutionary government made decisions.

The divisions within the FSLN had emerged from strategic disagreements over the location and tempo of revolution. Advocates of prolonged people's war expected to construct strength among rural communities over time. The proletarian tendency emphasized workers, class organization, and urban political development. The Terceristas promoted broader alliances and a general insurrection in which business groups and non-Marxist opposition could cooperate with armed revolutionaries. These labels are analytical tools, not wholly separate social worlds: militants crossed between tendencies, and all factions made practical adjustments when circumstances changed.

The March 1979 reunification brought the tendencies under a nine-member National Directorate composed of three representatives of each current. Reunification mattered because separate organizations could otherwise compete for arms, command channels, diplomatic representation, and access to towns. The integrated directorate did not end disagreements over future government, but it created an institution capable of presenting one leadership during the decisive campaign. The involvement of Cuban officials and other supporters in encouraging agreement should be acknowledged without substituting their influence for the choices made by Nicaraguan leaders.

The Group of Twelve had already given the Terceristas a connection to prominent priests, intellectuals, businessmen, and professionals. These intermediaries were valuable to foreign governments and to domestic sectors uneasy about rule by a single clandestine party. The revolutionary movement's public promise of pluralism, political rights, and a mixed economy was therefore more than a propaganda slogan; it was part of the coalition-building necessary to remove Somoza. Yet terms such as 'pluralism' could be interpreted differently by a revolutionary vanguard and by independent opposition leaders. That ambiguity was manageable while the dynasty remained the common enemy and more consequential after July 1979.

The political reconstruction program issued in 1979 reflected this bargaining. It was designed to make the new government recognizable as a national administration, not merely an armed front, and to reassure outside donors while promising substantial social transformation. An effective historical account compares the 1969 FSLN Historical Program, written before the Front had a realistic prospect of power, with the 1979 Government of National Reconstruction program, negotiated amid a much wider alliance. Their differences reveal adaptation to a changed political situation rather than proof that either document should be read as an exact prediction of policy.

Section 89.14

The Final Offensive: Southern Front, Cities, and Popular Insurrection, May–July 1979

In May and June 1979, the Front launched what it called its final offensive. A Southern Front operated from Costa Rica under Edén Pastora; units in the north and west attacked Guardia garrisons; and in the cities, neighborhood committees and young fighters, known as muchachos, built barricades of paving stones and fought the Guardia street by street. A general strike called in early June paralyzed commerce. In Managua, the Guardia bombed and shelled working-class districts. On 20 June, an American television crew from ABC filmed a Guardia soldier shooting the network's correspondent, Bill Stewart, as he lay on the ground, and the footage, broadcast in the United States, provoked outrage. At the end of June, the Sandinista forces carried out a tactical withdrawal from Managua to Masaya, taking thousands of civilians and fighters with them, so as to avoid destruction of the capital's remaining districts; the Guardia, still strong in the capital, could not pursue effectively.

The final campaign joined several forms of combat that are difficult to capture with one military label. A southern front, operating with support networks reaching into Costa Rica, attempted to tie down Guard units near the frontier. In the western and northern cities, FSLN detachments, local activists, and neighborhood defenders confronted garrisons and sought to hold territory. Disrupting roads, supplies, and communications became as important as any single battle. At the same time, industrial and commercial strikes weakened normal government activity; health and food shortages made the cost of prolonged combat immediately visible.

The urban campaigns were neither uniform nor automatically successful. León and Estelí became major theaters of fighting, while Managua presented the most difficult combination of population density, military installations, and government defenses. When communities erected barricades they sometimes isolated Guard units, but also exposed civilians to retaliation. Aviation and artillery offered the regime means of punishment that the insurgents could not match. Civilians therefore faced a choice among remaining in endangered neighborhoods, moving through uncertain corridors, or crossing national borders. Religious workers and relief organizations became critical intermediaries, despite restrictions on access.

Individual commanders' decisions shaped the tempo of events. The Guard was an institution bound together by promotion, patronage, and family loyalty, but the pressures of simultaneous revolts exposed its limits. Soldiers could remain fiercely loyal in one city while units elsewhere lost cohesion. Officers had to choose between defending their immediate posts and reinforcing threatened colleagues. Attrition, desertion, uncertainty about foreign assistance, and Somoza's increasingly narrow political options complicated the regime's military response. It was a cumulative institutional failure, not a single battlefield defeat.

The FSLN's ability to coordinate insurrection with negotiation gave its leaders a stronger position by June. Yet the social meaning of victory varied: for political prisoners and their relatives it might mean release; for landless agricultural laborers, a prospect of reform; for business leaders, the end of monopolistic patronage; and for many on the Caribbean coast, an uncertain encounter with political forces centered in the Pacific. The strength of the anti-Somoza coalition should not be mistaken for consensus about what would replace the dynasty.

Historical Vocabulary
Insurrección

An insurrección (insurrection) in Sandinista usage was a mass, urban, largely neighborhood-based armed uprising, as distinct from a rural guerrilla campaign by a small vanguard.

The Tercerista strategy relied on insurrection as the means of toppling the regime: young people in working-class barrios, often with minimal training and homemade or captured weapons, fought the Guardia while civilians provided food, shelter, and information. The term helps explain why the revolution cannot be reduced to the story of a few hundred trained guerrillas. At the same time, it should not be taken to imply that all Nicaraguans supported the uprising; many people in these communities were caught in the violence, and the Guardia's reprisals targeted entire neighborhoods.

Section 89.15

International Diplomacy, the OAS, and the Provisional Government, June 1979

Diplomacy ran in parallel. In mid-June the Sandinista leadership announced in Costa Rica the formation of a five-person Junta of National Reconstruction: Daniel Ortega, the writer Sergio Ramírez, the physicist and businessman Moisés Hassan, the businessman Alfonso Robelo, and Violeta Chamorro. The Junta combined Sandinista and non-Sandinista figures, an attempt to present the future government as pluralist. At the Organization of American States in late June, the United States proposed an inter-American peacekeeping force that would have prevented a Sandinista takeover, and the proposal was rejected by most members; the OAS instead called for the replacement of the Somoza regime. The Carter administration's last efforts to arrange a transition that kept parts of the Guardia intact failed, and on 17 July 1979 Somoza resigned, handing power to an interim figure, Francisco Urcuyo, and flew to Miami. Urcuyo, instead of arranging a transfer, announced he would stay in office, and he fled to Guatemala the following day. The Guardia disintegrated, with many officers and soldiers fleeing toward Honduras and Costa Rica. On 19 July the Sandinista columns entered Managua, and the Junta arrived the next day.

The final negotiations revealed a struggle over the form of the state that would survive Somoza. Washington wanted the ruler to go but also wished to avoid the sudden collapse of the National Guard and a government dominated wholly by the FSLN. This position differed from simply defending Somoza until the end. The State Department's documentary record shows a series of proposals involving a cease-fire, a broadly representative transitional government, humanitarian relief, and reform or merger of military forces. These plans were responses to a rapidly changing battlefield, not evidence of a single coherent policy from 1977 onward.

The Organization of American States became a decisive diplomatic arena in June 1979. U.S. officials considered a peacekeeping force and a transition arrangement intended to forestall revolutionary monopoly. Other American governments resisted external military intervention and emphasized Nicaraguan sovereignty and the removal of Somoza. The eventual OAS resolution demanded the regime's replacement and called for a broadly representative government, respect for human rights, and future free elections. That resolution did not authorize an invasion to install the Sandinistas or establish that they alone represented the nation. It reflected an unusually wide agreement that the dictatorship's continuation was unacceptable.

The June 16 provisional government announcement in Costa Rica also had practical diplomatic purposes. Five designated junta members—Daniel Ortega, Sergio Ramírez, Moisés Hassan, Violeta Barrios de Chamorro, and Alfonso Robelo—could communicate with foreign states, humanitarian organizations, and potential donors as representatives of an emerging administration. Their different backgrounds made the coalition visible. Yet formal representation did not resolve how decisions would be made, and the armed movement's nine-member directorate retained organizational power outside the provisional government's public structure.

Declassified U.S. memoranda are essential evidence but must be read for their institutional vantage. A June 20 Brzezinski memorandum records the Carter administration's concern that a Sandinista-dominated outcome could be imminent and its desire for an alternative settlement. Such a document demonstrates what senior U.S. officials discussed, not how the FSLN leadership privately understood its own strategy. A balanced reconstruction places it beside OAS resolutions, the reconstruction program, contemporary Nicaraguan reporting, and later testimony.

Section 89.16

July 17–20, 1979: Somoza’s Departure, Urcuyo’s Failure, and Revolutionary Victory

The war had been costly. Estimates of the dead for the final period of fighting and its antecedents are commonly given between thirty thousand and fifty thousand, though precise numbers are not available, and large numbers of people were wounded, displaced, or left homeless. The economy was devastated: much of the productive infrastructure was damaged, the treasury was nearly empty, the new government inherited a foreign debt of well over a billion dollars, and Somoza's departure was accompanied by the flight of much of the country's remaining capital. Somoza himself went into exile and was assassinated in Asunción, Paraguay, in September 1980. The dynasty's end was complete, but the revolutionary government faced the task of governing a devastated country with no settled model, an untested leadership, and a coalition held together mainly by a shared enemy.

The last days of the Somoza presidency contained a brief but consequential attempt at controlled succession. After negotiations involving U.S. diplomats, Somoza resigned and left Nicaragua on July 17. The plan envisaged a successor who would quickly transfer authority to a broadly based government while preserving a force capable of providing public order. Instead Francisco Urcuyo, selected through the remaining constitutional machinery, announced that he intended to serve through the remainder of the presidential term. His effort was widely rejected and collapsed almost immediately as the Guard disintegrated.

This episode illustrates the distance between written constitutional succession and effective authority. The old institutions could still produce a president on paper, but the army no longer possessed the capacity or confidence to enforce that claim across Nicaragua. Many Guard members sought refuge in embassies, fled toward borders, or attempted to dissolve into civilian life. Sandinista fighters moved into Managua on July 19 amid celebrations, uncertainty, and widespread destruction. The Government of National Reconstruction formally began its administration the following day. The collapse of the Guardia was therefore a prerequisite for the transfer of actual power, while the junta's announcement supplied the constitutional and diplomatic vocabulary for the new order.

The conflict's human and material cost remained. An August 1979 UN economic assessment described destroyed housing, disrupted trade, damaged agriculture and factories, displaced households, and large numbers of dead or injured. Its estimates reflected incomplete information gathered immediately after the fighting. The commonly cited figure of roughly 35,000 deaths in the intense 1978–1979 conflict should be attributed to contemporary UN-linked estimates; it is not a known exact list of victims. Exaggerated certainty about casualties obscures the practical difficulties confronting families and reconstruction officials alike.

Victory also presented immediate problems of justice and restraint. Former Guard members were suspected of abuses, yet indiscriminate retaliation risked contradicting the new government's public commitment to legal procedure. Some communities celebrated the end of coercive local power; others worried about retribution or the destruction of government services. The question was whether a new government could replace the dynasty's armed institutions while preserving order and protecting political rights. The answer was not determined by crowds entering the capital, nor could it be reduced to the later Contra War.

Division V — The First Revolutionary Government and Its Contradictions

Section 89.17

The Junta of National Reconstruction, FSLN Directorate, and the New State

The new government's institutional structure was complicated and in some respects ambiguous. Formal executive authority lay with the five-member Junta of National Reconstruction, which governed under a Fundamental Statute issued in July 1979 that abolished the Somoza constitution and the Congress and was followed by a Statute of Rights and Guarantees. In practice, the nine-member FSLN National Directorate, which was not a formal organ of the state, made the major political decisions, and the Sandinista Front controlled the army, the police, and the mass organizations. The ministries were staffed by a mixture of Sandinistas and non-Sandinista technocrats, including priests such as Ernesto Cardenal at culture, Fernando Cardenal in the literacy campaign, and Miguel d'Escoto at foreign affairs. The arrangement gave the government a broad appearance while ensuring that the Front retained control of the main levers of power.

The new rulers faced the problem of the defeated Guardia. Thousands of former guardsmen were detained, and special tribunals tried those accused of crimes; the government abolished the death penalty and imposed prison terms, and critics from some human-rights organizations pointed to irregularities in proceedings and to instances of mistreatment and summary killings in the first days, while supporters stressed that the revolution had avoided mass executions on the scale many had feared. The government created a new army, the Sandinista Popular Army, under Humberto Ortega, as well as a Sandinista Police and a state security apparatus, with advisers from Cuba. The revolution's armed institutions were built from the guerrilla forces, and they were explicitly partisan, a feature that distinguished the new state from the nonpartisan model once imagined for the Guardia.

The public executive was a five-person Junta of National Reconstruction; the nine-member FSLN National Directorate, by contrast, controlled the revolutionary movement's political and military organization. This duality created uncertainties about where decisive authority rested. The five junta members came from different political backgrounds and had accepted a program promising democratic participation, national reconstruction, and respect for pluralism. Their membership gave the new administration a broader appearance than a one-party cabinet would have had. But the FSLN had won the military struggle and controlled the armed personnel and organizational networks essential to implementing decisions.

The Council of State, conceived in the Government of National Reconstruction program as a representative body of political and social organizations, was another site of tension. Who would be represented, how seats would be allocated, and whether organized groups controlled by the FSLN could form a majority were questions about the substance of political equality. In April 1980 changes to the Council's composition contributed to the resignations of Violeta Chamorro and Alfonso Robelo. Their departure was a key indicator that the coalition that defeated Somoza did not agree about the structure of the new political order. It did not mean all private enterprise disappeared or that every participant in the 1979 alliance switched immediately to opposition.

The creation of new security institutions posed dilemmas that neither revolutionary legitimacy nor anti-dictatorial rhetoric could solve alone. The army and police were built partly from successful guerrilla cadres and associated with a governing movement that regarded itself as the revolution's defender. Supporters saw this as necessary to prevent the return of the Guardia and protect reforms; critics questioned the absence of nonpartisan oversight and the potential for surveillance. The IACHR's return visit in 1980 assessed the new government's practices, demonstrating that the removal of a repressive dictatorship did not end the need for independent human-rights monitoring.

Administration was difficult after war. Ministries required staff, budgets, medical supplies, schools, and working infrastructure. Experienced civil servants could be essential even if they had served the former state. For the poorest households, the immediate measure of a new government might be whether food reached markets, displaced relatives returned home, and a school reopened. Those practical issues help explain why the distinction between political authority and administrative capacity was central to revolutionary reconstruction.

Section 89.18

Confiscation, the Mixed Economy, Land Reform, and Reconstruction

Economic policy was described by its authors as a "mixed economy." The state confiscated the property of Somoza and his associates, creating the Area of People's Property, which included a large share of the country's agricultural land and industry. The government nationalized banks, insurance companies, mines, and foreign trade in key commodities, while leaving much of the productive economy, particularly medium-sized agriculture and commerce, in private hands, at least initially. A land reform law in 1981 expanded the program. The Front hoped to encourage private producers to continue investing, but tensions over credit, prices, confiscations, and the government's political direction soon discouraged some. The government also depended on foreign assistance, including loans and aid from Western Europe, Latin America, the Soviet bloc, and, for a short time, the United States. In 1980 the Carter administration obtained congressional approval for seventy-five million dollars in aid, much of it intended for the private sector, though the aid was entangled in domestic American politics and was later suspended.

The confiscation of Somoza family holdings and properties associated with those who fled after the revolution created an important state-controlled economic sector. The July 1979 reconstruction program presented this as part of a mixed economy rather than a commitment to eliminate all private enterprise. Banking, external trade, key agricultural enterprises, and public finance were reorganized; private producers nevertheless continued to supply exports and basic goods. For the state, controlling coffee, cotton, sugar, and cattle revenues offered resources for reconstruction, while the collapse of production or withdrawal of business investment threatened its budget and access to imports.

The agrarian question was not simply how many hectares moved from one legal owner to another. Landless workers wanted secure livelihoods, small farmers sought access to plots and credit, and state planners favored maintaining large units capable of producing export revenue. Confiscated estates could be operated by state farms, cooperatives, or other associations instead of divided into individual parcels. These arrangements produced disagreements over productivity, labor rights, access to services, and local authority. The Inter-American Commission's early assessment of agrarian policy distinguished declared redistribution goals from the actual administrative forms created during 1979–1980.

Economic reconstruction was constrained by war damage and debt. A factory without spare parts could not resume production merely because its ownership had changed; a damaged road impeded both crops reaching markets and medicine reaching clinics. Foreign exchange was needed for fuel, machinery, and food. The government sought external loans and assistance from a variety of sources, including Western governments and international institutions, while forging closer ties to Cuba. That mix complicates depictions of the new state as already wholly subordinated to either side in the Cold War.

The political economy of the revolution was therefore a field of negotiation among ministers, employers, unions, peasant organizations, and international lenders. Supporters understood confiscation as restitution of wealth accumulated through dictatorship and as a means of providing services. Critics feared arbitrary property procedures, politicized management, and eventual elimination of independent economic actors. The differences cannot be settled by the language of official decrees alone: farm accounts, production data, union records, and household evidence are needed to assess implementation. The longer consequences of war and U.S. economic pressure belong in Chapter 90.

Section 89.19

The 1980 Literacy Crusade, Public Health, Women, and Mass Organizations

The revolution's most celebrated early achievement was the Literacy Crusade of March to August 1980. Tens of thousands of volunteers, many of them teenagers, with an often-cited figure of about sixty thousand young brigadistas, went to live in rural communities and teach reading and writing to peasants. Officially reported figures held that illiteracy fell from roughly half of the population to about thirteen percent, a result that international observers considered a major gain even as some questioned the precise statistics. The campaign also brought young urban Nicaraguans into direct contact with rural poverty, strengthened ties between the Front and rural communities, and cost the lives of several brigadistas killed by counterrevolutionary groups. Parallel campaigns in public health, including vaccination drives, reduced the incidence of preventable diseases and were praised by international health organizations. The government also built mass organizations: the Sandinista Defense Committees, neighborhood bodies that distributed rations, organized security, and mobilized support; AMNLAE, for women; labor federations; and associations for peasants and youth.

These organizations raised a persistent question about representation. Supporters regarded them as vehicles of popular participation that gave poor Nicaraguans, for the first time, a voice in public life. Critics, including some former allies, argued that they functioned as instruments of party control and that independent organizations were disadvantaged or harassed. The evidence suggests that both elements existed: the committees did involve large numbers of people in local organization and service delivery, and they were also used for political mobilization and surveillance. Historians continue to debate where the balance lay and how it changed over time, in part because most documentation comes from partisan sources.

The National Literacy Crusade began in March 1980 and continued through August. Its central educational achievement was not merely a lower estimated illiteracy percentage but a vast encounter between urban adolescents and rural households. Volunteers learned to teach, often living with families whose work routines and material circumstances were unfamiliar to them. Rural adults decided whether and how to participate while also maintaining crops, childcare, and household labor. Campaign reports, exercise books, volunteer diaries, and photographs survive in the collection recognized by UNESCO's Memory of the World programme. Those records enable historians to examine how teaching was organized, not only its celebrated results.

UNESCO summarizes official outcomes as an approximate reduction in illiteracy from 50 to 12 percent. This achievement deserves credit, but the estimate depends on the campaign's definition and measurement of literacy, and it cannot show whether every learner retained their skills or whether remote language communities were equally served. A complete analysis therefore asks whose language was used in instruction, what textbooks emphasized, whether adults found continued educational opportunities, and how the government balanced literacy with political mobilization. The teaching materials promoted participation in a revolutionary national project, an aspect viewed as inspiring by supporters and ideological by critics.

Public-health measures were another visible part of reconstruction. Vaccination campaigns and local clinics sought to address diseases associated with poverty and war-disrupted sanitation. Medical personnel and community volunteers delivered services in districts that had rarely seen regular care before 1979. Success should be assessed with time-series health data, rather than assuming that every later improvement began with the revolution. Meanwhile an expanded presence of women in schools, community committees, production, and public office challenged established hierarchies, but unpaid domestic work and gender inequalities persisted. The Association of Nicaraguan Women Luisa Amanda Espinoza (AMNLAE) and other organizations expressed both emancipatory aspirations and alignment with the FSLN.

Local Sandinista Defense Committees coordinated sanitation, distribution, celebrations, neighborhood surveillance, and political participation. They could connect citizens to services and mobilize those previously excluded from public affairs, while critics warned that membership or political loyalty might affect the treatment of dissenters. Everyday life therefore reflected the promise and the tensions of the new government together: one family could celebrate adult literacy, depend on a neighborhood committee for medicine, and worry about the limited space for opposing the governing movement.

Everyday Life and Social History

Barrios, Brigadistas, and the Households of the Revolution

For ordinary Nicaraguans the years from 1972 to 1980 were a succession of upheavals. The earthquake left tens of thousands of Managua families in makeshift shelters or relocated to new peripheral settlements, with markets and workplaces destroyed. During the insurrections, entire neighborhoods organized for survival: residents built barricades, dug shelters, hid young people from the Guardia, cooked for fighters, and cared for the wounded. Women, who had long managed households under scarcity, played central roles in these networks, and many of those who served as couriers, nurses, and cooks also fought. The Guardia's reprisals fell on families as well as fighters, and mothers of the killed and disappeared became public figures in the late 1970s.

After the victory, daily life changed again. Young volunteers in the Literacy Crusade lived in peasant households, often for the first time encountering rural poverty at close range, and peasant adults, some of them elderly, learned to write their names. Neighborhood committees distributed basic goods and organized cleanups and security. At the same time shortages, price controls, and the departure of professionals and merchants created new difficulties, and families divided over politics. The sources record these experiences unevenly: official publications emphasize achievements, opposition newspapers emphasize hardship, and the testimonies of ordinary people, collected in oral histories, are essential but shaped by later memory.

Section 89.20

The Fracturing Coalition: Pluralism, Church, Caribbean Coast, and Foreign Relations, 1979–1981

The political conflict should be framed as a struggle over the meaning of the revolution, not as an immediate replay of the Cold War. The 1979 reconstruction programme promised democratic participation and a mixed economy. By 1980 the FSLN Directorate defended its authority as necessary to protect the revolution, while several former allies demanded stronger independent political institutions and a clear timetable for elections. A distinction is essential: Decree No. 513 of September 1980 specified that the electoral process would begin in January 1984 and elections would occur in 1985. The vote was subsequently scheduled for November 1984, which belongs in the following chapter. Calling the earlier proposal a 1984 election is inaccurate.

Relations with the Catholic hierarchy also changed through stages rather than a simple reversal. Priests who had supported resistance and joined government ministries continued to see the social programmes as consistent with their faith; bishops who had criticized Somoza could object to the dominance of a political movement in schools and public organizations. Ordinary believers did not line up uniformly behind either position. The resulting disputes reflected genuine differences over religious authority, political ideology, and the rights of independent institutions. They were not adequately captured by describing all Christians as supporters or enemies of the revolution.

On the Caribbean coast, Miskitu, Mayangna, Rama, and Creole communities had different histories of landholding, language, church organization, and political relations with Managua. Some participated in the changes of 1979, while others questioned policies designed by Pacific leaders who understood national integration primarily in class terms. MISURASATA became one forum for claims about land, schooling, autonomy, and representation. Early tensions must be explained on their own terms, without projecting back the later escalation of fighting and mass relocations of 1982. Those subsequent developments are central to Chapter 90.

Relations with Washington remained unsettled across the Carter–Reagan transition. The Carter administration attempted cooperation through aid and diplomacy while pressing for political pluralism. The new Reagan administration viewed the FSLN with greater suspicion and moved toward supporting armed opponents. Describing the later policy merely as the inevitable response to 1979 would remove the agency of both governments and of Nicaraguan opposition groups. In 1980–1981 the revolution was still making institutional choices under severe pressure, and its future political form remained contested.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did the Somoza Regime Fall?

The question: Was the overthrow of Somoza primarily the result of a mass popular insurrection and the Sandinista Front's organization, or of the regime's loss of elite, institutional, and international support?

Interpretation A — A popular insurrection led by a revolutionary movement: Some scholars emphasize the depth of social grievance, the decades of organizing by the Front, the role of Christian base communities, and the extraordinary scale of popular participation in 1978–1979, from Monimbó to the Managua barrios. On this view, the regime fell because a broad and organized popular movement defeated it in the field and in the cities, and external support for the Front only supplemented a domestic revolution.
Interpretation B — A regime that lost its supports: Others emphasize that the dynasty was undermined by the withdrawal of business support after 1972, by the cut in American military aid and the failure of Washington to find an alternative, by the support of regional governments for the Front, and by Somoza's own personalization of the economy. On this view, the Sandinistas won because the regime had become isolated, and the Terceristas' alliance strategy, rather than rural guerrilla organizing, proved decisive.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of material: U.S. diplomatic records, Guardia and government documents, newspaper reporting, human-rights investigations, Sandinista memoirs and testimonies, and interviews with participants. Each type of source reflects a perspective, and none gives a full measure of public opinion under a dictatorship.

Why it matters: The question affects how the later revolution is understood. If the victory was primarily a popular insurrection, the Front's claim to represent the nation looks stronger; if the victory depended on a broad coalition and external circumstances, then the subsequent narrowing of the coalition raises sharper questions about the government's legitimacy. In practice the two interpretations overlap, and most historians combine them, differing mainly over emphasis.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Dynasty to Revolutionary State, 1977–1980

What Changed

  • The Guardia was dissolved and replaced by an army built from the guerrilla forces
  • Somoza properties were confiscated and a large state sector created
  • Mass organizations and literacy and health campaigns reached the poor in new ways
  • Women, young people, and urban poor entered public life on a scale unknown before
  • The government's international alignment shifted toward Cuba and the Soviet bloc

What Stayed the Same

  • The economy remained dependent on agricultural exports and foreign credit
  • Political power remained highly concentrated in a small leadership
  • The armed forces remained partisan rather than nonpartisan
  • The Caribbean coast remained at a distance from Managua's priorities
  • The United States remained the dominant external actor in Nicaraguan affairs

Who experienced change most directly: Former Guardia members and Somoza associates lost everything; poor rural and urban families gained access to schooling and health care; and women gained organizational voice. Greater continuity: Coastal communities, many small farmers, and the structure of the export economy. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a political revolution can transform institutions faster than social and economic structures, and that the gap between declared aims and achieved outcomes shaped the conflicts of the 1980s.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Fall of the Somoza Dynasty

Long-term causes

The legacy of U.S. occupation and the creation of the Guardia; Sandino's murder and nationalist memory; concentration of land and wealth in a narrow elite and the Somoza family; displacement of peasants by export agriculture; and decades of political exclusion.

Short-term causes

The 1972 earthquake and corruption; the alienation of business sectors; the Church's turn toward the poor; repression in the countryside; the Carter administration's human-rights policy; and Chamorro's assassination.

Event or transformation

The insurrections of 1978 and the final offensive of 1979, culminating in Somoza's departure and the Guardia's disintegration.

Immediate effects

Formation of a revolutionary government, dissolution of the Guardia, confiscation of Somoza properties, and the beginning of mass social programs.

Long-term consequences

Sharpened Central American and Cold War conflict, the Contra war, a decade of political polarization in Nicaragua, and a lasting debate over the meaning of the revolution.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Social grievance, organization, elite defection, external policy, and chance events combined, and the victory did not determine what would follow.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Nicaragua's path from the Somoza dynasty to the Sandinista revolution and argued that the revolution resulted from the convergence of long-term structures, a clandestine movement, and a rapid loss of support by the regime. The Somoza family held power for more than four decades through control of the National Guard, management of political parties, economic holdings, and a long alignment with the United States, but those same features made it vulnerable when its elite and external supports weakened.

The origins lay in the United States occupation of 1912–1933, which created the Guardia and bred a nationalist resistance under Augusto César Sandino. Sandino's war against the Marines made him a national symbol, and his murder in 1934 by Guardia officers cleared the way for Somoza García's rise. The Somoza system rested on the Guardia, the Liberal Party, pacts with Conservatives, family business holdings, and cooperation with Washington, and it passed to his sons after his assassination in 1956.

Under Luis and Anastasio Somoza Debayle the regime presided over economic growth that benefited a narrow elite and displaced many peasants. Opposition took several forms. The Sandinista National Liberation Front, founded in 1961, spent years in failure and clandestinity, while parts of the Catholic Church, influenced by Medellín and liberation theology, built Christian base communities that linked religious commitment to social criticism. These developments gave the opposition a social base that formal parties lacked.

The 1972 earthquake exposed the regime's corruption, alienated business groups, and weakened its legitimacy. The Front's 1974 raid raised its profile and provoked a long state of siege and brutal rural repression. Facing that pressure, the Front divided into three tendencies, and the Tercerista strategy of broad alliances, including the Group of Twelve, gave the movement a respectable civilian face and allowed non-Marxists to support it. Women played significant roles throughout, as combatants, organizers, and leaders.

The international context shifted in 1977 when the Carter administration reduced military aid on human-rights grounds. The assassination of Pedro Joaquín Chamorro in January 1978 produced mass protest and a business-backed general strike, and the Indigenous neighborhood of Monimbó rose against the Guardia. The August 1978 seizure of the National Palace humiliated the regime, and the September urban insurrections, though crushed at great civilian cost, destroyed the regime's international standing. American-led mediation failed because Somoza refused to accept a plebiscite and Washington had no alternative.

In 1979 the Sandinista tendencies reunited under a nine-member National Directorate, and a final offensive combined a southern front from Costa Rica, rural and urban fighting, and a general strike. Regional governments supported the movement, and the Organization of American States rejected an American proposal that would have blocked its victory. Somoza resigned on 17 July, and the Guardia collapsed. The Sandinistas entered Managua on 19 July 1979, at the end of a war that killed many tens of thousands of people.

The revolutionary government created a Junta of National Reconstruction, retained real power in the FSLN National Directorate, built a new partisan army, confiscated Somoza properties, nationalized banks and key resources, and launched major social programs, including the 1980 Literacy Crusade and public-health campaigns. Mass organizations extended participation to many Nicaraguans and also served as instruments of mobilization and control, and historians continue to debate the balance.

The coalition began to fray within months. Non-Sandinista Junta members resigned in 1980, business organizations and the Church hierarchy moved toward opposition, relations with the Miskitu and other Caribbean-coast peoples deteriorated, and the Reagan administration suspended aid and began covert support for the Sandinistas' armed opponents. The chapter ends with a revolution that had won power and begun to govern but whose character and survival were not yet settled. The Contra war, the elections, and the end of Sandinista rule are the subject of Chapter 90.

Students should also take away a method. The sources for this period, including diplomatic records, Sandinista programs and memoirs, opposition journalism, human-rights reports, and oral testimony, each carry a perspective and a purpose. Understanding a revolution requires reading them against one another, distinguishing declared aims from practice, and remembering whose voices, among them those of coastal communities, rural women, and ordinary Guardia conscripts, are least well represented.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the fall of the Somoza dynasty was the product of many forces acting together: a nationalist memory born in resistance to occupation, a regime that grew rich on a narrow base, an underground movement that survived defeat and division, a Church that turned toward the poor, a business class that lost patience, a foreign policy that shifted at a critical moment, and the courage and suffering of tens of thousands of ordinary Nicaraguans. No single factor is sufficient, and understanding how they combined is the main task of the chapter.

The chapter also shows how the history of one small country connected to larger processes. The Nicaraguan story is a chapter in the history of United States intervention and its consequences, of Cold War politics in Central America, of the Latin American Church's transformation after Medellín, and of the revolutionary tradition that ran from Mexico through Cuba. At the same time, it resists being treated only as an episode in someone else's story. Nicaraguans made the revolution, with their own motives, divisions, and sacrifices, and the movement's conflicts with the Church, business, and the Caribbean coast were as much domestic as external.

Chapter 90 takes up what followed. A revolutionary government that had won power in 1979 soon faced armed opposition supported by the United States, a regional war, a divided society, and elections whose meaning remained contested. Students should carry forward the view that the revolution of 1979 was neither a finished achievement nor a doomed experiment, but a beginning whose outcome depended on choices still to be made, and that the early tensions identified here shaped how those choices were made.
