Wari and Tiwanaku

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Two Highlands, Two States, One New Scale of Andean Life

Sometime in the middle centuries of the first millennium CE, travelers moving along the high, thin air of the Andean cordillera might have crossed paths with caravans of llamas carrying maize from warm valleys toward cold highland storehouses, with parties of masons hauling cut stone toward a growing terrace of temples beside a great lake, and with officials overseeing the construction of planned compounds hundreds of kilometers from the cities that had ordered them built. Two highland states — Wari, centered near modern Ayacucho in Peru, and Tiwanaku, centered near the southern shore of Lake Titicaca — were, at the same historical moment though not always by the same methods, reorganizing the political geography of the Andes on a scale the region had not seen before.

Chapter 17 examined Paracas, Nazca, and Moche as societies rooted in particular coastal valleys, developing extraordinary textile, ceramic, hydraulic, and artistic traditions within landscapes defined largely by single rivers descending from the mountains to the Pacific. This chapter shifts the scale of analysis. Between roughly the sixth and tenth centuries CE — a span archaeologists conventionally call the Middle Horizon, though its exact boundaries vary from region to region — two highland societies built political, religious, and economic systems that reached far beyond their home valleys, linking distant and ecologically distinct regions of the Andes through settlement, exchange, agriculture, religious imagery, and, in Wari's case, an extensive network of roads and planned administrative centers.

Wari and Tiwanaku are often introduced together in survey accounts of Andean prehistory, and for good reason: both flourished during roughly the same centuries, both employed strikingly similar religious imagery centered on a frontal, staff-bearing deity flanked by attendant figures, and both eventually declined or transformed around the close of the first millennium CE, opening the way for the regional kingdoms examined in Chapter 19. But treating Wari and Tiwanaku as a single phenomenon, or as two branches of one civilization, would seriously misrepresent the historical record. They developed in different highland environments — Wari in the puna and intermontane valleys around Ayacucho, at moderate elevation, and Tiwanaku on the treeless altiplano surrounding Lake Titicaca, at an elevation exceeding 3,800 meters — and, on current archaeological evidence, they built their systems of integration through partly different means. Wari archaeology is strongly associated with purpose-built administrative centers, colonial settlements, and roads connecting distant provinces to the Ayacucho heartland, sometimes with clear evidence of direct political intervention in local affairs. Tiwanaku archaeology is anchored by an extraordinary urban and ceremonial capital beside Lake Titicaca, by intensive raised-field agriculture, by long-distance llama-caravan exchange, and, increasingly, by evidence of colonies and even provincial temple complexes far from the Titicaca Basin — though the exact character, extent, and uniformity of Tiwanaku political control remain subjects of active archaeological debate.

This chapter also introduces a caution that will recur throughout Part IV: the temptation to read the much later, much better documented Inca Empire, examined in Chapter 20, backward into the Middle Horizon. Wari roads were not the Inca road system, even if some later routes may have reused portions of earlier corridors. Wari and Tiwanaku administrative practices were not simply earlier drafts of the Inca mit'a, the Inca system of provincial governors, or the Inca use of quipu record-keeping. Chimú and the early Inca state, examined in Chapter 19, developed within a political landscape that had already been substantially reshaped by the decline of Wari and Tiwanaku, not as their direct continuation. Students should resist any narrative in which Middle Horizon history exists chiefly to foreshadow Tawantinsuyu. Wari and Tiwanaku deserve to be understood on their own historical terms, as ambitious and only partially comparable experiments in extending political, economic, and religious authority across some of the most challenging terrain inhabited by any complex society in world history.

The evidence available for this chapter comes almost entirely from archaeology — excavated administrative centers, urban architecture, agricultural infrastructure, ceramics, textiles, and, increasingly, chemical and isotopic analysis of human remains and artifacts that can reveal where people and goods originated and how far they traveled. Wari and Tiwanaku left no deciphered writing system, and later Inca and colonial-era accounts of the deep past, when they mention earlier ruined cities at all, blend historical memory with myth in ways that make them useful primarily for understanding later Andean interpretations of ancient ruins rather than as direct evidence about Wari or Tiwanaku society itself. This chapter's conclusions, like those in Chapter 2 regarding the peopling of the Americas, should therefore be read as the current state of an active and still-developing field of research, one in which a single new excavation — such as a major 2025 discovery at a Tiwanaku-associated temple site far from Lake Titicaca, discussed later in this chapter — can meaningfully change scholarly understanding within a matter of months.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold two highland stories in view at once without collapsing them into each other. It moves back and forth between Ayacucho and the Titicaca Basin, between Wari's provincial centers and Tiwanaku's raised fields, and, in its central comparative section, brings both societies together at a single contested frontier in the Moquegua Valley of southern Peru, where Wari and Tiwanaku settlements existed within a few kilometers of one another for generations. Understanding how two highland states of comparable ambition could develop side by side — sometimes exchanging goods and ideas, sometimes remaining culturally distinct, and, on current evidence, apparently without a documented war between them — is one of the most instructive comparative exercises available anywhere in ancient Andean history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Middle Horizon and explain why its chronological boundaries vary somewhat by region and by scholarly tradition.
  2. Describe the growth of the city of Huari (Wari) and its role as a political, religious, and economic center in the Ayacucho highlands.
  3. Explain how Wari expansion is documented through planned administrative centers such as Pikillacta, Viracochapampa, and Cerro Baúl, and why archaeologists no longer assume that every such center represented identical, direct imperial rule.
  4. Analyze the role of roads, agricultural intensification, and storage in Wari efforts to integrate distant provinces, while distinguishing these efforts from the later, more extensive Inca road system.
  5. Interpret Wari textiles, ceramics, and religious imagery as evidence of political and religious authority, without assuming a single, uniform state religion.
  6. Describe Tiwanaku's urban and ceremonial core beside Lake Titicaca, including its major monuments, and explain its combined civic, political, and religious functions.
  7. Explain the agricultural foundations of Tiwanaku society, particularly raised-field cultivation, and evaluate competing views of how centrally that agriculture was managed.
  8. Assess evidence for Tiwanaku colonies, caravan exchange, and provincial religious architecture beyond the Titicaca Basin, including recent archaeological discoveries.
  9. Compare Wari and Tiwanaku as two highland systems of integration that operated through partly different methods, avoiding the assumption that they were identical, equivalent, or a single joint civilization.
  10. Evaluate the Moquegua Valley as a documented zone of Wari–Tiwanaku contemporaneity and interaction, and distinguish supported interpretations from speculative claims of outright warfare between the two states.
  11. Explain the decline and transformation of Wari and Tiwanaku around the end of the first millennium CE without reducing either process to a single cause.
  12. Distinguish Middle Horizon highland states from the later Inca Empire, avoiding anachronistic projection of Inca institutions backward onto Wari or Tiwanaku.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did Wari attempt to integrate distant provinces, and how uniform was that integration across different regions?
  3. What was the relationship between Tiwanaku's urban-ceremonial core and its surrounding agricultural landscape?
  4. Why do archaeologists debate whether Wari and Tiwanaku should be called "empires," and what is at stake in that debate?
  5. What does the presence of both Wari and Tiwanaku settlements in the Moquegua Valley suggest about relations between the two states?

  8. Why is it inaccurate to describe Wari as the direct precursor of the Inca road system or Inca provincial administration?

Historical Context

Chapter 17 traced Paracas, Nazca, and Moche as regionally rooted coastal societies whose scale and degree of political integration varied considerably and remain debated — especially in the Moche case — rather than as a single coast-wide territorial state, even though their histories substantially overlapped in time rather than following a single tidy sequence. By the middle centuries of the first millennium CE, two societies located not on the coast but in the Andean highlands — Wari, in the Ayacucho basin of south-central Peru, and Tiwanaku, on the altiplano surrounding the southern shore of Lake Titicaca — had begun to build systems of political, economic, and religious integration operating at a dramatically larger geographic scale.

Both highland regions presented severe environmental challenges: high elevation, cold nighttime temperatures, frost risk, thin atmospheric oxygen, and limited land suitable for intensive agriculture without substantial engineering. Both regions also offered environmental advantages that helps explain why ambitious states emerged there rather than only on the resource-rich coast: access to camelid herding (llamas and alpacas, whose importance as pack animals, wool sources, and ritual offerings only increases in significance at this altitude), proximity to multiple lower-elevation ecological zones reachable within a few days' travel, and, in the Titicaca Basin, one of the largest bodies of fresh water in South America, whose thermal mass moderates local frost risk near its shores and supports productive fishing and reed-harvesting economies.

Historians and archaeologists commonly organize Central Andean prehistory into a sequence of "horizons" and "intermediate periods," in which horizons denote periods of unusually wide-reaching cultural, religious, or political integration across large parts of the Andes, and intermediate periods denote times of greater regional diversity and localized political organization. On this scheme, the era examined in this chapter — often dated approximately 600 to 1000 CE, though archaeologists increasingly recognize that Tiwanaku's roots as a settlement and ceremonial center reach back to roughly 100–200 CE, considerably earlier than Wari's rise, even though Tiwanaku did not become the region's principal urban state until around the sixth century CE, and its political trajectory, on some interpretations, extended into the eleventh century — is called the Middle Horizon, distinguishing it from the Early Horizon associated with Chavín (Chapter 16) and the Late Horizon associated with the Inca Empire (Chapter 20). Students should treat "Middle Horizon" as a useful chronological label rather than as evidence that Wari and Tiwanaku formed a single integrated civilization; the label describes a shared span of time during which two separate highland states, developing according to their own internal histories, both achieved unusually wide-reaching influence.

Understanding Wari and Tiwanaku requires the same kind of careful, evidence-based reasoning introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of the peopling of the Americas. Because neither society left a deciphered system of writing, and because later Inca and Spanish colonial-era accounts of ancient ruins mixed historical memory with myth, archaeologists must reconstruct Wari and Tiwanaku political and social organization almost entirely from material evidence: architecture, settlement patterns, agricultural infrastructure, ceramics, textiles, human skeletal remains, and increasingly sophisticated chemical and isotopic techniques capable of identifying where an individual grew up, what they ate, and how far certain objects and materials traveled from their place of origin. As with the peopling debate, current scholarly understanding of Wari and Tiwanaku continues to change as new sites are excavated and new techniques are applied — a 2025 discovery near the Bolivian town of Palaspata, discussed later in this chapter, is a recent example of a single excavation substantially reshaping scholarly understanding of Tiwanaku's geographic reach.

Section 18.1

The Middle Horizon: A New Scale of Andean Integration

Archaeologists conventionally describe the centuries between roughly 600 and 1000 CE as the Middle Horizon of Central Andean prehistory, a period distinguished from the preceding Early Intermediate Period — the era of Paracas, Nazca, and Moche examined in Chapter 17 — by the emergence of political and religious systems reaching across much larger territories than any single coastal valley or highland basin. The label "horizon" signals that archaeologists find evidence of shared artistic styles, architectural forms, and religious imagery distributed across an unusually wide geographic area during this period, in contrast to "intermediate periods," when Andean societies tended to organize themselves at smaller, more regionally distinct scales — though, as Chapter 17 emphasized regarding Paracas, Nazca, and Moche, societies within an "intermediate period" could still overlap substantially in time and interact with one another rather than following a single, cleanly separated sequence.

Students should use this chronological label carefully. "Middle Horizon" describes a shared span of centuries and some genuinely shared elements of visual culture, especially imagery associated with a frontal, staff-holding deity figure, discussed further in Sections 18.6 and 18.9. It does not describe a single political system, a single religion, or a single people. Wari and Tiwanaku developed in different regions, under different local histories, and, on current archaeological evidence, achieved their respective forms of wide-reaching influence through substantially different combinations of methods. Tiwanaku's roots as a ceremonial and residential center near Lake Titicaca extend back several centuries before Wari's emergence as a major urban center in the Ayacucho basin, meaning that the two states' political trajectories, while overlapping for a long stretch of the Middle Horizon, were not perfectly synchronized.

This chapter accordingly moves between two highland regions rather than presenting a single continuous narrative. Sections 18.2 through 18.6 examine Wari, tracing its heartland, its expansion into distant provinces, its roads and agricultural investments, and its religious and artistic traditions. Sections 18.7 through 18.10 shift to Tiwanaku, examining its urban-ceremonial core, its agricultural foundations, its religious monumentality, and evidence for its colonies and exchange networks beyond the Titicaca Basin. Section 18.11 brings the two states together at their most clearly documented point of geographic overlap, the Moquegua Valley, before Section 18.12 traces their roughly contemporaneous, but not identical, decline around the end of the first millennium CE.

Section 18.2

The Wari Heartland and the City of Huari

The city of Huari, located in the Ayacucho basin of the south-central Peruvian highlands at an elevation of roughly 2,800 meters, grew during the sixth through tenth centuries CE into one of the largest urban centers yet built in the ancient Andes, with dense residential and administrative compounds, elite architecture, extensive craft-production areas, and monumental religious structures. Unlike the later Inca capital of Cusco, examined in Chapter 20, Huari was not laid out around a single dominant ceremonial plaza in a clearly legible urban plan; rather, archaeologists describe a dense, somewhat irregular agglomeration of walled compounds, many apparently associated with particular kin groups, elite households, or corporate institutions, suggesting a complex and possibly shifting balance of power among multiple groups within the capital rather than a single unchallenged central authority.

Excavations at and around Huari have recovered evidence of large-scale craft production, including ceramics and textiles produced partly for local use and partly, on current evidence, for distribution to provincial centers and allied communities as part of political and religious strategies discussed further in Sections 18.5 and 18.6. Huari's residents also constructed substantial religious architecture, including structures associated with mortuary ritual and ancestor veneration, indicating that religious practice, including the treatment of the deceased, played an important role in how political authority was expressed and negotiated within the capital itself.

Recent scholarship has moved away from older models that treated Huari simply as the unchallenged administrative summit of a tightly centralized state issuing uniform orders to subordinate provinces. Instead, researchers increasingly describe a more dynamic and contested process of state formation within the Wari heartland itself, in which emerging state institutions interacted, sometimes uneasily, with kin-based groups often referred to using the later Andean term ayllu — a term that must be used cautiously for this earlier period, since its precise meaning and social role in the Middle Horizon cannot simply be assumed to match its better-documented Inca and colonial-era usage. This body of research suggests that Wari state authority was actively built, negotiated, and at times resisted or reshaped by communities and lineages within Huari and its immediate hinterland, rather than descending as a finished, uncontested structure from a single ruling elite.

Section 18.3

Wari Expansion: Administrative Centers, Colonies, and Local Communities

The clearest archaeological signature of Wari expansion beyond the Ayacucho heartland is a series of large, formally planned settlements constructed in distant regions of Peru, generally referred to as Wari administrative centers or provincial centers. Two of the most extensively studied examples are Pikillacta, located near Cusco in the southern highlands, and Viracochapampa, located in the northern highlands near modern Huamachuco. Both sites display rectilinear, highly standardized compounds, wide formal entryways, and architectural planning that closely echoes construction techniques and spatial organization documented at Huari itself, strongly suggesting direct involvement by Wari state institutions in their design and construction rather than simple local imitation of Wari style.

A third major provincial settlement, Cerro Baúl, was built atop a steep, naturally defensible mesa in the Moquegua Valley of far southern Peru, several hundred kilometers from the Ayacucho heartland and, notably, within a valley also containing contemporaneous Tiwanaku settlements, discussed further in Section 18.11. Excavations at Cerro Baúl have recovered evidence of elite residences, a brewery for producing maize beer (chicha) apparently used in state-sponsored feasting, and religious architecture, suggesting that the site combined administrative, economic, religious, and possibly military functions in a strategically important frontier location.

It would be a serious oversimplification, however, to conclude from Pikillacta, Viracochapampa, and Cerro Baúl that every region touched by Wari material culture experienced identical, direct, and uniformly administered imperial rule. Current scholarship explicitly challenges older models in which any settlement displaying Wari-style architecture or artifacts was automatically assumed to represent a standardized outpost of centralized imperial administration. Archaeological survey at Ak'awillay, a local community in the Cusco region located near Wari colonization, indicates comparatively limited transformation of everyday village life, with continuity in local household organization, subsistence practices, and material culture alongside new opportunities for exchange with Wari settlers, rather than wholesale imposition of Wari administrative structures on local populations. This pattern suggests that Wari's relationship with provincial communities varied considerably by region, ranging from apparently direct and intensive administrative intervention, as at Pikillacta and Viracochapampa, to more limited engagement focused chiefly on selective exchange and coexistence, as at Ak'awillay. This changing interpretation also illustrates what archaeologist Justin Jennings has described as a "hermeneutic spiral" in Wari scholarship: once scholars developed a model of standardized administrative centers, later evidence was often interpreted through that inherited framework, reinforcing assumptions that themselves required reexamination. Students should therefore distinguish the archaeological evidence for uneven Wari relationships with provincial communities from the historiographic process through which scholars have interpreted that evidence, and should keep both cautions in mind when encountering older, more uniform accounts of "the Wari Empire."

Section 18.4

Wari Roads, Movement, and Interregional Administration

Connecting Huari to its distant provincial centers required sustained investment in overland transportation infrastructure. Archaeological survey has identified segments of road and trail associated with Wari settlement and provincial administration, linking the capital to centers such as Pikillacta, Viracochapampa, and the Moquegua Valley settlements, and facilitating the movement of officials, soldiers, laborers, agricultural produce, craft goods, and religious personnel across considerable distances and challenging highland terrain.

Students should exercise particular caution here, because this subject is one where a later and far better documented institution — the extensive Inca road system, or Qhapaq Ñan, examined in Chapter 20 — can easily be projected backward onto the Middle Horizon in misleading ways. It is neither accurate to say that Wari built "the" Inca road system, nor accurate to dismiss Wari infrastructure investment as insignificant simply because it was smaller in scale and reach than what the Inca state would later construct. Some later Inca routes appear, on the basis of overlapping paths and shared engineering logic, to have reused or incorporated segments of earlier corridors established during the Middle Horizon or even earlier periods, but the two road systems were built under different states, for different administrative purposes, and using resources and labor organization specific to each period. The safest and most defensible historical claim is that Wari's investment in roads and provincial centers represented an important and consequential earlier experiment in large-scale territorial integration in the Andes — one from which later Andean states, including the Inca, may have drawn practical lessons and, in places, physical infrastructure, without Wari roads themselves constituting an early version of the mature Inca system.

Movement along these routes was not limited to state officials. Evidence of long-distance exchange in ceramics, obsidian, textiles, and other goods, discussed further in Section 18.5, indicates that Wari roads and trails also carried caravaners, craft specialists, agricultural produce, exchanged goods, and people traveling to ceremonies or interregional gatherings, linking distant communities into overlapping networks of political obligation, economic exchange, and religious participation that together constituted much of what made Wari a functioning interregional system rather than merely a scattering of isolated fortified outposts.

Section 18.5

Wari Agriculture, Storage, and Political Economy

Wari's expansion depended on a political economy capable of supporting officials, soldiers, craft specialists, and religious personnel who did not themselves produce food, as well as capable of financing large-scale construction, long-distance exchange, and the feasting and gift-giving that appear to have played an important role in cementing political relationships. Archaeological evidence indicates substantial Wari investment in agricultural terracing and irrigation, particularly in highland valleys where flatland suitable for cultivation was limited, allowing for intensified production of maize and other crops on land that would otherwise have supported only limited farming.

Wari administrative centers and settlements also display significant storage infrastructure, including structures interpreted as state or elite storehouses for agricultural surplus, craft goods, and other valued materials. This storage capacity likely supported several overlapping functions: provisioning officials and laborers stationed at provincial centers, supplying feasts and ceremonies used to build and reinforce political relationships with local communities and allied elites, and providing a buffer against poor harvests in a highland environment where crop failure from frost, drought, or hail posed a constant risk.

Wari political economy also involved the procurement and exchange of non-agricultural resources over considerable distances. Research on Wari-period settlements in the Arequipa region of southern Peru indicates that Wari institutions participated actively in obsidian procurement and exchange and, by the late Middle Horizon, appear to have consolidated regional use around major high-quality sources, while local and bottom-up exchange networks continued to operate alongside state activity rather than being entirely displaced by it. Textiles, discussed further in Section 18.6, represented another major category of value within Wari political economy, produced through highly organized labor and used both as everyday clothing and as a medium for displaying rank, group affiliation, and political allegiance. Taken together, this evidence indicates that Wari's political economy combined staple-crop intensification, storage, craft production, and long-distance procurement of valuable materials into an integrated — though not necessarily uniform across every province — system for financing the state's political and religious ambitions.

Section 18.6

Wari Religion, Art, and Political Authority

Wari religious and political authority found expression through art and material culture as much as through administrative architecture. Wari ceramics, produced in distinctive polychrome styles, and Wari textiles, especially finely woven tunics constructed from camelid fiber, frequently display standardized imagery associated with a frontal, staff-holding deity figure and accompanying attendant figures, discussed further in the Historical Vocabulary feature below. These images were reproduced with recognizable consistency across a wide geographic area, suggesting that shared visual and religious themes functioned as an important medium for expressing political affiliation, rank, and participation in a broader Wari cultural and political order.

At the same time, close study of surviving Wari tunics reveals considerable variation in how individual weavers rendered these shared themes, adjusting proportions, color combinations, and specific decorative details even while maintaining recognizable overall patterns. This combination of broad standardization and local or individual variation cautions against assuming that Wari visual culture expressed a single, rigidly enforced state religion administered identically everywhere; it more plausibly reflects a shared visual vocabulary that different communities, workshops, and elites could adapt, display, and interpret according to local circumstances and status, while still signaling meaningful connection to Wari religious and political authority.

Feasting, closely tied to the storage and agricultural systems discussed in Section 18.5, provided another important arena for expressing and negotiating political authority. Maize beer production at sites such as Cerro Baúl suggests that hosting large-scale feasts, likely accompanied by gift-giving, ceremony, and the display of finely made textiles and ceramics, served as a key mechanism through which Wari elites built and maintained relationships with allied communities, local leaders, and provincial populations — a pattern of politically meaningful hospitality that recurs, in modified form, throughout later Andean history, including under the Inca state examined in Chapter 20.

Section 18.7

Tiwanaku and the Lake Titicaca Basin

The chapter now shifts its geographic focus southeast, from the Ayacucho highlands to the altiplano surrounding Lake Titicaca, where the site of Tiwanaku developed into one of the most extraordinary planned urban and ceremonial centers of the ancient Americas. Located near the southern shore of the lake at an elevation exceeding 3,800 meters, Tiwanaku grew from an earlier regional ceremonial center, contemporaneous with other Titicaca Basin societies such as Pukara, into a major capital combining monumental religious architecture, elite residential compounds, extensive craft production, and sophisticated hydraulic engineering.

Tiwanaku's core monuments include the Akapana, a large stepped platform mound incorporating internal drainage channels and interpreted by many archaeologists as a monumental representation of a sacred mountain; the Kalasasaya, a raised rectangular platform enclosure associated with public ceremony and, on some interpretations, astronomical observation; the Semi-Subterranean Temple, a sunken rectangular court lined with carved stone tenon-heads representing a range of human and possibly supernatural faces; and Pumapunku, a separate monumental complex renowned for extraordinarily precise cut-stone architecture, including large andesite and sandstone blocks worked with a level of precision that continues to attract scholarly attention regarding the technical and organizational capacities of Tiwanaku builders.

International heritage assessments of Tiwanaku emphasize that the site should be understood as simultaneously civic, political, and ceremonial in function rather than as a purely religious temple complex isolated from everyday political and economic life. This assessment aligns with the broader argument this chapter develops: Tiwanaku's monumental religious architecture and its political authority were not separate phenomena but were deeply intertwined, with religious ceremony, pilgrimage, and monumental construction serving as central mechanisms through which Tiwanaku elites built, displayed, and reinforced their authority over the surrounding region and, as later sections discuss, over more distant colonies and exchange partners as well.

Section 18.8

Raised Fields, Herding, and the Agricultural Foundations of Tiwanaku

Tiwanaku's political and religious achievements rested on an agricultural base adapted to one of the most demanding farming environments inhabited by any major pre-modern American civilization. The altiplano surrounding Lake Titicaca experiences frequent overnight frost, even during the growing season, posing a severe threat to crops. Tiwanaku-era farmers addressed this challenge through an intensive technique generally known as raised-field agriculture, in which farmers constructed elevated planting platforms separated by narrow, water-filled canals. The water in these canals absorbed solar heat during the day and released it slowly overnight, moderating the temperature immediately around growing plants and substantially reducing frost damage, while the canals themselves also supplied irrigation water, captured nutrient-rich sediment for use as fertilizer, and supported fish and other aquatic life that supplemented the local diet.

Older archaeological scholarship, based partly on the sheer scale and apparent engineering sophistication of raised-field systems near the Tiwanaku heartland, argued strongly for direct state coordination of raised-field construction, maintenance, and agricultural production, treating intensive raised-field farming as a centrally planned instrument of Tiwanaku state power. More recent scholarship has complicated this picture, raising important questions about the relative roles of individual households, local communities, and kin-based groups, on one hand, and centralized state institutions, on the other, in initiating, maintaining, and benefiting from raised-field systems. Some raised fields may have been constructed and managed largely at the community or household level, using labor and knowledge that predated or existed independently of state direction, even as the Tiwanaku state benefited from, encouraged, and in some documented cases likely directed particular episodes of raised-field expansion. Students should therefore treat the precise balance between household initiative and state coordination in Tiwanaku agriculture as an active and only partially resolved research question rather than a settled matter.

Raised-field cultivation of crops such as potatoes and other high-altitude tubers, along with quinoa and other cold-tolerant grains, worked alongside an equally important pastoral economy centered on llama and alpaca herding. Camelids provided wool for textile production, meat, dung used as fuel and fertilizer, and, critically, transportation capacity for the long-distance caravan exchange discussed in Section 18.10. The combination of intensified raised-field agriculture and large-scale camelid herding allowed the Titicaca Basin to support an unusually dense population at extreme altitude and provided the economic foundation for Tiwanaku's political and religious ambitions.

Section 18.9

Tiwanaku Religion, Monumentality, and Political Power

Tiwanaku's monumental architecture, introduced in Section 18.7, served as more than a backdrop for religious ceremony; it functioned as an active instrument of political authority. Public ceremonies conducted within the Kalasasaya and the Semi-Subterranean Temple, likely involving processions, offerings, ritual drinking from finely made ceramic vessels, and the display of textiles and other valued goods, drew pilgrims and political representatives from throughout the Titicaca Basin and, on evidence discussed in Section 18.10, from considerably more distant regions as well. Participation in these ceremonies at the Tiwanaku core plausibly reinforced political relationships between the capital and outlying communities, allowing Tiwanaku authorities to display and renew their religious and political legitimacy before an audience drawn from across their sphere of influence.

This chapter resists an older interpretive framework that treated Tiwanaku primarily as a peaceful religious or pilgrimage center exercising influence through voluntary cultural attraction alone, in implicit contrast to Wari's more overtly administrative and infrastructural mode of expansion. Current scholarship increasingly documents direct Tiwanaku state investment in religious architecture well beyond the Titicaca heartland. Research on a Tiwanaku sunken-court temple identified in the Moquegua Valley, discussed further in Section 18.11, demonstrates that provincial temple construction could itself function as an instrument of Tiwanaku state expansion, extending recognizably Tiwanaku religious space and, by extension, political and cultural influence into a distant valley also occupied by Wari settlers. Religious authority and political authority, in other words, should not be understood as separate or competing explanations for Tiwanaku's reach; the evidence increasingly suggests that Tiwanaku exercised political power in significant part through institutions saturated with religious meaning and monumental religious construction.

Tiwanaku's celebrated stonework — including massive gateways, finely carved monoliths depicting human or supernatural figures in elaborate dress, and precisely fitted ashlar masonry at Pumapunku — further reinforced this fusion of religious and political meaning. The carved figures examined in this chapter's Material Source Window, discussed below, display iconography, including staff-holding attendant figures, closely related to imagery documented at Wari sites, a visual connection explored further in the Historical Debate feature on the two states' possible relationship.

Section 18.10

Colonies, Caravans, and Tiwanaku Expansion

Tiwanaku's economic and political reach extended well beyond the immediate shores of Lake Titicaca through two closely related mechanisms: long-distance llama-caravan exchange and the establishment of colonies or enclaves in ecologically distinct regions. Camelid caravans, often involving substantial numbers of pack animals moving together under organized direction, connected the cold altiplano to warmer, lower-elevation valleys capable of producing maize, coca, chili peppers, and other crops that could not be grown at Titicaca Basin elevations, allowing Tiwanaku communities to access a wider range of agricultural products than their immediate environment alone could supply.

Archaeological evidence for Tiwanaku-associated colonies or enclaves has been documented in several regions beyond the altiplano core, most extensively in the Moquegua Valley of southern Peru, discussed further in Section 18.11, and in valleys extending toward Cochabamba in what is today Bolivia. These colonial settlements display Tiwanaku-style architecture, ceramics, and burial practices, suggesting the presence of communities either directly descended from Titicaca Basin populations or closely integrated into Tiwanaku's cultural and economic sphere, maintaining connections back to the highland core even while living permanently in lower-elevation valleys.

A major archaeological development that has meaningfully reshaped scholarly understanding of Tiwanaku's geographic reach is the identification, through research published in 2025, of a substantial Tiwanaku-associated temple complex at Palaspata, located roughly 215 kilometers southeast of Tiwanaku itself, in a position interpreted as a strategically significant gateway controlling interregional traffic between the Titicaca Basin and more distant regions such as Cochabamba. Radiocarbon evidence from the nearby settlement of Ocotavi 1 indicates Tiwanaku-associated occupation along this corridor extending across roughly 630 to 950 CE, though the excavating team emphasizes that the Palaspata temple itself has not yet been directly dated and that further fieldwork is needed to establish precisely when it was constructed. With that caveat in mind, the discovery provides evidence of substantial Tiwanaku state investment and attempted control of a key interregional corridor, strengthening arguments for meaningful direct state involvement in at least some corridors of interregional connection rather than reliance solely on informal exchange or gradual cultural diffusion.

Students should nonetheless avoid overcorrecting in the opposite direction. The presence of Tiwanaku-style pottery, textiles, or even architecture at a given site does not, by itself, prove direct political annexation or uniform administrative control identical to what evidence suggests at Palaspata or in parts of the Moquegua Valley. As with Wari provincial centers, discussed in Section 18.3, the intensity and character of Tiwanaku's presence appears to have varied considerably from place to place, ranging from apparently substantial direct state investment, as along the Palaspata corridor, to more limited and indirect forms of cultural and economic connection maintained chiefly through caravan exchange and shared religious participation.

Section 18.11

The Moquegua Frontier: Wari and Tiwanaku Together

The Moquegua Valley of far southern Peru offers historians and archaeologists an unusually direct opportunity to examine Wari and Tiwanaku as contemporaneous neighbors rather than as sequential or entirely separate phenomena. Excavations in this valley have documented the Wari settlement of Cerro Baúl, introduced in Section 18.3, situated on a defensible mesa, within a broader landscape that also contains Tiwanaku-affiliated colonial communities and, as discussed in Section 18.9, a Tiwanaku sunken-court temple complex. The two states therefore maintained settlements within the same valley system, in some cases only a few kilometers apart, for a span of several generations.

This proximity raises an obvious historical question: how did Wari and Tiwanaku communities in Moquegua actually relate to one another? Current archaeological evidence does not support a documented, specific war between the two states in this valley or elsewhere. Excavated Wari and Tiwanaku settlements in Moquegua show distinct architectural styles, ceramics, and religious practices existing in reasonably close proximity without clear evidence of large-scale destruction, conquest, or violent displacement of one group by the other during the period of their overlap. This pattern has led many archaeologists to characterize the relationship as one of coexistence, involving elements of economic interdependence, competitive display, and maintained cultural distinctiveness, rather than open warfare — though the possibility of localized tension, negotiated boundaries, or periodic conflict not clearly visible in the current archaeological record cannot be entirely ruled out, and further excavation could refine this picture.

Comparing the two states' presence in Moquegua highlights the broader argument of this chapter. Cerro Baúl functioned as a Wari administrative, religious, and economic center defended by its dramatic natural setting, complete with elite residences and feasting infrastructure connected back to Wari's interregional political economy discussed in Section 18.5. The nearby Tiwanaku settlements and temple, by contrast, extended Tiwanaku's colonial and religious network southward from the Titicaca Basin, discussed in Section 18.10, into a valley whose agricultural resources — including maize unavailable at Titicaca Basin elevations — offered clear economic incentives for both highland states to seek a durable presence. Rather than one state's history explaining or subsuming the other, Moquegua demonstrates that two highland systems of comparable ambition, built through different combinations of administration, colonization, religion, and exchange, could occupy overlapping territory for generations, each pursuing its own strategic interests within a shared but contested regional landscape.

Section 18.12

Transformation after Wari and Tiwanaku

Both Wari and Tiwanaku experienced significant decline or transformation around the end of the first millennium CE, though the timing, pace, and underlying causes of these processes differed between the two states and remain subjects of ongoing research rather than settled conclusions. Archaeological evidence from the Wari heartland and from provincial centers such as Pikillacta and Viracochapampa indicates reduced construction activity, changes in settlement patterns, and, in some regions, apparent abandonment or repurposing of formerly Wari-administered sites by roughly 1000 CE, suggesting a substantial reduction in the interregional political integration that had characterized the preceding centuries.

Tiwanaku's urban-ceremonial core and broader network of colonies and exchange relationships also underwent a significant transformation around the end of the first millennium CE, though recent research indicates that this transformation followed a somewhat different timeline and causal path than Wari's decline. Recent Bayesian modeling of more than a hundred radiocarbon dates places the breakdown of permanent residence at Tiwanaku itself at approximately 1010 CE, with a rapid city-wide unraveling of major community practices occurring within roughly 1010 to 1050 CE. Critically, this chronology places Tiwanaku's urban collapse well before the major reduction in regional precipitation that older scholarship commonly invoked as the primary cause of Tiwanaku's decline, substantially weakening a simple drought-collapse explanation for the urban core specifically. Climatic change may still be relevant to later or more regional transformations in the broader Titicaca Basin, but students should not treat drought as a sufficient, stand-alone explanation for why the city of Tiwanaku itself ceased to function as a populated urban center.

As with Wari, responsible historical explanation of Tiwanaku's transformation must avoid attributing the outcome to a single dramatic cause. Proposed contributing factors, discussed further in the Cause and Effect feature below, include internal political fragmentation and competition among elites or regional factions, disruption of long-distance exchange and caravan networks upon which the state's political economy substantially depended, changing social and religious practices within the urban core itself, and the increasing assertion of local political autonomy by communities and regions that had previously been more closely integrated into the Tiwanaku system. Wari's decline, examined earlier in this section, appears to have followed its own distinct combination of causes, and students should resist assuming that both states collapsed for the same reasons simply because their declines occurred within a roughly similar span of centuries.

The end of large-scale Wari and Tiwanaku political integration did not produce a historical vacuum. Instead, it opened a period, examined in Chapter 19, during which new regional kingdoms and political centers emerged across the territories these two Middle Horizon states had once connected. Chimú, on the north coast of Peru, and the early political developments around Cusco that would eventually give rise to the Inca state are among the most historically significant of these successor developments, but students should understand them as responses to a substantially transformed post-Wari, post-Tiwanaku political landscape rather than as direct continuations of either Middle Horizon state. The mature Inca imperial system examined in Chapter 20, in particular, developed several centuries later, under its own distinct political and military circumstances, and should not be read as simply Wari or Tiwanaku administration continued under a new name.

Historical Vocabulary
Middle Horizon

The Middle Horizon is an archaeological period label, conventionally dated to roughly 600–1000 CE, describing an era of unusually wide-reaching cultural, religious, and political integration across large parts of the Central Andes, associated primarily with the Wari and Tiwanaku states examined in this chapter. The term distinguishes this period from the preceding Early Intermediate Period, associated with more regionally distinct societies such as Paracas, Nazca, and Moche (Chapter 17), and from the following Late Intermediate Period of regional kingdoms (Chapter 19) that preceded the Late Horizon of Inca imperial expansion (Chapter 20).

Students should treat "Middle Horizon" as a chronological and stylistic label rather than as evidence for a single unified civilization. Wari and Tiwanaku developed independently, in different highland regions, and achieved their respective forms of wide-reaching influence through different combinations of administration, colonization, religion, and exchange. The label is useful for situating both states within the broader sequence of Andean prehistory, but it should never be used to imply that they were the same political system or that one simply derived from the other.

Historical Vocabulary
Staff Deity

The Staff Deity (sometimes called the Staff God) is a modern analytical label, not an ancient name, for a recurring frontal figure holding one or two staff-like objects that appears across several distinct Andean artistic traditions, including Chavín (Chapter 16), Tiwanaku, and Wari. The figure is often accompanied by smaller attendant figures arranged in profile, as seen in the Tiwanaku Gateway of the Sun examined in this chapter's Material Source Window.

Students must resist an easy but misleading inference: similarity of pose and general iconography across Chavín, Tiwanaku, and Wari art does not demonstrate that these societies worshiped one unchanged deity, shared a single organized religion, or belonged to one continuous political or religious system. The imagery was repeatedly reinterpreted, adapted, and recombined with local artistic conventions across different regions and periods separated by centuries. "Staff Deity" is best understood as a scholarly shorthand for tracking a recurring visual theme across Andean art history, not as proof of religious or political unity between the societies that used it.

Material Source Window

The Wari Tunic

Object type: Camelid-fiber tunic, tapestry weave · Approximate date: Middle Horizon, c. 600–900 CE · Findspot context: Wari heartland and provincial burial and settlement contexts

Context: Finely woven tunics were among the most labor-intensive and visually significant objects produced within Wari society. Constructed from camelid fiber using tapestry-weave techniques requiring enormous investment of skilled labor, these garments were worn by elite individuals and, in some cases, placed in burials, functioning simultaneously as clothing, as a display of rank and wealth, and as a medium for religious and political imagery.

Description: Surviving Wari tunics frequently display abstracted, geometric renderings of the Staff Deity and attendant figures discussed in this chapter, often repeated across the garment in a grid-like arrangement of small, brightly colored blocks. While the overall composition follows recognizable, widely shared conventions, close comparison of individual tunics reveals meaningful variation in color choice, proportion, and the precise rendering of figures, indicating that individual weavers or workshops exercised real creative latitude within a broadly shared visual tradition.

Analysis: The Wari tunic illustrates how portable, personally worn objects could carry political and religious meaning across an interregional state without requiring uniform, centrally dictated production. A tunic's imagery could signal its wearer's connection to Wari religious and political authority to observers across a wide geographic area, even as its specific execution reflected local or individual artistic choices. This combination of standardization and variation cautions against assuming that shared imagery alone proves uniform, centrally controlled religious practice.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the labor investment required to produce a Wari tunic suggest about the object's social and political significance?
  2. Why might rulers or elites want to display similar religious imagery across a wide geographic area?
  3. What does variation among individual tunics suggest about the limits of centralized artistic or religious control in Wari society?
  4. What can a textile tell historians that architecture alone cannot, and what are the limits of what a textile can establish about political administration?

The Gateway of the Sun, Tiwanaku

Object type: Carved monolithic stone gateway · Approximate date: Middle Horizon, Tiwanaku core period · Present location/context: Tiwanaku ceremonial precinct, near the Kalasasaya platform; original architectural placement uncertain

Context: The Gateway of the Sun is a single massive block of stone carved into a doorway and decorated with an elaborate relief composition, long recognized as one of Tiwanaku's defining monumental artistic achievements. International heritage assessments of the site identify the gateway's central figure and surrounding attendants as among the clearest examples of Tiwanaku's sophisticated stone-carving tradition and its capacity for monumental religious expression. Its original architectural setting is debated; the gateway was documented in the site's main ceremonial precinct near the Kalasasaya, but one influential reconstruction associates it instead with the Pumapunku complex, meaning its present position should not automatically be assumed to reflect its original placement or function.

Description: The gateway's upper register depicts a central frontal figure holding two staffs, wearing an elaborate headdress, and flanked by rows of smaller winged attendant figures arranged in profile, each rendered with considerable consistency across the composition. Scholars generally identify the central figure as a version of the Staff Deity discussed elsewhere in this chapter, closely related in form to imagery documented at Wari sites, though the exact religious meaning of the composition, and the precise relationship between Tiwanaku and Wari versions of this imagery, remain subjects of ongoing scholarly discussion.

Analysis: As a source, the Gateway of the Sun demonstrates the technical sophistication of Tiwanaku stoneworking and the centrality of monumental religious imagery to the site's civic and ceremonial function. However, the gateway alone cannot tell historians how Tiwanaku's political administration actually operated, how directly its religious authority translated into political control over distant regions, or how ordinary participants in ceremonies at the site understood the imagery's meaning. Interpreting the gateway responsibly requires combining it with the architectural, agricultural, and settlement evidence examined throughout this chapter.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the technical precision of the Gateway of the Sun suggest about the organization of labor and craft specialization at Tiwanaku?
  2. How does uncertainty over the gateway's original architectural placement — near the Kalasasaya versus at Pumapunku — affect how confidently historians can interpret its intended meaning and function?
  3. What are the limits of using a single carved monument to draw conclusions about an entire political system?
  4. How does the resemblance between this gateway's central figure and Wari Staff Deity imagery complicate easy assumptions about the two states' relationship?
Historical Debate

Were Wari and Tiwanaku Empires?

The question: Archaeologists and historians disagree about whether "empire" is an appropriate label for Wari, for Tiwanaku, for both, or for neither, and about what evidence should settle the question.

Interpretation A — Wari, at least, functioned as a genuine expansionist state deserving comparison to later empires: Proponents point to Wari's planned administrative centers at Pikillacta and Viracochapampa, its investment in roads connecting distant provinces to the Ayacucho heartland, its political economy of agricultural intensification and storage, and clear evidence of direct construction intervention at sites such as Cerro Baúl. These features, on this view, indicate a state capable of projecting administrative authority across considerable distances, even if the degree of control varied by region.
Interpretation B — Neither state matched the uniform, centralized administrative reach later achieved by the Inca, and "empire" risks overstating both states' actual control: Proponents point to evidence such as Ak'awillay, where nearby Wari colonization produced only limited transformation of local life, and to ongoing debate over how centrally Tiwanaku's raised-field agriculture was actually managed. On this view, both Wari and Tiwanaku are better understood as expansive but unevenly integrated political and economic systems — genuinely larger and more ambitious than the coastal societies of Chapter 17, but not directly comparable to the more thoroughly documented administrative uniformity later imposed by the Inca state.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of excavated administrative centers, provincial settlements, agricultural infrastructure, and comparative studies of local communities near Wari and Tiwanaku sites, but weigh the significance of uneven regional evidence differently.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they describe the Middle Horizon in relation to both the smaller-scale societies of Chapter 17 and the more centralized Inca state of Chapter 20. This textbook does not resolve the debate by declaring a single correct label; it uses "highland state" and "highland system of integration" rather than "empire" as a default, more cautious description, while acknowledging that some regions and functions of Wari rule in particular closely resemble what specialists elsewhere call imperial administration.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 18.2 through 18.11, construct an argument for whichever interpretation you find more persuasive, identifying which specific pieces of evidence you find most decisive and explaining what additional evidence might strengthen or weaken your case.

Historical Debate

How Centralized Was Tiwanaku Agriculture?

The question: Did the Tiwanaku state directly plan, construct, and manage the raised-field agricultural systems of the Titicaca Basin, or did local households and communities play the leading role, with the state benefiting from but not centrally directing agricultural production?

Interpretation A — Raised-field agriculture was substantially state-directed: This view points to the scale and apparent engineering sophistication of raised-field systems near the Tiwanaku core, arguing that coordinating construction and water management across such large areas plausibly required centralized planning and labor mobilization consistent with strong state involvement.
Interpretation B — Raised-field agriculture was substantially community- or household-managed: This view emphasizes that raised-field techniques may have developed and been maintained at the household or local community level using knowledge and labor arrangements that predated, or operated independently of, direct state direction, with the state benefiting from surplus production without necessarily controlling day-to-day agricultural decisions.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on archaeological survey of raised-field distribution, proximity to elite and non-elite settlements, and comparative ethnographic and agronomic studies of raised-field techniques documented elsewhere in the Andes and beyond.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the balance of power between Tiwanaku state institutions and local communities, and cautions against assuming that any large-scale ancient infrastructure automatically proves centralized state control.

Student task: Identify what kind of additional archaeological evidence — for example, evidence of household versus elite investment in specific raised-field sectors — would help resolve this debate.

Multiple Perspectives

Living within Expanding Highland States

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified across archaeological scholarship. They are not quotations from any named historical individual.

From the perspective of a resident of Huari: Daily life involved participation in a dense, competitive urban environment organized substantially around kin-based compounds, craft production, and religious ritual, in which political authority was actively negotiated among elite groups and institutions rather than descending unchallenged from a single ruler.
From the perspective of a farmer near Ak'awillay, in the Cusco region: The arrival of nearby Wari settlers brought new opportunities for exchange and new neighbors, but did not necessarily transform the fundamental rhythms of household farming, local social organization, or community identity, illustrating that Wari expansion did not automatically mean uniform administrative control over every nearby population.
From the perspective of a raised-field farmer in the Titicaca Basin: Agricultural life centered on the demanding, skilled work of constructing and maintaining raised fields, managing frost risk, and coordinating water in canals, work that may have depended as much on household and community knowledge passed across generations as on any direction from distant Tiwanaku authorities.
From the perspective of a Wari official stationed at Cerro Baúl, overlooking Tiwanaku-affiliated communities in the Moquegua Valley: Managing a frontier settlement in close proximity to a comparably ambitious neighboring state required balancing administrative duties to a distant capital with the practical realities of coexistence, exchange, and competitive display within a shared valley.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Coastal Polities to Highland States

What Changed

  • Political and economic organization expanded beyond the more regionally rooted coastal systems examined in Chapter 17 to interregional systems spanning hundreds of kilometers and multiple ecological zones
  • Purpose-built administrative centers, formal roads, and documented colonies appear for the first time at this scale in the archaeological record examined so far in this textbook
  • Religious imagery, particularly the Staff Deity, circulated across an unusually wide geographic area, suggesting new mechanisms for building shared political and religious identity across distant communities

What Stayed the Same

  • Irrigation and agricultural engineering, evident already in earlier coastal societies, remained central to political and economic life, now applied to highland terracing and raised-field construction
  • Feasting, craft specialization, and long-distance exchange, already important in earlier Andean societies, continued as key mechanisms for building and expressing political relationships
  • Local communities and households continued to exercise meaningful autonomy even under expanding highland states, as evidence from Ak'awillay and ongoing raised-field debates suggests

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine expansion in scale achieved by Wari and Tiwanaku and the underlying continuities with earlier Andean practices helps students avoid treating the Middle Horizon as a complete break with what came before, while still appreciating its distinctive achievements in interregional integration.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Highland States Seek Control of Multiple Ecological Zones?

Long-term causes

The extreme ecological compression of the Andes, in which dramatically different growing conditions exist within relatively short distances of elevation change, made access to multiple ecological tiers an especially valuable strategy for reducing agricultural risk.

Short-term causes

Growing populations at Huari and Tiwanaku, combined with elite and religious institutions requiring reliable surplus for feasting, construction labor, and craft production, created pressure to secure resources, such as maize and coca, unavailable in the immediate highland environment.

Effect

Both states established colonies, exchange relationships, and, in Wari's case, administrative centers in ecologically distinct and sometimes distant regions, including the Moquegua Valley, in order to access complementary resources.

Historical significance

This strategy of managing risk and resources across multiple ecological zones recurs, in more thoroughly documented form, in the later Inca state examined in Chapter 20, illustrating a durable pattern in Andean political economy even as specific institutions changed over time.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains why Wari and Tiwanaku each pursued interregional expansion; ecological risk management, population growth, religious ambition, and elite competition for prestige goods all likely interacted.

Everyday Life and Social History

The People Behind "Wari" and "Tiwanaku"

Terms such as "Wari state" and "Tiwanaku expansion" can obscure the ordinary people whose labor made these highland systems possible. Terrace farmers in the Ayacucho highlands cleared and maintained agricultural slopes generation after generation; raised-field cultivators near Lake Titicaca managed complex water systems through daily, skilled labor; llama-caravan drivers spent weeks moving goods and animals across difficult mountain terrain between ecological zones; potters and weavers in workshops at Huari and Tiwanaku produced the finely made ceramics and textiles that carried political and religious meaning across great distances; and settlers in colonial communities in Moquegua and Cochabamba built new lives far from their highland homelands while maintaining connections to distant political and religious centers.

Archaeological evidence for these lives comes from house floors, food remains, tools, burial goods, and skeletal evidence of diet and labor, allowing historians to reconstruct at least an outline of daily existence even in the absence of written testimony. As with the discussion of early Paleoindian and Archaic populations in Chapter 2, the limitations of this evidence should be treated as an important lesson about how the availability of sources shapes what historians can confidently claim, rather than as a reason to ignore the majority of the population who left no monumental record of their own.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Wari and Tiwanaku, two highland Andean states that, during the centuries archaeologists call the Middle Horizon, roughly 600 to 1000 CE, achieved systems of political, economic, and religious integration operating at a scale substantially larger than the coastal, valley-based societies examined in Chapter 17. Rather than treating the two states as a single joint civilization, the chapter has insisted throughout on their distinct regional origins, their partly different methods of expansion, and the genuine uncertainty that continues to surround important aspects of each society's internal organization.

Students have examined Wari's heartland at Huari, in the Ayacucho basin, tracing its growth into a dense, competitively organized urban center whose political authority appears to have been actively negotiated among elite groups and kin-based communities rather than imposed unilaterally from a single ruling summit. The chapter has traced Wari expansion through planned provincial centers such as Pikillacta and Viracochapampa, through the frontier settlement of Cerro Baúl, and through roads, agricultural intensification, and storage systems that financed the state's political ambitions — while emphasizing, through the case of Ak'awillay, that Wari's actual administrative reach varied considerably from region to region and should not be assumed to have been uniform.

The chapter then shifted to Tiwanaku, examining its extraordinary urban and ceremonial core beside Lake Titicaca, including the Akapana, Kalasasaya, Semi-Subterranean Temple, and Pumapunku, and explaining how raised-field agriculture and camelid herding provided the economic foundation for Tiwanaku's political and religious achievements. Students have encountered ongoing scholarly debate over how centrally Tiwanaku agriculture was actually managed, and have examined evidence, including the 2025 discovery of the Palaspata temple complex, indicating substantial direct Tiwanaku state investment and attempted control of key interregional corridors well beyond the immediate Titicaca Basin, even though the temple's own precise date of construction still awaits confirmation.

The Moquegua Valley served as this chapter's central comparative case study, demonstrating that Wari and Tiwanaku settlements existed within the same valley system for generations, apparently coexisting without documented large-scale warfare, even as each state pursued its own distinct strategies of administration, colonization, and religious expression. This case illustrates the chapter's central argument in concentrated form: Wari and Tiwanaku were contemporaneous, comparably ambitious, but methodologically distinct highland systems, not two branches of a single empire.

Finally, the chapter has traced the roughly contemporaneous, though not identical, decline or transformation of both states around the end of the first millennium CE, resisting single-cause explanations and noting that recent Bayesian radiocarbon modeling places Tiwanaku's own urban collapse, around 1010–1050 CE, before the major regional drought once used to explain it, pointing instead toward political fragmentation, disrupted exchange networks, and internal social change as more central factors. This decline opened the more regionally fragmented political landscape examined in Chapter 19, out of which new kingdoms, including Chimú, and the early political developments around Cusco eventually emerged — developments that should be understood as responses to a transformed post-Wari, post-Tiwanaku world rather than as direct continuations of either Middle Horizon state.

Chapter 18 has asked how two highland Andean societies built systems of integration reaching across hundreds of kilometers and multiple ecological zones. Chapter 19 turns to the regional kingdoms and rising powers, including Chimú and early Cusco, that emerged from the political landscape Wari and Tiwanaku left behind.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Middle Horizon
The archaeological period, roughly 600–1000 CE, marked by unusually wide-reaching political, religious, and cultural integration across the Central Andes, associated primarily with Wari and Tiwanaku.
Wari
A highland Andean state centered at the city of Huari in the Ayacucho basin, known for planned provincial administrative centers, roads, and interregional political economy.
Tiwanaku
A highland Andean state centered near Lake Titicaca, known for monumental urban-ceremonial architecture, raised-field agriculture, and long-distance colonies and exchange.
Huari (Wari)
The urban capital of the Wari state, notable for dense compounds associated with elite and kin-based groups rather than a single dominant ceremonial plaza.
Pikillacta
A large, formally planned Wari administrative center near Cusco, displaying rectilinear, standardized architecture.
Viracochapampa
A planned Wari administrative center in the northern Peruvian highlands, apparently never fully completed.
Cerro Baúl
A Wari settlement atop a defensible mesa in the Moquegua Valley, located near contemporaneous Tiwanaku communities.
Ak'awillay
A local Cusco-region community whose archaeological record shows limited transformation despite nearby Wari colonization, cautioning against assuming uniform Wari control.
Altiplano
The high, cold plateau surrounding Lake Titicaca, home to Tiwanaku and its associated agricultural and pastoral economy.
Akapana
A large stepped platform mound at Tiwanaku, interpreted by many archaeologists as a monumental representation of a sacred mountain.
Kalasasaya
A raised rectangular platform enclosure at Tiwanaku associated with public ceremony.
Semi-Subterranean Temple
A sunken rectangular court at Tiwanaku lined with carved stone tenon-heads.
Pumapunku
A monumental complex at Tiwanaku renowned for extraordinarily precise cut-stone architecture.
Gateway of the Sun
A carved monolithic gateway at Tiwanaku bearing a central staff-holding deity figure flanked by attendants.
Raised-field agriculture
An intensive farming technique using elevated planting platforms separated by water-filled canals to reduce frost damage and improve irrigation, associated with the Titicaca Basin.
Camelid
Llamas and alpacas, animals central to Andean highland economies for wool, meat, transportation, and ritual use.
Llama caravan
Organized groups of pack llamas used to move goods across long distances and different ecological zones in the Andes.
Staff Deity (Staff God)
A frontal, staff-holding figure appearing in Wari, Tiwanaku, and earlier Andean religious imagery, used as an important, though not proof of unified, shared visual and religious tradition.
Palaspata
A Tiwanaku-associated temple complex identified through 2025 research roughly 215 kilometers southeast of Tiwanaku, evidence of substantial Tiwanaku interregional investment; the temple itself still awaits direct dating.
Ayllu
A later Andean term for a kin-based social and landholding group; used cautiously for the Middle Horizon since its precise earlier meaning cannot simply be assumed from later Inca and colonial usage.
Political economy
The systems of production, storage, exchange, and resource control that finance and sustain a state's political and religious activities.
Provincial colony
A settlement established by a state in a distant or ecologically distinct region, maintaining cultural and economic ties to the home state.
Administrative center
A planned settlement built to house officials, laborers, and infrastructure supporting a state's control over a distant province.
Vertical ecology
The Andean pattern of accessing sharply different growing conditions across short distances of elevation change, a key motivation for interregional expansion.
Sunken court
A rectangular, below-ground-level ceremonial space, as found at Tiwanaku's Semi-Subterranean Temple and at a provincial Tiwanaku temple in Moquegua.
Ashlar masonry
Precisely cut and fitted stone blocks, exemplified by construction at Pumapunku.
Isotopic analysis
A scientific technique used to determine an individual's diet or place of origin from chemical signatures preserved in bone or teeth, increasingly used to study Wari and Tiwanaku population movement.
Feasting
Large-scale hosted meals and drinking events, often involving maize beer, used by Wari and other Andean states to build and reinforce political relationships.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold two highland histories in view at once — Wari's roads, provincial centers, and negotiated internal politics, and Tiwanaku's monumental capital, raised fields, and expanding colonial and religious network — without collapsing them into a single story or assuming that either simply anticipated the other. That discipline matters beyond this chapter alone. It models the kind of careful, comparative historical reasoning this textbook will ask of students throughout Part IV and beyond: resisting the urge to flatten genuinely distinct societies into a single narrative merely because they shared a chronological period, a region, or even elements of religious imagery.

The chapter has also reinforced a caution first introduced in earlier chapters of this volume: the danger of reading later, better-documented institutions backward onto earlier and less fully understood societies. Wari roads were not the Inca road system. Wari and Tiwanaku administrative practices were not simply early drafts of Inca provincial government. Recognizing Wari and Tiwanaku on their own historical terms, with their own distinctive achievements and their own genuine limitations and uncertainties, prepares students to encounter Chimú, the early Inca state, and the mature Tawantinsuyu of Chapters 19 and 20 without assuming that Andean political history moved along a single, inevitable line of increasing centralization.

Finally, this chapter's treatment of ongoing archaeological debate — over whether "empire" fits either state, over how centrally Tiwanaku agriculture was managed, and over what a 2025 discovery at Palaspata reveals about Tiwanaku's reach — illustrates that the study of the ancient Andes remains a living, actively developing field of research, not a closed body of settled facts. Students who understand Wari and Tiwanaku as two ambitious, only partially comparable highland systems, built through different combinations of administration, colonization, religion, and agricultural intensification, and who understand that scholarly interpretation of both continues to change with new evidence, are well prepared to read the regional kingdoms of Chapter 19 and the Inca Empire of Chapter 20 with the same careful attention to evidence and complexity.

The story of Andean civilization does not end with Wari and Tiwanaku's transformation around the close of the first millennium CE. It continues, as Chapter 19 explains, through new regional kingdoms and rising political centers that emerged from the landscape these two highland states had reshaped — a landscape whose infrastructure, agricultural techniques, religious traditions, and political lessons would, in modified and reinterpreted form, echo through the centuries leading toward the rise of Tawantinsuyu.
