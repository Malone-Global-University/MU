Chapter Opening: Four Pressures on One Region

Between 2020 and 2026, a pandemic, mass movement of people, the world's search for climate-regulating forests and battery minerals, and a realignment of global trade converged on Latin America. None was new in origin, and all arrived in societies whose inequalities and institutions had been shaped by the centuries described in earlier volumes.

In March 2020, governments across the region closed schools, borders, and workplaces within days of one another. The measures were similar, but their effects were not. Where a household depended on daily informal earnings, staying home could mean going without food. Where a hospital lacked oxygen or intensive-care beds, a respiratory epidemic became a catastrophe. The pandemic did not create these conditions. It exposed them with unusual speed and, in measurable ways, intensified them. Chapter 114 begins there because the pandemic became a shared reference point for nearly every social and economic question that followed.

The chapter's governing question is how the pandemic, migration, climate pressures, the energy transition, and shifting trade relationships transformed Latin America's development prospects, and why these changes produced new opportunities and new forms of dependency at once. The argument developed across five divisions is that economic growth, resource wealth, and geopolitical importance do not automatically produce inclusive development. Forests, lithium, copper, and ports have made the region more important to the world. Whether they make its people better off depends on institutions, ownership, regulation, and the participation of the communities who live where resources are found.

Migration forms the second division. Between 2021 and 2024 more than a million people crossed the Darién Gap, a roadless forest between Colombia and Panama, and Panamanian authorities registered 520,085 crossings in 2023 alone. In 2025 the number fell to roughly three thousand, and in the first months of 2026 it stayed in the hundreds. Meanwhile money sent home by migrants reached a record in 2025. These facts belong together: migration is not only a story of border crossings but of households, remittances, labor markets, and the policies of receiving states. The chapter distinguishes carefully among refugees, migrant workers, deportees, and others, because the legal categories shape what people may claim and what states owe them.

The third and fourth divisions turn from people to places. Amazonia is not an untouched wilderness. It is a region of long human settlement, rubber booms, highways, cattle frontiers, Indigenous territories, and rival national development plans. Brazilian Amazon deforestation fell in 2025–2026 to the lowest preliminary level in the official satellite record, yet a new financing mechanism launched at the 2025 climate conference in Belém had raised far less than its backers sought. In the Andes, the energy transition made lithium and copper strategic. Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia hold much of the world's known lithium resources, but they have converted those resources into production in very different ways and at very different speeds.

The fifth division places these developments in the context of international trade and rivalry. China became the region's second-largest trading partner and the largest for several South American economies. Peru's Chancay port, inaugurated in November 2024, became a symbol of Pacific connectivity and of disputes over sovereignty and regulation. The United States remained the largest single market for the region as a whole and the anchor of Mexican manufacturing, while its tariff and migration policies in 2025–2026 unsettled both. Latin American governments, firms, workers, and communities pursued their own, often conflicting, strategies. They were not passive objects of great-power competition, and the chapter does not treat them as such.

Evidence requires care throughout. Chapter 114 relies on statistics produced by institutions with different purposes: the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the OECD and World Bank, the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC), the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB), Brazil's National Institute for Space Research (INPE), the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), and the International Energy Agency (IEA). These figures measure different things: official deaths and excess deaths, crossing events and unique persons, resources and reserves, preliminary alerts and annual estimates. Misreading units is among the most common errors in contemporary history, and the chapter's quantitative features exist to practice avoiding it.

The chapter's boundaries matter. Its historical cutoff is 16 November 2026. Several subjects remained unresolved at that date: the consolidation of Brazil's 2026 forest-loss estimate, the disbursement of forest-finance pledges, the environmental approval of lithium expansions in Chile, the legal status of Chancay's regulation, and the annual reviews of the USMCA. These are presented as unfolding developments, not completed outcomes. Venezuela's political and international crisis is examined in Chapters 109 and 115; presidential politics in Chapter 113; gangs and illegal mining networks in Chapters 111 and 112; and the social movements of Indigenous and environmental activism in Chapter 110. This chapter supplies the social, environmental, economic, and geopolitical frame within which those stories unfolded.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how pre-pandemic health systems, informal employment, housing, and inequality shaped COVID-19's effects in Latin America.
  2. Distinguish officially reported COVID-19 deaths from modeled excess-mortality estimates, and explain why they differ.
  3. Compare vaccine procurement, domestic production, and uptake across several countries, separating availability from vaccination coverage.
  4. Assess the pandemic's educational, labor, and poverty effects and the limits of emergency social protection.
  5. Differentiate refugees, asylum seekers, migrant workers, internally displaced persons, and deportees, and apply the categories to regional cases.
  6. Interpret Darién Gap crossing data, remittance data, and migrant-stock data while distinguishing events, flows, and stocks.
  7. Evaluate remittances as both household survival strategy and a source of macroeconomic dependence, using absolute and GDP-share measures.
  8. Analyze Amazonian deforestation as the product of economic incentives, infrastructure, enforcement, land tenure, and politics, and distinguish alerts from annual estimates.
  9. Explain the role of Indigenous territories and institutions in environmental governance and the tensions between conservation and livelihoods.
  10. Distinguish mineral resources, reserves, production, refining, and manufacturing, and compare lithium strategies in Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia.
  11. Evaluate China's and the United States' differing economic roles in Latin America without reducing the region to a choice between them.
  12. Connect the chapter's five divisions to the question of who controls resources, who benefits, and which institutions manage long-term risk.

Historical Context

The twenty-first century opened with a commodity boom described in Chapter 108. From roughly 2003 to 2013, rising demand from China and high prices for oil, copper, soybeans, and iron ore raised export revenues across South America. Several governments used the windfall to expand cash-transfer programs and public spending, and poverty fell substantially. ECLAC's measurements show regional poverty declining from 51.2% in 1990 to 27.7% in 2014. Then the boom ended. Commodity prices fell after 2014, growth slowed, and ECLAC began describing the period as a second "lost decade," echoing the debt crisis of the 1980s examined in Chapter 93. By 2019, several countries had experienced political unrest and slow growth, and the regional economy entered the pandemic in a weak position.

The region's health, labor, and welfare institutions were products of earlier political settlements. Brazil's 1988 Constitution established a universal public health system; Mexico, Colombia, Chile, and Peru built fragmented systems that separated formal workers, the poor, and the wealthy; Central American and Haitian systems were chronically underfunded. The neoliberal reforms of the 1990s, examined in Chapter 95, had expanded private insurance and weakened public provision in some countries, while social-protection systems built during the Pink Tide, examined in Chapter 108, remained uneven and often dependent on fiscal conditions. Roughly half of the region's workers were in informal employment before 2020, without paid leave, unemployment insurance, or employer-provided health coverage.

Migration had been a feature of Latin American societies for generations, and Chapters 69, 91, 109, and 111 trace earlier waves: European immigration to the Southern Cone, rural-to-urban movement, flight from civil war, and, in the 2010s, the Venezuelan exodus and Central American displacement associated with violence and poverty. Remittances had grown steadily since the 1990s, and diasporas in the United States, Spain, and neighboring countries had become permanent features of national economies. The organizing patterns of the 2020s, including the Darién crossings and the reversal of flows after 2025, grew out of these earlier movements rather than appearing from nowhere.

The region's environment and resource economy had its own history. Amazonia had been a site of Indigenous settlement long before the rubber boom described in Chapter 69, and Chapter 23 examined the pre-conquest evidence for large-scale human modification of the forest. The twentieth century added highways, mining, ranching, and colonization schemes. Latin America's mineral wealth had shaped its colonial history, as Chapter 42's account of Potosí shows, and its twentieth-century politics, as Chapter 74 shows for Mexican oil and Chapter 105 for Venezuelan petroleum. Chile's nationalization of copper under Allende, examined in Chapter 85, left a state mining company, Codelco, that remained central to the lithium debates of the 2020s. Resource nationalism, foreign investment, and dependence on export commodities were therefore long-standing themes that the energy transition revived in new form.

Finally, the international setting changed. China's economic rise, the subject of Chapter 108 in its first phase, deepened through the 2010s and 2020s. The United States remained a crucial market, source of remittances, and security partner, but its policies shifted markedly after 2017 and again in 2025, with tariffs, migration restrictions, and new interest in critical minerals. Climate negotiations, culminating in the 2025 conference in Belém, made Latin American forests a subject of global diplomacy. Chapter 114 follows these threads into November 2026, treating Latin America as an arena in which many actors, local, national, and international, pursued competing aims.

The COVID-19 Pandemic and Latin America's Social Crisis

Health systems, mortality, vaccination, schooling, work, and poverty

Section 114.1

Latin America on the Eve of COVID-19: Inequality, Health Systems, and Vulnerability

Latin America entered 2020 with health systems built on very different principles. Brazil's Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS), created by the 1988 Constitution, guaranteed care as a right of citizenship and reached into primary-care clinics, vaccination campaigns, and community health agents across a country of continental scale. Costa Rica's social-security fund provided near-universal coverage on a smaller scale. Mexico, by contrast, divided its population among institutions for formal-sector workers, such as the IMSS and ISSSTE, and programs for the uninsured. The Seguro Popular created in 2003 was replaced in 2020 by a new federal institute that was itself restructured in 2023, so that the pandemic struck during an administrative transition. Colombia combined contributory and subsidized insurance under a 1993 law; Chile paired a public insurer with private insurers; Peru's system divided users among a ministry of health, a social-security institute, and the armed forces' and police services. In much of Central America and in Haiti, public spending on health remained among the lowest in the hemisphere.

These differences mattered, but so did a feature common to most of the region: the large informal economy. Before the pandemic, roughly half of Latin American workers earned their living without formal contracts, unemployment insurance, or paid sick leave. Street vendors, domestic workers, construction laborers, drivers, and small farmers could not work from home, and a lockdown that suspended their income also suspended their food supply. Informality was not evenly distributed. Women, young people, Indigenous people, and Afro-descendant people were more likely to hold informal jobs, and women in particular carried an unpaid burden of caring for children and elders that rose when schools and clinics closed.

Housing and infrastructure shaped vulnerability as well. Large shares of the urban population lived in informal settlements, among them Brazilian favelas, Peruvian asentamientos humanos, and Argentine villas, where families shared small rooms and where water, sanitation, or reliable electricity could not be assumed. Instructions to wash hands and keep distance assumed resources that many households lacked. Rural and Indigenous communities faced different problems: distance from hospitals, few specialists, and in some Amazonian regions, a journey by river of many hours or days to reach intensive care. Chronic diseases, especially diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, were widespread in several countries, notably Mexico, and raised the risk of severe COVID-19.

Inequality also shaped information and trust. Governments that had enjoyed strong public confidence, or that had long histories of effective immunization campaigns, could rely on social cohesion; those that had been weakened by corruption scandals or political polarization found their messages contested. The region's political context, treated in Chapter 113, therefore influenced the public-health response, though this chapter concentrates on the social and economic consequences rather than on the controversies surrounding particular presidents.

The central point of this section is analytical. The pandemic did not create Latin America's inequalities, and historians should resist explanations that treat it as a bolt from the blue. It acted on structures that had developed over centuries: the colonial distinctions of race and status examined in Chapter 45, the export-oriented economies of Chapters 68 and 69, and the uneven welfare states of the twentieth century. COVID-19 exposed and intensified these structures, and the measurable consequences of that exposure are the subject of the next three sections.

Racial and ethnic hierarchies deserve explicit mention. Indigenous and Afro-descendant people were overrepresented among the poor, the informally employed, and residents of areas with weak services, and their communities often had less access to hospitals, to testing, and to information in their own languages. Indigenous organizations in several countries, among them Brazil's national Indigenous coalition, compiled their own counts of infections and deaths because they distrusted or could not rely on official statistics. These practices belong to the long history of Indigenous self-representation traced in Chapters 96 and 110, and they remind students that health data are produced by institutions that do not see every community equally.

Section 114.2

The Arrival of COVID-19 and the Public-Health Emergency, 2020–2021

The first confirmed case in Latin America was reported in São Paulo on 26 February 2020, in a traveler returned from Italy. Within weeks, every country in the region had cases. Governments responded with border closures, school closures, curfews, and, in many places, stay-at-home orders. Some acted early and strictly. Peru declared a state of emergency and a national quarantine in mid-March 2020, and Argentina imposed a long national lockdown. Others, notably Mexico and Brazil, pursued less uniform strategies, with decisions often delegated to states and municipalities. Testing capacity was limited almost everywhere in the early months, and official case counts reflected how much a country tested as much as how many people were infected.

The emergency produced scenes that became symbols of the crisis. In April 2020, the Ecuadorian port city of Guayaquil saw hospitals overwhelmed and funeral services unable to cope, and families reported waiting days to retrieve bodies. In Peru, shortages of oxygen and intensive-care beds left families searching for cylinders, and the country recorded some of the highest COVID-19 death rates per capita in the world, despite its early and strict lockdown. Analysts have offered several explanations: crowded markets and transport on which informal workers depended, a health system with very few intensive-care beds, and the difficulty of keeping people at home who earned a living day to day. In January 2021, Manaus, the largest city of the Brazilian Amazon, experienced a collapse of oxygen supplies during a surge associated with the P.1 (Gamma) variant. Brazil recorded roughly 700,000 reported COVID-19 deaths in total, and Mexico roughly 330,000, among the highest absolute totals in the world, though population size and measurement differences complicate comparison.

The Caribbean followed a different course. Many island states closed borders early and, with small populations, avoided the largest early waves, but their tourism-dependent economies suffered severe shocks when travel stopped. Haiti, with a fragile public-health system and political crisis, recorded relatively few confirmed deaths, though limited testing makes the true toll uncertain. Cuba's strong primary-care network and early domestic vaccine development offered a different model, though the country also faced shortages and economic hardship during 2021.

Official death counts understate the pandemic's true mortality, and the size of the gap varies. Excess-mortality methods compare all deaths in a period with the number expected from earlier years, and thus capture deaths that were not tested, not recorded as COVID-19, or caused by interrupted care for other conditions. The OECD and World Bank estimated approximately 2.3 million excess deaths across Latin America and the Caribbean during 2020–2021, a figure higher than the reported COVID-19 total for the same years. Different institutions use different baselines and models, so estimates vary, and countries with weaker death registration, such as some Andean and Central American states, are harder to assess.

The consequences extended beyond death. Hospital systems postponed cancer screenings, routine immunizations, and treatment of chronic diseases, with effects that appeared in later health statistics. Health workers faced high infection and mortality rates and, in many countries, strikes and protests over pay and protective equipment. Emergency declarations changed state powers, and in some countries they opened new debates about police enforcement of curfews and the treatment of informal workers. This section's account stops at the medical emergency; the political controversies surrounding presidents' responses belong to Chapter 113.

The pattern of mortality also varied within countries. Dense, poor neighborhoods of large cities, river settlements with no hospital, and prisons, where overcrowding made distancing impossible, experienced severe outbreaks, while some wealthy districts had access to private intensive care and early vaccination. Differences in death registration complicate the story: countries such as Chile and Uruguay, with long-established civil-registration systems, can be measured with confidence, while others must be estimated. Students should therefore treat any ranking of countries by pandemic mortality as a statement about both disease and data, and compare excess-death estimates with official counts before drawing conclusions about government performance.

Historical Vocabulary
Excess mortality

Excess mortality is the difference between the number of deaths observed in a period and the number that would have been expected without the event under study, estimated from earlier years' patterns. It captures deaths directly caused by an epidemic, deaths from untreated other conditions, and deaths that were never tested or recorded.

The term matters because it prevents students from equating "reported COVID-19 deaths" with "people killed by the pandemic." It is a modeled estimate, not a count: different baselines produce different totals, and weak civil registration limits accuracy. Comparisons between countries should use deaths per population, not raw totals.

Analysis: These reports are valuable because they standardize information across dozens of jurisdictions and show how the epidemic moved through the region. Their limits follow from their sources: national reports differ in testing intensity and in how deaths are attributed, and early reports omit deaths that were never diagnosed. Because the data come from governments, they also reflect political incentives to emphasize or minimize figures. Reports say little about how households experienced the crisis.

Section 114.3

Vaccination, Scientific Cooperation, and the Unequal Recovery

Vaccines reached Latin America through three channels: multilateral purchase, bilateral contracts, and domestic development. COVAX, a mechanism coordinated by the World Health Organization, Gavi, and the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations, aimed to pool purchases for lower-income countries, and it supplied doses to many states, notably in the Caribbean and Central America, though deliveries lagged behind demand in 2021. Wealthier or better-connected governments signed bilateral agreements with manufacturers in the United States, Europe, China, and Russia. Mexico, Chile, and Costa Rica began vaccinating in late December 2020, and Argentina used the Russian Sputnik V vaccine early. Countries mixed products, sometimes from several suppliers, as availability changed.

Brazil's experience illustrates how domestic institutions mattered. The Butantan Institute in São Paulo partnered with the Chinese firm Sinovac on CoronaVac, and the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (Fiocruz) produced the Oxford–AstraZeneca vaccine under license. Brazil also had an unusually strong tradition of public immunization, anchored by a national program with wide reach, and despite political conflict over vaccine policy, it achieved high coverage by 2022. Cuba developed its own vaccines, Abdala and Soberana 2, and used them in a mass campaign that reached a very large share of its population, though the vaccines' international scientific evaluation was limited. Chile and Uruguay, with strong primary-care networks and effective logistics, vaccinated rapidly. Haiti, by contrast, received its first doses through COVAX only in mid-2021, and coverage remained very low for years.

Two distinctions guide interpretation. First, vaccine availability differs from vaccine uptake. A country could have doses but fail to deliver them because of weak logistics, or deliver them widely because public trust and primary care were strong. Misinformation and political polarization affected uptake in some places, though by 2022 the Americas as a whole had some of the world's highest vaccination coverage. Second, vaccination reduced deaths and hospitalizations but did not reverse the economic and educational damage already done. Children who had lost years of regular schooling, and workers who had lost jobs and savings, did not recover automatically.

Regional institutions did contribute. The PAHO Revolving Fund, a pooled-procurement mechanism that had long supplied routine vaccines at negotiated prices, became one channel for COVAX deliveries, and PAHO's technical advisory groups issued recommendations that national ministries adapted. Public laboratories in Brazil, Argentina, Mexico, and Cuba took part in manufacture, filling, or packaging. These institutional layers meant that a country's experience depended on more than its income: it depended on whether procurement, cold chains, regulation, and community health workers already existed and could be coordinated.

International relationships shaped access. China and Russia competed with Western suppliers, using vaccine diplomacy to build influence, while the United States donated doses to several Latin American countries in 2021 after earlier delays. Regional institutions played a smaller role than might have been expected, and efforts to develop regional manufacturing, including mRNA research platforms supported by PAHO, remained limited in scale. The episode taught policymakers that pharmaceutical supply chains were concentrated outside the region, a lesson that echoed in later debates about industrial policy.

The acute emergency ended formally on 5 May 2023, when the World Health Organization declared that COVID-19 no longer constituted a public-health emergency of international concern. In Latin America this meant a shift from emergency measures to routine management: booster programs, surveillance, and the integration of COVID-19 care into regular services. But the shift also exposed unfinished business. Health systems had to rebuild interrupted immunization schedules, address backlogs, and decide whether to invest in resilience or return to earlier patterns, and those decisions varied with each country's fiscal and political circumstances.

Section 114.4

Schools, Employment, Poverty, and the Long Social Aftermath

School closures were among the pandemic's most consequential decisions. Latin American schools closed for longer, on average, than those in most other regions, and many children had no reliable alternative. Households without computers, broadband, or quiet space could not take advantage of remote instruction, and rural and Indigenous students were particularly affected. Teachers improvised with radio, television, and messaging applications. The result was widespread learning loss, with disruptions to literacy and numeracy that researchers expect to affect earnings and mobility for years. Some adolescents left school to work, and some girls faced increased household burdens. ECLAC's 2025 Social Panorama drew attention to educational inequality as a central mechanism by which disadvantage reproduces itself.

Labor markets contracted sharply in 2020. Informal workers, who lacked formal protections, lost income quickly, and women's labor-force participation fell more than men's, partly because the closure of schools and care services shifted unpaid work to households. Domestic workers, who were overwhelmingly women, were particularly exposed. Recovery in 2021 and 2022 restored many jobs, but the character of employment changed: platform work, remote work in some sectors, and a larger share of small, informal enterprises. Food insecurity rose, and communal kitchens and neighborhood solidarity networks, with a long history in countries such as Peru, expanded in poorer districts.

States responded with emergency cash transfers whose scale and design varied widely. Brazil's emergency aid in 2020 paid substantial monthly sums to tens of millions of people and temporarily reduced poverty. Argentina, Colombia, Peru, and several other countries introduced or expanded transfers, using existing registries where they could and building new ones where they could not. Mexico provided comparatively limited new fiscal support, relying on existing programs. These measures protected many households, but they also raised public debt and fiscal deficits at a time of weak growth, and as emergency programs ended, inflation in 2021–2022 eroded purchasing power. Governments then faced pressure to cut spending or raise taxes while the social needs created by the crisis remained.

ECLAC documented the consequences. Regional poverty rose to 32.8% in 2020 and extreme poverty to 13.2%, reversing gains and wiping out years of progress. Subsequent recovery brought poverty down to 25.5% of the population, about 162 million people, in 2024, the lowest level in comparable data, and extreme poverty to 9.8%, about 62 million people, though still above the 2014 low. ECLAC attributed the 2024 improvement mainly to Mexico and, to a lesser extent, Brazil, which together hold more than half of the regional population. At the same time, the commission found income concentration still extreme, with the richest tenth receiving about 34% of income and the poorest tenth under 2%. The apparent paradox of falling poverty and persistent inequality is a central lesson: recovery did not remove the structures that had made the pandemic so damaging.

By 2026, the social effects included educational losses among cohorts who had been in school in 2020–2021, higher household debt in many countries, changed labor arrangements, and a renewed argument about social protection. ECLAC argued that Latin American states still lacked institutions capable of designing and financing durable social policies, noting that social spending was roughly 11.6% of GDP in 2024. Others argued that growth, fiscal restraint, and private-sector dynamism offered better routes to prosperity. The pandemic was both a public-health emergency and a turning point in social development, and its legacy shaped the migration, resource, and trade debates examined in the following divisions.

The care economy illustrates the point. When schools and daycare closed, mothers, grandmothers, and older sisters absorbed the added work, often leaving paid jobs or reducing hours, and the recovery of women's employment lagged behind men's in several countries. States that had invested earlier in child care and school meals were better able to cushion households, whereas others discovered how much of their social protection depended on unpaid labor. Debates after 2023 about national care systems, which several governments began to design, trace directly to this experience.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did COVID-19 Expose Existing Inequality or Fundamentally Change Latin American Development?

The question: Was the pandemic principally an accelerator of conditions that already existed, or an independent turning point that altered household finances, education, labor, state capacity, and political expectations?

Interpretation A — Exposure and continuity: The pandemic's effects followed the lines of existing inequality: informal employment, fragmented health systems, and weak social protection explain who suffered most. Poverty returned to pre-pandemic levels within a few years, and structural weaknesses remained. On this view, COVID-19 was a stress test.
Interpretation B — Distinct turning point: The pandemic produced lasting effects that would not have occurred otherwise: learning losses that will shape cohorts' lifetime earnings, higher public and household debt, new labor arrangements, and altered expectations of the state. On this view, the pandemic changed the trajectory.

Evidence: Both views use ECLAC and OECD data. The first emphasizes the recovery of poverty rates and the persistence of inequality; the second points to educational measures and fiscal data whose consequences unfold over decades and cannot yet be fully measured.

Why it matters: If the pandemic only exposed existing problems, policy should address those underlying structures. If it changed trajectories, targeted repair, such as learning recovery, becomes urgent. The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and evidence through 2026 is incomplete for long-term effects.

Student task: Argue whether the pandemic was mainly an accelerator, an independent turning point, or both, identifying what evidence from 2030 or later would help decide.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households under Lockdown

For a family in an informal settlement, lockdown meant sharing a few rooms with extended relatives, rationing water, and deciding who could safely leave to find work. Women often combined paid or informal labor with supervising children who had no school, and domestic workers lost jobs when employers sent them home. Section 114.5

Latin America in Motion: Migration beyond the Traditional Northbound Model

For much of the twentieth century, students could describe Latin American emigration with a single arrow pointing north, from Mexico and the Caribbean to the United States. By the 2010s that picture had become inadequate. Movement within South America grew rapidly, Central American migration extended through Mexico to the United States and also to Costa Rica, Belize, and Mexico itself, Haitians and Cubans moved through several countries, and return migration became a significant phenomenon. A region that had long exported people also became a region of transit and settlement.

Venezuela provides the largest example, examined in detail in Chapters 109 and 115. The Regional Inter-Agency Coordination Platform for Refugees and Migrants from Venezuela (R4V), led by UNHCR and the International Organization for Migration, counted several million Venezuelans abroad, most of them in Latin American and Caribbean countries. Colombia hosted the largest number, followed by Peru, Brazil, Ecuador, and Chile. R4V's published totals have been revised over time as people returned, acquired citizenship, or moved on, and one 2025 update cited about 6.8 million Venezuelans in seventeen response countries. These are estimates of stocks, not annual flows, and they differ from the number of people who crossed a particular border in a particular year. Colombia's decision in 2021 to grant ten-year temporary protection to Venezuelan migrants became a regional model of regularization, though it was not matched everywhere.

Other movements followed their own logics. Central American migrants, many from Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador, left for reasons that combined violence, which Chapter 111 examines, with poverty, family reunification, and weather-related loss of livelihoods. Nicaraguans left in large numbers after the political crisis that began in 2018 and intensified later. Cubans left in unusually large numbers in 2021–2023 amid economic hardship. Haitians moved to the Dominican Republic, Chile, Brazil, and Mexico, driven by insecurity, political breakdown, and disasters. Within Colombia, decades of armed conflict had already produced millions of internally displaced people. Each movement had distinct causes, destinations, and legal consequences.

Students must therefore distinguish legal and analytical categories. A refugee is a person who has crossed a border and meets the definition in the 1951 Convention, or in Latin America, the broader Cartagena Declaration of 1984, which includes people fleeing generalized violence. An asylum seeker has applied for protection and awaits a decision. A migrant worker moves primarily for employment. An internally displaced person has been forced to leave home but has not crossed an international border. A deportee or returnee has been removed or has come back to a country of origin. These categories overlap in practice, since one family might include a refugee, a labor migrant, and a child born abroad, but they carry different rights, and conflating them obscures both history and policy.

Host societies responded unevenly. Colombian officials presented regularization as both a humanitarian duty and a way to bring workers into the formal economy, while municipal governments in Peru, Chile, and elsewhere faced pressure from voters who blamed newcomers for crime and unemployment, charges that research in several countries has not supported on average. Venezuelan migrants worked in construction, commerce, delivery services, and care, often below their qualifications, and many sent remittances to relatives remaining in Venezuela, linking the region's migration story to the remittance economy discussed in Section 114.7.

The causes of movement were multiple. Economic crises pushed people from Venezuela, Cuba, and Haiti; insecurity from Central America and Haiti; family reunification kept flows continuing even when other conditions improved; labor demand in the United States, Chile, Argentina, and Brazil attracted workers; and environmental disruption, discussed in Section 114.8, added pressure. The geography of migration also reflects the policies of receiving states, which can redirect movement without removing its causes. That insight is essential for understanding the abrupt changes of 2024–2026 examined next.

Section 114.6

The Darién Gap, Migration Corridors, and Border Policy

The Darién Gap is a stretch of forest, swamp, and mountain on the border between Colombia and Panama, roughly one hundred kilometers across, through which no road runs. For decades it was crossed mainly by smugglers, traffickers, and local communities. After 2021 it became one of the world's busiest irregular migration corridors. Migrants, many of them Venezuelans, Haitians, Ecuadorians, and, increasingly, people from Asia and Africa, traveled by boat to the Colombian coast, walked for several days through the forest with guides, and emerged in Panama, from where they continued by bus through Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Honduras, Guatemala, and Mexico toward the United States.

The crossing was dangerous. Migrants faced river crossings, steep terrain, exposure, injuries, and illness, and they were vulnerable to robbery and sexual violence by armed groups. Humanitarian organizations such as Médecins Sans Frontières documented treatment of large numbers of people, including victims of violence. Families traveled with small children, and UNICEF reported that the number of children crossing in 2023 exceeded one hundred thousand, among them unaccompanied minors. A local economy grew up around the flow, including guides, boat operators, vendors, and lodging in Colombian and Panamanian border towns, and criminal networks exploited the migrants, a theme connected to the organized-crime systems of Chapter 112.

The numbers are striking but must be read carefully. Panama's National Migration Service registered about 133,000 crossings in 2021, 520,085 in 2023, and 302,203 in 2024. The counts record crossing events registered by authorities, not unique individuals, and the same person might in principle be counted more than once if they returned and crossed again, though this was uncommon. Nor do the counts include those who were not registered. The data also say nothing about the legal status of any individual, and irregular entry does not imply criminality; many crossers later sought asylum or other protection.

The corridor closed with remarkable speed. Crossings began to fall in mid-2024, as Panama, under President José Raúl Mulino, tightened controls and as migrants' expectations changed. After January 2025, when the United States ended the CBP One appointment system, restricted asylum access, and expanded removals, flows collapsed. Panama registered only about 3,100 crossings in all of 2025, most of them in the first months of the year, and about 500 through late September 2026. In their place appeared a reverse flow, with thousands of migrants, more than nine in ten of them Venezuelan according to Panamanian authorities, traveling south from Panama by sea or overland toward Colombia and South America. Other migrants were deported to Latin American countries from the United States, including, in some cases, people who were not nationals of the receiving country.

The Darién case shows that border policy can reshape routes, but it does not remove the reasons people leave, and the drop in crossings did not end the humanitarian problem. Migrants stranded in transit countries, forced to turn back or stay in precarious conditions, faced new risks, and humanitarian agencies warned that reduced visibility could conceal rather than resolve suffering. Governments in Panama, Costa Rica, Mexico, and Colombia each balanced cooperation with the United States against domestic politics and humanitarian obligations, and their choices, discussed further in Chapter 111 for Central America, reflected national interests as well as external pressure.

Mexico occupied a pivotal position between the Darién and the United States. Its government operated an immigration system that detained, processed, or dispersed migrants, ran reception and transport arrangements that changed several times, and, under U.S. pressure in 2019 and again after 2024, deployed security forces to slow movement. Southern Mexican towns, particularly in Chiapas, saw large numbers of migrants waiting for documents or work, and local economies and tensions shifted accordingly. When U.S. asylum access narrowed in 2025, migrants who had been waiting in Mexico faced choices among returning home, remaining, or seeking status in Mexico. The corridor was a chain of states, and a policy change at one end altered conditions at every other link.

Section 114.7

Remittances, Diasporas, and the Economics of Migration

Migrants send money home, and in Latin America these transfers, called remittances, became one of the largest sources of external income. The Inter-American Development Bank estimated that remittances to Latin America and the Caribbean reached a record $173.7 billion in 2025, a 7.3% increase over 2024 and the sixteenth consecutive year of growth in its projections. The IDB found growth far from uniform. Central America's remittances rose about 20% to roughly $55.5 billion, driven by Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, and Nicaragua, South America's rose about 10% to about $35 billion, and the Dominican Republic received about $11.9 billion. Mexico, the region's largest recipient and the second-largest in the world, was the main exception, with remittances falling from an exceptionally strong 2024 to roughly $61 billion in 2025.

The rapid growth in Central America deserves explanation. The IDB linked it to uncertainty about migration policy, since migrants who feared deportation or restrictions had reason to send savings home early, and to labor-market conditions in the United States. A new U.S. tax on cash-funded remittance transfers, taking effect in 2026, added a further consideration. By mid-2026 the IDB reported slower growth, with first-quarter 2026 flows rising about 5.7%, below the previous decade's average, and noted that the outlook depended on migrants' labor-market participation and currency movements. These dynamics show that remittances respond to the policies of sending and receiving states, not just to the needs of households.

Remittances financed everyday life. Families used them for food, rent, school fees, and health care, and when they exceeded daily needs, for building houses, buying land, paying debts, and starting small businesses. Studies in several countries link remittances to reduced household poverty and, in some cases, to higher school attendance. They also cushioned national economies, supplying foreign currency when export earnings fell. During the pandemic, remittances rose in many countries when other sources of income collapsed, an unexpected pattern that surprised forecasters.

Dependence is the other side. Two measures are necessary. The total value in dollars shows scale: Mexico's roughly $61 billion exceeds the total of most other countries. The share of national output shows dependence: according to IDB and central-bank estimates, remittances represent roughly a quarter of GDP in Honduras and Nicaragua, a substantial share in El Salvador, Guatemala, Haiti, and Jamaica, around 6% in Ecuador, about 3% in Colombia, and under 1% for South America as a whole. The Caribbean's remittances were estimated at about 10% of the subregion's GDP. A country with large flows relative to its economy is exposed to changes in employment or immigration policy in the United States or other host countries, and the households within it are exposed to the health of migrants' own jobs.

Critics point to costs. Remittances can sustain consumption without building productive investment, and they may reduce pressure on governments to create jobs and services. Emigration removes working-age people, and families left behind, especially children and elders, may be separated for years. Fees, informal channels, and exploitation by intermediaries also reduce what reaches households. Defenders respond that remittances are private income earned through migrants' labor, that they reach households directly and efficiently, and that they allow families to make their own choices. The interpretation depends on whether one asks about households, communities, or national development, and the evidence supports different answers at each level.

The cost of sending money also shapes outcomes. Fees and exchange-rate margins, which vary by corridor and provider, reduce what reaches families, and international bodies have set targets to lower them. Digital transfer services and mobile wallets expanded in the 2020s and lowered costs in some corridors, while people without bank accounts or identity documents, including many undocumented migrants and rural recipients, continued to depend on cash agents. Access to financial services therefore determines how much of a migrant's earnings becomes a household's income, and policies on identification, banking, and taxation can change that margin as much as migrants' wages do.

Historical Vocabulary
Remittance

A remittance is money or goods sent by a migrant to family or community in the country of origin. National statistics usually measure formal transfers recorded by central banks and money-transfer firms, so informal transfers carried by hand or through unregistered channels are undercounted.

The term matters because remittances are both a private household resource and a macroeconomic flow. Students should not treat them as foreign aid, since they are earnings of private individuals, and should distinguish nominal dollar totals from shares of GDP, which show dependence.

Households Connected across Borders

A remittance is often the result of a weekly or monthly routine: a worker in a distant city sets aside part of a wage, sends it through a transfer firm or phone application, and a relative collects it. Section 114.8

Migration, Climate Stress, and the Future of Regional Mobility

Climate and weather shaped migration in ways that are real but difficult to isolate. The Dry Corridor, a belt of seasonally arid land running through Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, and Nicaragua, supports millions of smallholders growing maize, beans, and coffee. Repeated droughts and erratic rains after 2012, along with coffee rust, reduced harvests and pushed some households toward debt and migration. In November 2020, hurricanes Eta and Iota struck Central America within two weeks, flooding homes and fields already strained by the pandemic. In the Caribbean, Hurricane Maria devastated Dominica and Puerto Rico in 2017, Haiti suffered a major earthquake in August 2021, and Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm, struck Jamaica in October 2025, with severe effects on housing and infrastructure.

Analysts distinguish disaster displacement from multi-cause migration. A hurricane can displace people immediately, and most of those displaced remain within their own countries or return home when conditions allow. Long-term migration across borders more often reflects a combination of pressures: failed harvests, debt, violence, lack of jobs, and the presence of relatives abroad who can help. It is misleading to attribute a household's departure to climate alone, and equally misleading to ignore climate as a contributing factor. The strongest evidence concerns households already poor, with limited ability to adapt.

Small island states face a distinctive version of the problem. A single hurricane can destroy a large share of a country's housing, tourism infrastructure, and crops, and the cost of rebuilding can exceed an annual budget. These states have argued in climate negotiations for financing to address loss and damage. Migration from the Caribbean, which has historically linked islands with Britain, France, Canada, and the United States, can serve as a form of insurance, since relatives abroad send money for reconstruction, which is one more way the remittance economy and the climate economy intersect.

Receiving communities experienced migration unevenly. In Colombia, regularization programs gave Venezuelans access to work, education, and health care, but also strained municipal services and produced tension in some cities. In Peru and Chile, large Venezuelan populations contributed to labor markets while becoming targets of resentment, and politicians in several countries used anti-migrant rhetoric. Migrants' access to legal work, schools, and clinics depended on legal status, which in turn depended on policy decisions that changed with governments. Refugee and migrant children faced obstacles to enrollment, and women faced particular risks of exploitation and violence.

Policy changes after 2024 altered regional geography. The United States restricted asylum access and humanitarian parole programs, expanded deportations, and pressed neighbors to cooperate, and Mexico and Panama adjusted their own enforcement. Migrants who might have continued north remained in Mexico or turned back, some Venezuelans moved south, and deportees returned to countries with limited capacity to receive them. Receiving states in Central America and the Caribbean faced new demands for reintegration, and remittance-dependent households faced new risks. By November 2026, migration had reshaped regional relationships, with Colombia, Panama, Mexico, and Central American states each negotiating their position between the United States and their own domestic constituencies.

This section closes the demographic story and opens the environmental one. Migration, remittances, and climate vulnerability all connect to the question of how Latin American economies use land, water, and minerals. The same rural households that send migrants abroad often live near forests, rivers, and mines, and the same governments that depend on remittances also seek revenue from natural resources. Divisions III and IV examine those resources, beginning with Amazonia.

Section 114.9

Amazonia as an Ecological, Economic, and Political Region

The Amazon basin covers roughly seven million square kilometers, shared by Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Bolivia, Ecuador, Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana, an overseas department of France. Brazil holds about sixty percent of the Amazon forest biome, Peru and Colombia most of the remainder. The Amazon River and its tributaries carry a very large share of the freshwater that flows to the world's oceans. The forest stores large quantities of carbon, recycles moisture into the atmosphere that falls as rain across much of South America, and shelters a biodiversity that scientists are still cataloguing. These functions explain why the forest matters to climate policy far beyond the nine countries that contain it.

Students should distinguish the Amazon basin from Brazil's Legal Amazon. The Legal Amazon is an administrative region of nine states, created in 1953 for development planning and now used for policy and statistical reporting. It covers about five million square kilometers, nearly sixty percent of Brazil's territory, and includes areas of savanna and other ecosystems as well as the rainforest. Brazilian deforestation statistics, including those of the PRODES system, refer to this administrative region, and comparing them with figures for the entire basin or for other countries requires attention to definitions.

Climate scientists emphasize that the forest is not only a carbon store but also a regulator of regional rainfall, and that Indigenous management has long shaped its composition, including the spread of useful species such as Brazil nut and açaí palms. The nine-country basin is home to tens of millions of people, according to regional estimates, including hundreds of Indigenous peoples and some groups living in voluntary isolation whose protection depends on keeping outsiders away. The forest's political importance rests on this combination of planetary function and local habitation, which is why conservation proposals that ignore either dimension tend to fail.

Amazonia is not an untouched wilderness. Chapter 23 described the evidence for large pre-conquest populations and ecological management, including the dark, fertile anthropogenic soils known as terra preta, and recent laser-scanning surveys have documented extensive settlement complexes, for example in the Llanos de Mojos of Bolivia and the Upano Valley of Ecuador. After the conquest, epidemics devastated Indigenous populations, as Chapter 29 explained, but peoples survived, and missionaries, traders, and colonial officials entered the region. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the rubber boom made Manaus and Iquitos wealthy and subjected Indigenous workers to brutal labor regimes, most notoriously in the Putumayo region.

The twentieth century brought state-led integration. Brazil's military government of the 1960s and 1970s built highways such as the Transamazônica and encouraged settlers to move into Rondônia, Pará, and Mato Grosso under programs that promised land, using the slogan of integration of the region into national life. Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, and Colombia pursued their own colonization and oil-development programs. These projects produced towns, ranches, and farms, and also conflicts over land, violence against rural activists such as the rubber tapper Chico Mendes, murdered in 1988, and the creation of extractive reserves in 1990, which gave forest-dependent communities legal rights to use forest resources sustainably.

Today Amazonia contains cities of millions, river communities (ribeirinhos), Afro-descendant quilombola communities, Indigenous peoples speaking hundreds of languages, small farmers, large ranchers, miners, loggers, and urban professionals. Its history, economy, and politics cannot be reduced to a contest between forest and development. The essential argument of this division is that Amazonia is a lived region with long histories of settlement and competition, and that conservation policy must engage the people who live there as well as the forest itself.

Section 114.10

Deforestation, Agriculture, Infrastructure, and Competing Development Models

Deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon has followed economic incentives. Cattle ranching has been the largest single use of cleared land, because pasture is cheap to establish, requires little labor, and secures claim to land. Soybean production expanded across the southern Amazon and the adjacent Cerrado savanna, driven by demand from China and Europe, and sometimes displaced ranchers who then cleared new forest. Logging, much of it illegal, opened roads and degraded forest. Speculators engaged in grilagem, the fraudulent seizure of public land, cleared forest to establish claims they hoped to legalize. Gold mining by informal miners (garimpeiros) polluted rivers with mercury and invaded Indigenous territories, producing the health emergency among the Yanomami that came to national attention in 2023.

Infrastructure shapes these pressures. Roads reduce transport costs and open land to markets, but they also bring settlers, speculators, and loggers. The paving of highways such as the BR-163, which links soybean areas in Mato Grosso to river ports in Pará, and debates over restoring the BR-319 between Manaus and Porto Velho, illustrate the tension between connectivity and conservation. Hydroelectric dams, among them Belo Monte on the Xingu River, which began operating in 2016, provided electricity and displaced river communities and Indigenous people. Mineral extraction and oil development created similar tradeoffs.

Brazil's history shows that policy affects outcomes. Deforestation in the Legal Amazon peaked at about 27,800 square kilometers in 2004 and fell to about 4,600 in 2012 after the government created an action plan with satellite monitoring, enforcement, expanded protected areas, a private-sector soy moratorium, and credit restrictions on illegal clearing. Rates rose again after 2013, and rose sharply in the late 2010s, reaching 13,038 square kilometers in the period ending July 2021, according to INPE's PRODES system, after enforcement agencies were weakened. Following the 2022 election, the government under President Lula da Silva restored enforcement, reactivated the Amazon Fund, which receives donations from Norway, Germany, and others, and saw clearing decline. These changes illustrate that deforestation responds to political choices as well as markets.

Other Amazonian countries followed different paths. In Peru, informal gold mining in Madre de Dios and agricultural expansion, including oil palm and cacao, drove clearing, and enforcement was limited by weak state presence in remote areas. In Ecuador, oil development has long shaped the northern Amazon, and in August 2023 voters approved by referendum halting oil drilling in Block 43 of Yasuní National Park, a decision whose implementation has been contested. In Bolivia, fires linked to land clearing for agriculture burned large areas in 2019 and again in 2024, and policies encouraging agricultural expansion sparked controversy. In Colombia, deforestation rose after the 2016 peace accord, when armed groups' retreat left land open to clearing and speculation, then declined later in the decade, a relationship connected to the armed-conflict history of Chapters 99 and 100.

Students should distinguish legal agricultural production from illegal clearing, and recognize that the two are connected in complex ways, since legal producers may buy from suppliers who clear illegally. Criminal networks that finance land grabbing, illegal mining, and logging are examined in Chapter 112. Governments often pursue conservation and extraction at once. In October 2025, shortly before COP30, Brazil's environmental regulator authorized the state oil company Petrobras to begin exploratory drilling in a block off the mouth of the Amazon River, even as the government promoted forest protection. Defenders argued that oil revenue could finance the transition, critics that exploration contradicted climate commitments. The episode captures the competing development models that run through the region.

International markets also matter. Buyers in Europe, China, and elsewhere increasingly face rules about the origin of beef, soy, and timber. The European Union adopted a deforestation regulation in 2023 whose application dates were later postponed, and major traders signed corporate commitments that have met with uneven compliance. Brazilian producers argued that such rules were disguised protectionism, while environmental groups argued that they were needed to change incentives. The dispute shows that deforestation is shaped by consumers and regulators far from the forest as well as by landholders within it.

Section 114.11

Indigenous Territories, Environmental Governance, and Conservation

Indigenous peoples and organizations are central to Amazonian governance. Legal recognition of collective territorial rights, which Chapter 96 traced as part of the Indigenous resurgence, expanded after the 1980s. Brazil's 1988 Constitution recognizes Indigenous peoples' original rights to the lands they traditionally occupy, and Indigenous lands, formally demarcated by the federal government, cover roughly an eighth of the country's territory. Colombia recognizes collective resguardos, Bolivia's 2009 Constitution recognizes Indigenous autonomy and territory, and Peru recognizes native communities. International Labour Organization Convention 169, adopted in 1989, requires states to consult Indigenous peoples about measures that affect them, and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ruled in the 2012 Sarayaku case that Ecuador had violated the rights of an Amazonian Kichwa community by permitting oil exploration without proper consultation.

Studies of satellite data in several countries have found that deforestation rates inside recognized Indigenous territories are generally lower than in comparable unprotected areas, though the effect depends on the strength of legal recognition, community organization, and state protection. Indigenous communities patrol borders, monitor illegal activity, and manage forest resources, and organizations such as the Coordinator of Indigenous Organizations of the Amazon Basin (COICA), founded in 1984, bring their voices into national and international forums. This activism extends the social movements discussed in Chapter 110. But Indigenous territories are not shielded from pressure: miners, loggers, and land-grabbers invade them, and Indigenous leaders and their allies face violence, as the 2022 murders of the journalist Dom Phillips and the Indigenous specialist Bruno Pereira in the Javari Valley showed.

Several models of conservation coexist. State-managed protected areas, such as national parks, restrict use and are enforced by government agencies; Indigenous territories rest on collective rights and community authority; extractive reserves allow sustainable use by traditional communities; private reserves and conservation concessions operate under landowners or companies; and international programs provide funding and standards. Each model has strengths and weaknesses. State parks may exclude local people who depend on the forest, creating resentment, whereas Indigenous territories depend on communities' capacity and external support. Private initiatives can be efficient but may be unstable if funding ends.

Tensions over livelihoods are real. Communities restricted by conservation rules imposed without consultation have contested them, arguing that fishing, hunting, small-scale farming, and gathering are part of their way of life. Some Indigenous groups also seek economic opportunities, including ecotourism, forest products, and in some cases, regulated mining or agriculture, and disagree among themselves about how far to engage markets. In Brazil, the so-called marco temporal dispute, over whether Indigenous peoples can claim only land they occupied on the date of the 1988 Constitution, was rejected by the Supreme Court in 2023 but then supported in legislation by Congress, leaving the issue in litigation and political conflict.

Women are prominent in these struggles. Indigenous women's organizations in Brazil, Ecuador, Peru, and Colombia have led marches, litigation, and local governance, and Brazil's creation in 2023 of a federal ministry for Indigenous peoples, headed by an Indigenous woman, marked an institutional change after decades in which Indigenous policy was handled by agencies with little autonomy. Women also bear much of the burden of water collection, farming, and food preparation when rivers are polluted by mining or forests are cleared, so environmental degradation falls unevenly.

The central question of this section is whether environmental protection can be durable without lasting institutions and meaningful participation by affected communities. Evidence from the region suggests that protection without participation is fragile, and that participation without resources and legal security is insufficient. The answer depends on political choices that change with governments, a lesson reinforced by the changing rates of deforestation described in the previous section and the global debates examined next.

Section 114.12

Amazonian Climate Politics, COP30, and Conservation through 2026

The Amazon entered global politics dramatically in August 2019, when fires in the Brazilian Amazon darkened São Paulo's sky, produced international outrage, and led Norway and Germany to freeze payments to the Amazon Fund. The government of President Jair Bolsonaro weakened environmental enforcement and rejected foreign criticism as interference. Drought and heat intensified the problem later: in 2023 the Rio Negro at Manaus reached its lowest recorded level in more than a century, and the 2024 fire season was among the most severe in years. Scientists warned that deforestation, degradation, and warming together might push parts of the forest toward a tipping point at which rainforest would convert to savanna, a risk that remains uncertain in timing and location.

Brazil hosted the 30th United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP30) in Belém, the Amazonian city at the mouth of the river, from 10 to 22 November 2025 (scheduled for 10–21 November, but closing negotiations continued into 22 November). Brazil framed it as a "forest COP" and promoted participation by Indigenous peoples, thousands of whom attended. The conference's outcome was mixed. The final decision called for efforts to at least triple adaptation finance by 2035, but contentious proposals for a roadmap away from fossil fuels did not appear in the final text, and many observers judged the results modest relative to expectations. Brazil also faced criticism for its own oil plans.

Brazil's signature initiative was the Tropical Forest Forever Facility (TFFF), launched on 6 November 2025 at the leaders' summit preceding the conference. The mechanism aims to raise sponsor capital from governments and invest it, with additional private capital, so that returns pay countries a fixed amount for each hectare of standing tropical forest, with a stated aim of directing at least twenty percent of payments to Indigenous peoples and local communities. The ambition was $25 billion in sponsor capital leveraged to a fund of $125 billion. Students must distinguish announcements from commitments and disbursements. At launch, reports counted about $5.5 billion in announced pledges: Norway announced about $3 billion over ten years, subject to conditions, Brazil and Indonesia $1 billion each, France indicated up to €500 million, and smaller sums came from Portugal and the Netherlands. Later reports put sponsor capital near $6.7 billion, well under a quarter of the initial target, and some analysts noted that only part of the announced sums had been formally committed. The facility's operation and the actual flow of money remained unresolved at the chapter's cutoff.

Brazil's forest-monitoring data in 2025–2026 showed declines, and their interpretation requires care. INPE operates two systems. DETER uses lower-resolution images to issue near-real-time alerts for enforcement, whereas PRODES uses higher-resolution Landsat imagery to produce the official annual rate for the period from 1 August to 31 July. DETER recorded 2,874 square kilometers of alerts between August 2025 and July 2026, the lowest total in that dataset, about 36% below the previous year. On 16 October 2026, Brazil's government reported a preliminary PRODES estimate of 3,335 square kilometers for the same period, 41.8% below the previous period's 5,731 square kilometers and the lowest in the series, which began in 1988. The figure is preliminary, based on a sample of satellite scenes, and a consolidated rate is typically calculated the following year. It also covers only deforestation in the Legal Amazon, not forest degradation by fire or selective logging, and not other biomes such as the Cerrado.

The decline does not settle the larger question. Observers attributed it to restored enforcement, the reactivated Amazon Fund, and stronger monitoring, but warned that it could reverse with changes in government, that fire and drought remain threats, and that conservation depends on whether standing forest can be made economically valuable. That is the central political question: whether payments such as the TFFF, carbon markets, and sustainable forest products can compete with the returns from cattle, soy, and gold. If they cannot, the pressure on forests will persist whatever the monitoring data show for any single year.

The Amazon Fund, managed by Brazil's national development bank BNDES, offers a point of comparison. Established in 2008 with donations from Norway and later Germany, it pays for enforcement, monitoring, and community projects on the basis of results, was suspended after 2019, and was reactivated in 2023. Its history shows that results-based finance can work when it supports existing state capacity, and that it can be withdrawn when political conditions change. The TFFF is a far larger and untested attempt to place a similar principle on a permanent financial footing.

Section 114.13

The Energy Transition and Latin America's Critical Minerals

Efforts to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions depend on materials. Lithium is a core ingredient of rechargeable batteries used in electric vehicles and in storage for solar and wind power; copper carries electricity through motors, wiring, and grids; nickel, cobalt, and graphite appear in battery cathodes and anodes; and rare earths are used in magnets. Governments describe such materials as critical minerals when their supply is considered economically or strategically vital and vulnerable to disruption. According to the USGS, batteries accounted for about 88% of lithium end use in 2025, a measure of how strongly lithium demand is tied to electrification.

Latin America holds a large share of several of these minerals, and its position is shaped by distinctions that students must keep clear. A mineral resource is a concentration of material in the earth's crust that may eventually be extracted. A reserve is the part of a resource that can be extracted economically with current technology and under current legal and market conditions. Production is what is actually mined in a given year. Refining converts raw ore or brine into chemicals or metals of battery or industrial grade, and manufacturing turns those materials into cells, batteries, and vehicles. A country can have huge resources and no production, large production and almost no refining, or both mining and refining without a battery industry. These distinctions explain why geological endowment does not equal economic power.

Brazil's position deserves separate mention. The country produces most of the world's niobium, used to strengthen steel, holds large reserves of graphite and nickel, and has been identified as having substantial rare-earth deposits, though development has been limited. Cuba's nickel and cobalt deposits at Moa are processed with foreign partners, and Colombia and Guatemala have nickel projects. Critical-mineral geography therefore extends beyond the Andes, and each mineral has its own market, owners, and politics.

Chile is the world's largest copper producer and Peru among the largest, and both also support significant lithium or copper expansion. Argentina and Chile mine lithium from salt-flat brines; Bolivia holds very large lithium resources with little commercial production. Brazil produces most of the world's niobium and holds substantial graphite, nickel, and rare-earth deposits, and Cuba and Colombia have nickel. The region's mineral wealth is not new, as Chapter 42 on colonial silver and Chapter 74 on oil nationalization showed, but the energy transition has given it new strategic value and attracted attention from China, the United States, Europe, Japan, and South Korea.

The International Energy Agency's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026, released on 16 July 2026, provides a comparative foundation. It reported that critical-mineral prices rebounded in 2025 and early 2026 after years of decline, that lithium prices more than doubled between January 2025 and April 2026, that supply concentration in refining increased further, with China, and for nickel Indonesia, accounting for more than three-quarters of growth in refined supply over two years, and that projected supply gaps for copper and lithium persist through 2035. The IEA also warned that export restrictions by major producers could disrupt downstream industry, and that investment in some minerals had fallen. Latin America's role in this picture is as a major source of raw material, with opportunities and constraints in infrastructure, financing, environmental governance, and industrial capacity.

Demand comes from several markets. China is the dominant refiner and battery manufacturer, and its firms hold stakes in Latin American lithium and copper projects. The European Union and the United States have adopted policies to diversify supply and reduce dependence on China, increasing interest in Latin American producers, and the USGS reported that Argentina and Chile supplied almost all U.S. lithium imports. Latin American governments therefore face buyers with competing interests. The question that follows is whether the region will capture a larger share of the value chain or remain primarily a source of raw materials.

Historical Vocabulary
Resources versus reserves

A resource is an identified concentration of a mineral that may become extractable; a reserve is the portion that is economically and legally recoverable under current conditions. Annual production is what is mined in a year, and it can be far smaller than either.

These terms matter because newspapers often call a country "lithium rich" on the basis of resources. Bolivia's very large resources, for example, coexist with no reserves reported by the USGS and very little commercial production. Students should always ask which measure is being used.

Section 114.14

Chile's National Lithium Strategy and the Politics of State Participation

Chile's lithium sits mainly in the Salar de Atacama, a vast salt flat in the Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on earth. Brine is pumped from beneath the salt crust into evaporation ponds, where sun and wind concentrate it over many months before it is processed into lithium carbonate or hydroxide. Two companies operate there: SQM, a Chilean firm, and Albemarle, a U.S. firm. Their rights derive from contracts with the state development agency, Corfo, because lithium was declared of strategic interest under the military government in the late 1970s and early 1980s and was excluded from ordinary mining concessions, even though earlier concessions were allowed to continue. Chile therefore combined state ownership in principle with private operation in practice.

That arrangement reflects a longer history. Chapter 85 described how Salvador Allende nationalized copper in 1971 and how Codelco, the state copper company, remained in public hands under the dictatorship while private investment later expanded. Chile's political left has long argued that strategic resources should benefit the nation, whereas the right and business groups emphasize private investment and technical expertise. The lithium debate renewed this argument. SQM's contract was due to expire in 2030, raising the question of what would follow, and some in Chile's Congress wanted lithium revenue to fund public needs.

On 20 April 2023, President Gabriel Boric announced a National Lithium Strategy. It called for a state role in the sector through Codelco and a new national lithium company, public–private partnerships, protection of salt-flat ecosystems, and consultation with Indigenous communities. Rather than expropriating existing contracts, the strategy proposed partnerships. Its design reflected a view that Chile needed both state participation and the capital, technology, and market access of private firms.

The principal outcome concerned the Salar de Atacama. Codelco and SQM signed a partnership agreement on 31 May 2024, under which Codelco would hold a majority share, fifty percent plus one, of a joint venture, with SQM holding the rest and retaining operational control until 2031. The venture, named NovaAndino Litio, would operate in the Salar de Atacama through 2060. Students should distinguish stages. The agreement was signed in May 2024; Chilean regulators approved the deal and the legal merger of Codelco's subsidiary into SQM's lithium subsidiary was completed in late December 2025; the rival Chinese lithium producer Tianqi, a major SQM shareholder, challenged the arrangement in court, and Chile's Supreme Court rejected its appeal on 26 January 2026, allowing the merger to take full effect. The partnership's economic rights were set to apply from 1 January 2025. The venture's environmental and production plans, including a reported multibillion-dollar expansion filed for environmental review in mid-2026, were still going through approval at the chapter's cutoff, and production results under the new structure were not yet established.

The debate over the arrangement is substantive. Supporters argue that it secures public revenue and long-term state control while retaining SQM's experience and avoiding years of litigation. Critics argue that the terms leave the private partner with operational control for years, that the state's majority stake is financial more than managerial, that the process lacked transparency, and that Indigenous communities were consulted inadequately. Chilean regulators also opened an audit of the deal. The case shows that twenty-first-century resource nationalism does not necessarily require the exclusion of private or foreign firms. It can mean negotiating the terms of their participation, and whether those terms serve the public, communities, and the environment is contested.

Albemarle, a U.S. company, operates in the Salar de Atacama under its own contract with Corfo, which runs to 2043, and Chile pursued separate arrangements for other salt flats, including a partnership between Codelco and Rio Tinto at Maricunga and a program by the state mining company ENAMI for smaller high-Andean salt flats. These parallel arrangements show the strategy's flexibility and its complexity: different salt flats have different owners, contract terms, ecological sensitivities, and community relationships.

Section 114.15

Argentina and Bolivia: Contrasting Paths to Lithium Development

Argentina and Bolivia hold very large lithium resources, and the USGS reported in 2026 that, together with Chile, the three countries hold roughly forty-three percent of the world's identified resources. Yet the three have taken very different routes. Argentina's constitution, as amended in 1994, gives provinces ownership of the natural resources within their territories, so lithium policy is made province by province under national mining laws that emphasize investor security. The salt flats of Jujuy, Salta, and Catamarca in the northwest host producing and developing projects, operated by firms from China, the United States, Australia, Japan, South Korea, and elsewhere, including the Olaroz and Cauchari-Olaroz operations in Jujuy and Fénix in Catamarca. Argentine lithium output expanded quickly in the mid-2020s as new capacity came online.

National policy under President Javier Milei, examined in Chapter 113, encouraged large foreign investment through an incentive regime for large projects created in 2024 that offered long-term tax and regulatory stability. Provincial politics complicates the picture. In 2023, protests in Jujuy over a constitutional reform, in which Indigenous communities of the Salinas Grandes region demanded consultation about lithium and water, drew national attention. The Argentine model therefore relies on private and foreign investment, with revenue shared among companies, provinces, and the national government, and with Indigenous consultation contested.

Bolivia chose state leadership. The 2009 Constitution reserves the exploitation of strategic resources, including lithium, to the state, and the government created a state company, Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos (YLB), to develop the Salar de Uyuni, the world's largest salt flat, which holds about 21 to 23 million tonnes of estimated lithium resources according to various sources. The effort proved technically difficult. The Uyuni brine has high levels of magnesium, which complicates processing, and the climate limits evaporation. A pilot plant at Llipi produced small quantities, and a German partnership collapsed in 2019 amid protests by regional groups who demanded a larger share of revenue. Bolivia's commercial production remained negligible, and the USGS lists no Bolivian mine production or reserves.

In 2024, under President Luis Arce, YLB signed contracts with a consortium led by the Chinese battery firm CATL's affiliates, known as Hong Kong CBC, and with the Russian state-linked Uranium One Group, to build direct-lithium-extraction plants at Uyuni, with the state holding a majority stake and the partners financing construction. The contracts were controversial: critics cited opacity, the lack of public disclosure, legislative disputes, and objections from Indigenous communities in Nor Lípez who feared effects on scarce water, and litigation began. Rodrigo Paz, who took office on 8 November 2025, had pledged during his campaign to reject the contracts while insisting that Bolivia should exploit its lithium, and his government reportedly reviewed them, announced a new lithium law, and negotiated with additional firms. The legal reservation of lithium to the state could be changed only by constitutional reform or referendum. The outcome remained unresolved at the chapter's cutoff.

Students should compare actual production, not the "Lithium Triangle" label. The USGS estimated world mine production at about 290,000 tonnes in 2025, with Chile near 56,000 tonnes and Argentina producing a smaller amount, together about a quarter of the total, while Bolivia was not reported separately. Chile holds the largest reserves by USGS accounting; Argentina's and Bolivia's reserves are smaller or unreported though their resources are very large. Regulatory structure largely explains the gap: Chile combines state ownership with long-established private operators; Argentina offers provincial ownership and incentives to private investors; Bolivia's state-led approach awaited technology and capital it could not secure quickly. None of the three has built large-scale lithium battery manufacturing, and exports remain mostly carbonate and other intermediate products.

Section 114.16

Water, Indigenous Communities, Industrial Development, and the Limits of the Green Transition

Lithium extraction affects water. In conventional brine evaporation, no freshwater is used directly to pump brine, but extraction removes large volumes of salty groundwater that may be hydrologically connected to freshwater aquifers, wetlands, and lagoons, and scientists and communities disagree about the effect on ecosystems. Processing plants use fresh water, and the Atacama's farmers, herders, and tourism operators compete for it. Roads, power lines, pipelines, and camps alter fragile landscapes, and flamingo populations and other species depend on salt-flat lagoons. In Chile, the partnership plan included a proposal to reduce brine extraction in the Salar de Atacama, and SQM obtained a high score under an independent mining-assurance standard, though these claims are contested by critics.

Direct lithium extraction (DLE) refers to a family of technologies that use adsorption, ion exchange, or solvents to pull lithium from brine more quickly than evaporation ponds, often returning treated brine to the ground. Proponents argue that DLE can recover a larger share of the lithium, use less land, and reduce evaporation losses. But DLE can require fresh water and energy, produces waste streams, and has been proven at commercial scale only in limited cases. Students should not assume that a newer technology automatically eliminates environmental impacts, only that it changes them, and the evidence is still developing. Bolivia's 2024 contracts, Argentina's newer projects, and Chile's future plans all rely to some extent on DLE.

Indigenous communities occupy the territories where brines lie. The Lickanantay (Atacameño) of northern Chile, the Kolla and Atacama communities of Argentina's Puna, and the Indigenous peoples of Bolivia's Nor Lípez and Uyuni region have long managed scarce water, herded llamas, farmed quinoa, and hold sacred sites in these landscapes. ILO Convention 169 requires consultation, and Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia have ratified or incorporated it in different ways. Communities disagree among themselves: some seek jobs, royalties, and revenue-sharing, others oppose extraction altogether, and many fear that consultation is procedural rather than binding. Local employment from lithium is limited because operations are capital-intensive, and public revenue depends on royalty and tax design that differs by country.

Copper illustrates related issues. Chile and Peru are among the largest producers, and expanding demand raises both revenue and conflict. In southern Peru, community opposition and road blockades have disrupted operations at mines such as Las Bambas, and the Tía María project in Arequipa has faced protests for years, reflecting disputes over water, land, and the distribution of benefits. Electricity generation, too, matters: Brazil, Uruguay, and Chile have expanded wind and solar power, and Chile's northern solar plants have at times produced more than the grid could carry, showing the need for transmission and storage. The region's energy transition is therefore both a source of minerals for others and a transformation of its own power systems.

Building industries beyond raw material is difficult. Latin American countries have little capacity to manufacture battery cells, and the IEA found that refining is highly concentrated elsewhere. Some governments have pursued industrial strategies, including Chile's attempts to attract cathode and lithium-chemical investment and Brazil's efforts to attract electric-vehicle assembly, including a Chinese automaker's plant in Bahia that began operations in 2025. Success will depend on energy prices, skills, infrastructure, markets, and stable policy. The governing question is whether Latin American countries can use the energy transition to move beyond exporting raw materials or whether the mineral economy will reproduce patterns of dependency traced since Chapter 42, with new owners and technologies but similar structures.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Does the Energy Transition Offer Economic Independence or Reproduce Resource Dependency?

The question: Will demand for critical minerals allow Latin American countries to diversify, build industry, and strengthen their bargaining position, or lock them into exporting raw materials whose prices and processing are controlled elsewhere?

Interpretation A — Opportunity: Rising demand, public–private partnerships, and new technologies create openings for revenue, technology transfer, and industrial development. Chile's state participation, Argentina's investment, and Brazil's manufacturing efforts show that countries can bargain for better terms, and the IEA's evidence of supply gaps strengthens producers' leverage.
Interpretation B — Dependency: Foreign ownership, volatile prices, concentrated refining in China and elsewhere, and limited local manufacturing keep value abroad, while communities bear water and land costs. Price volatility, including a rebound after years of decline, shows the exposure.
Interpretation C — Territorial and environmental rights: Indigenous and local communities argue that the debate between public and private ownership overlooks who holds rights to land and water. State ownership does not guarantee consent or benefit, and a state-controlled project can harm a community as much as a private one.

Evidence: Production and revenue data from Chile and Argentina, the stalled Bolivian projects, IEA analysis of refining, and community testimony and litigation. National cases differ, and the evidence supports different conclusions for Chile, Argentina, Bolivia, Peru, and Brazil.

Why it matters: The interpretations imply different policies: bargaining for better terms, building processing industries, or giving communities stronger veto and benefit-sharing rights.

Student task: Choose one country and evaluate which interpretation best fits the evidence, and what indicator in 2030 would change your view.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life around the Salt Flat

In oases along the edge of the Salar de Atacama, Lickanantay families have irrigated small plots of maize, alfalfa, and fruit trees for generations, using canals managed by community water committees, and they celebrate festivals tied to water and the agricultural calendar. Tourism brings visitors to lagoons and geysers, and mining companies employ some local residents as drivers, technicians, and guards, while others commute to distant copper mines.

Women hold key roles in farming, crafts, and tourism, and in community organizations, though they are less visible in negotiations with companies. Residents' views of lithium differ within the same family, balancing wages and community funds against anxiety about water. Official records capture contracts and revenues, while daily negotiations over water, work, and tradition appear mainly in community testimony and ethnographic research.

Section 114.17

China's Economic Expansion in Latin America

China's commercial presence in Latin America grew from a minor one in 2000 to a major one by the 2020s. Chinese demand for soybeans, iron ore, copper, oil, and other commodities rose with its industrial growth, and the commodity boom described in Chapter 108 was in large part a Chinese boom. The OECD's Latin American Economic Outlook 2026 reports that China accounted for approximately 13% of Latin America's exports in 2024, far above its share at the start of the century, while North America remained the largest destination, at about 47%, down from 61% in 2000. China became the region's second-largest trading partner overall and the largest partner of several South American economies, including Brazil, Chile, and Peru. In the first quarter of 2026, the IDB reported that Latin American exports to China rose about 25% over the same period of 2025, faster than exports to any other major market, though the United States remained the largest buyer.

The composition of trade matters. Latin America exports to China mainly primary products: soybeans and beef from Brazil and Argentina, copper from Chile and Peru, iron ore from Brazil, petroleum from several producers, and increasingly lithium-related materials. The OECD found that commodity-based products made up about 62% of the region's goods exports between 2022 and 2024, reflecting persistent difficulty in industrial upgrading. In the other direction, the region imports Chinese machinery, electronics, vehicles, solar panels, and consumer goods. Analyses of customs data suggest Chinese exports to Latin America nearly doubled between 2014 and 2024, with Mexico and Brazil together accounting for a large share. Chinese electric vehicles became prominent in several markets, and steel industry associations complained of competition from Chinese steel, arguments by an interested industry that should be read as such.

Students should distinguish categories of economic relations. Bilateral trade refers to goods bought and sold. Foreign direct investment (FDI) means ownership stakes in companies, mines, power plants, or ports. Government lending, mostly through China's policy banks, financed infrastructure and energy projects, including oil-backed loans to Venezuela and Ecuador examined in Chapters 107 and 109, and lending fell sharply after the late 2010s as Beijing grew cautious. Construction contracts are paid projects built by Chinese firms and need not involve Chinese ownership. Development finance covers a mix of credit and grants. Estimates of each differ among data sources, which use different definitions: one Chinese government figure put Chinese direct investment in Latin America at about $14.7 billion in 2024, but analysts caution that official statistics and independent trackers do not match, and that investment routes through third countries.

China pursued relationships through free-trade agreements with Chile, Peru, Costa Rica, Ecuador, and Nicaragua, through the Belt and Road Initiative, which more than twenty regional governments endorsed, and through political and cultural diplomacy. Not all moves were one-way. Panama announced in early 2025 that it would leave the Belt and Road initiative, under U.S. pressure, and several governments tightened scrutiny of Chinese investment in strategic sectors. Uruguay and Argentina debated trade arrangements with Beijing, and Brazil used its BRICS membership to build ties with China and other emerging economies while maintaining relations with Washington and Europe.

Chinese demand supported export growth, government revenue, and employment, and it offered an alternative market when the United States or Europe imposed barriers. It also raised concerns. Commodity concentration leaves economies exposed to Chinese demand cycles, trade is asymmetric, since China sells manufactured goods and buys raw materials, and imports can compete with local industry. Such relationships are not unique to China; they resemble earlier patterns with Britain and the United States, examined in Chapter 68. Whether China's presence reinforces or loosens dependency depends on what Latin American governments negotiate: local-content rules, technology transfer, environmental standards, and diversification of exports.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: China's Rise as a Latin American Trading Partner

Long-term causes

China's industrialization and urbanization after the 1980s, Latin America's established role as a commodity exporter, and the end of earlier U.S.-centered trade dominance.

Short-term causes

The commodity boom of the 2000s, Chinese state financing, free-trade agreements, and Latin American governments' desire for new markets.

Event or transformation

China's share of regional exports rises to about 13% by 2024, with China the leading partner for several economies.

Immediate effects

Export growth, higher fiscal revenue during booms, rising imports of Chinese manufactures, and new infrastructure investment.

Long-term consequences

Commodity concentration, exposure to Chinese demand, disputes over industrial competition, and U.S. concern about strategic influence.

Effects differ by country: Mexico's trade is dominated by North America, Chile and Peru are heavily tied to China, and Central America is more tied to the United States.
Section 114.18

Infrastructure, the Chancay Port, and Chinese Investment

Chinese firms participate across Latin American infrastructure: ports, railways, power plants and transmission lines, telecommunications, and industrial parks. Their involvement takes different legal and financial forms. Some projects are built by Chinese contractors for governments that own them, such as certain hydroelectric dams in Argentina and Ecuador; some are owned by Chinese state-owned or private companies, such as the acquisition of electricity-distribution and transmission assets in Chile and Brazil; some are joint ventures with local firms. Treating every Chinese-financed project as identical in ownership, contractual obligations, or geopolitical purpose would be misleading, and several projects have encountered technical and financial problems, including the Coca Codo Sinclair dam in Ecuador, where defects and debt became political issues.

The principal case study is the deepwater port of Chancay, about eighty kilometers north of Lima on the Peruvian coast. It was developed by Cosco Shipping Ports Chancay Perú, a company controlled sixty percent by China's state-owned COSCO Shipping Ports, with the remainder held by the Peruvian mining firm Volcan, in an investment reported at roughly $1.3 billion for the first phase. The terminal was inaugurated on 14 November 2024, during the APEC summit hosted by Peru, in the presence of China's president, Xi Jinping, and Peru's then-president, Dina Boluarte. Chancay is a deepwater port able to receive large container ships, and shipping services reduced the sailing time between Chancay and Shanghai to roughly 23 days, a saving of about ten days compared with routes through older ports, according to the operator.

Its early performance was strong, though sources report volumes differently. COSCO reported that the port handled more than 500,000 TEU (twenty-foot equivalent container units) and 2.4 million tonnes of cargo from the start of operations through May 2026, and 200,000 TEU in the first half of 2026, up about 71% on the same months of 2025. Other reports gave smaller first-year figures, and the differences reflect varying definitions of the first year and sources, a reminder to cite the source and period of any figure. Chinese goods accounted for a majority of imports through the port, and a substantial share of Peru's agricultural exports to China, such as fruit, moved through it. Analysts noted that a large share of traffic was transshipment, supporting Peru's ambition to become a Pacific logistics hub, and discussions in 2025 about a rail link across South America to Brazil's Atlantic coast showed the project's wider ambitions, though that idea remained at an exploratory stage.

Anticipated benefits included lower logistics costs for exporters of agricultural products and minerals, new jobs and investment, and competition for the older port of Callao. Questions concerned regulation, environment, and who benefits. Peruvian courts addressed whether the state transport regulator, Ositrán, could oversee the port, since COSCO argued that the port was privately financed and not operating under a public concession. A lower court sided with the company in January 2026, the U.S. State Department then warned that Chinese control threatened Peruvian sovereignty, and in July 2026 a higher court restored the regulator's powers. COSCO appealed to Peru's Constitutional Court, which admitted the case on 24 July 2026 and had not ruled by the chapter's cutoff. Local residents and fishing communities had also complained that they had not shared fully in the benefits.

Chancay shows the complexity of infrastructure sovereignty. Peru retained legal authority over its territory and regulators, and its courts, rather than any foreign government, decided the oversight question, but the dispute also took place under the shadow of U.S.–China rivalry. Peruvian officials stressed that the port served commercial interests and that Peru traded with both powers. The port's future will depend on cargo volumes, regulation, environmental permitting, and the terms of its contracts, none of which can be inferred from geopolitical labels alone.

Section 114.19

The United States, Nearshoring, Trade Agreements, and Strategic Competition

The United States remained central to Latin American economies. According to the OECD, North America took about 47% of the region's exports in 2024, and the share is far higher for Mexico and Central America, whose economies are integrated into North American production networks. Mexico became the largest source of U.S. imports by value in the 2020s, overtaking China. Its manufacturing sector, concentrated in automobiles, electronics, appliances, and medical devices, depends on cross-border supply chains governed since 2020 by the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), which replaced NAFTA, examined in Chapter 95. Central American economies, linked by the CAFTA-DR agreement, export apparel, agricultural products, and, in Costa Rica, medical devices and microelectronics. Chile, Peru, and Colombia have free-trade agreements with the United States, while Brazil does not.

Several concepts help explain strategies after 2020. Nearshoring means relocating production closer to the consumer market, here moving factories from Asia to Mexico or Central America to reduce transport times and political risk. Friend-shoring means shifting supply chains toward politically aligned countries. Trade diversification means seeking multiple markets to reduce exposure. Supply-chain resilience became a policy goal after pandemic disruptions. Mexico benefited from investment announcements, but its capacity to capture nearshoring was limited by energy supply, security, infrastructure, regulatory uncertainty, and the rules of origin that determine whether a product qualifies for USMCA preferences.

Policy under the second Trump administration after January 2025 unsettled this picture. The United States imposed tariffs on many trading partners, with exemptions for goods qualifying under USMCA, raised duties on steel, aluminum, and autos, and used trade measures to press for action on migration and drug trafficking. Brazil faced steep additional U.S. tariffs in 2025, and in July 2026 Brazil requested consultations at the World Trade Organization, according to trade reports. Legal challenges and changes in the statutory basis for tariffs meant that the rules shifted several times. On 1 July 2026, at the first scheduled joint review of the USMCA, the United States declined to confirm a sixteen-year extension, saying it did not agree to renew the agreement in its current form, while Mexico and Canada supported extension. The agreement remained in force, with preferences unchanged, but moved to annual reviews, and negotiations between Washington and Mexico City continued on automotive content, steel, and other issues. Whether the USMCA would be extended, revised, or allowed to lapse in 2036 was unresolved at the chapter's cutoff.

Strategic competition extended to minerals and infrastructure. The United States sought partnerships with Latin American producers of copper and lithium, and U.S. officials warned about Chinese ownership of ports and mines, as in the Chancay case. Mexico responded to pressure to limit Chinese content in its supply chains, and in late 2025 approved higher tariffs on imports from countries without trade agreements, a measure widely read as directed at China, though Mexico presented it as protection for domestic industry. Bolivia's new government reportedly sought to balance its Chinese and Russian lithium contracts with Western financing. Across the region, governments tried to extract benefits from both powers.

The economies of the region occupy very different positions. Mexico is deeply integrated with North America but also heavily dependent on Chinese inputs. Brazil trades heavily with China, exports to the United States, and diversifies through Mercosur and agreements elsewhere, such as the long-negotiated Mercosur–European Union accord, whose ratification was still being worked out. Chile and Peru depend on Chinese demand for copper but maintain agreements with the United States. Colombia retains close U.S. ties while expanding Asian trade, and Central American economies remain tied to U.S. markets and remittances. The central point is that Latin American countries are not forced to choose exclusively between China and the United States. Their strategies, a mixture of hedging, bargaining, and diversification, reflect domestic interests as much as great-power pressure.

Section 114.20

Historical Synthesis: Development, Dependency, and Sovereignty through 2026

The five divisions of this chapter describe developments that look separate but share a logic. The pandemic exposed how fragile the region's social foundations were, with informal work, uneven health systems, and unequal schooling. Migration and remittances showed that millions of households had built economic strategies that crossed borders, tying national economies to labor markets and immigration policies elsewhere. Amazonia revealed that a region of global climate importance depended on domestic enforcement, local governance, and unreliable international finance. Lithium, copper, and other minerals offered new opportunities and familiar risks. Trade and infrastructure relations with China and the United States gave governments leverage and exposed them to pressure. In each case, increased importance did not automatically bring inclusive development.

Different national strategies produced different results, and no single model succeeded everywhere. Public investment and social protection reduced poverty after the pandemic where states had fiscal capacity, but did not remove inequality. Migration management ranged from Colombia's regularization to restrictive enforcement elsewhere. Mineral nationalization in Bolivia stalled for lack of technology and capital, while Chile's public–private partnership secured state participation at the cost of accepting private operation, and Argentina's investor-friendly provincial model expanded output with contested social effects. Environmental regulation in Brazil reduced deforestation when it was enforced, and weakened when enforcement was cut. Export diversification advanced slowly, and industrial policy faced obstacles of scale, energy, and skills. Region-wide generalizations should therefore be treated with caution.

Three questions organize the evidence. First, who controls the region's resources and infrastructure? The answer ranges from state companies such as Codelco and YLB, to private firms, to Chinese and other foreign state-owned enterprises, to Indigenous communities holding collective title, and often to combinations of these. Second, who receives the economic benefits? Revenue may go to national treasuries, provincial governments, shareholders, workers, migrants' families, or communities, and the distribution is often disputed. Third, which institutions protect communities and manage long-term risk? Courts such as Peru's and Chile's, regulators such as Ositrán, monitoring agencies such as INPE, consultation mechanisms, and international bodies all play roles, and their strength varies with political support.

The chapter also supports a caution about sovereignty. Latin American governments exercised real agency: they negotiated lithium contracts, chose whether to join Chinese initiatives, set deforestation policy, and designed social protection. But their choices were constrained by commodity prices, debt, the migration and trade policies of the United States, and the pace of technological change elsewhere. Sovereignty in this period meant the capacity to bargain and to build institutions, not simply the legal possession of territory.

The smaller economies of Central America and the Caribbean illustrate how narrow bargaining capacity can be. Their exports, tourism, and remittances depend on a single large partner, their exposure to storms and drought is high, their fiscal space is limited, and their leverage with investors or lenders is weaker than that of Brazil or Mexico. For them, regional institutions, multilateral lenders, and climate-finance mechanisms matter more, which is why debates about loss-and-damage funds, remittance costs, and trade preferences carry particular weight outside the largest economies.

Chapter 115 turns to Venezuela, the country that holds some of the world's largest oil reserves, that produced one of the largest displacements in the region's history, and whose political and international crisis in 2026 brought together many themes of this chapter: resource dependence, migration, and great-power rivalry. The unresolved questions examined here, about ownership, benefit, and institutions, will frame the way students interpret that case.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the pandemic, migration, climate pressures, the energy transition, and shifting trade relations transformed Latin America's development prospects, creating new opportunities and new forms of dependency, and that economic growth, resource wealth, and geopolitical importance do not by themselves produce inclusive development.

COVID-19 struck a region whose health systems were fragmented, whose economies depended on informal work, and whose housing and schooling were unequal. Brazil's universal system, Mexico's divided institutions, and the weaker systems of Central America and Haiti met the same virus with different capacity, but the pandemic's effects followed the lines of existing inequality.

Official counts understated the mortality of 2020–2021. The OECD and World Bank estimated about 2.3 million excess deaths, and national experiences in Peru, Ecuador, Brazil, and Mexico showed how oxygen shortages, crowded work, and weak registries shaped outcomes. Excess mortality is a modeled estimate, and comparisons require population adjustments and attention to measurement.

Vaccines arrived through COVAX, bilateral contracts, and domestic production, and by 2022 coverage was among the world's highest, though access and uptake varied. Vaccination did not repair the losses in schooling, jobs, and public finances. ECLAC measured poverty at 32.8% in 2020 and 25.5% in 2024, the lowest in comparable data, while income inequality remained extreme.

Migration diversified beyond the northbound model. Venezuelans moved within South America, Central Americans moved through Mexico, Cubans, Nicaraguans, and Haitians left in large numbers, and legal categories of refugee, migrant worker, internally displaced person, and deportee carried different rights. The Darién Gap saw 520,085 crossings in 2023 and 302,203 in 2024, then collapsed to about 3,100 in 2025 under enforcement and policy change, with a reverse flow southward. Crossing counts are events, not unique persons.

Remittances reached a record $173.7 billion in 2025, led by Central America, with Mexico the exception. They supported households and national balances, but dependence differed greatly: a modest share of GDP in Colombia and Ecuador, a quarter or more in some Central American economies. Remittances respond to the policies and labor markets of host countries as well as to household need.

Amazonia is a lived region of Indigenous peoples, river communities, cities, ranches, mines, and roads. Deforestation in Brazil's Legal Amazon fell from peaks in 2004 and 2021 after enforcement was strengthened, reaching a preliminary 3,335 square kilometers for August 2025 to July 2026, the lowest in the official series. DETER alerts and PRODES estimates measure different things, and the 2026 figure is preliminary and excludes degradation.

Indigenous territories, legal recognition, and prior-consultation rights are central to forest governance, though they depend on state protection and community organization. COP30 in Belém made the forest a subject of global diplomacy, and the Tropical Forests Forever Facility attracted announced pledges of about $5.5 billion against a goal of $25 billion in sponsor capital, with announcements, conditional commitments, and disbursements distinct.

The energy transition raised the strategic value of lithium and copper. The IEA's 2026 outlook stressed concentration in refining and persistent supply gaps. Resources, reserves, production, refining, and manufacturing are different measures. Chile pursued a public–private partnership through NovaAndino Litio, Argentina relied on provincial ownership and private investment, and Bolivia's state-led strategy produced little output and controversial contracts.

Lithium extraction raises questions of water, Indigenous consent, and revenue sharing, and direct lithium extraction does not automatically remove environmental costs. The debate between opportunity and dependency applies differently to Chile, Argentina, Bolivia, Peru, and Brazil, and communities argue that ownership debates overlook territorial and environmental rights.

China became the region's second-largest trading partner, with about 13% of exports in 2024, while North America took about 47%. Chancay, inaugurated in 2024, showed both the commercial promise of Pacific links and disputes over regulation and sovereignty. The United States remained crucial through trade, remittances, and security, but its tariffs and the 2026 USMCA review created uncertainty. Latin American governments pursued mixed strategies and were not compelled to choose between powers.

The chapter's closing questions are who controls resources and infrastructure, who receives the benefits, and which institutions protect communities and manage long-term risk. Several developments remained unresolved at the 16 November 2026 cutoff, and Chapter 115 turns to Venezuela, where resource dependence, migration, and great-power rivalry converged.

Key Terms

Excess mortality
Deaths above the number expected from earlier patterns, estimated by models; it captures untested and indirect deaths.
Informal employment
Work without formal contracts, social insurance, or legal protections, held by about half of regional workers before 2020.
SUS
Brazil's Unified Health System, created by the 1988 Constitution to guarantee universal care.
COVAX
A WHO-, Gavi-, and CEPI-coordinated mechanism to pool vaccine purchases for lower-income countries.
Emergency cash transfer
Temporary payments to households, such as Brazil's 2020 emergency aid, to offset pandemic income loss.
ECLAC (CEPAL)
The UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean, publisher of the Social Panorama.
Second lost decade
ECLAC's description of weak growth and stalled poverty reduction after 2014, echoing the 1980s.
Refugee
A person outside their country who meets the 1951 Convention definition or the broader Cartagena Declaration of 1984.
Asylum seeker
A person who has applied for international protection and awaits a decision.
Internally displaced person
A person forced from home who remains inside their own country.
Deportee
A person removed from a country by its authorities; also a returnee in the origin country.
Migrant stock
The number of migrants living abroad at a given time, as opposed to annual flows.
Crossing event
A registered border passage; a count of events is not a count of unique individuals.
Darién Gap
The roadless forest on the Colombia–Panama border that became a major migration corridor from 2021 to 2024.
R4V
The UN-led regional platform that estimates Venezuelan refugees and migrants and coordinates the response.
Remittance
Money sent by migrants to family or community at home; private earnings, not aid.
Dry Corridor
A seasonally arid belt of Central America where drought and erratic rain strain smallholder agriculture.
Legal Amazon
Brazil's nine-state administrative region used for policy and statistics, larger than the rainforest biome.
PRODES
INPE's annual high-resolution system producing Brazil's official clear-cut deforestation rate, August to July.
DETER
INPE's near-real-time alert system used for enforcement; alerts are not the official annual rate.
Grilagem
Fraudulent seizure of public land, often by clearing forest to establish a claim.
Terra preta
Dark, fertile anthropogenic soils in Amazonia, evidence of pre-conquest land management.
Indigenous territory
Land held under collective legal title by Indigenous peoples, such as Brazil's demarcated Indigenous lands or Colombia's resguardos.
Prior consultation
The duty under ILO Convention 169 to consult Indigenous peoples before measures affecting them.
Marco temporal
The Brazilian dispute over whether Indigenous land claims are limited to lands occupied in 1988.
Extractive reserve
A protected area allowing sustainable use of forest resources by traditional communities.
COP30
The 30th UN climate conference, held in Belém, Brazil; meetings began 10 November and the conference concluded on 22 November 2025, one day after its scheduled close.
Tropical Forests Forever Facility (TFFF)
A Brazilian-led mechanism proposed to pay countries per hectare of standing tropical forest from investment returns.
Critical minerals
Materials judged vital and vulnerable to supply disruption, such as lithium, copper, nickel, cobalt, graphite, and rare earths.
Resource (mineral)
An identified concentration of material that may become extractable.
Reserve (mineral)
The part of a resource recoverable economically under current conditions.
Brine
Salty groundwater beneath salt flats, from which lithium is recovered.
Direct lithium extraction (DLE)
Technologies that pull lithium from brine by adsorption, ion exchange, or solvents, instead of evaporation ponds.
Resource nationalism
Policies asserting state control over natural resources; it can include partnerships with private firms.
NovaAndino Litio
The Codelco–SQM joint venture for the Salar de Atacama, with Codelco holding 50% plus one share.
YLB
Yacimientos de Litio Bolivianos, Bolivia's state lithium company.
Nearshoring
Relocating production closer to the consumer market, as from Asia to Mexico.
USMCA
The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement in force since 2020; under annual review since 1 July 2026.
Belt and Road Initiative
China's infrastructure and investment program, endorsed by more than twenty Latin American governments.
Dependency
A condition in which an economy relies on exporting raw materials and importing manufactures, leaving it exposed to external prices and decisions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Latin America's importance to global health, migration, climate, mineral supply, and trade grew in the early twenty-first century without settling the older questions of inequality, institutional capacity, sovereignty, and dependency. The pandemic revealed how unevenly protection was distributed. Migration and remittances showed how households built economic strategies across borders. Forests and minerals made the region strategically valuable to others.

The chapter also shows the importance of reading evidence carefully. Deaths, crossings, remittances, deforestation, and lithium were each measured in different units by institutions with different purposes. Distinguishing counts from estimates, alerts from annual rates, and resources from output is not a technicality. It is how historians avoid mistaking announcements for outcomes, a skill that will matter in the study of 2026, where many developments remain unresolved.

Finally, the chapter connects regions that earlier chapters treated separately: Mexico's factories, Central America's remittances, Brazil's forests, Chile's salt flats, Peru's ports, and the Caribbean's storms. Their experiences differed, and the chapter has resisted a single regional story. What they share is a set of questions about who controls resources, who benefits, and which institutions protect communities, and Chapter 115 takes those questions into Venezuela, whose oil, exodus, and 2026 crisis bring many of them together.
