Chapter Opening: A Neighborhood Burns

In the early hours of 20 December 1989, residents of El Chorrillo, a crowded district beside the headquarters of Panama's armed forces, woke to the opening of a foreign invasion launched by a government that had cooperated with their ruler for years.

Panama in December 1989 was not simply the setting for a confrontation between an American president and a Panamanian general. It was a republic whose people had struggled for generations to reclaim authority over a canal corridor administered by another country, and whose internal political institutions had been dominated by the armed forces for more than two decades. The intervention of 20 December brought those histories together in one violent night.

On the eve of the invasion, the civilian opposition could point to a substantial electoral victory in May that the military-backed authorities had annulled. The armed institution remained capable of intimidation, detention and violence. Yet the presence of United States forces, bases and treaty rights made any U.S. decision to intervene unusually consequential for sovereignty and international law. Panamanians did not face the choice of supporting a dictator or abandoning their country’s independence; many rejected both propositions.

El Chorrillo, beside the headquarters of the Panama Defense Forces, makes the costs of that conflict visible. The neighborhood suffered destructive fighting, fires, displacement and deaths. A military headquarters could be a lawful strategic objective without making nearby homes or the people inside them expendable. The human consequences require a separate investigation from the question of whether Noriega deserved prosecution.

The history begins with the 1903 separation from Colombia and the exceptional canal arrangements, passes through the 1964 flag crisis and the 1977 treaties, and follows the making of a military regime under Omar Torrijos and Manuel Noriega. It then reconstructs the disputed 1984 election, drug-trafficking evidence, the Spadafora killing, the Civic Crusade, the 1988 indictments and sanctions, and the crisis of 1989.

The chapter considers documentary evidence at several levels: treaties and diplomatic clarifications for international obligations; Panamanian political and human-rights records for domestic repression; federal indictments and trial records for specific criminal allegations; presidential statements and military histories for U.S. decision-making; and independent civilian investigations for the consequences of battle. Each kind of record illuminates different questions and has distinct limitations.

Finally, this is a chapter about transitions that did not happen simultaneously. Noriega lost practical power in December 1989 and surrendered in January 1990; the federal conviction followed in 1992; Panama entrenched its decision not to maintain a standing army in 1994; and the canal transfer scheduled in 1977 was completed in 1999. Later human-rights findings continued to test the historical account of the invasion.

As the concluding chapter of Volume IV, the story provides a bridge from Cold War interventions and dictatorships to Volume V’s investigations of democratization, financial globalization and organized crime. The end of a military ruler could create a new constitutional opening without settling the legality of the means used or the obligations owed to civilians injured along the way.

Historical Context

The isthmus of Panama joins South and Central America and separates Atlantic-Caribbean and Pacific maritime systems by a short overland distance. Its transport infrastructure was developed under Spanish imperial rule, Colombian government and, from 1903, an independent Panamanian republic whose treaty obligations sharply limited control of the canal corridor. The physical setting gave canal ports, rail links and bases international importance out of proportion to the republic’s population.

Panama’s population was socially diverse. Afro-Panamanian communities, including descendants of West Indian canal workers, were central to transit commerce, neighborhoods, labor politics and culture. Urban bankers, professionals, wage workers, small traders, farmers, Indigenous communities and residents of outlying provinces did not necessarily experience the same benefits of canal trade or military development. These differences shaped the politics of strikes, sanctions and reconstruction.

The National Guard’s 1968 coup created a system of military political oversight. Torrijos negotiated the 1977 treaties while governing without liberal-democratic accountability; Noriega later operated through a reorganized combined security institution and nominally civilian governments. The 1980s regional Cold War added intelligence exchanges, clandestine networks and drug-trafficking disputes. The chapter therefore analyzes both an internal constitutional crisis and its entanglement with a much older international relationship.

For the wider period, Chapters 70 and 89–91 address American hemispheric expansion and contemporary regional civil wars. This chapter does not retell those histories; it develops Panama’s institutional and social trajectory in its own terms and assesses the legal, political and humanitarian consequences of the 1989 invasion.

Division I — Sovereignty, the Canal, and the Making of Military Power
Section 92.1

The Canal Zone and the Struggle for Sovereignty

Panama's place at the narrowest crossing between the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean made it valuable to maritime empires well before the twentieth century. After independence from Spain in 1821, Panama belonged to successive Colombian political formations. Transit commerce through the isthmus, especially after the nineteenth-century railroad, made the region unusually exposed to foreign commercial pressure. Panama's 1903 separation from Colombia therefore had several constituencies: Panamanian separatists seeking autonomy, transisthmian interests seeking dependable transit, and a United States government determined to build a canal. United States military support made separation possible in the immediate crisis. That dependence did not nullify Panamanian political agency, but it decisively constrained the new republic's freedom of negotiation.

The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty of 18 November 1903, signed by a French engineer representing Panama, granted extensive U.S. rights in and around the route of the projected canal. Bunau-Varilla had little organic political mandate among Panamanians, which made the agreement controversial from its inception. The treaty's language gave the United States rights of use, occupation, and control 'as if it were the sovereign' while leaving the question of underlying sovereignty a source of enduring controversy. In practice, a U.S. jurisdictional enclave emerged with its own police, schools, courts, hospitals, and military installations. The canal opened to traffic in 1914, but the infrastructure joining oceans simultaneously divided the social and political life of the isthmus.

Canal labor illustrates why sovereignty could not be understood as diplomacy alone. Construction and operation drew workers from the Caribbean, notably people of West Indian origin, together with Panamanians and workers from other regions. Segregated wage and employment categories—historically known as gold and silver rolls—distributed privilege through race and employment status, though these classifications changed over time. U.S. facilities and wages generated opportunity while also producing exclusion, tension, and a hierarchy visible in housing and public services. Afro-Panamanian communities, including descendants of canal workers, were central participants in this society; they must not disappear from a diplomatic narrative concerned only with presidents.

In January 1964 a dispute over raising Panama's flag alongside the U.S. flag at Balboa High School escalated into clashes between students, residents, police, and U.S. forces. The confrontation of 9 January and subsequent violence killed approximately twenty-one Panamanians and three Americans according to contemporary U.S. governmental summaries; accounts differ in how they classify deaths in the broader unrest. In Panama the anniversary became the Day of the Martyrs. The crisis ruptured diplomatic relations and transformed longstanding demands for treaty reform into a compelling national issue. It also showed that a dispute about a symbol rested upon larger grievances concerning jurisdiction, dignity, inequality, and control of territory.

Any interpretation of the 1989 invasion must begin with this memory. For many Panamanians, the presence of U.S. troops was not merely a technical matter of treaty defense but an extension of a history in which foreign military power could determine political outcomes. Yet public opinion was not monolithic: people could simultaneously resent historic intervention, welcome transfer of the canal, oppose military rule, and favor removing Noriega. Historical analysis therefore separates the legitimacy of Panamanian grievances, the popularity or unpopularity of particular rulers, and the international legality of outside military action. The most important continuity across 1903, 1964, 1977, and 1989 was not the survival of any one government but the unresolved relationship between national self-government and extraordinary strategic dependence.

Section 92.2

The 1968 Coup and Torrijos's Military Nationalism

The National Guard's seizure of power on 11 October 1968 overthrew President Arnulfo Arias just days after he had assumed office. Military officers presented their action as a response to factional conflict and institutional danger, but the coup interrupted constitutional government and transferred the effective power of veto from elected officials to the armed institution. Omar Torrijos gradually emerged as the dominant commander. His leadership rested not solely on coercion but also on negotiations with labor groups, rural supporters, business interests, the United States, and factions inside the Guard. The result was a military-centered political regime that claimed a revolutionary mandate while sharply limiting opposition.

Torrijos pursued roads, schools, health and housing programs, agrarian measures, and a more assertive role for the state. These policies helped some communities previously excluded from elite urban politics; implementation was uneven, and reform did not eliminate inequalities of landownership or wealth. Popular political participation was channeled through institutions that the military could supervise. The 1972 Constitution and its revisions made room for a particular civilian political order without permitting genuine independence from the Guard. The distinction matters: a regime may possess popular accomplishments and nationalist legitimacy in some constituencies while remaining authoritarian in the exercise of power.

The canal became Torrijos's most effective national cause. He linked treaty reform to the promise that Panama could attain meaningful independence without renouncing transit commerce. Diplomats, students, trade unionists, churches, and political rivals had pressed the issue before him. His achievement was to coordinate domestic pressure and international diplomacy at a moment when Washington increasingly recognized that indefinite colonial-style administration imposed diplomatic costs. Support from other Latin American governments widened Panama's negotiating leverage. The treaty campaign therefore demonstrates Panamanian agency within an unequal international relationship, not a concession appearing spontaneously from a benevolent foreign president.

Authoritarian costs were substantial. Opposition politicians faced exile, surveillance, censorship, and detention; autonomous labor or student action could provoke repression when it challenged military interests. The regime relied on a security apparatus that gathered intelligence inside Panama as well as along the country's regional borders. A person who worked in that apparatus, Manuel Antonio Noriega, learned to connect domestic surveillance, military promotion, and external intelligence partnerships. Institutional methods built to protect Torrijos's political project later enabled a different style of rule under Noriega. Students should resist treating the 1968–1981 period as either a golden age of sovereignty or a prelude whose only importance was producing the next dictator.

Torrijos's rule also reveals tensions between sovereignty and accountability. The government demanded that foreign authorities respect Panama's rights while offering comparatively few independent means by which its own people could question the military. Political nationalism can oppose unequal treaties without guaranteeing constitutional pluralism; conversely, demands for open elections need not signify indifference to national autonomy. This double tension explains why the later opposition to Noriega drew on both democratic language and the earlier national struggle for dignity, while his regime attempted to claim the legacy of Torrijos even after its governing methods had changed.

Section 92.3

The Torrijos–Carter Treaties

On 7 September 1977 President Jimmy Carter and General Omar Torrijos signed two separate agreements at the Organization of American States in Washington. The Panama Canal Treaty governed the replacement of the old Canal Zone system and the transition of canal operations. When the new arrangements took effect on 1 October 1979, the Canal Zone as a distinct U.S.-administered political entity ceased to exist, even though the United States continued operating the canal and maintained military facilities under the transitional agreements. Final operational control was scheduled to pass to Panama at noon on 31 December 1999. This timetable was a legal commitment, not a promise dependent on the survival of Torrijos's government.

The second instrument, the Treaty Concerning the Permanent Neutrality and Operation of the Panama Canal, concerned the canal's continued accessibility to vessels of different nations. The United States and Panama associated themselves with its protection; debates arose over what defensive actions that language authorized. Negotiators clarified the problem in their 14 October 1977 Joint Statement of Understanding. That statement specified that protecting neutrality did not authorize U.S. intervention in Panama's internal affairs, and that permissible action was directed to the canal's openness and security. Accordingly, a claim of canal defense was not by itself an unlimited license to overthrow a Panamanian government.

Ratification required difficult domestic bargaining. In the United States, supporters argued that a negotiated transition protected a strategic waterway better than perpetual unilateral control; critics feared loss of influence or vulnerability. Senate debates added conditions and interpretive declarations. In Panama, the treaties were celebrated by many as a historic recovery of sovereign authority, but opponents questioned both the long transitional period and the absence of democratic legitimacy under military rule. The differing audiences mattered: Washington's emphasis fell on continued safe transit, Panama's on the restoration of national dignity and control. These priorities overlapped without being identical.

The two agreements established a structure of coexistence that survived severe diplomatic disputes. U.S. forces remained present during the transition; Panamanian public authorities progressively assumed responsibilities outside the former Zone; and canal operations were managed by a treaty-defined commission. This settlement created obligations for both governments, including cooperation and protection, but also ensured that diplomatic friction could have practical consequences for the workers and communities dependent on canal institutions. The period from 1979 to 1999 is therefore best described as an orderly transfer schedule subjected to a profound political crisis, rather than as an era of restored sovereignty already completed in 1979.

When the 1989 invasion occurred, the United States argued, among other reasons, that treaty integrity and canal security were at stake. Critics answered that the neutrality agreement and its interpretation prohibited intervention in Panama's internal political affairs. The central legal dispute cannot be resolved by quoting one provision outside its historical context. A student must compare the two treaties, the 1977 clarification, the obligations described by the United States, and the separate law governing the use of force between states. The fact that the canal transfer was eventually completed in 1999 establishes continuity in the treaty timetable; it does not retrospectively establish the legality of every military action undertaken during the transition.

Section 92.4

Noriega's Rise and the Death of Torrijos

Manuel Antonio Noriega rose within a military order in which the control of information could be as consequential as command of combat units. Trained as an officer and associated with intelligence work, he accumulated experience with internal surveillance, regional security, and relationships with foreign agencies. Noriega was not the elected president of Panama. His position came from control of the armed institution, influence over civilian leaders, and the coercive capacity of commanders answerable to him. Distinguishing formal office from practical power is essential because the regime retained the vocabulary of constitutional civilian government while many decisive political choices were made elsewhere.

Torrijos died in an aircraft crash on 31 July 1981. The circumstances encouraged competing accusations and conspiracy claims, particularly after Colonel Roberto Díaz Herrera later asserted that Noriega was implicated. The fact of the crash is established; the assertion that Noriega arranged it is not established by the evidence reviewed here and must not be presented as historical fact. Torrijos's death opened a struggle among military commanders. Noriega ultimately took charge of the National Guard in August 1983, and the integration of military, police, and investigative bodies into the Panama Defense Forces strengthened the commander as a central political broker.

Noriega's importance to outside intelligence organizations preceded his consolidation of power. U.S. officials had valued information about Central American conflicts, Cuban contacts, and security networks. Those relationships developed alongside allegations that Panama's territory and financial institutions could facilitate drug trafficking, money laundering, and clandestine transactions. The historical challenge is reconstructing who knew what, when, and how that knowledge influenced policy. A later criminal conviction establishes specified offenses proved in a particular court; it does not automatically prove every allegation made by a former official or demonstrate that every U.S. agency knew the same facts at the same time.

As head of the PDF, Noriega could promote and transfer officers, investigate rivals, intimidate journalists, and influence the selection or survival of civilian presidents. These abilities combined patronage with fear. Officers who feared dismissal and civilians dependent on military favor could reinforce each other's calculations. Yet loyalty was not automatic. The attempted coups and denunciations of the late 1980s reveal splits within the command structure, and Noriega's system had to police its own ranks. The same information network that helped the ruler identify opposition also made its officers potentially dangerous witnesses when political loyalties changed.

This institutional reading prevents two distortions. It avoids portraying Noriega as an all-powerful individual acting outside Panama's political history, and it avoids making him simply an American creation devoid of domestic choices and supporters. The coercive apparatus had Panamanian roots, drew on Torrijos-era practices, and adjusted to external pressures. Its eventual collapse in December 1989 was swift in military terms, but the longer transformation of authority—away from generals and toward accountable civilian institutions—required years of political, constitutional, and administrative work.

Historical Vocabulary

Sovereignty, Jurisdiction and De Facto Rule

Sovereignty refers to ultimate political authority over a state and its territory. Jurisdiction is the legal capacity to govern particular people, activities or places. The 1903 treaty made U.S. governmental authority in the Canal Zone exceptionally broad while Panamanian sovereign claims remained politically important. A de facto ruler can control government decisions without holding the formally responsible elected office. Noriega was the latter; that did not make his acts legally immune from scrutiny.

Interpretive test: Explain how formal independence and foreign control could coexist in one territory, and why the existence of a president did not prove independent civilian rule.

Division II — Noriega's Military Regime, Intelligence Networks, and Opposition
Section 92.5

The Defense Forces and Civilian Presidents

In the middle 1980s, Panama formally possessed constitutional offices, political parties, courts, and electoral procedures. Actual authority was divided unequally. The commander of the Panama Defense Forces controlled the armed men whose obedience could determine whether a president governed, resigned, or remained a symbol without power. This distinction between a legal office and an effective veto is often the hallmark of military tutelage: civilians may hold the titles of state, but officers reserve power over security and major political decisions. It also created opportunities for plausible deniability, because public decrees could be signed by ministers who did not initiate them.

The 1984 presidential election exposed the fragility of this arrangement. Nicolás Ardito Barletta, the candidate backed by the governing coalition, was officially declared winner over Arnulfo Arias by 1,713 votes. Opponents alleged systematic fraud; international observers and later testimony raised serious questions about the process. These objections require precise characterization: the declared result was an official event, whereas the accusation that the tally was manipulated rests on evidence and interpretation that historians must evaluate. The IACHR's 1989 account recorded the controversy and identified broader institutional disadvantages imposed on opposition parties.

Barletta's presidency lasted little more than a year. When the murder of physician and dissident Hugo Spadafora provoked demands for an investigation, Barletta indicated that an impartial inquiry should occur. Military leaders pressured him out in September 1985, according to his later statements and the IACHR's historical review, and Vice President Eric Arturo Delvalle took his place. This sequence showed that a president could not easily investigate alleged abuse by the armed forces. The problem was not merely a corrupt ballot box; it was that the command hierarchy could override the independent authority of elected or appointed civilian officials.

The PDF linked regular military units, policing, investigative functions, and intelligence. That concentration reduced the institutional checks that might otherwise separate army, domestic security, and criminal investigation. Uniformed personnel could influence election security, access information about critics, and manage coercive operations while the ordinary judiciary lacked effective independence. The state also operated in a dense urban economy of banks, trading firms, labor organizations, and canal-related services. Military patrons could reward cooperating commercial interests and punish opponents through regulation, contracts, access, and intimidation. These mechanisms made the regime more resilient than an explanation focused only on Noriega's personal charisma would suggest.

The existence of a substantial opposition matters equally. Lawyers, doctors, students, business owners, religious leaders, wage workers, and residents of different political sympathies rejected the military's dominance for reasons that did not always coincide. Some sought the restoration of traditional party competition; others demanded freedom from intimidation, a credible judiciary, and the possibility of independent associations. The same society could contain beneficiaries of military national-development programs and critics of military repression. Treating Panama as a country universally united either behind or against Noriega would erase the political competition that made the crisis historically important.

Section 92.6

Intelligence, Narcotics, and Financial Networks

Panama's geographic position helped make it a transit and communications center: a canal joining two oceans, major airline connections, shipping services, and a sophisticated offshore financial sector. Such institutions were not criminal by their nature. Banks, legal firms, and trade companies served legitimate international business, but secrecy, rapid movement of funds, and overlapping jurisdictions also created opportunities for laundering proceeds from illicit activity. The cocaine economy linking Colombian production and trafficking organizations to consumers in the United States gave criminal networks incentives to seek secure routes, corrupt officials, and dependable financial intermediaries. Noriega's importance stemmed partly from his potential ability to provide or deny that protection.

U.S. intelligence relationships added another layer. During the Cold War, agencies often sought partners who could supply information on Cuba, Nicaragua, and guerrilla movements, even when the partners' human-rights records were poor. Contemporary press reporting, congressional oversight materials, and later declassified CIA records show both an intelligence relationship with Noriega and growing alarm about drug trafficking. The existence of cooperation does not prove that U.S. policy was uniformly coordinated, nor that every intelligence payment or information exchange was a payment for crime. It does, however, demand examination of the incentives that encouraged officials to value short-term access over longer-term institutional consequences.

Federal prosecutors in Florida eventually assembled evidence concerning Noriega's role in cocaine shipments and protective arrangements with traffickers. The Miami trial heard testimony from traffickers and investigators; a jury convicted him in April 1992 of eight counts including racketeering and drug offenses. Some witnesses had their own incentives to cooperate, which defense attorneys contested. A conviction demonstrates that the prosecution proved the elements of particular charges beyond reasonable doubt to the jury; an indictment had merely alleged them. Neither outcome licenses a historian to assign every illegal shipment through Panama to Noriega or to equate all Panamanian financiers with criminal accomplices.

A careful account distinguishes several overlapping networks: legitimate transit commerce, illicit drug movement, laundering of proceeds, clandestine regional arms contacts, and intelligence exchanges among states. They intersected, but a map showing the Panama Canal or a major bank does not by itself identify a criminal route. Likewise, the phrase 'narco-state' can conceal more than it explains unless the author specifies the degree of criminal influence on institutions, the evidence of official collusion, the political beneficiaries, and competing centers of state authority. In Noriega's Panama, some officials assisted criminal operations while other Panamanians, including public employees and members of civil society, opposed them.

This history helps explain the deterioration of U.S.–Panamanian relations without presenting the drug indictments as the only cause. Once Washington treated Noriega as a drug-trafficking defendant rather than a useful security partner, previous intelligence ties became politically embarrassing and legally contentious. The apparent reversal is real, but it unfolded through changing executive priorities, congressional scrutiny, prosecutorial investigations, the collapse of domestic legitimacy in Panama, and escalating confrontations between security forces. Historians should compare the timing of each development rather than assume one secret decision explains the entire shift.

Section 92.7

The Murder of Hugo Spadafora

Hugo Spadafora was a Panamanian physician, former vice minister of health, and participant in regional political struggles. He had opposed Nicaragua's Somoza dictatorship before later associating with armed resistance to the Sandinista government. His political trajectory illustrates how Cold War alignments could shift across national borders; a person might oppose an authoritarian right-wing regime without accepting every subsequent revolutionary government. Spadafora also made accusations concerning drug traffic and corruption connected with Panamanian power. In September 1985 he traveled from Costa Rica toward Panama as relations with the military regime deteriorated.

Spadafora was killed and his mutilated body discovered in Costa Rica. Accounts of his detention and movements raised grave concerns about official involvement. Panama's domestic institutions failed to deliver an independent and credible accounting. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights later found the Panamanian government internationally responsible in the case, emphasizing the state's obligation to protect life and investigate the killing. Responsibility at the state level is not identical to a criminal judgment against a named individual. Allegations that Noriega personally ordered the murder need to be assessed against specific testimony and legal findings, not repeated as settled merely because they circulated widely.

President Barletta's reported interest in an impartial inquiry became a test of civilian autonomy. His forced resignation in September 1985 suggested that even senior government officials faced limits when they challenged military conduct. The incident intensified mistrust among professional organizations and citizens who might previously have accepted the military's claimed role in guaranteeing stability. A murder could thus become a constitutional crisis when it revealed that ordinary avenues of investigation and accountability were blocked. The consequences also extended beyond elite politics: journalists, students, and families of victims had reason to fear that the system could not protect critics or establish facts about disappearances and killings.

Historical methods matter here. An official death certificate can establish bodily injury but not necessarily who commanded an operation. Human-rights petitions may record testimony and procedural failures, while a military intelligence assessment may contain information never tested in open court. Newspaper reports provide chronology and public reactions, but anonymous sources may pursue political aims. Students should compare contemporaneous records with later reconstructions and clearly distinguish eyewitness statements, institutional conclusions, accusations, and proven judicial facts. This approach neither minimizes violations nor invents certainty.

Spadafora's case became a touchstone because it involved the intersection of civilian life, regional insurgencies, official force, and the limits of law. The state's treatment of his death was part of a broader pattern involving detention, intimidation, censorship, and threatened association. It also sharpened the tensions within the ruling coalition, contributing to the emergence of opposition groups that did not have to agree on every economic or foreign-policy question in order to demand an end to impunity. By 1987, discontent that had accumulated around individual cases was becoming a more organized challenge to the military political order.

Section 92.8

The 1987 Crisis and the Cruzada Civilista

In June 1987 retired Colonel Roberto Díaz Herrera publicly accused Noriega and other officers of serious misconduct, including manipulation of the 1984 election and involvement in earlier political crimes. His allegations were politically explosive because they came from a former senior insider. They were still allegations requiring corroboration; in particular, claims linking Noriega to Torrijos's 1981 death should not be treated as proved. The response to Díaz Herrera demonstrated the stakes of challenging military power. The government imposed emergency measures, arrested opponents, and restricted constitutional guarantees. The IACHR documented the worsening atmosphere of repression and limitations on expression and assembly.

Protests expanded through the Cruzada Civilista Nacional, commonly called the Civic Crusade, an alliance spanning professional associations, business groups, civic organizations, students, and other opposition constituencies. White handkerchiefs became a recognizable sign of public dissent. Business shutdowns and demonstrations placed economic and reputational pressure on the government, though they also burdened workers and households not able to stop earning wages. The coalition did not represent every Panamanian, and not every participant desired identical policies. It could nevertheless unite around a minimum platform: civilian political rights, an end to military coercion, and a credible process for choosing leaders.

Panamanian women organized, marched, and sustained family and neighborhood networks that made dissent visible, even when most international reporting focused on male generals and presidential contenders. The Catholic Church offered institutions capable of recording testimony and mediating conflict, but the Church itself was not a single political faction. Labor unions and poorer communities faced distinct incentives from bankers or employers. Some feared price increases, job losses, or foreign retaliation more immediately than constitutional formulas; others had experience with both Torrijos-era social reforms and Noriega-era repression. An inclusive social history asks who could afford a strike, whose businesses reopened, and whose household faced the risks of police action.

The government's response combined coercion with arguments about sovereignty. Noriega's supporters pointed to U.S. pressure and the history of intervention to depict opponents as instruments of a foreign power. External encouragement of anti-Noriega political actors did exist, making the question of foreign influence legitimate. Yet foreign support did not erase the domestic origins of grievances about fraudulent elections, violence, and the absence of judicial independence. Conversely, domestic legitimacy did not automatically justify all foreign means used on the opposition's behalf. The crisis involved competing claims to speak for Panama, not a simple opposition between patriotism and treason.

The 1987 protests were a turning point because they converted fragmented dissatisfaction into sustained national confrontation. The Armed Forces could still suppress marches and maintain command, but it increasingly lacked a convincing path back to accepted civilian rule. International organizations began to collect more detailed evidence, and Washington's willingness to cooperate with Noriega diminished. The following year would show how legal indictments, economic sanctions, and attempted political compromises interacted with this domestic movement—often producing instability without achieving a peaceful transfer of authority.

Division III — The Breakdown of U.S.–Panamanian Relations
Section 92.9

Indictments and Sanctions, 1988

On 5 February 1988 federal grand juries in Florida announced narcotics-related charges involving Noriega and associates. Indictment turned an international political dispute into a prospective U.S. criminal case: prosecutors were now seeking to bring a foreign military ruler before a domestic federal court. An indictment states allegations established to the grand jury's legal threshold, not a conviction or a neutral historical verdict. The later April 1992 conviction may be used to describe offenses proved at trial, but historians must keep the two dates and their different evidentiary meanings clear. The announcement also made cooperation between the United States and Noriega politically more difficult to defend.

In February 1988 President Eric Arturo Delvalle attempted to dismiss Noriega from command. The military ignored the decision, and the regime-aligned legislature removed Delvalle and elevated Manuel Solís Palma to an acting executive role. Washington continued to recognize Delvalle, producing a diplomatic conflict over which authorities lawfully represented Panama. The case demonstrates the peculiar relationship between legal continuity and political control: a president recognized abroad could lack practical power at home, while officials governing the ministries could be denied international recognition. Such disputes affected embassy communications, public funds, and negotiations without resolving the underlying military veto.

Washington escalated pressure through financial measures, including restrictions on transfers to the Panamanian government and the freezing of certain assets. The Reagan administration's April 1988 Executive Order 12635 provides a precise documentary anchor for part of this campaign. Disrupted payment flows exacerbated the banking crisis and contributed to uncertainty over wages, credit, and public services. These measures were designed to weaken military rule, but their costs were not confined to military leaders. Shopkeepers, bank employees, households, and public-sector workers faced changing access to cash and ordinary transactions. A strategy's political intention and its distributional consequences must be analyzed separately.

The military survived the first wave of pressure. Coup plotting, attempted accommodation, business opposition, and outside sanctions did not dismantle Noriega's network. Sanctions could even reinforce the regime's claim that Washington was punishing the whole nation; repression reduced the ability of critics to coordinate a decisive domestic change. U.S. officials also differed over the appropriate balance among negotiation, covert support, interdiction, and force. It would be misleading to tell the story as a single uninterrupted master plan leading inevitably to invasion. Policy hardened through a series of setbacks, debates, and reactive choices.

The escalation exposed a wider dilemma for U.S. policy in Latin America. Officials could invoke democracy and the rule of law while confronting the legacy of earlier security partnerships with authoritarian actors. The legal language of an indictment created an argument for judicial accountability, but the methods needed to arrest a sitting foreign ruler posed serious questions of jurisdiction and sovereignty. For Panamanians, the urgent problem remained whether a political transition could occur without deeper economic damage or an armed confrontation on their own territory. The 1989 election was a critical test of that possibility.

Historical Vocabulary

Indictment, Evidence, Verdict and Jurisdiction

A grand-jury indictment formally alleges criminal charges based on the threshold required for prosecution; it is neither a verdict nor proof of guilt. A conviction follows criminal adjudication under the applicable standard of proof. Jurisdiction determines which court may hear a case. Noriega was indicted in 1988 but convicted in 1992. The validity of an indictment or trial does not automatically settle whether the military force used to seize the accused was lawful under international law.

Section 92.10

The Panama Crisis in American Policy

American policy toward Panama was shaped by institutions that did not always share identical priorities. The State Department needed workable diplomacy and regional allies; military commanders were responsible for U.S. service members and installations; prosecutors pursued criminal charges; intelligence agencies evaluated security assets; members of Congress questioned narcotics cooperation and democratic legitimacy. Presidents Reagan and George H. W. Bush inherited commitments and dilemmas from earlier administrations. By 1988 the public image of Noriega as a drug-trafficking strongman sat uneasily beside the record of his prior cooperation with U.S. agencies.

Washington tried several methods short of direct invasion: diplomatic isolation, economic restrictions, encouragement of opposition forces, and support for possible negotiated departures. The Organization of American States also pursued a regional diplomatic response that rejected repression while reaffirming the principle of non-intervention. These parallel tracks could interfere with one another. Panamanian authorities portrayed U.S. sanctions as evidence of interference; opposition groups feared that negotiations might leave the military command intact; governments elsewhere in Latin America were reluctant to normalize a precedent in which an external power could settle the fate of a neighboring government by force.

Military preparations developed while political means were still being tested. Contingency planning is a normal part of military administration, so the existence of a plan does not establish that invasion was already inevitable. Changes in troop deployment, rules of engagement, and the security of U.S. personnel mattered because thousands of American troops and civilians remained in Panama under the treaty transition. Friction around roads, bases, and checkpoints blurred the boundary between planned deterrence and confrontational escalation. Military commanders assessed whether the PDF could threaten the canal, U.S. bases, and people; political leaders considered how much force could be defended domestically and internationally.

The Cold War context was shifting rapidly. Throughout the 1980s, fighting in Nicaragua and other Central American states had influenced the strategic value assigned to Panama's intelligence networks. By late 1989, East European communist governments were collapsing and superpower relations were changing, but Cold War institutional habits did not vanish overnight. U.S. officials still worried about military access and regional credibility even as the language of democracy, law enforcement, and post-Cold War security gained prominence. The invasion would later be read as an early example of a more assertive hemispheric order, yet the operation itself grew out of long-standing canal commitments and a specific Panamanian political crisis.

One of the essential historical questions is whether officials exhausted realistic nonmilitary alternatives. That is an interpretive question, not a fact established simply by a presidential speech asserting exhaustion. The evidence includes OAS mediation records, private diplomatic proposals, U.S. policy memoranda, the chronology of sanctions, attempts by Panamanian officers to remove Noriega, and the timing of the December crisis. Students should distinguish why a policymaker publicly justified a decision from the wider range of motives contemporaneous documents reveal. This distinction prepares the ground for assessing the May election and subsequent choice of force without replacing analysis with either official vindication or retrospective condemnation.

Section 92.11

The May 1989 Election and Its Annulment

The general elections of 7 May 1989 offered a direct test of whether the military would permit the constitutional electorate to determine political succession. The principal opposition alliance, Alianza Democrática de Oposición Civilista (ADOC), supported Guillermo Endara for president with Ricardo Arias Calderón and Guillermo Ford as vice-presidential candidates. The government coalition supported Carlos Duque. Voters were not simply choosing between two personalities: they were deciding whether the PDF would remain the ultimate authority behind the electoral system. International attention was unusually intense because earlier elections had left a legacy of disputed counting and intimidation.

The environment was deeply unequal. The IACHR's 1989 report documented restrictions on independent media, harassment of opposition organizations, questionable electoral administration, and complaints about voter lists and the conduct of military personnel. These conditions compromised electoral fairness even before a ballot was cast. Yet observers also reported high participation, and available polling-station returns strongly indicated an opposition victory. It is important to distinguish the two analytical claims: the campaign environment was compromised by government interference, while the available election returns nevertheless provided substantial evidence of the opposition's support.

The regime did not accept the outcome. On 10 May 1989, Panama's Electoral Tribunal issued Decree No. 58 annulling the elections across elective offices. It cited disruption, misconduct, and improper foreign influence, but this official explanation did not resolve the evidence of an opposition victory. The IACHR later recorded the opposition's compilation from 80.9 percent of polling stations, showing approximately 65 percent for the opposition and 26 percent for the ruling coalition. Those numbers should be identified as opposition tabulations of the available records, not presented as a universally completed official final tally. The distinction protects the evidentiary value of the returns without overstating their status.

Violence followed. Images and reporting of the assault on opposition figures, including Guillermo Ford, became widely circulated evidence of coercion. Panamanian protests and international condemnation deepened, while OAS envoys worked to find a negotiated settlement. The political calendar continued to matter: the constitutional terms of existing officials expired on 1 September, and regime-aligned authorities installed Francisco Rodríguez through a procedure that the IACHR described as lacking a constitutional basis. From this point onward, the legal claim of the election winners and the practical control of ministries and armed units were increasingly separated.

The annulment altered the strategic choices available to all parties. It undermined Noriega's assertion that his government could win legitimate authority through elections, increased outside willingness to recognize Endara, and gave Washington a clearer democratic justification for intervention. Yet recognition of an opposition electoral victory did not automatically confer an international-law right to invade. Panama's sovereignty remained a legal interest independent of the character of its rulers. For Panamanians, the election mattered because it expressed political agency in the face of military coercion; that agency should not be reduced to the claim that a foreign army later 'gave' the country democracy.

Section 92.12

Rebellion and the Road to Intervention

As negotiations failed to bring Noriega's resignation, internal splits within the security establishment became increasingly important. On 3 October 1989 officers attempted to overthrow him but failed. The episode revealed that not every armed commander supported the regime, yet it also demonstrated the difficulty of coordinating a change when the ruler controlled communications, loyal units, and intelligence. Subsequent arrests and reported executions of participants intensified fear within the armed forces. Outside observers debated whether Washington should have intervened on behalf of the rebels, but those judgments must be assessed in the light of incomplete knowledge about the plotters' intentions, resources, and chances of success.

The failed uprising had broader consequences. It made prospects for an internally organized transfer appear weaker, and it alerted Noriega to officers who might act again. The government strengthened personal security and relied on irregular proregime groups, commonly described as Dignity Battalions, alongside the PDF. Such groups created a disturbing problem of command and responsibility: violence could be inflicted by armed supporters whose exact relationship to formal units was less transparent than that of regular soldiers. Civilian opponents faced the prospect that demonstrations and even elections would be met by a combination of official and irregular coercion.

In December the confrontation became acute. Panama's regime-aligned National Assembly adopted a declaration portraying the country as being in a state of war because of U.S. aggression, and Noriega received an extraordinary political designation. U.S. authorities described increasing harassment of their personnel. On 16 December PDF soldiers killed Marine Lieutenant Robert Paz at a roadblock; associated encounters included the detention and mistreatment of other U.S. service members and a spouse. These events are the immediate triggers identified in contemporary American military and presidential accounts. A careful chronology separates a contested political declaration, the fatal shooting, and the subsequent American decision, rather than collapsing them into a single event.

President George H. W. Bush authorized the invasion on 17 December after consulting advisers. Operational preparations had been under way well before that meeting. This distinction is crucial for analyzing causation: the December incidents helped precipitate the decision to execute a plan, but the plan itself had been developed in response to a much longer crisis. Bush and his advisers anticipated a rapid defeat of organized Panamanian resistance, removal of Noriega, and the installation of the electoral opposition. They also confronted risks of fighting near civilian homes, bases, and transit infrastructure. The scale of those risks became apparent after the invasion began.

For civilians, the final days of the crisis were shaped by uncertainty rather than by the neat chronology visible in a government archive. Some feared a coup, others a crackdown, others a foreign attack. Families working in banking, transportation, the canal economy, commerce, and informal labor all faced economic disruption. No one inside Panama could simply leave the consequences of failed diplomacy to generals and presidents. When historians describe an intervention as a rational choice or an inevitable culmination, they must ask whose alternatives were evaluated, whose safety entered the calculation, and whose losses were counted only after the fighting.

Division IV — Operation Just Cause, Civilian Consequences, and International Law
Section 92.13

Bush and the American Justification

President Bush presented the intervention of 20 December 1989 as a response to a regime that threatened Americans, prevented democratic rule, enabled drug crime, and endangered the canal treaty arrangement. His address to the nation specified four objectives: safeguard U.S. lives, defend democracy, combat drug trafficking by bringing Noriega to justice, and protect the integrity of the Panama Canal treaties. The objectives were politically intelligible and not all invented for the occasion. Each, however, requires separate legal and historical examination. A government may have a powerful concern about nationals abroad without acquiring an unlimited license to overthrow another state's administration.

American officials highlighted the fatal shooting of Lieutenant Paz, threats to other service members, and the long pattern of harassment around installations. They also cited the annulled election and claimed that an Endara administration already possessed democratic legitimacy. The nature of the claimed democratic mandate raises an important distinction: electoral returns and international recognition can strengthen the legitimacy of a domestic political actor, but the use of force to install that actor remains governed by separate international rules. Similarly, a criminal indictment provides a basis for prosecutorial action within U.S. law; it does not itself give armed forces an unrestricted extraterritorial arrest power.

The treaty justification was especially contested. Article IV of the neutrality framework and the history of canal defense were invoked to stress continuing U.S. security obligations. Opponents pointed to the 1977 joint clarification that defense of neutral transit must not be understood as intervention in Panama's internal affairs. An intervention to protect canal installations and an operation to destroy the Panamanian military and replace the government are not necessarily identical actions. Students must ask whether the scope, targets, and stated purposes of a campaign fit the legal rationale advanced and how they relate to the UN Charter's prohibition on force and its narrow exceptions.

Bush's decision also reflected the politics of credibility. The administration had faced repeated confrontations with Noriega without removing him through sanctions or negotiated pressure. U.S. commanders believed their personnel remained vulnerable; regional political observers questioned the usefulness of continuing stalemate. Some Panamanians welcomed the prospect of Noriega's removal, and Endara's electoral support was real. But foreign popularity claims require evidence as carefully as claims of opposition. Accounts based on interviews with expatriates, urban demonstrators, or officials cannot be assumed to represent all working-class neighborhoods or rural communities.

A rigorous assessment separates five questions: What happened in Panama before the operation? What did U.S. leaders believe? What reasons did they announce? What legal authorizations were actually available? And what consequences followed for Panamanians? A persuasive historical explanation must answer all five rather than treating political legitimacy, military success, criminal conviction, and international legality as interchangeable measures. That distinction is the foundation for evaluating Operation Just Cause as a military campaign and as a defining controversy in the changing regional order.

Section 92.14

20 December 1989: The Invasion

Operation Just Cause began in the early hours of 20 December 1989 with coordinated U.S. attacks on command centers, airfields, barracks, and transportation nodes. Military planners sought to overwhelm the Panama Defense Forces quickly, prevent coherent counterattack, protect American installations, and capture Noriega. The United States could draw upon forces already stationed in Panama as well as major reinforcements deployed from abroad. Airborne units and special operations forces were assigned different objectives: securing runways, neutralizing defensive positions, seizing command facilities, and maintaining transit across key routes. The operation's complexity reflected Panama's compact geography and the intermingling of military and civilian spaces.

Among the principal targets were La Comandancia in Panama City, the PDF headquarters adjacent to El Chorrillo; Tocumen airport to the east; and Río Hato, an important military installation farther down the Pacific coast. Operations also sought to secure bridges and canal approaches. The military history produced by the U.S. Joint Chiefs of Staff gives a detailed reconstruction of coordination, reinforcement, and the shift from combat to civil affairs. Like all operational histories written within an institution, it is strongest on command decisions and deployment records and must be read alongside civilian testimony and independent investigation of harm.

Fighting was uneven. Several PDF formations were neutralized or surrendered rapidly, while other forces and armed groups continued resistance in particular areas. The speed of the main conventional campaign did not eliminate risks of small-arms fire, mistaken identification, house searches, and looting. The capital's street pattern exposed residents to violence in places that were not isolated battlefields. U.S. weapons and tactics capable of defeating fortified military positions could have severe effects in dense neighborhoods. Official descriptions of precision and speed therefore need to be evaluated beside evidence about target selection, warnings, evacuation, fires, and civilian shelter.

Guillermo Endara and his vice presidents assumed office in conjunction with the beginning of the intervention, with a disputed and politically sensitive account of the precise location and timing of the ceremony. Contemporary reporting placed the ceremony at a U.S. military installation, while some participants offered differing descriptions; the fact of the new government's immediate U.S. backing is not in dispute. Describing Endara simply as 'installed' risks overlooking the May electoral mandate, while describing the transfer as a routine constitutional succession ignores the force that enabled it. Both facts belong in the same explanation.

The organized defense collapsed much more quickly than Noriega himself could be located. American forces and their partners faced responsibilities beyond combat: collecting weapons, preventing reprisals, reopening roads, protecting supplies, and restoring public administration. Later operations were grouped under a reconstruction and civil-affairs effort called Promote Liberty. The U.S. Army's campaign dates extend to 31 January 1990, illustrating why a military operation cannot be reduced to a single night. The rapid defeat of the PDF is well documented; claims that the campaign was therefore a low-cost or wholly surgical intervention are contradicted by the extent of civilian injury, destruction, and unresolved casualty accounting.

Section 92.15

El Chorrillo and the Human Cost

The most concentrated civilian catastrophe occurred in El Chorrillo, a densely populated Panama City district adjoining La Comandancia. Working-class families occupied wooden and other closely packed buildings in an urban landscape where military and residential sites were separated by short distances. Heavy fighting and fires destroyed extensive housing. The causes and sequence of particular fires remain contested and should be attributed carefully; proximity to a military target does not establish the responsibility of each individual soldier or the ignition source of every blaze. What is established is that the neighborhood suffered enormous destruction and that many residents lost shelter, possessions, and relatives.

Civilian experiences cannot be summarized only through a casualty number. People displaced from El Chorrillo faced interrupted employment, missing documentation, loss of household savings, and separation from neighbors. Relief workers and medical personnel confronted damaged infrastructure, uncertain security, and the difficulty of distinguishing wounded combatants from civilians requiring aid. Reconstruction affected property claims, tenancy, public housing, and the right to return. A family that survived the fighting might still suffer years of insecurity. Historical accounts focused exclusively on troop movements therefore miss a fundamental dimension of the intervention's consequences.

Estimates of the dead differ by source and method. U.S. Southern Command reported 202 Panamanian civilians killed; Americas Watch estimated approximately 300 and urged a new, independent forensic inquiry. Some later claims presented much higher numbers, but scholars cannot treat all figures as equally verified or merge different categories. The count of U.S. military fatalities was 23. Those categories must remain separate from Panamanian military deaths, injuries, missing persons, and displaced civilians. Neither a low official estimate nor a higher claim should be adopted without establishing who was counted, when the count was compiled, and how bodies and identities were verified.

Americas Watch and Physicians for Human Rights questioned the adequacy of post-invasion investigation, including burial procedures and individual identification. Civilian investigators also criticized conduct that they believed failed to sufficiently minimize harm. Their reports are evidence-informed investigations, not transcripts of every eyewitness and not automatically identical in methodology. U.S. military accounts offer records of forces and declared rules of engagement, but official compilation does not substitute for independently establishing causes of death. The appropriate scholarly response is to compare evidentiary methods, discuss uncertainty openly, and avoid false precision.

The IACHR's 2018 merits report later found that the United States had not taken adequate protective precautions and was responsible for specified harms to civilians and property. That finding belongs beside the invasion's military chronology because it describes consequences that remained legally and politically unresolved decades later. El Chorrillo also exposes the limits of a purely binary political narrative: residents could detest Noriega and still question a foreign attack that destroyed their homes. A history of Panama must restore their experiences to the center, not treat them as an afterthought to the fate of generals and presidents.

Section 92.16

The UN, the OAS, and the Legal Debate

The response of international institutions demonstrated the distinction between disapproval of Noriega's rule and acceptance of a foreign military solution. In May 1989 the Organization of American States had criticized violations of Panamanians' political rights while affirming the principle that no state should intervene in another's internal affairs. After the invasion, regional governments differed in tone and policy, but a broad concern about unilateral force remained. Diplomatic disapproval did not imply support for Noriega. It instead reflected anxiety that the security interests of a powerful government might override the sovereignty of smaller states whenever their domestic politics became objectionable.

On 29 December 1989 the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 44/240 by 75 votes in favor, 20 against, and 40 abstentions. It strongly deplored the intervention as violating international law and Panama's sovereignty and demanded the withdrawal of invading forces. The resolution matters as an authoritative record of international diplomatic opinion. It is not an International Court of Justice judgment, nor is a General Assembly vote ordinarily a binding enforcement order in itself. The UN Charter's general prohibition of force, possible claims of self-defense, treaty interpretation, and disputed intervention doctrines must be analyzed through their respective legal sources, not compressed into a single 'UN approval' or 'UN conviction.'

The 1977 neutrality framework further complicates the legal argument. The Carter–Torrijos joint clarification explicitly rejected interpreting canal-defense rights as an authority to intervene in Panama's internal political affairs. U.S. officials nevertheless asserted that protection of the canal treaties formed one of their purposes. Critics disputed the relationship between that purpose and the campaign's main action against the PDF and Noriega. A student may conclude that the regime's brutality created genuine moral urgency while still finding the legal justification for unilateral force inadequate. Historical judgment should not be reduced to a choice between endorsing a dictator and endorsing invasion.

Decades afterward, the IACHR issued Report No. 121/18 in Case 10.573, José Isabel Salas Galindo and others, publicized on 3 December 2018. It found U.S. international responsibility for violations affecting life, personal integrity, security, children, property, and access to justice. The Commission concluded that conduct during the operation did not comply with humanitarian-law principles of distinction, necessity, proportionality, and precaution and that adequate steps to warn, evacuate, and protect civilians had not been shown. These are substantive findings about civilian harm; the Commission is not the ICJ, and its report should not be misrepresented as a final ICJ ruling on the invasion's entire lawfulness.

The United States disputed aspects of the Commission's legal interpretation and resisted a proposed reparations mechanism, while pointing to reconstruction assistance. That disagreement should be recorded alongside the Commission's findings rather than used to erase them. The historical record thus contains several distinct documents: a presidential rationale for military action, treaty provisions and clarifications, a General Assembly condemnation, regional diplomatic statements, and a later human-rights merits report. Each asks a different question and has a different institutional authority. Learning to distinguish those forms of evidence is central to serious historical and legal analysis.

Historical Vocabulary

Non-intervention and Protection of Civilians

Non-intervention concerns outside coercion of a state's political independence. The UN Charter separately limits interstate force. Distinction requires separating civilian persons and objects from lawful military targets; proportionality and precaution constrain civilian harm during attacks. These are distinct legal tests. A military campaign's swift success or its opponent's wrongdoing cannot substitute for evaluating them.

Historical Debate

Did the End of Noriega's Regime Justify the Means?

Question: How should historians assess the relationship between democratic legitimacy, unilateral intervention, and civilian harm?

Interpretation A — removal and democratic opening: The annulled May vote, repression, refusal to negotiate and dangerous armed confrontations made force appear to some policymakers and Panamanians the only remaining route to remove Noriega and permit Endara to govern. Its evidence includes election reports, eyewitnesses and the record of failed initiatives.
Interpretation B — sovereignty and humanitarian cost: The campaign violated restrictions on interstate force and subjected civilians to harms insufficiently mitigated by the attacking state. Its evidence includes the 1977 clarification, UN Resolution 44/240, casualty investigations and IACHR merits findings.

Important qualification: These interpretations assess different propositions and are not mutually exclusive. There is strong evidence that the election was subverted and strong documentary basis for serious condemnation of the use and conduct of force. Section 92.17

The Nunciature, Surrender, and Prosecution

Noriega initially escaped capture as the PDF's formal command structure collapsed. His flight to the Vatican's diplomatic mission—the apostolic nunciature—in Panama City created a tense standoff. Diplomatic premises enjoy important protections, but the episode was not a straightforward case of a conventional extradition request conducted through ordinary peacetime channels. U.S. forces surrounded the site while Vatican representatives engaged in negotiations. The standoff became a media spectacle, including controversial efforts to exert pressure through loud music, yet its diplomatic significance exceeded the spectacle: U.S. commanders needed to apprehend a wanted defendant without openly attacking a protected mission.

On 3 January 1990 Noriega surrendered and was transferred to U.S. custody, then flown to Florida to face federal charges. President Bush announced the surrender that evening. Noriega's arrest closed an immediate operational objective but opened difficult questions about the use of military force to obtain a criminal defendant from another country. The military's success did not eliminate the need for a lawful criminal proceeding, competent defense counsel, the presentation of admissible evidence, and a jury's independent verdict. These requirements are especially important because the invasion had already made Noriega an emblem of U.S. policy.

The trial began in Miami in 1991. Prosecutors presented testimony and records concerning the transport and protection of cocaine shipments and the use of Panamanian authority to assist trafficking networks. Defense attorneys challenged the reliability of witnesses and argued about U.S. intelligence contacts. On 9 April 1992 a federal jury convicted Noriega of eight of ten submitted counts, including racketeering and drug-related offenses; two counts did not result in convictions. The verdict should be presented accurately, rather than claiming that he was convicted of every possible allegation or that later criminal judgment validated every justification offered for the 1989 operation.

Noriega also asserted protections related to prisoner-of-war status. U.S. litigation addressed the status and treatment questions within a complex post-capture legal process; prisoner-of-war status did not simply immunize him from civilian criminal prosecution. Later proceedings involved custody, punishment, and extradition. In subsequent years he was transferred to France, then to Panama, where he faced separate Panamanian criminal sentences, and he died in 2017. These later developments deserve only enough space to explain that the Miami conviction was a major phase, not the complete end, of his legal history. The chapter's central concern remains the 1989–1990 transition.

The episode exposes a paradox of the intervention. Washington framed the operation partly as service to the rule of law, but the means of obtaining the defendant involved a unilateral armed invasion widely criticized under international law. Students should distinguish a fair or valid verdict on drug charges from the separate question of whether resort to armed force was lawful and proportionate. The two issues do not cancel one another. Noriega's culpability for specific crimes and the independent rights of civilians harmed during his removal can both be established without making either one a justification for neglecting the other.

Section 92.18

Endara and the Reconstruction of Civilian Government

Guillermo Endara entered office with a genuine claim grounded in the opposition victory of May 1989, but his government also depended immediately on the conditions created by foreign force. This dual origin shaped the problem of legitimacy. Endara and his vice presidents had to build functioning ministries, repair finances, restore relations abroad, and establish authority over armed personnel in a society emerging from economic sanctions and violent upheaval. Recognition by foreign governments improved the administration's position, yet durable authority required more than diplomatic acceptance: citizens needed services, reliable courts, security, and room for political competition.

The Panama Defense Forces were dismantled as the core military institution of Noriega's rule. This was not identical to permanently abolishing every security function. Police, border, maritime, and related services remained necessary and had to be reorganized under civilian institutions. The constitutional changes of 1994 entrenched Panama's decision not to maintain a standing army, while retaining public security services under civilian government. It is therefore inaccurate to say the constitutional abolition happened on 20 December 1989. The period between the military defeat and the later constitutional reform involved the difficult work of personnel screening, training, accountability, and defining permissible police powers.

Immediate reconstruction faced the physical and social consequences of fighting. Housing was destroyed, commercial districts were disrupted, and families sought compensation or replacement homes. International assistance could reopen services but also create dependencies and disputes over contracting or distribution. Noriega's fall did not dissolve problems of inequality, corruption, political patronage, or the tensions between private financial activity and public accountability. Civilian democracy created a framework in which those conflicts could be contested openly, but it did not guarantee that they would be solved. Political institutions must be assessed by their performance over time, not simply by the removal of one ruler.

The restoration of parties and elections had an important longer effect. Subsequent governments could compete without a military commander holding the same overt veto that had defined the 1980s. Yet opposition forces were heterogeneous. The coalition forged against Noriega did not necessarily agree about labor policy, the canal economy, national expenditures, or Washington's continuing influence. Political pluralism meant some disagreement was an achievement rather than evidence of failure. The ability to dismiss leaders through elections instead of coups was a critical institutional break with the earlier order.

For survivors of the invasion, accountability remained unfinished. Human-rights groups pursued records of deaths, property loss, and legal responsibility long after reconstruction programs had moved on. The later IACHR process demonstrates that a political transition may be described as successful by national and foreign authorities while some communities continue to experience the event as an unresolved humanitarian injury. Panama's democratic reconstruction should consequently be studied on two levels: the stabilization of civilian institutions and the uneven social distribution of the costs of getting there.

Section 92.19

The Canal after 1989

The invasion did not cancel the 1977 schedule for the canal's transfer. That continuity is historically striking because the treaties had been negotiated under Torrijos and implemented during the very military system later defeated by U.S. force. From the late 1970s onward, the gradual replacement of Canal Zone administration and the preparation of Panamanian personnel continued, though relations between the two governments became deeply strained in the late 1980s. Canal workers and technical managers were therefore participants in a transfer process that crossed multiple political regimes, not simply employees waiting for a ceremonial handover.

On 31 December 1999 Panama assumed full control of canal operations and the remaining U.S. treaty-period military installations were returned according to the agreement. The handover was a milestone in sovereignty, but also a practical institutional transition. Panama needed administrative capacity to maintain locks, scheduling, engineering, environmental management, workforce systems, and the neutrality obligations of a major international waterway. The existence of a functioning canal after the transfer matters because it shows that the treaty created a durable governing framework capable of surviving the 1989 political rupture.

The canal shaped a wider national economy. Transit revenues, port development, logistics, maritime services, banking, tourism, and trade linked Panama to markets far beyond the isthmus. However, commercial gains were not distributed uniformly among workers, urban neighborhoods, Indigenous territories, and rural communities. Canal income did not automatically eliminate inequality. Furthermore, Panama's importance to international finance placed governance and transparency questions on the agenda long after Noriega was imprisoned. Those developments belong chiefly to the later volume's discussion of democracy and global markets, but the historical roots are visible here.

In evaluating the 1999 transfer, students should avoid the argument that one event proves another. Successful eventual transfer does not prove the invasion lawful; likewise, condemnation of the invasion does not mean the treaties were irrelevant to canal defense. The commitments made in 1977 operated across different arenas: jurisdiction, defense, commerce, national symbolism, and diplomacy. Their persistence reveals an important continuity in U.S.–Panamanian relations even as the power of Panama's armed forces was radically transformed. The sovereignty recovered through negotiations and implementation should be distinguished from the political system reconstructed after 1989.

The completion of canal transfer also changes how the earlier nationalist movement is remembered. Students and workers who protested foreign control in 1964 could see the outcome realized by a later generation. Torrijos's treaty achievement remained significant even though his regime's authoritarian institutions came to be rejected. Endara's government and successors could claim civilian political renewal without claiming that sovereignty began with the invasion. This layered memory makes Panama a particularly useful case for understanding how nationalist, democratic, and social transformations can have different timelines and different authors.

Section 92.20

Panama and the Changing Hemispheric Order

The December 1989 invasion occurred within weeks of the fall of the Berlin Wall and during the dissolution of old Cold War alignments. It was not an invasion of a Soviet client in the conventional sense. Noriega had previously worked with U.S. intelligence while maintaining various external contacts, and his regime's domestic crisis had a distinct Panamanian history. Nevertheless, the campaign anticipated debates that would become prominent in the 1990s: the use of military power for democracy promotion, counter-narcotics policing, protection of nationals abroad, and intervention in a supposedly new international order. These categories were not interchangeable, and they did not displace older legal principles about sovereignty.

The contrast with Nicaragua, El Salvador, and Guatemala clarifies what was distinctive. The prior chapters examined prolonged civil wars, revolutionary coalitions, counterinsurgency, human-rights catastrophes, negotiations, and electoral transitions shaped by domestic conflict and international pressure. Panama's December military confrontation was shorter and more decisively dominated by a direct U.S. assault on state security institutions. Yet the preceding Panamanian political crisis had also lasted years, and the consequences were not over when the principal military objectives were achieved. Comparing the cases therefore requires common questions about civilian rights and institutions while recognizing different forms of violence and intervention.

Several narratives competed to define what had happened. One emphasized the liberation of a population denied its electoral choice. Another condemned a major power for using armed force against a smaller state, thereby endangering a principle vital to Latin American independence. A third centered on the destruction of El Chorrillo and the insufficiency of civilian protection. These readings need not exclude one another: an elected opposition's legitimacy, a criminal ruler's removal, the violation of civilian rights, and a disputed international legal basis can coexist. Strong history assigns each proposition to the evidence appropriate to it rather than demanding a single verdict upon every aspect of the event.

Panamanians were not passive recipients of a new order. Civic organizations, voters, journalists, religious institutions, professional groups, and security reformers had challenged the regime before foreign troops entered. Their action created the political context in which Endara could plausibly claim a democratic mandate. At the same time, communities harmed by the invasion continued to contest official accounts and seek accountability. Their persistence shaped historical memory and made later human-rights findings possible. The history belongs as much to those political and social actors as to Noriega, Bush, and senior military commanders.

Chapter 92 closes Volume IV without suggesting that democracy was an inevitable destination. The military's fall made electoral and constitutional rule possible, but questions about inequality, organized crime, foreign influence, and institutional capacity remained. The later decades of Latin American history would include democratic openings alongside debt crises, market reforms, new criminal networks, social movements, and renewed struggles over state legitimacy. Panama offers a precise lesson about the transition between eras: the end of one authoritarian order can open a new political framework without resolving the competing demands of sovereignty, security, justice, and ordinary life.

Cause and Effect

From Regime Crisis to Intervention and Reconstruction

Long-term causes: exceptional canal jurisdiction, military dominance since 1968, the concentration of intelligence authority and prior U.S.–Noriega cooperation.

Intermediate changes: Spadafora's killing, the 1987 civic mobilization, drug indictments, sanctions, the failed 1989 election and the October officers' revolt.

Immediate triggers: the December war declaration and confrontations involving U.S. personnel, including Lieutenant Paz's death, followed by the 17 December presidential decision.

Immediate effects: defeat and dissolution of PDF command structures, Endara's assumption of authority, Noriega's flight, and civilian deaths and displacement.

Long-term consequences: U.S. prosecution, civilian security reform, 1994 constitutional changes, canal transfer, and unresolved human-rights claims. No single grievance explains every policy choice or its unequal social costs.

Continuity and Change

1903–1999: Three Different Clocks of Change

Sovereignty clock: a 1903 concession gave way to 1977 treaty reform, the 1979 end of the Zone and 1999 completion of canal transfer.

Political clock: 1968 military rule, Noriega's 1983 command, the 1989 electoral crisis, and postinvasion civilian government moved on a separate schedule.

Social clock: racialized canal labor hierarchies, inequalities of urban housing, and the long effects of civilian loss changed unevenly and did not vanish when regimes fell.

Why it matters: Historians should not mistake the success of any one constitutional or diplomatic transition for the full realization of democratic rights, equitable development or reparative justice.

Chapter Summary

Panama’s late-twentieth-century crisis rested on much older disputes over canal sovereignty. The republic’s separation from Colombia and the 1903 treaty gave the United States exceptional jurisdiction over an internationally important corridor. Canal employment and commerce also produced distinctive social hierarchies and communities. The January 1964 flag conflict made the demand for a new treaty central to Panamanian public life.

The National Guard coup of October 1968 transferred effective political power to the military. Omar Torrijos combined reform and an assertive nationalist program with restrictions on opposition and civil liberties. His diplomatic achievement was the 1977 treaty settlement, which ended the old Canal Zone regime in 1979 and scheduled full canal transfer for the last day of 1999.

The two Torrijos–Carter treaties were not interchangeable. One established the transition of operations and jurisdiction; the other addressed permanent canal neutrality. Their 1977 interpretation rejected reading canal protection as a general license for intervention in Panama’s domestic affairs. This distinction later became indispensable to analyzing the invasion.

Manuel Noriega consolidated power within security institutions shaped under the earlier military government. His influence depended on intelligence, promotions, coercion and the ability to control civilian presidents. His external intelligence relationships and the emergence of cocaine-trafficking evidence need to be reconstructed chronologically without treating accusation, intelligence reporting and courtroom conviction as the same kind of proof.

The disputed 1984 election and the killing of Hugo Spadafora in September 1985 revealed weaknesses in electoral and judicial accountability. Barletta’s removal showed that civilian leaders could not independently investigate the military. The 1987 Civic Crusade transformed dissent into broad mobilization, although political, social and economic interests within the opposition remained varied.

Federal indictments and financial restrictions in 1988 escalated the crisis without dislodging Noriega. Economic pressure operated through banking and government payments and affected ordinary households as well as the intended regime. American agencies and regional diplomats pursued different strategies, while the OAS criticized political repression and remained concerned about intervention.

The general election was held on 7 May 1989; the Electoral Tribunal annulled it on 10 May. Contemporary investigations and polling records indicated a substantial opposition victory. The attempted officers’ coup of 3 October failed. Escalating confrontations culminated in the death of Lieutenant Robert Paz on 16 December and Bush’s authorization of invasion on 17 December.

Operation Just Cause began on 20 December, rapidly overcoming most organized PDF resistance and enabling Endara’s government. Military success did not remove the obligation to account for civilian harm, particularly in El Chorrillo. Official and independent civilian-fatality estimates differed, and the absence of a fully accepted forensic inquiry limited precision.

The UN General Assembly condemned the intervention in Resolution 44/240 on 29 December 1989. The later IACHR merits findings identified U.S. responsibility for specified civilian injuries, deaths and property losses and for inadequate humanitarian precautions. These institutional acts differ in authority and subject matter; they should not be confused with a single binding judgment from an international court.

Noriega surrendered on 3 January 1990 and was convicted on eight U.S. federal charges on 9 April 1992. Panama reconstructed civilian institutions and constitutionally abolished the standing army in 1994. Completion of the canal transfer in 1999 reflected the durability of the 1977 agreement. The chapter’s lasting argument is that democratic legitimacy, sovereignty, civilian protection and criminal accountability are related but independently testable historical questions.

Chapter Reflection

The history of Panama reveals that sovereignty is a process sustained by treaties, institutions, workplaces, neighborhoods and collective memory—not simply a statement in a constitution. The canal corridor gave the United States extraordinary leverage, but Panamanian citizens repeatedly challenged the terms on which that leverage was exercised. The 1964 protests and 1977 negotiations remind students that small states possess political agency even within unequal relationships.

It also reveals the difficulty of distinguishing national defense from the defense of a ruling institution. Torrijos secured important gains while restricting political competition. Noriega inherited an apparatus that could claim to protect independence while suppressing elections and detaining opponents. The civilian campaign against military domination was Panamanian, even when foreign governments tried to influence its outcome.

The December 1989 operation warns against using a single standard of success. Removing a corrupt and coercive ruler, obtaining a criminal conviction, restoring elections and honoring a canal timetable were meaningful events. None of them eliminates the independent question of whether international force was permitted or civilians were adequately protected. Those killed, injured or displaced deserve a place in the record independent of the political fate of the ruler.

Volume V begins where this chapter leaves off: civilian institutions face a changing economy, international finance, drug networks, new social movements and demands for accountability. Panama’s history suggests that the end of the Cold War did not abolish the problems of external influence, inequality or the capacity of states to use force. It changed the terms in which those problems would be argued, governed and remembered.
