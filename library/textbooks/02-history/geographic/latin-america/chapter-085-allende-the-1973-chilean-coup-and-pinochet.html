Chapter Opening: A Constitutional Experiment and Its Violent End

On the morning of 11 September 1973, the president of Chile was inside the presidential palace while aircraft of his own country's air force prepared to attack it. By evening, a four-man military junta ruled a republic that had changed governments by election for most of the twentieth century. Seventeen years later, the same country would end that dictatorship through a vote the dictator himself had written into the rules.

This chapter studies one of the most consequential political sequences in modern Latin America: the election of Salvador Allende in 1970, his attempt to pursue socialism through existing constitutional institutions, the military coup that overthrew him, the dictatorship of General Augusto Pinochet from 1973 to 1990, and the process by which Chileans voted that regime out of office. It spans roughly 1964 to 1990, with its narrative weight on 1970–1973 and 1973–1990.

Chile mattered beyond its size. It had a strong party system, a mobilized labor movement, an economy that depended on copper, and a reputation, accurate in many respects, for constitutional continuity. Allende's government was therefore watched across the hemisphere as a test of whether a Marxist-led coalition could transform property relations without abandoning electoral democracy. Its overthrow was read in opposite ways: as proof that the road was closed, or as the rescue of a country from economic chaos and political violence. Historians treat both readings as claims to be examined, not conclusions to be adopted.

The chapter's central question is how Chile moved from an elected socialist-led government to military dictatorship, why that dictatorship transformed both the state and the economy so radically, and how Chileans eventually compelled a transition to elected civilian government. Answering it requires holding several stories together: a polarized democracy, an economy under strain, a conspiracy within the armed forces, covert and overt pressure from the United States, a system of state terror, a free-market revolution carried out without electoral consent, and a patient reconstruction of opposition through churches, unions, neighborhood groups, women's organizations, and eventually political parties.

The chapter rests on three interpretive principles. First, Allende's government must be judged within its actual constitutional and economic circumstances, not through hindsight. Second, Pinochet's dictatorship must be distinguished both from the democratic crisis that preceded it and from other South American military regimes, including Brazil's, which Chapter 84 examined. Third, economic reform, repression, resistance, and democratization must be analyzed together, because each shaped the others.

Evidence is unusually rich and unusually contested. Declassified United States documents, including those compiled in the Foreign Relations of the United States series and the 1975 Senate report on covert action in Chile, document American covert efforts in 1970 and afterward. Chilean sources include the laws and decree laws of the period, the 1991 Rettig Report and the 2004 Valech Report, the archives of the Vicaría de la Solidaridad, court records generated decades later, newspapers and magazines, film, music, and thousands of testimonies. Each source type has its own purpose and limits, and the chapter's primary-source windows are designed to show how.

The chapter's boundaries are deliberate. Brazil's twenty-one-year regime is treated in Chapter 84 and appears here only through comparison. Argentina's dictatorship belongs to Chapter 86, and the full multinational apparatus of Operation Condor to Chapter 87. Chilean human-rights organizations receive full domestic coverage here, while regional advocacy networks belong to Chapter 88. The chapter ends in March 1990 with the restoration of civilian presidential government; the longer history of transitional justice belongs to Chapter 94.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Chile's party system, copper economy, and reform politics under Eduardo Frei shaped the setting for Allende's election.
  2. Describe the Popular Unity coalition and the constitutional process by which Allende became president in 1970.
  3. Distinguish United States covert action aimed at preventing Allende's inauguration in 1970 from later American policy, and from the Chilean decisions of 1973.
  4. Analyze Popular Unity's economic and social program, including copper nationalization, and the divisions within the coalition over its pace.
  5. Trace how inflation, shortages, strikes, and mobilization from both left and right escalated the crisis of 1971–1973.
  6. Reconstruct the events of 11 September 1973 and distinguish contemporaneous claims from later forensic and judicial findings.
  7. Explain how Pinochet consolidated personal authority within the military government and how the junta justified its legitimacy.
  8. Assess the methods, institutions, and victims of repression, and interpret the different mandates of Chile's truth commissions.
  9. Evaluate the 1980 Constitution as an instrument of authoritarian institutionalization rather than democratic restoration.
  10. Analyze Chile's neoliberal restructuring using quantitative evidence, including the recessions of 1975 and 1982.
  11. Explain how protest, opposition coalitions, and the 1988 plebiscite brought a negotiated transition, and why institutional legacies persisted after March 1990.

Historical Context

Chile entered the 1960s as a long, narrow republic of about nine million people, urbanized and highly literate by regional standards. Its political system had been shaped by the 1925 Constitution, which established a presidential republic with a Congress that played a real role. Since the 1930s, the party system had divided roughly into thirds: a Right of Liberals and Conservatives (merged in 1966 into the National Party), a Center of Radicals and, after 1957, Christian Democrats, and a Left of Socialists and Communists. Presidents were commonly elected with pluralities, and Congress had traditionally ratified the candidate who finished first.

The economy depended heavily on copper. The large mines of the "Gran Minería" were controlled by United States firms, notably Anaconda and Kennecott, and copper supplied the majority of export earnings, making public finances vulnerable to world prices. Land ownership was highly concentrated, and rural labor on large estates, including inquilinos (resident tenants), was governed by dependence that reformers of several parties wanted to end. Industrialization since the 1930s had created an urban working class and a trade-union movement, with the Central Única de Trabajadores (CUT) founded in 1953 and led by Socialists and Communists.

Chile also lay within the Cold War's hemispheric politics. The Cuban Revolution (Chapters 80–82) transformed debates about reform and revolution, and the United States, through the Alliance for Progress and covert funding, had already intervened in Chilean elections in 1964 to prevent an Allende victory. Indigenous Mapuche communities in the south, historically dispossessed of land, were also drawn into debates over agrarian reform. These conditions meant that when Allende finally won in 1970, his government would operate under intense domestic and international scrutiny.

Students should avoid two simplifications. One treats pre-1970 Chile as a placid democracy that radical politics destroyed. The other treats it as a society whose breakdown was inevitable. Chile's institutions were real and had absorbed major conflicts; they were also under growing strain from competing programs of reform that left little room for centrist compromise.

Section 85.1

Chilean Democracy, Eduardo Frei, Agrarian Reform, and the Road to 1970

The election of 1964 set the terms for what followed. Eduardo Frei Montalva, the candidate of the Christian Democratic Party, won with a majority of about 56 percent, helped by the decision of the Right, frightened by the prospect of an Allende victory, to support him, and by substantial covert United States funding that later investigations documented. Allende, running for the third time, received roughly 39 percent. Frei promised a "Revolution in Liberty," a program of reform meant to show that deep social change was possible without Marxism and without dictatorship.

The reforms were substantial. Frei's government expanded primary schooling and housing programs, organized neighborhood associations and mothers' centers (centros de madres) through its "Popular Promotion" initiative, and legalized rural unions in 1967. Its copper policy, called "Chileanization," bought a controlling state share in the large mines while leaving American firms as partners and promising expanded output. Its most contentious measure was agrarian reform. A 1967 law, built on a constitutional amendment, allowed the state to expropriate large and inefficient estates with compensation, and the government expropriated well over a thousand properties before 1970, settling peasants in cooperative-style units called asentamientos.

Reform produced opposition from both flanks. Landowners and the National Party argued that expropriation violated property rights and that rural unions were politicizing the countryside. The Left argued that Frei's measures were too slow, too dependent on foreign capital, and too ready to use force against strikes and land seizures; the killing of striking miners at El Salvador in 1966 and of settlers in Puerto Montt in 1969 became symbols of that charge. Within the Christian Democratic Party itself, a left wing split off in 1969 to form the Movement of Unitary Popular Action (MAPU), which later joined Allende.

This background matters for two reasons. First, significant social reform was underway before Allende; the argument in 1970 was less about whether to change Chile than about how far, how fast, and under whose leadership. Second, the Christian Democratic Party had defined itself as an alternative to both Right and Left, which left it, by 1970, without natural allies. The political center that had anchored earlier governments was thinning, and the three-way contest of that year, in which no candidate could expect a majority, was the product.

The Mapuche South illustrates the reach of reform. Under Frei and then Allende, Mapuche communities in Cautín and neighboring provinces pressed to recover lands lost since the nineteenth-century occupation of the Araucanía, sometimes through occupations called corridas de cerco. A 1972 Indigenous law under Allende recognized communal rights more fully than earlier legislation. These claims were part of the reform politics of the period, not a separate story, and their later reversal under Pinochet (Section 85.15) belongs to the same history.

Section 85.2

Salvador Allende, Popular Unity, the 1970 Election, and the Constitutional Path to Socialism

Popular Unity was formed in late 1969 as an alliance of the Socialist and Communist Parties with the Radicals, MAPU, and smaller groups, with a later addition, the Christian Left, in 1971. Allende was a physician from Valparaíso, a founder of the Socialist Party, a long-time senator, and a former minister of health. His coalition combined an enormously disciplined Communist Party, which favored gradual change and alliances with the middle sectors, with a Socialist Party that had grown more radical and which many of its members identified with a revolutionary strategy, though it too participated in elections. This internal tension shaped the government from its first day.

The coalition's program promised to replace what it called the dominance of foreign capital, monopolies, and the landed oligarchy with a "people's government." It proposed three sectors of the economy: a social sector of nationalized enterprises, including copper, banks, and large industry; a mixed sector; and a private sector of small and medium firms. It also promised agrarian reform, expanded health and education, and a new constitution that would add a popular assembly to the state. Its stated method was electoral: to win the presidency, use legal authority to carry out the program, and then seek wider legitimacy.

The presidential election of 4 September 1970 was a three-way race. Allende received about 36.2 percent, the former president Jorge Alessandri of the National Party about 34.9 percent, and the Christian Democratic candidate Radomiro Tomic about 27.8 percent. Since no one had a majority, the Constitution assigned the choice between the top two to a joint session of Congress. By convention, Congress had always chosen the candidate with the largest vote, but nothing in the text required it, and in the weeks that followed the question of whether it would do so became a national and international crisis.

The Christian Democrats, whose votes were decisive, agreed to support Allende in exchange for a Statute of Constitutional Guarantees, a constitutional amendment promising to respect the independence of the armed forces and the press, the pluralism of political parties and trade unions, and education. Congress ratified Allende on 24 October 1970 by a large majority. Whatever else he did, Allende became president through the rules of the existing constitutional order, and any account that describes him as an unelected ruler is wrong.

Meanwhile, the United States pursued two covert paths. Track I used diplomatic and economic pressure and encouraged political maneuvers to block ratification. Track II, authorized by President Richard Nixon on 15 September 1970, directed the Central Intelligence Agency to explore a military coup, even though the agency's own officers and the American ambassador, Edward Korry, doubted it would succeed. CIA officers contacted Chilean military plotters. One group, led by retired General Roberto Viaux, and another, linked to General Camilo Valenzuela, planned to kidnap Army Commander René Schneider, who upheld the military's constitutional neutrality. Schneider was shot on 22 October and died on 25 October. The 1975 U.S. Senate investigation distinguished the two groups: CIA-supplied submachine guns went to officers associated with the Valenzuela plot, while Chilean courts attributed the fatal October 22 kidnapping attempt to participants in the Viaux conspiracy. The inquiry did not find that the CIA provided guns were used to shoot Schneider; it established extensive U.S. involvement in coup plotting, not a U.S. order to murder him. The CIA later reported the Valenzuela weapons were returned unused. (U.S. Senate inquiry; declassified document collection.)

The result was the opposite of what the plotters wanted: Schneider's death deepened the political and military commitment to constitutional procedure, at least for the moment. It is important to be precise. The 1970 evidence is explicit about American intent to prevent Allende from taking office. It does not show that Washington directed the military's decision in 1973, which depended on Chilean officers, parties, and organizations. After inauguration, American policy shifted to economic pressure and covert support for opposition groups, subjects taken up in the following sections.

The Popular Unity Government Program (1969–1970)

Context: Drafted by the coalition parties in late 1969 and published as the basis for Allende's 1970 campaign; a digitized edition is held by Memoria Chilena of the National Library of Chile. Source type: A political party program, composed to unite parties and persuade voters.

Editorial paraphrase: The program describes Chile as dominated by foreign capital, domestic monopolies, and a landed elite, and proposes to end that dominance by a "people's government" that would nationalize basic resources and large firms, organize a three-sector economy, carry out agrarian reform, expand social services, and replace the existing constitution with a new one including a popular assembly. CIA Headquarters Cable to Santiago, 16 October 1970

Source type: A classified operational communication from agency headquarters to its station.

Source record: FRUS vol. XXI, document 154: CIA headquarters telegram, 16 October 1970 · This window is an attributed editorial paraphrase/summary, not a source-facsimile transcription.

Editorial paraphrase: The cable instructs the Santiago station that the policy of the American government is to see Allende overthrown by a coup, directs it to continue contacts with Chilean military officers, and asks that the American role be concealed.

  2. Why might an official wish to conceal a government's hand in a coup effort?

  4. Which Chilean perspectives are absent from a U.S. internal communication?
Section 85.3

Copper Nationalization, Agrarian Reform, Popular Unity Economics, and Social Policy

Allende's first year was the government's most successful. Its economic team, led by Pedro Vuskovic, pursued expansion: raising wages and salaries, freezing prices, increasing public spending, and using idle industrial capacity to meet increased demand. Real wages rose sharply in 1971, unemployment fell to historic lows, and the economy grew rapidly, roughly 8 percent by most estimates. Programs distributed free milk to children, expanded health services in poor neighborhoods, and extended literacy and adult education. Municipal elections in April 1971 gave the coalition about half the national vote, its best result.

The program's structural core was transfer of ownership. The government bought controlling shares in banks and in large textile and other firms, and used a 1932 decree to requisition enterprises struck by workers or in dispute, a controversial use of what critics called legal loopholes (resquicios legales). By 1972 the state controlled most banks and a large share of industrial output. The state sector grew from a few dozen firms to several hundred. Many enterprises were taken over not by plan but by worker occupations that the government then legalized, a pattern that produced both mobilization and chaos.

Agrarian reform accelerated. Building on Frei's law, the government expropriated far more estates in three years than its predecessor had in six, bringing the combined total to roughly ten million hectares across both governments and ending the large private estate as the dominant form of rural property. The speed brought conflicts: seizures by peasants and Mapuche communities outpaced the legal process, estate owners resisted, and agricultural production declined, adding to food shortages.

The central act was the nationalization of large-scale copper mining. In July 1971, Congress approved a constitutional reform, Law No. 17,450, which declared the state the owner of the nation's mineral resources and nationalized the Gran Minería. It passed unanimously, with the votes of Allende's opponents, because copper nationalization had been a goal of nearly every major party since Frei's Chileanization. What was contested was compensation. The law instructed the Comptroller General to deduct "excessive profits" earned since 1955 from the amounts owed to the companies, and, in 1972, the government decided that Anaconda and Kennecott owed Chile more than their property was worth. The firms rejected the finding, pursued claims abroad, and received the support of their government, which broadened its economic pressure on Chile.

Popular Unity was not united about all this. The Communist Party and Allende favored consolidating gains, limiting expropriations, and seeking an agreement with the Christian Democrats, since the coalition lacked a congressional majority. The Socialist Party's leadership, with the Christian Left, MAPU, and especially the Movement of the Revolutionary Left (MIR), which remained outside the coalition, wanted to advance rapidly, arguing that compromise would stall the revolution and that mass mobilization would defend it. Allende, whose own position was closer to the first view, depended on both. The distinction between legislative achievement and implementation is crucial: the law passed, but the state lacked the managers, the foreign exchange, the data, or the discipline to run hundreds of firms smoothly.

Historical Vocabulary I
Popular Unity, constitutional socialism, nationalization, agrarian reform, polarization

Popular Unity (Unidad Popular) was the coalition of left and center-left parties supporting Allende. Constitutional socialism, the phrase historians use for its strategy, meant seeking to transfer major means of production to the state through elections, laws, and existing institutions rather than armed seizure of power; participants differed on whether that was achievable.

Nationalization is the transfer of private property, here mines, banks, and firms, to state ownership, with or without compensation. Agrarian reform is the redistribution of rural land, usually by expropriating large estates. Polarization describes the narrowing of political life into two hostile camps, with centrists squeezed. Students should remember that all these terms were contested by the actors who used them.

Section 85.4

Inflation, Shortages, Popular Mobilization, and the Growing Political Crisis, 1971–1972

The expansion of 1971 contained the seeds of 1972. Wage increases and price controls raised consumption faster than output. Public deficits grew, financed largely by printing money. Copper prices fell, foreign-exchange reserves were exhausted, and foreign credit, previously plentiful, tightened as American banks and agencies reduced lending, a pressure that American officials later described as part of a deliberate effort to make the economy "scream," though the effect of this "invisible blockade" is difficult to separate from Chile's own policy choices. Imports of food and parts became harder to finance, and inflation, which had been about 22 percent in 1971, passed 100 percent in 1972 by most estimates.

Shortages became visible in daily life. Price controls encouraged hoarding and a black market; lines formed for staples such as cooking oil and sugar. The government responded with neighborhood Supply and Price Committees (JAP) to distribute goods, which supporters saw as popular self-organization and opponents as partisan rationing. Whatever the precise mix of causes, the shortages shifted the political balance. Middle-class households and small shopkeepers, who had not been targets of the program, increasingly saw themselves as its victims.

Women were central to this politics on both sides. In December 1971, thousands of women, many from affluent neighborhoods, marched in Santiago banging empty pots in the "March of the Empty Pots," an event organized by opposition groups and celebrated by the opposition press. Their movement, Poder Femenino, became a major vehicle of anti-government mobilization. At the same time, women were among the activists in neighborhood committees, unions, and the Mothers' Centers, and in poor poblaciones they organized supply and food distribution. Women's politics in the period cannot be reduced to one side.

The decisive confrontation of 1972 was the October stoppage. It began on 9 October when the Confederation of Truck Owners, led by León Vilarín, struck over a plan to create a state trucking enterprise in the far south, and it widened into a national shutdown joined by shopkeepers, professionals, and some bank and transport workers. It lasted about three and a half weeks and paralyzed distribution. Business organizations, opposition parties, and professional guilds supported it. American covert funds supported opposition parties and media during this period; investigators in 1975 found no conclusive evidence that the CIA directly financed the strike, though money flowed to groups that supported it.

The strike produced a counter-mobilization from below. Workers in the industrial belts of Santiago, notably Cerrillos-Maipú and Vicuña Mackenna, kept factories running and organized cordones industriales, coordinating committees of factory workers that connected workplaces and distributed goods. The cordones were, for the revolutionary Left, a model of popular power; for the Communists and parts of the government, a risk of duplicating institutions and alienating allies; for the Right, evidence of revolutionary intentions. Their actual size and significance are debated, but they reveal that the Left itself was divided over how far to rely on legal authority.

Allende resolved the strike by bringing military officers into the cabinet in November 1972, including Army Commander General Carlos Prats as interior minister. This ended the stoppage but it also established a new precedent: the military as arbiter of the government's survival. By the end of 1972, a constitutional experiment had become a standoff in which each side could point to the other's violations and each saw its own actions as defense. The evidence supports neither a story of inevitable failure nor one of uncomplicated success; it shows a government that had achieved major structural changes and was losing control of its economic management and its political coalition.

Democratic Breakdown, the Coup, and the Construction of Military Rule

How a constitutional republic was forcibly replaced by military government, and how the regime concentrated power while suppressing organized opposition.

Section 85.5

Constitutional Conflict, Military Conspiracy, and the Final Crisis of 1973

The congressional elections of 4 March 1973 were the last test of public opinion. The opposition coalition, the Confederation of Democracy (CODE), hoped to win the two-thirds majority needed to impeach Allende. It did not: it won about 55 percent of the vote to Popular Unity's roughly 44 percent, a result that fell short of impeachment but also showed that the government's support had grown since 1970, despite inflation. Both sides drew opposite conclusions. The government argued that it retained a popular mandate; the opposition argued that a majority opposed it and that only extra-parliamentary means remained. Compromise became harder.

Constitutional conflict now moved to the institutions. Congress and the executive fought over a bill defining the three property sectors, and Allende used vetoes and administrative decrees that the opposition called unlawful. The Comptroller General and the Supreme Court sided with Congress in disputes over requisitions, and in May 1973 the Supreme Court sent Allende a letter accusing the government of undermining judicial authority. The opposition-controlled Chamber of Deputies declared the government's conduct a breach of the Constitution. Chileans on both sides invoked legality, and each denied the other's claim to it.

Paramilitary violence rose. The extreme-right group Fatherland and Liberty (Patria y Libertad) carried out sabotage and bombings, while elements of the revolutionary Left armed workers and organized in the cordones. Political killings, including the assassination of Allende's naval aide-de-camp, Captain Arturo Araya, in July, heightened the sense of breakdown. A second truck owners' strike began in late July and further paralyzed transport, and prices, shortages, and the black market worsened. Annual inflation by mid-1973 was in the hundreds of percent by most series, though figures vary.

The armed forces were the key. On 29 June, Lieutenant Colonel Roberto Souper led an armored regiment against La Moneda in what became known as the Tanquetazo. General Prats, commanding forces loyal to the government, put it down within hours, and it ended without a broader uprising. It nonetheless revealed that officers were prepared to use force, and it served as a rehearsal. In the following weeks, the Navy's leadership, with Admiral José Toribio Merino, arrested sailors accused of plotting with the Left, and detainees reported torture, which Allende's government did not effectively investigate.

On 22 August the Chamber of Deputies approved a resolution, by about 81 votes to 47, declaring that the government had systematically violated the Constitution and calling on the military ministers to restore constitutional order. The resolution did not call for a coup in its text, but many Chileans, and later historians, read it as an invitation. The next day, General Prats resigned both as minister and as army commander, after a demonstration by officers' wives outside his house and the loss of support among his generals. He recommended that General Augusto Pinochet, the senior officer after him, succeed him. Allende named Pinochet on 23 August, believing him to be loyal.

Efforts at compromise failed. The Church, through Cardinal Raúl Silva Henríquez, mediated talks between Allende and Patricio Aylwin, the Christian Democratic Party president, in August. The Christian Democrats demanded that Allende change his cabinet, rein in the cordones and occupations, and respect Congress. Allende, for his part, was under pressure from his own coalition not to concede. Many accounts report that Allende intended to propose a plebiscite on the government's future, but he never announced it. Historians disagree about how much room remained for compromise by September, and about whether the opposition leadership as a whole wanted compromise at all. What is clear is that by the end of August a majority of the military leadership had decided to act.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did Chilean Democracy Collapse, and How Should Domestic Conflict and U.S. Intervention Be Weighed?

The question: Was the collapse of 1973 chiefly the product of Chilean political and economic conflict, or of external pressure, and how should historians assign weight?

Interpretation A — Domestic breakdown: Scholars in the tradition of Arturo Valenzuela emphasize institutional and political dynamics: a presidential system that allowed a minority-vote president to govern without a congressional majority; a three-way party system with little tolerance for centrist coalition; the Christian Democrats' refusal to cooperate; economic mismanagement and mobilization that alarmed the middle classes; and a military that eventually concluded it must intervene. On this view, American actions were real but secondary to Chilean conflict.
Interpretation B — External pressure as a major factor: Scholars such as Peter Kornbluh, who analyzes declassified records, and Tanya Harmer, who places Allende's Chile in the inter-American Cold War, stress that Washington, Brazil, and others worked to isolate the government: covert funding to opposition parties and media, an economic squeeze on credit, and the 1970 coup plot. On this view, outside pressure widened the opposition's room to maneuver and strengthened military conspirators.

Evidence: The two readings use partly the same sources: declassified American cables and the 1975 Senate report, Chilean legislation and congressional records, military memoirs, economic series, and testimony. They differ over causation, since the documents show intent and support but cannot by themselves measure effect.

Why it matters: If domestic factors dominate, the lessons concern democratic institutions and political choice. If external pressure dominates, the lessons concern sovereignty and the limits of reform in the Cold War. Most current scholarship treats the two as interacting: Chilean actors made the decisions of 1973, within conditions that outside actors helped shape.

Section 85.6

September 11, 1973: The Military Coup, La Moneda, and the Death of Salvador Allende

The coup was a coordinated action of the Army, Navy, Air Force, and the Carabineros, the national police. It began in the port of Valparaíso, where naval units took control early on the morning of 11 September. By around seven o'clock, Allende, alerted, had gone to La Moneda in Santiago with a group of bodyguards and a few advisers, having learned of the rising by telephone. Radio communications among the commanders, later recorded and published, show the plotters coordinating the capture of key positions and expressing concern about whether Allende would resign or leave the country.

The junta issued proclamations on stations it controlled, demanding Allende's resignation and giving him an offer of a flight out of the country, which he refused. Opposition and pro-government radio stations were shut or bombed, with the exception of Radio Magallanes, a Communist Party station, through which Allende broadcast his final address in the late morning. He thanked the workers, said he would not resign, predicted that the betrayers would be defeated, and said that history would be made by the people. The speech, discussed in the source window below, became one of the best-known documents of the period.

Ground units, including tanks, surrounded the palace, and at around eleven o'clock the army delivered an ultimatum. Allende refused to surrender. Shortly before noon, Hawker Hunter jets of the Air Force fired rockets into the building, setting it on fire. The president's residence on Tomás Moro Street was also attacked. Allende dismissed the women in the palace, including his daughters, and urged others to leave, while some of the bodyguards and others remained. At around two in the afternoon, soldiers entered the burning building. Allende died inside, in the Independence Hall, while it was under assault.

Rumors circulated for decades about how he died. The junta announced from the beginning that he had killed himself, and some supporters believed he was killed by soldiers. Official autopsies in 1973 concluded suicide, and the families and physicians who saw the body were divided in their early statements. In 2011, a Chilean judicial investigation, supported by an international forensic panel, reopened the case, exhumed the body, and again concluded that Allende died by suicide with a rifle during the military assault. That finding is accepted by Chilean courts and by most historians. It does not change the political responsibility of those who attacked the palace: Allende died because the armed forces overthrew him by force.

The constitutional order collapsed within hours. A four-man junta, composed of General Augusto Pinochet, Admiral José Toribio Merino, General Gustavo Leigh of the Air Force, and General César Mendoza of the Carabineros, took power that evening. A curfew was imposed. Ministers, officials, and many workers and activists were arrested. Soldiers raided homes, factories, universities, and poor neighborhoods, burned books, and destroyed records. Allende was buried quietly in Viña del Mar, without a public funeral; he was reburied with state honors in Santiago in 1990.

International reactions were divided. The Soviet Union and Cuba broke relations with the junta, as did several European and Latin American governments, while the United States, along with Brazil, recognized it quickly. The 1975 Senate report found no evidence of direct American involvement in the coup itself, but it documented prior covert support for opposition groups and awareness of the military's plotting. The coup thus had Chilean authors and a hemispheric context, and the two should be kept analytically separate.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Military Coup of 11 September 1973

Long-term causes

Chile's three-way party system and weak centrist coalitions, copper dependence, land and income inequality, Cold War anti-communism in the armed forces and elites, and decades of reform politics.

Short-term causes

A minority-vote presidency, inflation and shortages, the strikes of 1972–73, escalating political violence, institutional deadlock, the failed Tanquetazo, the 22 August resolution, and American covert and economic pressure.

Event or transformation

A coordinated armed-forces uprising overthrows the constitutional government on 11 September 1973.

Immediate effects

Death of Allende, a four-man junta, curfew, mass detention, suppression of parties and unions, and international realignments.

Long-term consequences

Seventeen years of military rule, systematic repression, a free-market economic revolution, a new constitution, exile for tens of thousands, and long-lasting disputes over memory and justice.

Historical complexity

Economic crisis, polarization, and external pressure do not by themselves explain the coup; Chilean military officers and civilian supporters made choices, and other outcomes, including negotiation, were possible.
Section 85.7

The Military Junta, Rule by Decree, and Pinochet's Consolidation of Personal Authority

The junta's first act, Decree Law No. 1 of 11 September 1973, declared that the commanders of the armed forces and the Carabineros had assumed supreme command of the nation and formed a junta to restore what it described as national identity, justice, and institutional order. It stated that the junta would guarantee the independence of the judiciary and respect the Constitution and the laws to the extent that the situation of the country permitted, a formula that gave the junta the authority to override any of them. The document is useful because it reveals the junta's claim of legitimacy: it presented itself as a rescuer of the nation, not as a new regime.

The junta rapidly dissolved the institutions of civilian politics. It declared Congress closed and later abolished it, banned Marxist parties and put other parties into "recess," a status that became prohibition in 1977, burned the electoral registers, and placed mayors, university rectors, and the leadership of public agencies in military hands. It suspended the 1925 Constitution except where convenient and governed by decree laws, which had the force of law without any legislative process. It imposed press censorship and closed or seized opposition newspapers and radio stations. Military tribunals, acting under what the junta called a "state of internal war," replaced civilian courts for many political offenses, and civilian courts for the most part declined to challenge the junta.

The junta justified itself with the national-security doctrine, an outlook taught in the region's military schools, which defined the main threat to the nation as an internal enemy, Marxism, and made the armed forces responsible for eliminating it. It also circulated a document known as "Plan Z," alleging that the Left had planned a mass killing of officers; investigators later concluded that the claim lacked credible evidence. The junta's White Book of the Change of Government reinforced the argument that the coup had prevented a civil war.

Pinochet began as one among four. Although a rotation of the junta presidency was initially contemplated, it never occurred: Decree Law No. 1 designated Pinochet president of the junta on 11 September 1973. Decree Law No. 527 (17 June 1974, published 26 June) established his position as Supreme Chief of the Nation, and Decree Law No. 806 (17 December 1974) gave him the title President of the Republic. These distinctions mark the progressive formalization of his personal authority. The consolidation reflected his control of the army, the largest service, and his skill at manipulating rivals. It also reflected the junta's divisions: the Air Force commander, General Leigh, objected to the army's dominance, to the DINA, and to the economic policy of the Chicago Boys. After a public interview in July 1978 in which he criticized Pinochet's plan for a long-term regime, Leigh was dismissed and replaced by General Fernando Matthei. The remaining members were loyal enough that the junta became primarily a legislative body under Pinochet's authority.

This personalization is one of the features that distinguishes Chile from Brazil, where the presidency rotated among generals chosen by the military hierarchy and a limited party system continued. In Chile, the regime was identified with one man who held command of the army for the entire period. It also explains the regime's staying power: Pinochet could balance services, civilians, and technocrats, and there was no scheduled succession. These contrasts are developed in the comparative study later in the chapter.

Historical Vocabulary II
Military junta, national-security doctrine, DINA, CNI, enforced disappearance

A military junta is a governing committee of senior officers that rules in place of civilian authority, here the commanders of the Army, Navy, Air Force, and Carabineros. The national-security doctrine was an ideology, taught in Latin American military schools in the Cold War, which defined the main threat to the state as internal subversion and justified wide military power to eliminate it.

The DINA (Dirección de Inteligencia Nacional) was the secret police established in 1974; the CNI (Central Nacional de Informaciones) replaced it in 1977. Enforced disappearance is the arrest or abduction of a person by state agents followed by refusal to acknowledge the detention or reveal the person's fate. The category is distinct from execution, detention, and torture, though many victims experienced several.

Section 85.8

The First Years of Repression: Mass Detention, Executions, and the Destruction of Political Opposition

The most intense killings took place in the first months. Tens of thousands of people were detained, and records from later truth commissions suggest that a majority of the deaths and detentions recorded took place in 1973 and 1974. Detention sites included the National Stadium in Santiago, where several thousand people were held at times, the Chile Stadium, ships and naval bases, regimental barracks, and camps in remote locations such as Chacabuco in the northern desert, Pisagua, and Dawson Island in the far south. Prisoners included union leaders, party activists, government officials, students, professors, and ordinary residents of the poblaciones and rural estates. Foreigners, including refugees from other Latin American dictatorships, were also targeted.

Torture and executions occurred in the stadiums and barracks. The singer and theater director Víctor Jara, a supporter of Allende, was arrested at the Technical University, held in the Chile Stadium, and killed between 15 and 16 September; the stadium was later renamed in his honor. Decades later, a Chilean court convicted former military personnel in 2018; subsequent proceedings culminated in Supreme Court decisions in August 2023 upholding final convictions for the killings of Jara and Littré Quiroga. Jara's case became a global symbol, but it represents one of thousands whose names and stories are less known, including peasants killed on estates, workers killed at factories, and members of the Mapuche community killed in the South.

The "Caravan of Death" in October 1973 shows how the regime handled prisoners. General Sergio Arellano Stark, acting as a delegate of Pinochet, traveled by helicopter to cities in the north and south, and officers under his authority, with local commanders, removed and killed prisoners who had been held or sentenced under the military's own proceedings. Estimates of the number killed range from the seventies to nearly a hundred; some bodies were hidden for decades. The case later became an important test of judicial accountability.

The repression had a plan. Parties of the Left were banned, their leaders hunted, and their organizations dismantled. Armed resistance existed, from groups such as the MIR and members of the Socialist Party who fought, and some junta supporters were killed; but the evidence shows that most victims of the first years were unarmed civilians, and that the regime's category of "extremist" covered political activists and union members rather than combatants. The MIR's leader, Miguel Enríquez, was killed in a confrontation with DINA agents in October 1974. Women were among those detained, and later investigations documented sexual violence against women in custody. Michelle Bachelet, later a president of Chile, was detained with her mother at Villa Grimaldi in 1975, after her father, General Alberto Bachelet, who had remained loyal to Allende, died in custody in 1974.

The system included forced exile. Thousands of people were released conditionally and expelled, others were forced to leave, and some were banned from returning. Families were separated, and in many cases children grew up abroad. Meanwhile, relatives began a search that became the foundation of the human-rights movement. They went from stadium to barracks to morgue, and asked courts to compel the state to tell them where their relatives were.

Section 85.9

DINA, CNI, Secret Detention Centers, Torture, and Enforced Disappearance

The regime's repression passed from improvised violence to institutional system with the creation of the National Intelligence Directorate. The DINA grew out of a secret group organized in late 1973 under Colonel Manuel Contreras, an army officer trained in intelligence, and was formalized by Decree Law 521 in June 1974. It answered directly to the junta and, in practice, to Pinochet. It had its own personnel, drawn from the armed forces and the police, its own budget, and power to arrest and hold people outside the normal system. Its agents operated in civilian clothes, in unmarked vehicles, and at night.

The DINA ran a network of clandestine detention centers. Londres 38, a former Socialist Party building in central Santiago, was used in 1973–1974; Villa Grimaldi, a country estate on the city's edge, served from 1974 as one of the largest, and many other sites existed in Santiago and the provinces. Survivors' testimonies, collected by the Vicaría and later by the Valech Commission, describe prolonged interrogation, electric shocks, beating, near-drowning, rape and sexual violence, mock executions, and the presence of medical personnel who monitored victims. Thousands passed through Villa Grimaldi alone, and several hundred never returned. At the same time, the DINA hid its victims: bodies were buried in secret graves, dumped at sea, or exhumed and concealed, as in a later operation to remove remains from earlier burial sites.

The agency also fabricated explanations. In 1975, in what became known as Operation Colombo, the regime's media published reports that more than a hundred missing Chileans had died in clashes among leftists abroad, a story constructed to explain away disappearances and later shown false. The DINA's targets shifted over time: first the MIR, then the Socialists, and then the Communist Party, which suffered heavy losses in 1976. Its work followed intelligence assessments of an enemy network and treated family members, friends, and neighbors as sources of information.

The DINA's activities abroad, including the assassinations discussed in Section 85.10, led to its replacement. After the Letelier case drew international pressure, the junta dissolved it in 1977 and created the CNI under Decree Law 1878. The CNI was nominally less autonomous and had less open killing, but it continued surveillance, detention, and torture, and its agents were involved in political killings in the 1980s.

Students must use the later truth commissions carefully. The National Commission on Truth and Reconciliation, created by President Aylwin in 1990 and known as the Rettig Commission, reported in 1991 and examined deaths and disappearances, recognizing 2,279 victims in its initial report, among them people killed by state agents and some killed in political violence. Later review bodies raised the total of recognized dead and disappeared to a little over three thousand. The Valech Commission, formally the National Commission on Political Imprisonment and Torture, reported in 2004 on a different category, survivors of political detention and torture. It recognized more than 27,000 people in its initial report, and a successor commission in 2010–2011 added thousands more, for a total of about 40,000 recognized. These numbers measure different things and rely on people coming forward and meeting the commissions' criteria, so they are likely minimum figures, and they should not be added together or treated as a single victim total.

Section 85.10

Exile, International Repression, the Letelier Assassination, and Chile's Foreign Relations

Exile reshaped Chilean society. Estimates of the number who left in the 1970s and 1980s start at about 200,000 and are sometimes higher, drawn from every class, though the political leadership, intellectuals, and union organizers were over-represented. Some were expelled, some fled embassies, and others left because their jobs or safety disappeared. Destinations included Argentina, until its own coup in 1976, then Mexico, Venezuela, Cuba, Sweden, Italy, France, the two Germanys, the Soviet Union, Canada, Australia, and the United States. Exile communities published newspapers, held concerts, and lobbied governments, and artists such as the musical groups Inti-Illimani and Quilapayún carried Chilean song abroad. Children raised in exile often returned in the late 1980s to a country they hardly knew.

The DINA pursued opponents abroad. In October 1975, a gunman in Rome wounded the Christian Democratic leader Bernardo Leighton and his wife, Anita Fresno. On 30 September 1974, General Carlos Prats and his wife, Sofía Cuthbert, were killed by a car bomb in Buenos Aires, where they had settled after his resignation; later investigations by Argentine and Chilean courts linked the DINA, through its agent Michael Townley, a United States citizen, to the killing. Prats had been a constitutionalist general and a potential rallying figure for dissident officers, which explains the regime's interest in him.

The most consequential attack came on 21 September 1976 in Washington, D.C. Orlando Letelier, who had been Allende's foreign minister and ambassador to the United States and was then working at a research institute, was killed by a bomb under his car on Embassy Row, and his colleague Ronni Moffitt, an American, died with him. Moffitt's husband Michael survived. The investigation by the FBI and American prosecutors led to Townley, who pleaded guilty and testified, and to Cuban exile accomplices. In Chile, the courts eventually convicted Contreras and his deputy Pedro Espinoza in 1993, after years of resistance by the regime and then by the military. The killing on American soil became a major international scandal and contributed to the DINA's dissolution.

These attacks belonged to a wider pattern in which South American security services cooperated across borders to locate, kidnap, and kill political opponents. In November 1975, Contreras hosted a meeting in Santiago of intelligence chiefs from the region, which is often cited as the founding of what became known as Operation Condor. The full multinational history of that system, including its relations with Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Bolivia, and Brazil, is reserved for Chapter 87. For the purposes of this chapter, the Chilean case shows that repression did not stop at the border.

Chile's diplomatic position deteriorated even as its economy was reoriented. The United Nations General Assembly repeatedly condemned the regime's human-rights record, and a special rapporteur investigated. Western European governments, Mexico, and others restricted relations, while the Soviet bloc and Cuba became centers of solidarity for the exiled Left. In the United States, Congress in 1976 restricted military assistance to Chile, and the Carter administration criticized the regime, though American business and security ties continued. In 1978 Chile and Argentina came close to war over three islands in the Beagle Channel, a conflict defused by papal mediation. The regime was isolated but not defeated, because it controlled the state, the economy was growing for a time, and Cold War alliances protected it from sanctions.

Section 85.11

The Catholic Church, Human-Rights Organizations, Families of the Disappeared, and Cultural Resistance

The earliest organized protection came from religious institutions. In October 1973, Cardinal Raúl Silva Henríquez, archbishop of Santiago, helped establish the Committee of Cooperation for Peace, an ecumenical body including Catholic, Lutheran, Jewish, and other leaders. It provided legal assistance, food, medical care, and job help to those persecuted. Pinochet forced its dissolution in late 1975, as the DINA's activities became more visible. The Cardinal immediately created a replacement under the Church's own authority, the Vicaría de la Solidaridad, which began operating in January 1976 and became the most important human-rights organization in the country.

The Vicaría's lawyers filed petitions of amparo, a form of habeas corpus asking courts to order the production of detainees, in thousands of cases. The courts almost always rejected them, accepting the security services' denials. But the petitions served a different function: they created a documentary record of each disappearance, with names, dates, circumstances, and witnesses, which later supported the commissions and judges. The Vicaría also provided legal defense in political trials, ran soup kitchens and health clinics in poor neighborhoods, supported workers dismissed for political reasons, and published a magazine, Solidaridad, that reported what the controlled media would not. Other organizations followed: the Group of Relatives of Detained-Disappeared (founded in 1974), the Group of Relatives of the Politically Executed, the Foundation for Social Aid of the Christian Churches (FASIC), and, in 1980, the Committee for the Defense of the Rights of the People (CODEPU).

Women were central. Mothers, wives, sisters, and daughters of the disappeared formed the core of the family groups, using public marches, vigils, and, at times, chaining themselves to public buildings to demand information. Women in the poblaciones, many of them unemployed or with unemployed husbands, organized communal kitchens (ollas comunes) and workshops where they sewed arpilleras, scrap-cloth pictures that depicted disappearances, hunger, and police violence. The Vicaría helped sell them abroad, which brought both income and international attention. Women's groups such as Women for Life, formed in 1983, later became a major strand of the opposition.

Culture offered coded and open resistance. The New Song movement of the 1960s, associated with Violeta Parra and Víctor Jara, continued in exile and, within Chile, in a quieter form of folk music called Canto Nuevo. Theater companies used allegory and workshop performance, poets such as Raúl Zurita and visual artists in the Art Actions Collective (CADA) staged public interventions, and filmmakers such as Patricio Guzmán documented the Allende years, working abroad with footage smuggled out of Chile. All were operating under censorship and surveillance. They were not mass movements until the 1980s, but they preserved memories and languages that the regime wished to erase.

The Church's position was not monolithic. Many bishops and priests supported the regime, and the hierarchy's willingness to confront it varied over time. Cardinal Silva Henríquez, and his successor from 1983, Juan Francisco Fresno, took different approaches. But parish priests, nuns, and lay workers in the poblaciones were often the only organized presence of the Church in poor areas and became important links between the opposition and ordinary people. The 1987 visit of Pope John Paul II, which included meetings with opposition figures, shifted public attention, as discussed in Section 85.17.

Section 85.12

The Constitution of 1980, Protected Democracy, and the Institutionalization of Military Power

From the beginning, the junta promised a new institutional order. A constitutional commission began work in late 1973 under the lawyer Enrique Ortúzar, and a Council of State advised Pinochet from 1976. A speech given by Pinochet in July 1977 at the Chacarillas hill outlined a long-term plan: recovery, a transition in which civilians would gradually join the government, and finally a "protected," "authoritarian," and "participatory" democracy. Jaime Guzmán, a young conservative jurist who founded the Independent Democratic Union, was the leading intellectual of this project, arguing that democracy needed to be shielded from Marxism and from what he saw as the failures of the 1960s.

The text was finished in 1980 and submitted to a plebiscite on 11 September, the seventh anniversary of the coup. The conditions were not those of a free vote. The electoral registers had been destroyed in 1973, so voters were identified by their identity cards; there was no independent electoral authority; the opposition had almost no access to radio or television, though former President Frei and others spoke at an indoor rally in Santiago in August; and a state of emergency was in effect. The government announced that about 67 percent of the votes had approved the text, with about 30 percent opposed. The vote cannot be treated as a measure of public opinion in a competitive election.

The Constitution, which entered into force on 11 March 1981, combined durable provisions with transitional articles. It created a strong presidency, with an eight-year term, extensive emergency powers, and the ability to dissolve the lower house under some conditions. It established a National Security Council on which the armed forces' commanders held half the seats and which could issue opinions on any matter it considered a threat to national institutions. It placed the commanders of the services beyond removal by the president, created a Constitutional Tribunal, set high quorums for amendment, and provided for a Senate with appointed members, including former commanders, in addition to elected senators. Article 8 outlawed doctrines that promoted class struggle or a totalitarian order, a provision aimed at the Marxist Left.

The transitional articles governed the period until 1989. They extended Pinochet's presidency for eight years and provided that, at the end, the commanders of the junta would propose a single candidate for a plebiscite in which voters would accept or reject him. If the vote was against the candidate, the president would continue for another year, and there would be competitive elections. The authors likely expected the plebiscite to confirm Pinochet. The mechanism, however, provided the opposition with a legal channel when conditions changed, a development explained in Section 85.18.

Students must distinguish between a constitution issued under authoritarian conditions and the restoration of democratic constitutional government. The 1980 text had the form of a constitution but was designed to limit what elected governments could do. It was drafted by regime appointees, approved without open debate or neutral supervision, and written to ensure military influence after any return to elections. It was amended in 1989 and many times later, and in 2005 further amendments removed most of the authoritarian enclaves, although the text's origin remained a subject of debate in Chile for decades.

The Neoliberal Experiment and Its Social Consequences

Chile's economic transformation as a historically consequential but uneven process: stabilization, market reform, recession, recovery, inequality, and the conditions under which policy was imposed.

Section 85.13

The Chicago Boys, Economic Shock Therapy, and the Shift toward Market Liberalization

The junta inherited a crisis. Inflation in 1973 stood in the hundreds of percent by most series, the fiscal deficit was very large, foreign-exchange reserves were nearly exhausted, and prices and exchange rates were controlled in ways that produced shortages. The military had no economic program of its own. Its first economic officials, including naval and air force officers and the civilian minister Fernando Léniz, freed most prices, unified the exchange rate, and returned to their owners many of the several hundred firms that the Allende government had requisitioned or intervened. These measures were market-oriented but gradual, and the regime's early economic direction was still contested among officers, some of whom favored a state-led nationalist model.

The economists who would eventually dominate policy had a distinct background. Beginning in the 1950s, an exchange program between the Catholic University of Chile and the University of Chicago brought Chilean graduate students to study under economists associated with Milton Friedman and Arnold Harberger. Returning to Chile, they built a network in the university and the business press. Before the coup, a group of them had drafted a long policy document for the 1970 Alessandri campaign and again in 1972–73, which became known as El Ladrillo, "The Brick." It argued for opening the economy, reducing the state's role, and relying on markets. It was not published until 1992, and its direct influence on the junta is debated, but it shows that an alternative program existed before the coup.

The turn came in 1975. A severe external shock, a collapse in copper prices and a rise in oil prices, combined with continuing inflation. Jorge Cauas, appointed finance minister, imposed what was called a "shock treatment" in April, with deep cuts in government spending, and Sergio de Castro, a leading Chicago-trained economist, became minister of economy and later of finance. Milton Friedman visited Chile in March 1975 and gave lectures, and his advice, though brief, became a symbol of the regime's direction. The shock program produced a deep recession: gross domestic product fell by something on the order of 13 percent in 1975, and unemployment rose sharply. Inflation, which had been around 340 percent in 1974 by most measures, fell to about 100 percent in 1976 and to roughly 30 percent by 1980.

Over the late 1970s, the program broadened. The government cut tariffs from an average of well over 90 percent in 1973 to a uniform 10 percent by 1979, liberalized the financial system, reduced the public sector, and in 1979 fixed the peso to the dollar at a rate of 39 to 1. It encouraged export industries and foreign investment through Decree Law 600 (1974). Defenders pointed to controlled inflation, fiscal balance, a boom in non-traditional exports, and growth averaging around 7 percent between 1977 and 1981. Critics, including exiled economists such as Orlando Letelier, who wrote an influential article in 1976, argued that the model depended on repression: with unions banned and parties dissolved, no organized force could challenge it.

It is important not to present the dictatorship's economic record as one unchanging policy. The first two years were improvisation and stabilization, 1975–1981 saw radical liberalization, the crisis of 1982 forced reversals, and the later 1980s combined market policy with pragmatic state intervention. The regime's economic decisions were also shaped by military politics: Pinochet supported the Chicago Boys over rivals such as General Leigh, but he also protected the armed forces' budget and the state-owned copper company. Economic policy was thus imposed by a regime that could ignore opposition, which is a central fact for evaluating it.

Historical Vocabulary III
Neoliberalism, privatization, individual capitalization, protected democracy, plebiscite

Neoliberalism is a label, used by both critics and some supporters, for economic policy that emphasizes markets, low inflation, open trade, private ownership, and a reduced state role. Historians use it analytically, but its precise meaning varies, and Chile's version combined market reform with strong state authority and continued state ownership of copper. Privatization is the sale or return of state-owned assets to private owners.

Individual capitalization is the pension model created in 1980, in which workers contribute to individual accounts managed by private companies rather than to a common fund. Protected democracy was the regime's term for a political order with elections but with built-in safeguards, including military roles, against parties it considered subversive. A plebiscite is a direct vote on a single question, here the 1980 Constitution and, in 1988, Pinochet's continuation in office.

Section 85.14

Privatization, Copper, Foreign Investment, and the Restructuring of Chilean Capitalism

Privatization proceeded in two main waves. In the first, from 1974 to about 1979, the government returned requisitioned firms and sold large public enterprises, including banks, industrial companies, and insurance firms, often through quick sales to buyers who borrowed to finance their purchases. The number of state firms fell from several hundred to a few dozen. In a second wave in the mid-1980s, after the crisis, the government sold some utilities and other companies, in a policy its supporters called "popular capitalism" because it encouraged employees and small investors to buy shares. The sales were criticized, then and later, because many assets were sold at low prices and because those with connections and capital benefited disproportionately.

The restructuring created large business groups. Holding companies such as those headed by Manuel Cruzat and Fernando Larraín, and by Javier Vial, built conglomerates around banks and financed purchases of firms with foreign borrowing. This concentration, combined with a lack of regulation, played a major role in the financial crisis of 1982. At the same time, openness to trade and investment changed the structure of production. Industries that had grown under protection, such as textiles and some manufacturing, shrank or closed, and new export sectors grew: fresh fruit in the central valleys, forestry on plantations encouraged by subsidies from Decree Law 701, fishing and fishmeal in the north and south, and eventually salmon farming. These industries employed many people, including women in seasonal fruit packing, but also placed new pressures on land and water.

Copper was the exception that must not be forgotten. Chile's economic transformation was not the privatization of everything. The Gran Minería remained nationalized, and in 1976 the government created the National Copper Corporation, CODELCO, as a state enterprise to run the mines. The regime also negotiated settlements with Anaconda and Kennecott that reversed part of the Allende-era compensation decisions. The military kept a share of copper revenue for its own purchases through a law dating from 1958 that Pinochet's government maintained. Meanwhile, Chile opened new deposits to private and foreign capital under the Foreign Investment Statute and a mining law of the early 1980s, and over the following decades private mining, led by new projects, grew in importance, so that the state enterprise's share of national output shrank without its being sold.

Assessing the model requires distinguishing national growth, private profitability, and distribution. Between 1976 and 1981 the economy grew quickly, exports diversified, and inflation fell. But a large share of the gains went to owners of capital and to the upper-income strata, and income inequality, as measured by household surveys, increased in the 1970s and 1980s. Foreign borrowing financed much of the expansion. Later chapters on Latin American neoliberalism (Chapters 93 and 95) place Chile in comparative perspective, as the first and most complete case of market reform in the region, imposed by a dictatorship.

Section 85.15

Pension Privatization, Labor Reform, Education, Housing, and Everyday Life

The regime's "seven modernizations," announced in 1979, extended the market model to labor, pensions, health, education, agriculture, justice, and administration. The Labor Plan of 1979, designed by the minister José Piñera, legalized unions and collective bargaining again after years of prohibition, but on new terms: bargaining at the level of the firm rather than the industry, limits on strikes (replacement workers could be hired after a period), and a ban on political activity by unions. Union membership, which had been about a third of the workforce at its height before 1973, fell well below that, and the country's historically strong labor movement lost much of its bargaining power.

The most durable reform was the pension system. Decree Law 3,500 of November 1980 replaced the old pay-as-you-go system, in which current workers financed retirees, with individual accounts managed by private pension fund administrators (AFPs). Workers contributed a share of their wages, and their pensions depended on accumulated savings and investment returns. The armed forces and police retained their separate system. The reform created a large pool of domestic investment capital and has been widely studied abroad. But it left outcomes dependent on stable employment and high contributions, so workers with intermittent jobs, including most women and informal workers, received low pensions, and the transition cost to the state was large.

Education and health were restructured. In 1981 the government transferred public primary and secondary schools to municipalities and introduced a per-student subsidy that also funded private schools, while higher education was opened to private universities and cut in public funding. Health care was split between a public system and private insurers (ISAPRES) that attracted higher-income contributors. These changes produced a more segmented system in which quality and access varied with income and location.

The social effects were felt in daily life. Unemployment, which was high even before 1982, left many households dependent on emergency public-works programs, the Minimum Employment Program and the Occupational Program for Heads of Household, which paid less than the minimum wage. In the poblaciones of Santiago and other cities, residents organized communal kitchens, bought food collectively, and shared housing. The regime also forced the relocation of tens of thousands of poor families from informal settlements in wealthier municipalities to the urban periphery, which changed the social geography of the capital. A retrospective statistical comparison cited in the Chilean Chamber of Deputies reported that 17 percent of households were poor nationally in 1970, compared with 38.1 percent in 1987; an estimate for people in poverty in 1987 was 44.4 percent, a different denominator. Because historical poverty definitions and survey methods were not fully consistent, these are approximate comparisons, not a seamless modern time series. Poverty declined in the 1990s. (Chilean parliamentary record.)

In the countryside, the agrarian reform was reversed. About a third of expropriated land was returned to former owners and much of the rest was divided into individual plots, many of which were then sold, leaving many peasants landless. The new fruit and forestry export economy depended on seasonal labor, much of it by women, called temporeras, who worked long hours for low pay with limited protection. Mapuche communities faced a specific blow. Decree Law 2,568 of 1979 ended the legal protection of communal reservation lands, allowing their division into individual holdings that could be sold, and the expansion of forestry plantations in the Araucanía placed additional pressure on land. These policies were the basis for conflicts over land and resources that continued into the twenty-first century.

Everyday Life and Social History

Kitchens, Workshops, and Households under the Dictatorship

For a household in a Santiago población in the early 1980s, the macroeconomic story arrived as a change in the weekly budget. A worker who lost a factory job might take a place in an emergency employment program at a fraction of the minimum wage; his partner might sew arpilleras in a church-sponsored workshop, take in laundry, or join a communal kitchen in which neighbors pooled food to feed their children. Young people, who faced the highest unemployment, often became the core of protest movements in these neighborhoods.

The regime also shaped private life through its promotion of a conservative family ideal. Pinochet's wife, Lucía Hiriart, directed a national network of mothers' centers, and official rhetoric extolled motherhood and the nation. Meanwhile many women entered the labor force out of necessity, and others became political actors by searching for the disappeared or organizing for food. In the countryside, temporeras traveled to packing sheds during the harvest, and Mapuche families watched plantation forests replace pastures and fields. The sources, which come largely from governments, churches, and later oral-history projects, record these experiences unevenly.

Section 85.16

The Financial Crisis of 1982, Bank Rescues, Unemployment, and Economic Reorientation

The boom of the late 1970s rested on foreign borrowing. After the peso was fixed to the dollar in 1979 and capital controls were relaxed, banks and business groups borrowed heavily abroad, where interest rates were low, and lent at home. Imports surged, the current-account deficit reached roughly 14 percent of output in 1981, and foreign debt more than tripled in a few years. The fixed exchange rate made imports cheap and exports less competitive, and many assets, including real estate and shares, rose in value on credit. Regulation was limited, and the banks that belonged to business groups often lent to affiliated firms.

The conditions changed in 1981 and 1982. World interest rates rose, the prices of Chile's exports fell, and foreign lending shrank as the international debt crisis began (Chapter 93). Under pressure, the government abandoned the fixed exchange rate in June 1982, and the peso lost much of its value, making dollar debts far heavier. Firms and banks failed. Gross domestic product fell by approximately 14 percent in 1982, among the largest declines in the region. Official unemployment peaked at around 20 percent, and when workers in emergency programs are included the rate was substantially higher, possibly near 30 percent. Real wages fell, and poverty rose. This chapter uses figures from Chilean and international series for the period, which differ in detail, and students should always identify the series being used.

In January 1983 the government intervened in several major banks and liquidated others, and, through the central bank, took over the troubled loan portfolios and guaranteed depositors and, in effect, much of the private foreign debt. The decision to rescue banks in an economy run by self-described free-market economists was a striking reversal, which defenders explained as necessary to prevent systemic collapse and critics described as a transfer of private losses to the public. The costs over the following years were large, a significant share of public finances through the decade, and the groups that had led the boom lost control of their banks. Within the regime, the Chicago Boys lost influence, and a series of finance ministers followed Sergio de Castro, who left in 1982.

A more pragmatic phase followed. After the crisis, the government renegotiated external debt with the International Monetary Fund and creditor banks, devalued the currency, and raised some tariffs temporarily. From 1985, the finance minister Hernán Büchi, a technocrat, combined fiscal restraint, export promotion, new bank regulation, a second round of privatizations, and a competitive exchange rate. Growth recovered, with several years of expansion averaging around 6 or 7 percent in the later 1980s, driven by exports, investment, and copper. By 1989 unemployment had fallen and output was well above its 1981 level, though real wages recovered more slowly and many households remained poor.

Evaluating the record thus requires a series of judgments. Inflation was reduced; exports grew and diversified; the economy was opened; and growth in the later 1980s was strong. Against this, the shock of 1975 and the collapse of 1982 imposed huge costs on workers, poverty rose sharply, inequality increased, the state financed a large rescue, and the model was imposed without the consent of those who bore the costs. The sources do not allow a single verdict. What students can say is that the Chilean economy underwent a historically consequential restructuring, that its results were uneven across time and groups, and that the regime's capacity to impose it depended on the political system described in earlier sections.

Section 85.17

The National Protests of 1983–1986, Social Movements, and Armed Opposition

The economic collapse broke the regime's long quiet. On 11 May 1983, the first National Day of Protest, called by the copper workers' confederation under its president Rodolfo Seguel, brought residents of Santiago's neighborhoods into the streets and onto their balconies, banging pots, honking horns, and withdrawing from public spaces at appointed hours. Protests recurred roughly monthly through 1983 and continued, with varying intensity, into 1986. The government responded with army patrols and mass arrests, and dozens of people were killed during the protest cycles, many of them young residents of the poblaciones.

The protests were not led by a single organization. Labor unions, notably in copper and transport, organized strikes. Students in the universities, whose leaders had been appointed by the regime, formed federations again. Women's groups, including Women for Life and groups of relatives of the disappeared, staged demonstrations, and the Movement against Torture Sebastián Acevedo, named for a man who set himself on fire in 1983 to protest the detention of his children, organized public protests against torture. Priests and nuns accompanied marches. The Church provided meeting space and legal help. Political parties, still formally banned, reorganized: Christian Democrats, Radicals, Social Democrats, and a faction of the Socialists formed the Democratic Alliance in August 1983.

The government alternated between concession and force. In August 1983 Pinochet appointed a civilian conservative politician, Sergio Onofre Jarpa, as minister of the interior, who opened talks with the Democratic Alliance but achieved no agreement. A state of siege was declared in November 1984 and lasted until June 1985, with mass raids on poblaciones. In March 1985 three Communist professionals were kidnapped and killed by Carabineros agents, a case that shocked public opinion and led to the resignation of the police commander, General César Mendoza, from the junta. A 1985 National Accord proposed by Cardinal Fresno among a broad range of civilian leaders called for a transition, and the regime rejected it.

Armed opposition grew in parallel. The Communist Party, which had earlier emphasized mass organization, in 1980 adopted a policy of "popular rebellion" that included armed struggle. In 1983 it formed the Manuel Rodríguez Patriotic Front (FPMR), which carried out sabotage, bank robberies, and attacks. In 1986, a large cache of weapons smuggled into the country was discovered at Carrizal Bajo, and on 7 September 1986 FPMR commandos ambushed Pinochet's motorcade in the Cajón del Maipo outside Santiago. Five of his bodyguards were killed, and Pinochet survived with minor injuries. The regime responded with a new state of siege and a wave of reprisals, including killings of opposition figures.

The failure of the 1986 attack and the weapons discovery shifted opinion within the opposition. Many leaders concluded that armed struggle could not defeat a unified military and that it risked driving moderates back to the regime. At the same time, 1986 had been declared by the opposition's Civic Assembly as "the decisive year," and it was not. The result was a strategic debate between those who still sought a mass uprising and those who argued for using the regime's own rules. In April 1987 the visit of Pope John Paul II, who met opposition figures and spoke about human rights and reconciliation, and a reshaping of American policy under Ambassador Harry Barnes toward supporting a democratic transition, strengthened the second view. Armed and electoral strategies did not disappear, but the second now had the initiative.

Section 85.18

Opposition Coalitions, Political Reorganization, and the Campaign for the No Vote

The opposition's strategic problem was to accept the legitimacy of the 1980 Constitution's mechanism without accepting the regime. The transitional articles required a plebiscite on a candidate chosen by the junta. A number of opposition leaders, including Christian Democrats, moderate Socialists, and the Social Democrat and later Party for Democracy leader Ricardo Lagos, argued that the opposition should register voters and campaign for a No, in effect using the regime's own rule to defeat it. Others warned that participation would legitimize the process and that fraud was likely. The argument was decided by several developments: the regime's own law in 1987 allowing parties to register (excluding Marxist parties under Article 8), the failure of insurrectionary strategies, and a growing sense that a large, organized vote could not be ignored.

Voter registration became the first test. Civic organizations and parties encouraged citizens to register, and by mid-1988 about 7.4 million Chileans, over 90 percent of those eligible, had done so. The opposition created the Command for the No, and the Party for Democracy, formed in 1987 as an instrumental party to allow participation by those whose own parties were barred. In February 1988, sixteen parties, from Christian Democrats and Socialists to Radicals and smaller groups, formed the Concertación of Parties for the No. Patricio Aylwin, the Christian Democratic leader who had negotiated with Allende in 1973, became its spokesperson. The Communist Party remained outside the formal coalition and had earlier favored other strategies, but its supporters largely voted No.

Why could parties with such different histories cooperate? Several factors converged. The experience of repression had changed attitudes: Christian Democrats who had supported the coup in 1973 and Socialists who had rejected compromise had both reassessed. A Socialist "renewal," led by intellectuals in exile and at home, revalued liberal democracy and institutions. The shared goal was narrow, ending Pinochet's presidency by a vote, which allowed them to postpone disagreements over program. And the opposition perceived that division in 1970–1973 and earlier had contributed to the catastrophe, and it was unwilling to repeat that.

The campaign itself was unusual. Under the law, each side received fifteen minutes of television time nightly for the month before the vote. The No campaign, working with advertising professionals and social scientists, turned away from the memory of the Allende years and toward an appeal to hope: its slogan, "Joy is coming," and its jingle, along with testimonies, humor, and interviews with ordinary Chileans, aimed to overcome fear. It also included footage of repression. The Yes campaign stressed economic order, warned of a return to chaos, and promoted Pinochet's achievements. Grassroots volunteers canvassed neighborhoods and trained poll watchers, and a parallel vote-counting system was prepared to guard against manipulation. American and European organizations, including the United States National Endowment for Democracy, provided funding for voter registration and civic education, though the campaign was overwhelmingly organized and staffed by Chileans.

Section 85.19

October 5, 1988: The National Plebiscite, the Victory of the No, and Constitutional Negotiations

On 5 October 1988, voters answered one question: whether they accepted the junta's candidate, General Pinochet, for another eight-year term. Participation was very high, around 97 percent of registered voters. The process was monitored by the Chilean electoral service, and by a large number of poll watchers and international observers. The opposition's parallel count, based on tallies from voting tables across the country, allowed it to know the trend within hours. As the official count came in slowly that night, and the government delayed announcing totals, a tense period followed in which some members of the regime reportedly considered refusing the result. Air Force General Fernando Matthei, a member of the junta, acknowledged late that night that the No had won, and the Interior Ministry soon confirmed it.

The final certified result gave 3,967,579 votes to the No and 3,119,110 to the Yes. As a share of the valid votes cast for the two options, that is approximately 56 percent against 44 percent. Some presentations report approximately 54.7 percent for the No, because they calculate it as a share of all ballots cast, including blank and null ballots, which numbered in the low hundreds of thousands, for a total of roughly 7.25 million. Both calculations are correct; they use different denominators. This chapter uses the share of valid votes for the two options unless otherwise stated.

Pinochet's acceptance of the defeat was the result of several pressures. The armed forces were not unanimous in wanting to disregard the vote, and Matthei's public statement was a signal. The regime had designed the plebiscite and had committed itself publicly to its rules; reversing would have meant an open dictatorship without even formal legitimacy. International attention, including that of the Reagan administration, which had signaled that it expected a fair outcome, raised the cost of fraud. And the No campaign's organization, its parallel count, and its mobilization of several million voters left little room for manipulation. Pinochet remained president for another seventeen months and remained commander of the army.

The terms of the transition were then negotiated. Under the Constitution's transitional articles, competitive presidential and congressional elections were to be held within about a year. The opposition and the regime's supporters, led by the party National Renewal, negotiated constitutional changes in early 1989. The package of 54 amendments, approved by the 30 July 1989 plebiscite with 6,069,449 Apruebo votes against 581,605 Rechazo votes—85.70 percent of all ballots cast, or approximately 91.25 percent of votes cast for those two options— repealed the ban on Marxist doctrines in Article 8, increased the number of elected senators relative to appointed ones, equalized civilian and military membership of the National Security Council, and eased the requirements for future constitutional amendments. It left in place the appointed senators, the irremovability of service commanders, and the electoral system that disadvantaged the largest coalition. The negotiated amendments reflect the transition's character: the opposition accepted a framework it did not design in order to ensure that elections would take place.

Section 85.20

The 1989 Elections, Patricio Aylwin, March 1990, and the Legacies of Military Rule

On 14 December 1989 Chileans voted in the first competitive presidential and congressional elections since 1970. Patricio Aylwin, the candidate of the Concertación, won about 55 percent of the vote. Hernán Büchi, the former finance minister, supported by the parties of the Right, received about 29 percent, and the independent businessman Francisco Javier Errázuriz about 15 percent. In the congressional elections, the Concertación won a majority of the Chamber of Deputies, but the electoral system, which gave two seats per district to the first and second lists, and the appointed senators, meant that the Right held enough seats to block constitutional changes. On 11 March 1990, Pinochet handed the presidential sash to Aylwin in a ceremony at the National Congress in Valparaíso, and the following day Aylwin addressed a large crowd at the National Stadium, the site of detention in 1973.

The transition was negotiated rather than a rupture because of the balance of forces. The opposition had won a plebiscite and elections, but the armed forces remained united and under Pinochet's command, the regime's supporters had a large minority in the electorate, and the Constitution gave them veto positions. Aylwin's government had to decide whether to press confrontation or accept the framework and seek change within it. It chose the second, a decision that has been defended as necessary to avoid a return to violence and criticized as leaving authoritarian enclaves in place. Both views rest on judgments about what the military would have done.

Pinochet did not leave public life. He remained commander in chief of the army until March 1998, when he became a senator for life, a status the Constitution gave to former presidents. The 1978 Amnesty Law, which covered crimes committed between 1973 and 1978, remained in force, and many cases of human-rights abuse after that date were in the courts. Aylwin created the Rettig Commission in April 1990 and, on receiving its report in 1991, publicly apologized on behalf of the state to victims and their families. The longer story of prosecutions, Pinochet's arrest in London in 1998, and the continuing work of truth commissions belongs to Chapter 94.

The economic model also persisted. The Concertación governments kept the open-market framework, the private pension system, and the export orientation, while increasing social spending and the minimum wage and reforming labor law and taxation. Poverty fell substantially in the 1990s. The continuity, for supporters, showed that the model had been accepted; for critics, that the inherited constraints, including the Constitution and the concentration of economic power, limited the choices of elected governments. By the 2010s, mass protests over education, pensions, and inequality (Chapter 110) showed that these questions remained unresolved.

March 1990 should therefore not be described as complete institutional democratization. Civilian presidential government returned and the political parties were free, but the armed forces retained a degree of autonomy, designated senators and a restrictive electoral system distorted representation, and questions of accountability and memory were unresolved. The seventeen years of military rule had changed the state, the economy, and social life, and the transition left the task of reckoning with that inheritance to the next generation.

What Changed

Who experienced change most directly: Political prisoners, the families of the disappeared, exiles, workers, and poor households bore the sharpest effects. Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Chile from the reform politics of the 1960s through Salvador Allende's constitutional experiment, the military coup of 1973, Augusto Pinochet's dictatorship, and the negotiated return to civilian government in 1990. Its central argument is that these were connected but distinct historical phases, each requiring its own explanation, and that the story cannot be reduced to a single cause, whether economic failure, American intervention, or the will of one man.

Chile entered the 1960s as a constitutional republic with a strong party system divided into Right, Center, and Left, an economy dependent on copper controlled by American firms, and a deeply unequal countryside. Eduardo Frei's Christian Democratic "Revolution in Liberty" began agrarian reform and partial copper nationalization, showing that major change was already underway. Reform provoked opposition from both sides and left the political center without natural allies.

Allende won a plurality in September 1970 at the head of Popular Unity and was ratified by Congress after agreeing to constitutional guarantees. He became president through the existing order. The United States pursued a covert effort in 1970 to prevent his inauguration, documented in declassified records, which ended with the killing of General Schneider by Chilean plotters. That evidence is clear, and it does not by itself explain the events of 1973.

Popular Unity nationalized copper by unanimous vote of Congress, expropriated estates, took over banks and firms, and expanded wages and social services. The first year brought growth and popular support. But spending outran output, reserves were exhausted, inflation passed 100 percent in 1972, and shortages spread. Divisions between those who wanted to consolidate and those who wanted to advance quickly weakened the coalition.

The truck owners' stoppage of October 1972, the rise of the cordones industriales, the March 1973 elections, the Tanquetazo, and the resolution of 22 August showed institutions in deadlock and both sides claiming legality. Generals Prats and Pinochet represented different options within the army. By September, the military leadership had decided to act, and on 11 September the armed forces overthrew the government. Allende died during the assault, a death forensic and judicial investigations have determined to be suicide.

The junta dissolved Congress, banned parties, and ruled by decree. Within months Pinochet emerged as its dominant figure and, by 1974, held the presidency; General Leigh's removal in 1978 completed the process. The regime justified itself with the national-security doctrine and the claim that it had saved the nation. It also built a system of repression, from the stadiums of 1973 to the DINA and CNI, with torture, enforced disappearance, exile, and assassinations abroad, culminating in the Letelier killing in Washington. The truth commissions documented thousands of dead and disappeared and tens of thousands of survivors of imprisonment and torture, in categories that must be kept distinct.

Civil society preserved forms of protection and opposition. The Committee for Peace and the Vicaría de la Solidaridad, with lawyers, families of the disappeared, women in the poblaciones, artists, and journalists, created records and networks that later proved essential. The 1980 Constitution institutionalized military power in a protected democracy, approved in a plebiscite without free conditions, but its transitional articles also contained the mechanism by which the regime would eventually be defeated.

The economy was transformed. After stabilization and the shock of 1975, the Chicago Boys and others pursued trade opening, privatization, labor and pension reform, and the restructuring of health and education, while keeping state ownership of the largest copper mines. Growth and export diversification were real, but so were unemployment, poverty, and inequality. The financial collapse of 1982 forced a costly state rescue of private banks and a more pragmatic management under Büchi. Evaluation requires quantitative series, attention to different groups, and awareness that the program was imposed by a regime without electoral consent.

The 1982 crisis opened space for mass protest, beginning in May 1983, with unions, students, women's groups, Church bodies, and parties, while the FPMR pursued armed struggle and nearly killed Pinochet in 1986. After the failure of insurrectionary strategies, opposition parties with very different histories joined in the Concertación and campaigned for the No in the 1988 plebiscite, which they won with about 56 percent of valid votes for the two options. Negotiated amendments, the elections of December 1989, and Aylwin's inauguration on 11 March 1990 restored civilian presidential government.

The transition was incomplete. Pinochet commanded the army until 1998, the Constitution retained authoritarian features, the amnesty law remained, and the economic model persisted. Chile thus exemplifies how a dictatorship can transform a society, be defeated at the ballot box, and still shape the terms of the democracy that follows. Chapter 94 takes up this problem comparatively.

  5. Write. In a 4–6 page essay, assess how a civic institution creates evidence under repression, and note where its perspective is limited.

Chapter Reflection

Chile's experience mattered far beyond its borders because it posed, in unusually clear form, questions that recur across Latin American history: whether deep social change can be pursued through existing institutions, what happens when democratic politics polarizes beyond compromise, and what costs a society pays when armed forces claim the right to settle political disputes. The answers Chileans gave, in 1970, in 1973, and in 1988, were all made under pressure and with real alternatives, which is why the period resists both fatalism and simple morality tales.

The chapter also shows how repression, economic restructuring, and political institutions reinforced one another. The secret police and the banning of unions and parties made radical economic reform possible; the 1982 crisis weakened the regime's claim to competence; and the Constitution designed to perpetuate military influence contained the plebiscite that ended Pinochet's presidency. Understanding that interaction is more useful than assigning the period a single label.

Many continuities outlasted March 1990: the pension and education systems, the open economy, the Constitution's constraints, the army's autonomy, and, above all, divided memory. Mapuche land claims, labor weakness, and inequality also carried forward, and they return in Chapter 110. The Chilean case also connects regions: Brazilian support for the junta, Argentine and Chilean intelligence cooperation, exile networks across Europe and the Americas, and American covert and diplomatic policy were all part of the story.

Chapter 86 turns to Argentina, where a military regime that took power in 1976 pursued a still more lethal campaign of disappearance and then collapsed after the Malvinas/Falklands war, a different path from Chile's electoral defeat. Chapter 87 will return to the regional apparatus, Operation Condor, whose origins appeared here in the cases of Prats and Letelier. Students should carry forward the habit of separating phases, distinguishing sources, and asking who bore the costs of each political and economic choice.
