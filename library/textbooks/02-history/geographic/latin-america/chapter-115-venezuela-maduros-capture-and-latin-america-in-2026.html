Chapter Opening: A President Removed, a System Remaining

3 January 2026: the president was seized. The institutions through which he ruled remained.

On 3 January 2026, United States forces carried out a military operation in Venezuela and seized Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores. They were transported to New York to face federal criminal charges. The operation decisively ended Maduro’s physical exercise of power, but it did not establish a new popular mandate or settle the question of Venezuela’s constitutional succession.

Within forty-eight hours, Delcy Rodríguez, the vice president under Maduro, had assumed presidential responsibilities. The government apparatus and much of its security hierarchy continued to operate. Washington began working with an interim administration drawn from the same governing movement whose former leader it had forcibly removed. That paradox is the starting point of this final chapter.

The event raised distinct questions that must not be treated as a single verdict. Was Maduro’s claim to democratic legitimacy credible after the disputed 2024 presidential election? Did the Venezuelan judiciary lawfully classify his forced removal as temporary absence? Was the U.S. operation consistent with the United Nations Charter? Could an American court exercise criminal jurisdiction over the seized defendants? The answers depend on different documents and institutions.

The crisis also produced unusually rapid changes in natural-resource policy. On 29 January the National Assembly adopted major hydrocarbon-law amendments. Washington modified sanctions licenses and supervised arrangements through which proceeds from Venezuelan oil and other resource sales were handled. A later accounting described more than twelve billion dollars of covered proceeds through September, but that figure cannot be confused with unrestricted government cash or national prosperity.

Human rights provided another measure of change. An amnesty law and releases of detainees offered hope for families affected by years of political persecution. UN investigators nevertheless found that key structures of repression persisted, even as some practices changed. An administration’s willingness to release prisoners did not prove that the judiciary, prisons and intelligence services had become independent.

On 24 June two powerful earthquakes struck north-central Venezuela, killing thousands and damaging housing, hospitals, public infrastructure and livelihoods. The catastrophe made the effectiveness and impartiality of the interim administration a matter of immediate physical survival. Dated reports by PAHO, UNICEF and UN investigators are essential to understand the scale and the contested quality of the response.

The consequences reached well beyond Venezuela. Latin American governments rejected or debated the legality of the unilateral military action; foreign powers recalibrated their commercial and security interests; millions of Venezuelans abroad watched for a credible prospect of safe return; and the unresolved controversy over Guyana’s Essequibo region continued through international judicial proceedings.

This chapter closes the five-volume MGU history at a specified historical cutoff: 16 November 2026. It records what had happened by that date, distinguishes official claims from independently supported findings, and identifies legal and political outcomes that remained open. Its larger subject is the relationship among sovereignty, institutions, resources, rights and the agency of the people whose history the textbook has traced.

Historical Context: Chavismo, the Oil State, and the 2024–2025 Crisis

This chapter is the conclusion, not a repetition, of Venezuela’s petroleum and political history. Chapters 105–109 explained the oil state, the 1999 Constitution, the Bolivarian movement, economic crisis, sanctions, and mass migration. Chapter 112 examined Tren de Aragua and other organized-crime networks; Chapter 114 examined resource geopolitics and changing migration patterns. Chapter 115 focuses on the specific events of 2026 and the unsettled consequences of those events.

The central sources are distinct in provenance and purpose. The Carter Center provided election observation; the U.S. Justice Department stated criminal charges; Venezuela’s Supreme Tribunal issued a constitutional ruling; the UN investigated patterns of rights violations; OFAC published license conditions; PAHO, UNICEF and the USGS documented earthquakes; and the international press reported political negotiations and accounting records.

Government statements reveal what officials claimed and intended. They are not automatic confirmation of independent facts. Prosecutorial documents establish charges but not guilt; fact-finding reports have stated evidentiary methods but are not federal criminal judgments; casualty reports are snapshots from particular dates; and a newspaper account of an audit is distinct from independent public verification of every underlying transaction.

Because this chapter covers a period still in progress, all claims are bounded by 16 November 2026. The text does not incorporate, forecast, or retrospectively resolve events scheduled after that date. Legal and political controversies are labeled according to their status, and students are encouraged to return to dated primary documents when new evidence appears.

This distinction is especially important in a five-volume history. The aim is not to predict which political movement will ultimately prevail. It is to explain the institutional, geographic, economic, humanitarian, legal and diplomatic processes through which Venezuelans and other Latin Americans confronted a year of extraordinary change.

The Final Maduro Crisis

Legitimacy, the disputed election, rising pressure, and the 3 January operation

Section 115.1

The Crisis of Presidential Legitimacy

The removal of Nicolás Maduro in January 2026 cannot be understood as an ordinary change of government. By then Venezuela had lived through more than a decade of contested executive authority, an economy damaged by oil dependence and institutional failure, repeated cycles of protest and repression, and an outward migration unprecedented in the country's modern history. President Hugo Chávez had constructed an electoral and political project that combined social redistribution, military support, presidential concentration of power, and a language of popular sovereignty. After his death in 2013, Maduro inherited the movement without enjoying Chávez's distinctive authority or the same commodity-price environment. The state's ability to convert petroleum revenue into public services deteriorated as production collapsed, fiscal problems accumulated, and international restrictions increasingly constrained transactions. None of these processes alone explains the crisis: policy decisions, corruption, damaged infrastructure, external sanctions, and domestic political confrontation interacted.

An unresolved problem of presidential legitimacy developed alongside the socioeconomic emergency. Venezuelan institutions continued to issue laws, convene elections, and claim constitutional legitimacy, but the ability of opposition actors to contest power fairly was disputed. The opposition was itself plural: parties, electoral coalitions, civic organizations, exiled politicians, and local leaders differed over whether negotiation, electoral participation, international pressure, or civil resistance could produce change. Many poorer citizens continued to identify with the social promises of Chavismo even while objecting to worsening services and declining wages. Other Venezuelans rejected the official portrayal of political disagreement as foreign conspiracy. Treating either camp as socially homogeneous obscures why the crisis persisted through repeated electoral cycles.

The 1999 Constitution is central to the dispute because different actors invoked its promises of popular sovereignty, elections, and constitutional succession. Critics argued that a judiciary and electoral authority aligned with the governing party could not credibly arbitrate contests in which the government was a party. Government defenders stressed constitutional continuity and resisted foreign efforts to determine leadership. That argument was not merely rhetorical: a state's institutions can continue to function even when their impartiality is contested. A court can issue a legally consequential order while the legitimacy of its composition and reasoning remains a subject of political and constitutional dispute. Students must separate the existence of an official decision from agreement that the decision settled the underlying conflict.

The armed forces and security agencies also became central political institutions. Senior military figures held administrative responsibilities and participated in patronage and economic networks; the military was not merely a neutral instrument awaiting instructions from an elected president. Intelligence institutions and armed civilian groups operated within a wider security environment that UN investigators associated with arbitrary detention, torture, and intimidation of critics. Yet personnel, missions, command relationships, and incentives differed among organizations. The history of Venezuelan power is not explained by assuming that every police officer, soldier, judge, or public employee followed one identical political conviction. Questions of hierarchy and accountability matter precisely because authority was distributed unevenly.

In this context, foreign governments made conflicting judgments about the Venezuelan presidency. Some recognized opposition claims to democratic legitimacy; others maintained diplomatic ties with Maduro's administration or emphasized nonintervention regardless of electoral assessment. Recognition is a diplomatic and legal practice, not an independent nationwide ballot count. It also varies by government and over time. The contested presidency affected commercial agreements, control over assets, passports, international representation, and the ability of Venezuelans to secure remedies. These practical consequences made political legitimacy more than an abstract debate over a president's popularity.

The crisis therefore had three interlocking levels by late 2025: the validity of electoral authority, the durability of an entrenched state apparatus, and the relationship between Venezuelan sovereignty and pressure exercised by outside powers. January 2026 abruptly altered the third level. It did not automatically settle the first two. That distinction explains the apparently paradoxical outcome: the leader who personalized executive authority was removed, while many structures that sustained his rule remained capable of governing. The question carried through this chapter is not simply who occupied the presidency, but who possessed the capacity and recognized right to govern, under what constraints, and on whose behalf.

Historical Vocabulary

Legality, legitimacy, and effective control

Legality asks whether an action conforms to an applicable legal rule. Legitimacy concerns the accepted right to exercise authority; people may dispute it despite the existence of a formal judgment. Effective control identifies the actors who actually operate institutions and enforce decisions. The three concepts overlap but are not synonyms.

Apply the terms to January 2026: the court issued a succession decision, the Rodríguez administration controlled executive institutions, and citizens continued to debate whether this government possessed a sufficiently renewed democratic mandate. A strong analysis specifies which question a source answers.

Section 115.2

The Disputed 2024 Election

The presidential election of 28 July 2024 became the immediate legitimacy crisis behind the events of 2026. María Corina Machado had emerged as a dominant opposition figure but could not register as a presidential candidate after authorities enforced a ban on her holding public office. The main opposition coalition instead backed Edmundo González Urrutia, a former diplomat. The substitution mattered because it demonstrated both the opposition's ability to coordinate and the obstacles it faced. An election may have voters, ballots, machines, and candidates yet still fail to satisfy standards of fairness if the candidate-registration process, media environment, and treatment of opposition organizers systematically favor the incumbent.

The electoral authority, the Consejo Nacional Electoral (CNE), announced that Maduro had won. Its preliminary announcement claimed approximately 51.2 percent for Maduro and 44.2 percent for González. The opposition published collections of polling-station records that it argued showed a decisive González victory. The resulting dispute was not only a clash of speeches: it involved the records that should connect local votes to a national total. The Carter Center's observation mission emphasized the absence of publicly released polling-station results from the CNE and concluded that the process did not meet international standards of electoral integrity. The Center observed the technical aspects of voting and tabulation separately from wider conditions of political competition; the two inquiries should not be conflated.

An important methodological lesson follows. Ballot-box transparency depends on an auditable chain from voting to local tabulation to national aggregation. A machine's successful operation does not prove that an announced national result reflects all machines' returns. Conversely, evidence offered by a party should be assessed for coverage, authenticity, and consistency rather than accepted solely because that party opposes the government. The Carter Center's final report provides a structured basis for analyzing both the electoral conditions and the evidentiary dispute. Students should compare its findings with the CNE's announced totals and with available polling-station documentation, keeping the source and date attached to each number.

The political aftermath deepened the legitimacy problem. Authorities pursued critics, restricted protest, and made arrests; the UN fact-finding mission documented patterns of serious violations surrounding the post-electoral period. Opposition organizers operated under pressure, and González left Venezuela for Spain in September 2024. These consequences did not erase the participation of voters who supported Maduro or prove that every government official supported repression. They do establish that the path for translating disputed electoral evidence into an agreed institutional settlement narrowed rather than expanded. International demands for verifiable results and talks over guarantees could not produce a consensus government before January 2026.

Disagreement over the election also shaped claims about the U.S. operation. American officials asserted that Maduro lacked democratic legitimacy and presented his removal as a response to criminal charges and security threats. Opponents of the operation argued that a flawed election did not grant another state authority to use armed force in Venezuelan territory. These positions address different questions. The quality of a domestic election may affect a government's claim to democratic legitimacy, but the UN Charter regulates interstate force through a separate legal framework. Even those who rejected Maduro's electoral claim could oppose foreign military intervention while demanding a new Venezuelan election.

The 2024 result remained an unresolved constitutional and political issue through the chapter's 16 November 2026 cutoff. No contemporary documentary record available to this chapter establishes that the rival camps reached a binding settlement of the 2024 vote. Nor did the creation of an interim executive in 2026 retrospectively authenticate one version of the results. The central issue for historians is how a dispute over counted ballots developed into a struggle over institutions, coercive authority, and external recognition. A democratic transition would have to address not only the person at the top but the public's ability to verify future results without relying on the assurances of those already in power.

Section 115.3

Sanctions, Maritime Pressure, and Escalation in 2025

The final months before Maduro's capture were marked by escalating U.S.–Venezuelan confrontation. Washington had long imposed sanctions on individuals, institutions, financing, and oil-related activity, and its policy changed over time through general licenses, restrictions, and selective exemptions. Sanctions are legal and economic instruments; they should not be treated as identical to naval deployment or armed attack. An individual asset freeze, an oil-trading prohibition, a general license authorizing a defined transaction, and a military strike operate through different authorities and affect different populations. Their consequences must be traced separately before they can be assessed together.

The United States justified growing pressure partly by citing drug trafficking, criminal organizations, and the perceived threat posed by Venezuelan officials. The relationship between trafficking networks and the state was a legitimate subject of criminal investigation, but official allegations were not judicial findings. Drug markets were transnational: cultivation, cocaine production, maritime shipment, corrupt facilitation, banking, and consumption occurred in multiple jurisdictions. Describing Venezuela as a single undifferentiated trafficking organization would hide the institutional distinctions that prosecutors would have to establish and the many Venezuelans uninvolved in illicit trade. It would also obscure the demand and financial infrastructure outside the country that made trafficking profitable.

Military and maritime operations increased the danger of miscalculation. Public reporting in late 2025 described U.S. force deployments, strikes against vessels suspected of drug activity, threats of additional action, and contested claims about their legal foundation. International critics questioned whether lethal force was justified against suspects who could potentially be apprehended and tried. The distinction between an armed conflict and a law-enforcement operation matters: the applicable standards for necessity, imminence, evidence, and review are not interchangeable. Government statements about a vessel's cargo or affiliation are claims requiring corroboration; they are not substitutes for published findings about each encounter.

For the Venezuelan government, external military pressure provided a rationale for mobilization and emergency rhetoric. Officials invoked sovereignty and national defense and portrayed internal dissent as potentially connected to a foreign offensive. That strategy raised the risk that ordinary political opposition would be confused with support for hostile military action. For ordinary people, uncertainty affected prices, transportation, remittances, and expectations about renewed scarcity. The domestic and external crises reinforced one another: Washington's pressure altered the ruling coalition's calculations while Caracas's repressive response reinforced international concern over rights and democracy.

Regional governments faced an equally difficult choice. Neighbors wanted to prevent wider conflict, additional displacement, and disruption of border commerce, but they differed in their assessments of Maduro and of U.S. policy. Colombia had direct responsibilities along a long and porous land frontier, including humanitarian protection and the containment of armed networks. Caribbean governments and shipping interests watched maritime routes, energy flows, and prospects for escalation. Brazil, with extensive northern-border ties and a tradition of regional diplomacy, had reasons to oppose a precedent that could expose any state to unilateral external force. Differences in ideological orientation did not map neatly onto differences in legal concerns.

The escalation also exposed the limits of relying on one instrument for several goals. Sanctions might restrict a targeted actor's ability to transact but could also complicate oil production and payments. Criminal indictments might establish a basis for domestic proceedings but did not by themselves provide international authorization for armed entry. Military coercion might remove a leader quickly while leaving political reconstruction to institutions that could not be rebuilt by air power. These distinctions form the causal background to 3 January 2026. The operation did not come out of an empty sky, but neither was it an inevitable result of earlier sanctions or the disputed 2024 election.

From electoral dispute to military escalation

Causal chain: disputes over the 2024 vote weakened acceptance of Maduro’s mandate; continuing repression impeded domestic settlement; sanctions and trafficking allegations intensified U.S. pressure; military deployment and threats increased the risk of interstate force; the January raid removed Maduro without dismantling existing state institutions.

Section 115.4

3 January 2026: The Operation and the Capture

In the early hours of 3 January 2026, U.S. forces carried out strikes and a special-operations raid in and around Caracas. Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, were seized at their residence and transferred to U.S. custody. President Donald Trump publicly acknowledged authorizing the operation, and U.S. officials described it as an apprehension mission undertaken in connection with criminal indictments. Venezuelan officials described a violation of the country's sovereignty and an armed attack. These contrasting descriptions reflect not simply political disagreement but different legal frameworks: the U.S. executive portrayed the goal as law enforcement, while other states evaluated the use of armed force across an international border.

The operation required substantial planning, military coordination, intelligence, and control of access routes. American accounts stressed the ability to suppress defenses and reach the target rapidly. Public records and reporting identify explosions and strikes affecting military facilities and surrounding areas; the precise operational sequence should not be reconstructed as settled simply from public-relations summaries. A technical account of aircraft movements or the geography of the city is useful only when the record supports it. Caracas is a capital in a mountainous coastal region where military sites, residential districts, transport corridors, and strategic installations occupy a constrained geographic setting. The proximity of civilian populations made the consequences of strikes particularly serious.

Casualty reporting illustrated how difficult it was to establish a single immediate toll. U.S.-sourced reporting in the first week cited roughly seventy-five people killed; Venezuela's interior minister subsequently stated that one hundred had died. These are attributed contemporary accounts, not reconciled and independently audited final figures. Death tolls can differ because an initial operational assessment counts known casualties at a certain moment, whereas later state statements may include additional civilian, military, or unidentified victims. The appropriate historical method is to preserve the date, institution, reported category, and uncertainty rather than present the highest or lowest number as unquestionably authoritative.

Maduro and Flores were taken to New York, where federal criminal proceedings followed. Their physical capture was a verifiable event. It must nevertheless be distinguished from a voluntary transfer, negotiated extradition, trial verdict, or lawful international arrest. Extradition normally requires a legal relationship between states and a specified procedure; military seizure on foreign soil raises a different set of questions. U.S. criminal jurisdiction and the international legality of the operation are examined separately in later sections. The fact that defendants can appear before a court does not itself answer whether the preceding resort to force complied with the UN Charter.

The immediate political outcome in Caracas defied the simple prediction that removing Maduro would transfer power to the opposition. The vice president, Delcy Rodríguez, and other senior officials remained connected to functioning institutions. The Supreme Tribunal of Justice issued a decision about succession the same day, and Rodríguez took the presidential oath on 5 January. The armed forces did not dissolve; the National Assembly did not become an opposition legislature overnight; ministries, security bodies, and state-owned enterprises continued operating. Maduro's disappearance from the presidential residence was therefore a sharp personal rupture within a state whose administrative machinery and political networks were not automatically displaced.

For Venezuelans, the meaning of the event differed according to experience. Some who had endured detention, exile, or election exclusion saw an opening for political change. Others feared the precedent of foreign intervention, the human cost of the strikes, and uncertainty over state services. Many held both concerns simultaneously. Families attempted to establish the safety of relatives, obtain reliable information, and anticipate whether armed confrontation would expand. The historical importance of 3 January is consequently larger than the removal of one man: it brought competing theories of sovereignty, law enforcement, democracy, and political continuity into direct collision, without producing an immediate democratic settlement.

Section 115.5

Maduro and Flores before the U.S. Courts

The capture brought Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores into the federal criminal system of the United States, but the case did not begin in January 2026. U.S. prosecutors had advanced narcotics-related accusations in earlier years, and a superseding indictment released after their seizure developed charges concerning trafficking, weapons, and alleged abuse of official positions. As the litigation proceeded, prosecutors had to prove each charged offense under the applicable rules of evidence. The defendants' appearance in a New York courtroom did not turn an indictment into a verdict. The presumption of innocence and the opportunity to challenge evidence, witnesses, and jurisdiction remained essential to any accurate description of the case.

At their initial appearance on 5 January, the defendants contested the proceedings. Public reporting described their subsequent detention in the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn. The location mattered in institutional terms: once in U.S. custody they became defendants subject to detention orders, motions, hearings, and rules of criminal procedure, rather than political figures available to be exchanged by decree. At the same time, their defense could raise extraordinary claims because Maduro asserted presidential status and immunity. Courts would have to distinguish the defendant's former position, the nature of alleged conduct, the U.S. executive's recognition policy, and relevant domestic and international legal doctrines.

On 22 July 2026, a U.S. district judge set an initial trial date of 1 June 2027, while defense counsel indicated an intention to challenge prosecution on immunity grounds. The proceeding's schedule did not remain static. On 8 October the Justice Department announced a further superseding indictment that added an allegation of conspiracy to commit torture to the existing narcotics and weapons allegations. At a hearing on 15 October the defendants entered not-guilty pleas to the new accusations; subsequent reporting placed the trial on 14 June 2027, with an immunity argument scheduled for a later date. The original June 1 date and the revised June 14 date belong to separate procedural moments and should not be merged.

The torture allegations also demand an evidentiary distinction. Independent human-rights investigators had extensively documented violence against political detainees within Venezuela. Such institutional findings are relevant historical context, but they are not the same as proof beyond a reasonable doubt that these particular defendants committed every act alleged in a U.S. indictment. Prosecutors alleged a conspiracy involving coercive state institutions; defense counsel had the right to contest factual allegations, admissibility, jurisdiction, and attribution of responsibility. A historian may describe evidence of an organized system of repression and still avoid prejudging a specific defendant's criminal guilt. The standards and institutional purposes of UN fact finding and federal prosecution differ.

The international political implications of the prosecution were profound. A criminal conviction could impose a judicial consequence on individuals, but would not by itself resolve Venezuela's constitutional leadership, reconstruct courts at home, or determine ownership of the nation's natural resources. Conversely, a dismissal on jurisdictional or immunity grounds would not erase the historical record of repression or decide whether the U.S. military operation was lawful internationally. Legal outcomes can answer particular questions without answering every political question that motivated the dispute. This chapter treats the forthcoming proceedings accordingly: relevant to accountability, but not an automatic substitute for Venezuelan democratic choice.

As of 16 November 2026, the trial had not begun, no criminal conviction had been entered against Maduro or Flores in the case described here, and contested questions of immunity and the legality of capture remained unresolved. A hearing scheduled beyond the historical cutoff cannot be narrated as if its outcome were known. In a contemporary textbook, such limits are not a weakness to conceal. They mark the difference between established procedural history, prosecutorial allegation, defense argument, and an adjudication that has not yet taken place. For students the case illustrates how the criminal justice system can become a central site of international political conflict while retaining its own rules and evidentiary burdens.

Section 115.6

Delcy Rodríguez and the Succession Question

Delcy Rodríguez assumed presidential responsibilities under a judicial decision rather than through an opposition victory or an immediately conducted national election. The Constitutional Chamber of Venezuela's Supreme Tribunal of Justice issued decision No. 0001 on 3 January 2026, responding to Maduro's removal and invoking constitutional provisions concerning the temporary absence of the president. It interpreted Articles 234 and 239 in light of its authority under Article 335, and ordered the executive vice president to assume the functions of the presidency. Rodríguez was sworn in on 5 January. For state administrators and foreign interlocutors, this provided an operative line of authority; for constitutional critics, it opened substantial questions about the nature and limits of the absence.

Article 234 governs temporary absence, including the vice president's role and periods for which executive functions can be exercised. Other constitutional provisions address permanent absence, the calling of elections, and the succession that follows. The difficult issue was how to classify an incumbent removed by a foreign military operation, held in another state's custody, and unable to discharge executive duties. Unlike death or voluntary resignation, forced absence did not neatly match every constitutional category. The court's choice offered continuity, but did not mean that every legal scholar accepted it as the sole possible interpretation. An institution can face an unprecedented fact pattern and still have its own interests in preserving governing continuity.

Some Venezuelan legal analysis emphasized a ninety-day period and asked whether legislative action or an extension should follow. Around early April 2026, the expiration of an initial period became the focus of constitutional debate. It would be inaccurate to treat an asserted date as though a neutral court had definitively ruled that the interim presidency automatically ended on that day. The status of the National Assembly, the interpretation of the Constitution, the court's earlier ruling, and the contested distinction between temporary and permanent absence all mattered. The Academy of Political and Social Sciences and other jurists could challenge the legal theory without thereby gaining power to implement an alternative succession.

Rodríguez's position exposed a tension between formal continuity and political transformation. She was a senior figure in the previous administration, with longstanding responsibility for economic and diplomatic affairs. Her access to ministers, public enterprises, the National Assembly leadership, and the military made her capable of negotiating with Washington and conducting government business. But institutional capacity is not the same as an independently renewed popular mandate. Supporters could argue that the state needed continuity during crisis; critics could argue that a temporary official should not undertake long-lasting reforms without an election and trustworthy checks on executive power. Both claims must be assessed in light of specific constitutional provisions and public conduct.

The continued role of Jorge Rodríguez, Delcy's brother and president of the National Assembly, highlighted the influence of existing political networks. Diosdado Cabello and senior security figures remained consequential. The question was therefore not whether one individual could issue statements as president, but how decisions were being made within a political coalition that had survived a foreign intervention. A cabinet announcement, a law approved by the legislature, and a judicial order were different institutional acts, even if they emerged from overlapping alliances. The public could observe significant policy change without evidence that ruling-party domination of the state had ended.

Interim authority was also affected by international recognition. Washington worked with Rodríguez despite having used force to remove Maduro. The ability of foreign governments to transact with the interim administration did not supply a complete domestic constitutional answer; it reflected the practical need to engage whoever exercised governmental functions. Legal continuity, diplomatic effectiveness, and democratic legitimacy diverged. Students should compare the court's January decision, the text of the constitutional articles, the April debate about time limits, and later political promises. Only by keeping the dates and categories separate can they understand why Venezuela could have an effective interim executive while still lacking a settled account of who should govern after it.

Section 115.7

Armed Forces, Intelligence Services, and State Continuity

The most important evidence that the fall of Maduro was not the fall of the entire political system lay in the persistence of security and administrative institutions. The armed forces, intelligence agencies, police services, prosecutors, and prison authorities did not cease operating on 3 January. Personnel who had served the earlier administration continued occupying important posts, although public reporting also described retirements, reassignments, and negotiated institutional adjustments during the year. Some government functions were indispensable regardless of the occupant of the presidency: hospitals needed supplies, borders required administration, wages and pensions had to be paid, and the petroleum system needed technical staffing. Such continuity can preserve life and order while also protecting coercive arrangements from scrutiny.

The UN independent international fact-finding mission's 2026 report made this duality central. It assessed events after the U.S. operation and concluded that important structures and patterns of repression persisted, although the volume and character of some abuses had changed. That institutional analysis challenged any assumption that one leader personally administered every detention, authorized every interrogation, or independently maintained every network of intimidation. It also suggested that sustained reform requires changing practices, commands, oversight, and incentives. The replacement of one minister may matter, but what counts for accountability is the behavior of the institution and the possibility of independent investigation.

Intelligence services have historically wielded influence through information gathering, detention, and surveillance. In Venezuela, human-rights reporting repeatedly examined facilities and chains of authority associated with the civilian and military intelligence systems. Security personnel could have different motives: loyalty to a political project, professional identity, fear of retaliation, access to economic benefits, or concern for their own safety in a negotiated transition. An analysis that treats them as a single bloc ignores fractures that can shape both reform and resistance. In an uncertain political environment, senior officers may seek guarantees while lower-ranking personnel continue ordinary operational work.

Civilian armed groups commonly described as colectivos added another layer. The term includes organizations with different histories, local roles, and relationships to state officials, and it must not be used to imply that every community organization was armed or violent. The UN mission examined cases in which such groups were linked to coercion, intimidation, and state-linked abuses. Repression sometimes occurs through informal actors precisely because a government benefits from denying direct responsibility. The evidentiary challenge is to show contacts, command or coordination, acts of violence, and the protection or prosecution of perpetrators, not to infer guilt from political affiliation alone.

The 24 June earthquakes tested the security and administrative system in a different way. Military units, police, civil protection, medical services, and local volunteers were drawn into search, rescue, logistics, and public-order tasks. A capable emergency response could enhance the credibility of government institutions, while politicized distribution of aid, restrictions on independent assistance, or failures to communicate could deepen mistrust. UN investigators recorded concerns that civil society had raised about restrictions on relief and information. It would be equally wrong to assume that every rescue operation was propaganda and to declare the response successful solely on the basis of the number of official press releases. Emergency governance became an observable measure of the state's relationship with citizens.

Security-sector continuity raised a difficult transition problem: how to reduce abuse without suddenly disabling the institutions that provide basic order. Wholesale purges might provoke resistance or administrative collapse; unconditional guarantees might entrench impunity. Credible reform requires transparent detention registries, enforceable judicial review, civilian oversight, protection for whistleblowers, and individualized accountability for serious crimes. Civilian and military actors need clearly defined roles. By late 2026 there were signs of altered practices and personnel, but no verified wholesale reconstruction of the security state. The Venezuelan case thus illustrates a broader principle of historical change: the physical displacement of a ruler and the institutional transformation of power proceed on different timetables.

Section 115.8

Political Prisoners and the Amnesty Law

The treatment of detainees became one of the clearest tests of whether the interim government represented more than a change of leadership. Venezuelan rights organizations had long documented arrests associated with protest, union activity, journalism, political organizing, and allegations of conspiracy. The phrase political prisoner needs careful handling: it is often employed by advocates to describe people detained for peaceful expression or participation, while courts may record formal charges that the government portrays as ordinary crimes. The crucial questions are whether the alleged conduct was genuinely criminal, whether proceedings respected due process, whether detention was arbitrary, and whether access to counsel and humane treatment were guaranteed.

Beginning before January 2026, and continuing afterward, authorities released numbers of detainees. The UN fact-finding mission later estimated that approximately 800 to 1,000 people were released between September 2025 and August 2026. It did not say that the entire total was freed only after the U.S. operation. Some releases were conditional; some were difficult to verify because official public lists were incomplete. Researchers must distinguish release from acquittal, release from termination of a case, and a judge's authorization from the actual person's departure from custody. Families awaiting confirmation understandably attached significance to each announcement, but aggregate figures were insufficient to describe individual legal status.

On 19 February 2026 the National Assembly approved the Law on Amnesty for Democratic Coexistence. Its stated purpose was reconciliation, and it purported to address political events spanning from 1999 into 2026. That broad date range can give an impression of comprehensive coverage, but eligibility depended on the kinds of incidents listed, exclusions, and judicial implementation. Some exclusions were consistent with the principle that serious international crimes should not be erased by a general political amnesty. Other exclusions were criticized as vague enough to exclude peaceful dissenters accused under broad security offenses. The law's text therefore matters as much as its title, and the legal procedure by which an application is granted matters as much as a public pledge of reconciliation.

Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International raised concerns about the restrictions and about the use of discretion. One reported example involved union workers whose amnesty applications were denied because their alleged acts were deemed outside the specified political events. In April the interim leadership announced that the amnesty process had reached a new administrative stage while the law remained in force; such declarations did not establish that every eligible person had received relief. UN monitoring reported that hundreds remained arbitrarily detained and that ill-treatment and the legacy of abusive detention facilities persisted. The aggregate picture was mixed: fewer long-term arbitrary detentions and meaningful releases for some families, combined with institutions still able to prosecute or hold critics on disputed grounds.

The law also raised questions about victims of torture and other serious abuses. Democratic reconciliation is not identical to forgetting. Victims may seek truth, access to archives, reparations, acknowledgment, and criminal accountability for conduct that international law does not permit a state to excuse simply by changing leadership. A well-designed amnesty must specify the relationship between relief for persecuted demonstrators and accountability for unlawful detention or torture. It must also be administered by credible adjudicators rather than becoming another political filter. A detainee's release without restoration of civil rights, employment, or reputation can leave many effects of repression intact.

By the cutoff, a historian could document a genuine change in the scale of some forms of detention without concluding that Venezuela had completed a rights-based transition. Independent organizations disagreed with official presentations of success for reasons grounded in identifiable cases and legal exclusions. The best measure was not the size of a televised release ceremony but whether future critics could organize without arbitrary arrest, whether detention records became transparent, and whether judges could act without executive instruction. This issue also affects elections: voters and parties cannot compete freely if the cost of criticizing the state remains imprisonment, surveillance, or exile.

Section 115.9

Petroleum after Maduro: The Hydrocarbon Reform

Oil had shaped Venezuelan statehood long before Maduro, financing national budgets, imports, infrastructure, public employment, and social programs while exposing the economy to fluctuating prices and production problems. That history, developed in Chapters 105–109, explains why petroleum occupied the center of negotiations after January 2026. A country with extensive underground reserves does not automatically possess the cash flow of a prosperous oil exporter. Revenue depends on wells, maintenance, skilled labor, diluents, refineries, pipelines, ports, insurance, sales contracts, and access to payment systems. Past investment interruptions and deteriorating facilities made any promise of rapid recovery contingent on costly reconstruction.

On 29 January 2026, Venezuela adopted significant amendments to its organic hydrocarbons law. They expanded opportunities for private participation in primary activities and changed the commercial and contractual arrangements under which foreign and domestic operators could work. Supporters argued that an industry starved of capital, technology, and market access required a more flexible legal framework. Critics warned that extraordinary political circumstances, uncertain oversight, and external influence could make national resources vulnerable to deals negotiated without adequate public scrutiny. The legislation remained a formal act of the Venezuelan state; its content should be read directly rather than inferred from optimistic investor statements or partisan labels.

The reform did not simply abolish state ownership or prove that output would double. Ownership of underground hydrocarbons, the right to conduct extraction, the choice of contractor, the right to market crude, and the division of revenue are different legal matters. A government can retain constitutional ownership of resources while granting companies stronger contractual powers. Royalty, tax, cost recovery, arbitration, and environmental responsibilities determine whether a contract provides public benefit. Comparing the new framework to the earlier dominant role of PDVSA requires attention to individual provisions and negotiated terms, not a binary choice between total nationalization and complete privatization.

American sanctions policy was another necessary condition for large-scale international reengagement. The U.S. Treasury issued and repeatedly amended general licenses that authorized carefully defined transactions involving Venezuelan-origin oil, diluents, equipment, selected enterprises, and related services. A general license is not the disappearance of a sanctions program. It establishes conditions, exceptions, and sometimes reporting requirements that can be altered again. Businesses therefore had to assess not only Venezuelan legislation but U.S. law, financing risk, contract enforceability, physical security, and the possible future change of political leadership. The legal architecture of trade became part of the political relationship between the two states.

Oil diplomacy also carried a distributional question. If an increase in exports produced foreign currency, would funds improve water systems, hospitals, schools, wages, and housing, or primarily support debt service, politically connected intermediaries, and infrastructure benefiting exporters? The answer depended on budgets and institutions rather than only barrels shipped. Environmental costs likewise mattered in western oil fields, eastern heavy-oil zones, and coastal processing areas. Pollution, methane losses, damaged equipment, and the health of nearby communities are part of petroleum history; a record of extraction without a record of environmental consequence is incomplete.

By late 2026, the reform represented a significant legal and diplomatic shift, but one unfolding under an interim government whose democratic authority remained contested. It was not yet evidence that Venezuela had achieved durable energy-sector reconstruction or a transparent social contract. A careful evaluation would track verified production volumes, audited receipts, contract terms, workforce conditions, environmental compliance, and public spending across several years. The case illustrates an enduring problem across Latin American resource economies: states may gain bargaining power from valuable reserves yet remain vulnerable when the institutions that convert resource wealth into public benefit lack stable accountability.

Section 115.10

Sanctions Relief, Oil Revenues, and Economic Sovereignty

The financial arrangements created after the operation made sovereignty measurable in bank accounts, approvals, and disbursements. Rather than merely asking who owned Venezuela's oil, analysts had to ask who could authorize its sale, where buyers deposited proceeds, which rules governed transfers, and who determined the final use of the money. The Treasury's licenses and U.S.-managed revenue arrangements made financial infrastructure an instrument of foreign policy. Washington described these mechanisms as safeguards intended to improve accountability and support recovery; critics considered the degree of outside control an infringement on Venezuelan economic self-determination. Both positions should be tested against the published rules and flow of funds.

A KPMG accounting report released publicly through the U.S. State Department in November described more than twelve billion dollars in Venezuelan resource-sale proceeds handled during the first nine months of 2026. Reporting on the audit put gross proceeds at approximately $12.26 billion, overwhelmingly related to crude oil but also including smaller sales of gold, other minerals, and fertilizer. These categories are important. The figure was not a measure of annual gross domestic product, not proof of twelve billion dollars of new foreign investment, and not a count of physical barrels. Nor did it mean that the full amount was simply paid into the unrestricted treasury of the Rodríguez administration.

The accounting described funds moving through designated arrangements and substantial disbursements through foreign-exchange transactions and commercial banking channels in Venezuela. Reporting cited a balance of approximately $168.6 million in a particular U.S.-managed account as of 30 September. Such a remaining-account balance cannot be subtracted from the gross figure to prove the entire difference was paid to one government ministry: flows may involve separate accounts, intermediaries, transaction dates, and designated beneficiaries. The specific period and reporting boundaries must accompany every total. Revenue received, revenue held, proceeds distributed, and spending finally audited are distinct indicators of financial control.

For ordinary Venezuelans, foreign currency availability could affect imported food, medicine, industrial inputs, and domestic price stability. A foreign-exchange auction might allow firms to buy dollars for legitimate imports but could also favor companies with privileged access. Supply chains react not only to headline revenue but to settlement delays, exchange rates, the reliability of banks, and household purchasing power. Even a well-audited oil-revenue mechanism cannot guarantee broad welfare if wages are low, public services remain damaged, and participation in the economy is unequal. The use of foreign contractors also requires scrutiny of procurement standards and beneficial ownership, especially where corruption has previously distorted state investment.

The arrangement created unusual accountability questions across borders. U.S. officials had discretionary authority under their sanctions and revenue framework, but Venezuelans possessed the strongest democratic stake in the use of their country's resources. U.S. congressional review, independent accounting, and press reporting could make portions of the system more transparent while leaving the question of Venezuelan parliamentary oversight unsettled. Whether emergency custodial arrangements could become normal mechanisms without domestic public authorization was a political controversy, not merely an accounting technicality. Students should treat the report as evidence about money moving through a specific system, while evaluating the legitimacy of that system separately.

The November audit was an important historical document because it allowed claims about billions of dollars to be compared with categorized transactions and a dated accounting period. Yet it also exposed what an audit does not prove: that social spending reached intended households, that contracts were fair, that environmental harms were addressed, or that constitutional authority to impose the arrangement was uncontested. By the cutoff, renewed oil trade and diplomatic cooperation were real developments, but durable economic sovereignty remained a contested goal. The economic case therefore mirrored the political case: practical functioning had resumed faster than broad agreement over who should control institutions.

Section 115.11

Gold, the Orinoco Mining Arc, and Minerals

Petroleum was not Venezuela's only strategic resource. In the south, the Orinoco Mining Arc and surrounding regions contained gold and other minerals that attracted formal investors, informal miners, criminal organizations, public security actors, and Indigenous communities with territorial and environmental claims. The geography differs sharply from the coastal petroleum system. Mining activity occurs in forested and riverine landscapes where official supervision may be thin, transport routes contested, and environmental damage difficult to reverse. Gold is unusually portable and valuable, which creates opportunities for smuggling, illicit taxation, corruption, and money laundering through networks extending beyond Venezuela.

The United States adjusted mineral-related authorizations during 2026. Treasury notices included a sequence of licenses covering transactions in Venezuelan-origin minerals, supply of equipment or services, and negotiation of potential contracts. The 2 September amendments, including General Licenses 51D, 54C, and 55A, had distinct purposes and conditions. Treating the announcement as blanket permission for every mining venture would be inaccurate. Businesses faced compliance, traceability, beneficial-ownership, environmental, and sanctions questions, while Venezuelan communities faced decisions about whether outside investment would produce legitimate employment or accelerate pressure on land and water.

The economic incentives of gold differed from those of crude oil. Petroleum requires capital-intensive infrastructure and large-scale export channels; gold can move through opaque intermediaries in small quantities, making it harder to trace from a mine to a legal buyer. A license permitting specified transactions cannot alone guarantee that the metal was extracted lawfully, that workers were protected, or that Indigenous residents had consented to operations affecting their territories. Independent verification requires information about mine sites, chain of custody, tax payments, armed control, and community testimony. Claims that a kilogram of gold is lawful solely because it passes through a licensed exporter should be treated with caution.

Illegal mining had also intersected with organized crime and security operations. Armed actors sometimes sought to control roads, rivers, equipment, workers, and the movement of gold rather than to manage a stable industrial mine. The transnational group Tren de Aragua was discussed in this wider security environment, but its origin, membership, activities, and individual responsibility should not be collapsed into a general accusation against everyone in migrant or mining communities. A U.S. air strike in June 2026 reportedly killed Héctor Rusthenford Guerrero Flores, known as Niño Guerrero, during a coordinated security operation. The killing was a consequential event, but the disappearance of a leader did not establish the disappearance of every criminal network attributed to him.

Indigenous nations in southern Venezuela confronted a different relationship to minerals. Territorial governance, cultural continuity, food production, river systems, and health could be affected by mercury contamination, deforestation, armed intimidation, and unregulated excavation. There is no single Indigenous position: communities can disagree internally about employment, conservation, territorial authority, and engagement with the state. Nonetheless, their right to meaningful participation cannot be reduced to an afterthought once concessions have been announced. The history of resource extraction across Latin America repeatedly shows that formal legal ownership and local social legitimacy may diverge, especially when revenues flow toward distant capitals and intermediaries.

A genuinely successful mineral-policy transition would therefore involve more than replacing restricted trade with licensed trade. It would require independent geological and environmental information, control over armed criminal activity, credible labor and safety rules, territorial safeguards, transparent contract terms, and a public account of revenues. By November 2026 the policy shift was visible; its long-term impact on illegal mining and community welfare remained uncertain. The mineral economy illustrates how resource diplomacy, criminal governance, environmental damage, and Indigenous rights were interconnected rather than separate topics competing for the attention of the Venezuelan state.

Section 115.12

Everyday Life in 2026

National crises are experienced first through the practical routines of daily life. For Venezuelans in 2026, questions about the interim presidency, U.S. sanctions, and petroleum law coexisted with shortages of reliable water, intermittent electricity, low real earnings, medical costs, crowded housing, and family separation. Economic policy may improve the availability of imported goods before it improves a public-school building or a neighborhood clinic. An announcement that foreign currency has entered the country says little about the monthly budget of a pensioner or informal worker unless prices, wages, and services are studied. Politics becomes tangible through access to medicine, transport, employment, and basic safety.

On 24 June, two powerful earthquakes struck north-central Venezuela in close succession. The U.S. Geological Survey recorded a magnitude 7.2 event at 22:04:31 UTC and a magnitude 7.5 event thirty-three seconds later. Damage was especially severe in La Guaira, the Greater Caracas region, and neighboring states, with important consequences for roads, buildings, hospitals, schools, and utilities. The geology of northern Venezuela, near the boundary between the Caribbean and South American plates, created shaking and landslide hazards that no political settlement could prevent. But the human consequences were mediated by housing quality, building standards, geography, emergency access, and the state of health infrastructure before the disaster.

Early humanitarian reports produced dated and changing casualty counts. PAHO's 14 July information cited 4,333 deaths, 16,740 injuries, and 17,907 displaced people at that reporting point. UNICEF's report dated 5 August reproduced official figures as of 3 August of 6,125 deaths and 60,992 people treated in hospitals, while recording 23,811 people in temporary camps. These figures should not be combined as though they measure identical categories. Being injured, treated in a hospital, displaced, residing in a camp, and recorded as dead are distinct variables; denominators, geographic coverage, and revisions change over time. Later reporting does not make the earlier report false; it alters the state of knowledge.

The disaster's effects continued long after the first rescue period. Authorities had evaluated tens of thousands of homes by early August, many with damage or restrictions on use. Water-and-sanitation systems required emergency supplies. Families needed temporary accommodation and safe school settings, while health teams addressed both traumatic injuries and continuity of ordinary treatment. Children faced risks from separation, disrupted education, and emotional distress; pregnant women, disabled people, and older residents needed services that could be interrupted by damaged facilities. Humanitarian responders had to balance national coordination, local capacity, international assistance, and the practical difficulties of delivering supplies to affected neighborhoods.

The political implications were unavoidable. Authorities could use emergency programs to demonstrate governing capacity, yet relief distribution could also be shaped by patronage or restricted access. The UN fact-finding mission recorded civil-society concerns about aid politicization and constraints on independent activity and media reporting. Such concerns call for scrutiny, not automatic assumptions that every government response was ineffective. PAHO documented concrete deliveries of medical equipment and technical support, and UNICEF reported services reaching affected children and families. The history of a disaster must evaluate both real assistance and unmet need; neither a government claim of success nor a critic's general denunciation substitutes for evidence from individual communities.

Everyday life also illuminates the divided experience of the diaspora. Relatives abroad sent remittances, worried about family safety, and considered visiting or returning, while people living in damaged areas sometimes moved within Venezuela rather than across an international border. A family displaced by the earthquakes was not automatically an international refugee; a migrant who had left years earlier was not a 2026 earthquake casualty. The country faced overlapping but analytically distinct crises of infrastructure, governance, livelihoods, and mobility. Any account of national recovery that begins and ends in the presidential palace misses how most Venezuelans judged 2026: by whether they could live securely and plan a future.

International Law and Regional Relations

Use of force, jurisdiction, diplomacy, and contested territory

Section 115.13

The UN Charter and the Legality of the Operation

The central international-law dispute was not whether governments could criticize Maduro or investigate drug trafficking. It was whether the United States could use armed force inside Venezuela to seize a political leader without Venezuela's consent or authorization from the UN Security Council. Article 2(4) of the United Nations Charter generally prohibits the threat or use of force against another state's territorial integrity or political independence. Recognized exceptions include force authorized by the Security Council and the right of self-defense under Article 51 when its conditions are met. A domestic arrest warrant or indictment is not expressly among those exceptions. Consequently, the legal characterization of the January operation depended on more than its announced purpose.

U.S. officials described the raid as an apprehension of indicted persons and also invoked security threats from narcotics and transnational crime. A president's public explanation is primary evidence of the administration's position, but it cannot settle the international-law assessment. When an operation uses aircraft, strikes against military targets, and armed personnel crossing borders, other states may assess it as a use of force even if a government calls the ultimate seizure law enforcement. The scale of the action and the military means employed cannot be removed from the analysis by vocabulary. Domestic statutory questions about U.S. presidential and congressional authority are additional questions rather than replacements for the Charter framework.

The alternative legal argument raised by critics was that no relevant exception authorized unilateral intervention. On 5 January, the Security Council discussed Venezuela, with the UN Secretary-General warning about destabilization and a troubling precedent for relations among states. The meeting revealed sharp differences over the legitimacy of Maduro and the operation, but a council debate was not a judicial ruling. The United Nations has no general power to retroactively authorize a military act merely because member states issue statements. Nor does an inability to adopt a condemnation prove an action lawful: Security Council politics and rules of voting affect what the body can decide.

Some commentators explored whether the refusal to acknowledge Maduro as a legitimate president altered the sovereignty analysis. Diplomatic recognition affects representation and dealings with a government, but it does not extinguish the international legal personality of Venezuela as a state. Even an authoritarian or electorally illegitimate government cannot simply be treated as lacking borders. The rights of Venezuelans to political participation and freedom from arbitrary detention remain applicable regardless of the leader's standing. One can consistently condemn the state's repression and reject an unauthorized external use of force; neither concern logically cancels the other.

Analogies to earlier Latin American interventions were powerful but imperfect. The 1989 U.S. invasion of Panama and removal of Manuel Noriega demonstrated the long history of a dominant external power combining accusations of criminal conduct with military action against a sitting leader. Yet the legal arguments, diplomatic environment, scale, and subsequent political developments of two events separated by decades are not identical. Historical comparison works best when it identifies specific continuities—regional inequality of military power, intervention rhetoric, the problem of sovereignty—and specific differences, rather than claiming that one episode mechanically repeats another.

For students, the decisive method is to keep legal questions in separate columns. Was the use of force lawful under the Charter? Did U.S. law authorize the operation? Could the defendants be prosecuted in federal court? Were particular wartime or law-enforcement rules followed when lethal force was used? Did Venezuelan constitutional institutions respond validly? These questions engage different legal texts and decision makers. As of the cutoff there was no final international judicial judgment resolving the legality of the operation. The dispute nonetheless changed regional debate by demonstrating how quickly claims about crime and democracy could become claims about force and sovereignty.

Section 115.14

Immunity, Jurisdiction, and Accountability

The U.S. criminal case raised another set of issues that should not be collapsed into the Charter dispute. Federal courts ordinarily exercise jurisdiction over offenses defined by federal law when the necessary jurisdictional requirements are satisfied. Some crimes have cross-border components, such as conspiracies concerning imports into the United States; prosecutors must establish the charged elements and the court's authority. But a former or purported current head of state may invoke immunity doctrines that complicate an otherwise ordinary prosecution. The question of a court's power to hear an indictment is distinct from the question of how the defendant was physically brought into custody.

Head-of-state immunity is often discussed in two forms. Personal immunity, or immunity ratione personae, may protect certain incumbent senior officials from another state's criminal jurisdiction during their term, subject to complex questions of recognition and exception. Conduct-based immunity, or ratione materiae, concerns some official acts and may be argued after an officeholder leaves office, though its scope and relation to serious international crimes remain debated. Domestic courts draw on their own law, executive recognition positions, and international-law arguments. Neither category can be resolved by the bare assertion that a defendant once occupied a presidential residence or that a government dislikes the defendant.

The U.S. position included the claim that it had not recognized Maduro as Venezuela's legitimate president. Defense lawyers argued that his claimed status and the circumstances of capture barred the prosecution. These were adversarial legal positions, not verdicts. A court might need to consider recognition doctrine, the timing of alleged conduct, whether acts were private or official, and whether specific statutes conferred the necessary jurisdiction. Even the meaning of 'former president' could differ between the U.S. executive's description and the Venezuelan state's official account of events. Such distinctions show why legal analysis requires definitions rather than rhetorical labels.

Accountability for torture posed an additional challenge. International human-rights bodies had documented serious abuses associated with Venezuelan detention systems and had recommended investigation and accountability. Their findings could contribute to public understanding, but criminal attribution requires linking charged conduct to individuals under an applicable legal standard. A former leader's political responsibility for a system and personal criminal liability for a particular conspiracy do not always coincide. Courts must evaluate evidence about orders, knowledge, assistance, intent, and applicable offenses. The assumption that every documented abuse automatically proves every accusation against Maduro or Flores would abandon the evidentiary care demanded of a textbook.

There were also questions about remedies. Victims might seek domestic prosecution of perpetrators, truth commissions, reparation programs, institutional reform, or access to international mechanisms. A high-profile U.S. case could attract attention to abuses while leaving the majority of Venezuelan victims outside the proceedings if their experiences did not match the charged federal offenses. Conversely, a rights-based transition should not define accountability only through the interests of an outside state. It ought to create opportunities for Venezuelan families, former detainees, and independent organizations to secure evidence and participate in justice. Legal punishment, truth seeking, institutional reform, and reconciliation are related but not interchangeable.

At 16 November 2026, the case offered no final answer to the immunity controversy or to the substantive criminal charges. The presumption of innocence remained operative. Yet the unresolved status of the litigation did not oblige historians to treat the documented existence of arbitrary detention and torture as mere rumor; the relevant UN investigative findings were separately grounded in multiple sources and a stated method. The historical task is to explain the relationship between competing forms of accountability without pretending that a criminal court is a universal tribunal over Venezuela's politics, the UN Charter, or the entire history of the Bolivarian state.

Historical Vocabulary

Head-of-state immunity and jurisdiction

Personal immunity (ratione personae) concerns protections associated with certain serving senior officeholders. Conduct-based immunity (ratione materiae) concerns some official acts. Jurisdiction refers to a court’s legal authority to hear an accusation. The scope and interaction of these doctrines can be contested.

A defendant may challenge immunity, territorial jurisdiction or the circumstances of seizure separately. None of those issues can be settled by assuming that a criminal indictment is itself a conviction or a universally valid international warrant.

Section 115.15

Latin America's Responses

The capture divided Latin American diplomacy, but the fault line did not simply separate supporters and opponents of Maduro. Many governments could reject his claim to a free electoral mandate while objecting to unilateral force. On 4 January, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Uruguay, and Spain issued a joint statement expressing serious concern and rejecting the military action on international-law grounds. Their stance highlighted a traditional regional principle: intervention by a stronger state can threaten every country's autonomy, whatever the domestic character of the government targeted. The reaction reflected both legal principle and regional memories of past intervention.

Colombia's concern was especially immediate. Its lengthy frontier with Venezuela had already borne consequences of conflict, illicit economies, migration, and political instability. A wider military confrontation could displace new populations, close border crossings, disrupt trade, or benefit armed networks exploiting uncertainty. Brazil faced different geographic and humanitarian responsibilities along the northern frontier, including Indigenous territories and trade routes far from the capitals. Caribbean states watched the risks to shipping and tourism as well as regional security. In each case foreign-policy statements were responses to concrete national vulnerabilities, not merely ideological gestures.

Other governments emphasized the significance of Maduro's removal or supported U.S. security objectives. Still others attempted to hold both positions: criticism of repression in Venezuela together with concern about the means used by Washington. It is misleading to reduce all governments to one pro-American or anti-American category. Official statements varied in whether they discussed democracy, criminal allegations, sovereignty, civilian casualties, migration, oil, or the need for negotiations. A comparative table of statements issued at the same point in time is more revealing than assigning countries permanent labels, especially as several governments modified their practical engagement with Caracas during 2026.

The Organization of American States also became a forum for assessing the crisis. Its secretary general issued a statement on 3 January, while member governments debated the meaning of democracy and nonintervention. The OAS did not have a single unified regional voice. Regional organizations depend on member states with distinct commitments and changing governments; a public statement from a secretariat is not equivalent to a binding decision adopted by all states. This lesson is important for students accustomed to treating 'Latin America' as a political actor capable of a unified policy. Geographic regions do not possess one collective government simply because they share a past shaped by empire and intervention.

Responses also changed as the immediate military phase ended and diplomacy resumed. Governments had to decide whether they would work with Rodríguez on migration, health, border control, and trade despite unsettled legitimacy. A refusal to endorse the operation did not require permanent diplomatic isolation, just as engagement did not necessarily signal approval of the succession process. The speed with which practical issues reappeared showed the constraints on ideological purity in foreign relations. Countries could negotiate public-health responses after the June earthquakes while maintaining legal objections to the January military action.

The dispute illustrated the continuing importance of Latin American agency within a hemispheric order shaped by U.S. power. The region was not merely reacting to Washington; neighboring states used diplomatic forums, public statements, and bilateral relationships to assert interpretations of international order that served their own interests and experiences. Nevertheless, their influence over the outcome was uneven. This asymmetry is part of the story, as is the refusal of several governments to accept that disagreement with Maduro implied consent to invasion. The enduring historical issue was how sovereign states with different political systems could disagree about democracy without abandoning constraints on the use of force.

Section 115.16

Cuba, China, Russia, Guyana, and Wider Relations

Venezuela's crisis reached beyond its immediate South American neighbors because the country had built diplomatic, commercial, security, and energy ties across several regions. Cuba had long maintained political and technical cooperation with Chavista governments, exchanging services and benefiting from energy relationships. Changes in Venezuelan oil exports and governance could therefore affect Cuban economic conditions as well as Caracas's choices. The history is not reducible to external control by Havana: Venezuelan leaders made domestic decisions, while Cuban relations were one component of a broader political alliance. Nevertheless, the future of that relationship became a practical question under the new U.S.–Venezuelan arrangements.

China's position involved petroleum sales, debt exposure, investment, diplomatic principles, and concern about precedents for unilateral intervention. Chinese officials could oppose the use of force and still negotiate with whichever Venezuelan administration exercised effective control over shipments and contracts. Commercial interests were not equivalent to unconditional political support for Maduro. Changes in sanctions and financial routing affected the practical means by which Chinese purchasers, lenders, and contractors could transact. The longer history of Chinese economic engagement in Latin America, examined in Chapter 114, contextualizes the episode without making China the principal author of Venezuelan politics.

Russia had historically provided military and diplomatic support to Venezuelan governments, and Russian officials condemned the U.S. operation. Their interests included strategic relationships, arms cooperation, and challenges to U.S. regional predominance. Yet public condemnation and the capacity to reverse the transfer of Maduro to New York were very different. The episode revealed both the reach and limits of external partners' influence. Countries that had treated Venezuela as a site of geopolitical competition now confronted a practical administration in Caracas negotiating closely with Washington, while ordinary Venezuelans experienced the consequences of choices made in several capitals.

Guyana introduced a distinct territorial issue. The century-old controversy over the Essequibo region had already been brought before the International Court of Justice, which had issued jurisdictional rulings and provisional measures. The 2026 crisis did not extinguish that dispute. In May 2026 the Court held hearings in the case concerning the 1899 arbitral award, while Guyana reaffirmed its position that the existing boundary was valid and Venezuela continued to contest the framework. As of 16 November, there was no final merits judgment settling the question in this chapter. The sovereignty of Guyana cannot be suspended because Venezuela changed its executive, and Venezuela's right to contest a case through lawful procedures does not authorize force against neighboring territory.

Petroleum and maritime resources made these relations economically consequential. Guyana's offshore oil development and Venezuela's own reserves formed part of a larger Caribbean and Atlantic energy geography. Investment decisions, shipping security, insurance, and boundary uncertainty could affect neighboring economies as well as diplomatic relations. Geography, however, does not determine inevitable conflict: judicial proceedings, regional diplomacy, and transparent negotiation provide alternatives to escalation. It would be premature to forecast that the January operation either ended the Essequibo dispute or necessarily made conflict more likely. The outcome depended on decisions and institutions not yet settled at the cutoff.

The wider lesson is that Venezuela's international position cannot be described as simple dependence on a single ally or adversary. Its relationships with the United States, Cuba, China, Russia, Guyana, Caribbean governments, and international institutions intersected in commerce, law, diplomacy, security, and humanitarian relief. Some ties altered rapidly after January, while others rested on historical commitments and geographic facts that persisted. Evaluating sovereignty therefore requires attention to all the external relationships through which a country negotiates its options, not just the most powerful government whose military action transformed the immediate political landscape.

Normalization, Mobility, and Unfinished Transition

Diplomacy, migration, elections, and the five-volume historical synthesis

Section 115.17

Normalization and the Limits of Partnership

Diplomatic relations between Washington and Caracas changed more rapidly than Venezuela's political institutions. After the January operation, American officials cooperated with the interim administration on petroleum trade, security matters, and the mechanics of engagement. The United States formally reopened its embassy in Caracas on 30 March 2026 after years of closure, an observable diplomatic milestone. The reopening of a building and resumption of diplomatic staffing should not be confused with the immediate restoration of all visa and consular services. Contemporary reporting noted that some consular arrangements still required contact with the U.S. embassy in Bogotá. Diplomacy had resumed, but its scope and convenience for citizens remained limited by practical capacity.

Normalization also did not signify that the United States accepted every Venezuelan constitutional claim. Washington could choose to deal with an interim executive as the actor capable of making commitments without conceding that the 2024 electoral controversy had been settled. Venezuela's officials could likewise enter commercial and diplomatic arrangements without abandoning public insistence on sovereignty. The result was a relationship of considerable practical cooperation paired with unresolved arguments over legitimacy. It illustrated a general feature of diplomacy: states often transact with effective authorities during transitional periods because problems involving borders, trade, detained persons, or public health cannot be indefinitely suspended.

Oil and foreign-exchange arrangements gave the partnership an unusually intrusive financial dimension. Licenses specified who could trade; U.S.-managed accounts affected the timing and channel of proceeds; and contracts potentially reshaped an industry central to public finance. American officials described these arrangements as supervision intended to protect the use of resources and stabilize the economy. The UN fact-finding mission, by contrast, assessed the extent of U.S. influence over political, economic, and security decisions as exceptionally strong. These evaluations need not be reconciled into one slogan. A historian should establish which decisions were made, by whom, and under what formal authority before assessing the extent of dependence.

There were important differences between dependence, cooperation, and consent. A government may invite technical assistance voluntarily, accept commercial conditions because it urgently needs investment, or act under the shadow of superior military power. The historical record of Venezuela in 2026 contained elements of all three. Signed agreements and diplomatic photographs reveal public conduct, but do not necessarily disclose every bargaining constraint. Unequal power does not erase Venezuelan agency, nor does a Venezuelan official's signature prove that the wider population endorsed a major long-term change. The degree of democratic accountability and availability of independent review are essential to judging whether the partnership was legitimate.

The June earthquakes made cooperation both more necessary and more politically delicate. Health agencies, donor governments, and local authorities had to coordinate urgently over medical equipment, reconstruction, and transport. The interim administration's ability to allow effective and impartial international assistance became a test of state capacity. In disaster response, a foreign government's cooperation may save lives while the same government's military or economic policy remains contested. One relationship can simultaneously involve conflict, dependency, and genuine common interests. Students should resist the temptation to label the entire bilateral relationship either liberation or domination without analyzing its component policies.

Normalization also had political limits at home. Political prisoners remained, institutions lacked agreed independence, and election dates were not formally fixed at the cutoff. Reopening an embassy was not the same as reopening electoral competition. Investment in oil wells was not the same as fair access to public services. A decrease in repression did not automatically confer a new popular mandate. These differences matter for the interpretation of international recognition, which can help a government operate before it has completed domestic reforms. The historical lesson is that international engagement may facilitate a transition, stabilize an interim arrangement, or preserve an authoritarian coalition in modified form; evidence through November 2026 did not establish which long-term path would prevail.

The partnership nevertheless transformed the immediate political economy of the Caribbean and northern South America. Firms explored opportunities that had been legally restricted; officials reopened channels of communication; and migrant families watched for signals about documents and travel. The United States acquired significant leverage over commercial flows, while the Rodríguez administration demonstrated that it could exercise authority despite its origins in the previous political system. A credible evaluation must measure outcomes beyond ceremony and headline investment announcements: the independence of courts, publication of contracts, civilian control of security institutions, transparent aid distribution, and the opportunity for Venezuelans to replace leaders by a verifiable vote.

Chain of effects: Maduro’s capture created an interim administration; the new administration was able to negotiate with Washington; changes to hydrocarbon law and U.S. licenses opened channels for transactions; audited revenue-management arrangements created a new form of external influence; and diplomatic operations resumed.

Limits: this sequence does not demonstrate a fair election, comprehensive restoration of rights or completion of oil-sector recovery. Those outcomes depend on institutional choices and verified social results rather than the speed of diplomatic announcements.

Section 115.18

Refugees, Return, and Regional Consequences

The Venezuelan diaspora entered 2026 with millions of people living outside their country, many for years. UNHCR's country overview placed the global number of Venezuelan refugees and migrants at nearly 7.9 million, based on data compiled from governments and partners. This is a cumulative population figure, not a count of people leaving during 2026 and not a measurement of those displaced by June's earthquakes. Its categories combine people with different legal statuses, migration dates, reasons for departure, and degrees of vulnerability. Policy discussions become misleading when a stock of people abroad is described as a sudden new exodus or every migrant is classified under one legal definition of refugee.

Colombia hosted a large share of Venezuelans and faced practical questions about documentation, housing, schools, work, and cross-border health services. Peru, Ecuador, Chile, Brazil, and other Latin American and Caribbean destinations experienced different combinations of humanitarian needs and domestic political debate. Host-country policies changed over time as governments weighed labor-market integration, social services, electoral pressure, and the treatment of irregular migration. Venezuela's neighbors could not wait for a complete political settlement in Caracas to determine whether a child would attend school or an adult could obtain a work permit. Their policies were part of the historical consequences of the crisis, not peripheral reactions to a national event.

The January operation created new expectations without producing automatic returns. Some Venezuelans hoped that reduced repression, improved commercial conditions, and future elections would make home safer. Others feared that the institutions that had driven their departure were still present, that they might face legal or economic obstacles on return, or that work and schooling abroad had become too important to abandon. Return migration is a household decision involving children, savings, housing, documents, legal risk, and the ability to find employment. The removal of one political leader is only one factor. Research must distinguish public statements of intent to return from verified movements recorded at borders or in administrative systems.

The 24 June earthquakes added a different displacement pattern. Damaged homes and disrupted services forced people into shelters, temporary camps, relatives' residences, and other locations within Venezuela. UNICEF reported 23,811 people remaining in temporary camps as of 3 August, while the wider humanitarian response dealt with affected populations beyond camps. These were not necessarily all international migrants or even all people displaced since the initial event. Camp residents, internally displaced people, and households affected but still living at home form overlapping but distinct categories. Geography matters: families in La Guaira and the Caracas coastal corridor encountered damaged roads and housing, while Venezuelans living abroad followed the disaster through calls, remittances, and efforts to locate relatives.

The diaspora was not politically uniform. Some exiles supported Maduro's capture; others rejected a foreign military operation even while opposing his government; still others focused on family safety rather than political declarations. People who migrated because of inflation, poverty, or family reunification could have different experiences from those who fled direct political persecution. Women often sustained family networks through caregiving, informal employment, and remittances, and faced distinct vulnerabilities when documentation was insecure. Indigenous communities living along international borderlands also navigated migration and state policies that did not always align with their traditional territorial relationships. The term Venezuelan diaspora should not erase these social differences.

Transnational criminal networks complicated the picture, but the vast majority of migrants were not members of criminal organizations. Tren de Aragua and other groups exploited weaknesses in border and labor protection systems, sometimes victimizing migrants through extortion or trafficking. Equating Venezuelan nationality with membership in such groups distorted evidence and could produce discrimination against people seeking protection or legal work. Reliable analysis identifies specific organizations, documented incidents, and criminal prosecutions while keeping a clear boundary between collective migration patterns and individual criminal responsibility. Host-country security policy works best when it protects migrants from predation rather than treating the entire population as suspect.

The regional dimension of Venezuela's future lay in these concrete ties. Children born abroad, workers integrated into foreign labor markets, binational families, and communities connected by remittances would persist even if a democratic election occurred quickly. The diaspora was simultaneously an outcome of Venezuela's historical crisis and a constituency with legitimate interests in its reconstruction. By the cutoff, no evidence established a massive comprehensive reversal of migration. Regional governments and international agencies therefore faced long-term obligations to support access to documents, education, employment, protection, and voluntary, safe, informed choices about return.

Section 115.19

Elections, Reconciliation, and the Unresolved Transition

The political question left open after Maduro's removal was whether Venezuela would hold elections that the population and independent observers could verify and accept. The Rodríguez administration and U.S. officials made statements favoring elections, but promises were not an enacted timetable. By November 2026 no fixed, generally accepted presidential election date had been established in the documents reviewed for this chapter. Discussion of an election during 2027 was a proposal and political objective, not evidence that the event had been legally scheduled. The distinction matters because an indefinite interim period can normalize extraordinary authority, even if that authority began as an emergency response.

Election administration was itself part of the reform agenda. The 2024 crisis had shown that publicly available local tabulations, independent monitoring, access for all candidates, and genuine competition were not optional technical details. U.S. officials stated in October 2026 that Venezuela's electoral institutions required major change before a credible vote could occur, and reported negotiations concerning judicial and electoral bodies. Those descriptions were political plans, not proof that new independent institutions had been fully created. Replacing the names on an electoral council without transparent selection, guaranteed access to records, and meaningful judicial review would risk reproducing familiar patterns under a new leadership.

Pressure from within Venezuela became more visible late in the year. Reuters reported nationwide opposition demonstrations on 22 October calling for elections and political change. María Corina Machado returned to Venezuela on 25 October, a major event for an opposition movement whose leaders had faced exclusion, hiding, or exile. Her status as the 2025 Nobel Peace Prize laureate increased her international profile but did not itself decide Venezuela's constitutional rules. She and other opposition figures demanded credible institutional reform, while the interim leadership had incentives to manage the timing and terms of competition. Edmundo González Urrutia's 2024 claim remained part of the unfinished debate about representation.

Reconciliation would involve more than bargaining among well-known politicians. Former political prisoners, labor organizers, journalists, military personnel, victims of violence, public employees, Indigenous communities, business owners, and migrants possessed different interests in accountability and stability. A process that only redistributed cabinet posts could fail to address abuses at the local level or economic conditions for ordinary people. Conversely, a policy of indiscriminate exclusion of everyone who served the previous state could make basic administration impossible. Transitional arrangements need credible protection against serious crime, opportunities for peaceful participation, and mechanisms to distinguish individual responsibility from membership in a broad political movement.

The constitutional issue raised by Article 234 remained relevant. A genuinely temporary executive should have a clear legal basis, an identifiable mechanism for review, and a route toward a mandate obtained from voters. If legal institutions were themselves contested, agreement over how to supervise the interim period would be difficult. The legislature, judiciary, electoral authority, and civilian organizations could become either checks on the executive or extensions of a ruling coalition. Public confidence would depend on observable practices, such as the publication of official acts, equal treatment by courts, press freedom, and the independent investigation of detention abuses.

The June earthquakes complicated this transition. Reconstruction requires emergency procurement, long-term investment, and sometimes exceptional administrative powers. The government could argue that immediate elections would disrupt recovery, while opponents could argue that prolonged emergency rule allowed the same authorities to spend major resources without a renewed mandate. These concerns need not produce a single universal schedule; they do make transparent budgeting, legislative oversight, and independent humanitarian access essential. Citizens displaced from their homes would also need practical guarantees that a future vote could reach them without discriminatory barriers. A transition that excludes disaster-affected residents would reproduce rather than remedy unequal citizenship.

Three broad trajectories were visible by the cutoff, none yet established as the outcome. Negotiated reform could produce credible elections and rights guarantees. Interim cooperation might preserve much of the Chavista administrative system while changing foreign partnerships and economic policy. Renewed confrontation or institutional breakdown could interrupt both political and humanitarian recovery. These are analytical possibilities, not predictions masquerading as facts. The most useful indicators were measurable: functioning independent courts, a verifiable voter register, released political detainees, protection of opposition organization, accountable security forces, published election rules, and an effective process for resolving disputes. Venezuela's future was still being made, rather than already decided by the events of 3 January.

Section 115.20

Historical Synthesis: Sovereignty and Latin America through 2026

Chapter 115 closes a history that began long before the modern nation-state of Venezuela existed. Across five volumes, the book has examined societies that organized power, labor, knowledge, trade, spiritual life, and territory on their own terms; empires that claimed political and economic authority; independent states that struggled to make constitutional promises real; and twentieth-century movements that promised national renewal while often concentrating executive power. The Venezuelan crisis of 2026 was not a simple repetition of any earlier epoch. Yet it made visible recurring questions: who can speak for a community, who controls resources, what limits the exercise of force, and how can people challenge those who govern them?

The first volume emphasized that Indigenous peoples possessed sophisticated histories before European arrival. That premise remains relevant when today's resource policies are debated in the Orinoco basin and borderlands. Indigenous communities are not merely interest groups appearing after mineral concessions are signed; they have territorial histories, institutions, and contemporary rights. Likewise, the history of Atlantic slavery and colonial society helps explain why racial hierarchy, labor coercion, and unequal access to political power continued to matter after independence. An account of 2026 that concentrates exclusively on ministers, military commanders, and outside presidents would miss the social histories that made the wider textbook necessary.

The independence era raised the problem of sovereignty as both a declaration and a practice. Nineteenth-century republics asserted that legitimate government should derive from local political communities, yet their institutions were often restricted by inequality, regional conflict, and foreign pressure. In 2026 the Venezuelan controversy again separated formal sovereignty from actual control. The U.S. operation challenged the principle of nonintervention. The interim government exercised administrative power without a settled renewed electoral mandate. External actors influenced oil revenues and policy. Sovereignty therefore could not be judged solely by a flag, constitutional text, or diplomatic recognition: it involved who could make and enforce decisions and whether citizens had meaningful authority over those decisions.

The twentieth century added further lessons about charismatic politics, revolution, military influence, and state-led development. Chavismo invoked social justice and redistribution, while critics documented electoral exclusions, concentration of power, and rights abuses under Maduro. The historical assessment must account for both the movement's popular roots and the documented institutional costs of authoritarian governance. Foreign opponents of an authoritarian leader can also act unlawfully. Understanding one form of injustice does not require excusing another. The most rigorous analysis resists the reduction of Venezuela's history to either the personality of Maduro or a story in which the United States is the central protagonist.

Economic history is equally continuous. Silver, sugar, coffee, nitrates, copper, petroleum, and gold have linked Latin America to global markets while shaping conflict over taxation, labor, territory, and public spending. The January hydrocarbon reform and later U.S.-managed revenue arrangements reopened older disputes about who captures the value of exports. A resource contract can be technically profitable and still fail to deliver broad public benefit. A transparent account of gross receipts cannot replace evaluation of spending, environmental harm, or democratic authority. The region's dependence on commodities has never been a purely geological fact; it has reflected choices about ownership, institutions, fiscal policy, transport, labor, and international bargaining.

The June earthquakes revealed the limits of a history organized only around presidents and wars. A natural hazard became a major human disaster through its interaction with settlement patterns, vulnerable buildings, medical capacity, and the distribution of aid. PAHO, UNICEF, the UN, and Venezuelan responders produced a dated record of deaths, injuries, damage, displacement, and assistance; their measures required careful comparison rather than careless aggregation. The disaster tested state legitimacy through the delivery of services and the treatment of vulnerable residents. It also showed that the most urgent problem for a family may be shelter or clean water even while a nation debates the constitutional status of its interim president.

The diaspora made Venezuelan history part of the histories of Colombia, Peru, Brazil, Chile, the Caribbean, North America, and beyond. Nearly 7.9 million refugees and migrants abroad, according to UNHCR's overview, cannot be represented as a single political faction or a one-year movement. Their lives demonstrate that states are never wholly enclosed within their borders. Remittances, work, children, discrimination, legal status, and return decisions connect distant communities. Criminal networks that prey on people along migration routes should be studied and prosecuted on evidence, not used to justify collective suspicion against a nationality. The migration history of the twenty-first century is a history of agency and difficult choices, not merely of population totals.

This chapter's cutoff is 16 November 2026. By that date Maduro and Flores were in U.S. custody and awaiting trial; Delcy Rodríguez exercised interim presidential functions; the oil and mineral policy framework had changed; diplomatic engagement had expanded; and major rights and humanitarian problems remained. The 2024 election dispute, criminal proceedings, the contested legality of the January operation, constitutional succession questions, the Essequibo case, the future electoral timetable, and the long-term distribution of oil proceeds were not finally resolved. The appropriate historical conclusion is not a confident prediction about a democracy achieved or lost. It is a statement about the evidence available and the questions still requiring accountable decisions.

Historical education ends not with certainty about the future but with the capacity to distinguish records from claims, institutions from individuals, and short-term events from long-term transformation. The capture of a president could occur in hours, while reforming detention systems, rebuilding earthquake-damaged communities, restoring public trust, and reestablishing credible elections could take much longer. The five-volume history has treated Latin America as a region of diverse societies, not an arena whose fate is determined in one capital. Venezuela in 2026 brings that method into the present: history remains open, and its meaning depends on evidence, democratic participation, human rights, and the continuing choices of Latin Americans themselves.

Chapter Summary

Maduro’s seizure on 3 January 2026 was a dramatic personal and military rupture. It did not dissolve the political coalition or state structures through which he had governed Venezuela.

The July 2024 presidential election remained a major legitimacy dispute. The CNE announced a Maduro victory without providing the station-level official results necessary for transparent verification; the Carter Center found serious failures of electoral integrity.

Escalating sanctions, maritime pressure and allegations of trafficking preceded the U.S. operation. Each instrument had a different legal and political basis; none made the operation historically inevitable.

The capture involved armed force on Venezuelan territory and was followed by transfer to New York. Casualty figures circulated in January were attributed contemporary reports rather than an agreed, audited total.

Maduro and Flores faced ongoing federal criminal proceedings. The October 8 superseding indictment added allegations involving torture; those charges remained allegations, and the trial and immunity arguments lay ahead.

The Constitutional Chamber’s January 3 ruling allowed Delcy Rodríguez to exercise presidential functions under an interpretation of temporary absence. Its validity, duration and relation to the electorate continued to be debated.

State institutions remained in operation, including important armed and intelligence structures. UN investigators found meaningful changes in some patterns of detention but also persistent repressive practices.

The February 19 amnesty law and releases of prisoners offered relief, but eligibility rules, exclusions, continuing detention and weak judicial independence limited the conclusion that repression had ended.

The January 29 hydrocarbon amendments expanded private participation in oil activities, while U.S. licenses reshaped commercial possibilities. They did not independently establish production gains or a transparent division of resource wealth.

The November reporting on a KPMG audit recorded approximately $12.26 billion of covered resource-sale proceeds through September. Gross receipts, funds disbursed and balances held are different financial measures.

Mineral licensing changes raised questions of legal trade, illegal mining, Indigenous territorial interests, environmental damage and criminal networks. Permission to transact did not establish the lawful origin of every commodity.

The June 24 earthquakes caused massive death, injury, displacement and infrastructure damage. PAHO and UNICEF reports must be compared by indicator and reporting date, and the response became a test of government capacity.

Latin America’s responses to the intervention, changes in U.S. diplomatic cooperation, the Guyana territorial case and the Venezuelan diaspora revealed a regionally interconnected crisis. By November 16, a binding election timetable and many legal outcomes remained unsettled.

Final Series Reflection

The five-volume history of Latin America has been organized around peoples, institutions, and conflicts rather than a sequence of presidents alone. The Venezuelan crisis of 2026 returns us to those premises. A foreign raid removed one ruler, but did not remove the social inequalities, constitutional controversies, economic dependencies, and institutional traditions through which people experience government.

Historians must resist the temptation to resolve a year of unfinished events by adopting the position of the strongest government or the most prominent opposition figure. Prosecutors, judges, election observers, aid agencies, regional governments, and families affected by violence offer different kinds of evidence. The distinctions between an allegation and a verdict, a receipt and a disbursement, a dated casualty report and a final count, are fundamental to responsible scholarship.

The events of January reopened long-standing questions about sovereignty and intervention. The expansion of oil trade reopened the question of whether resource wealth could finance a more accountable state. The amnesty law reopened demands for justice, yet demonstrated that relief and institutional reform are not the same achievement. The June earthquakes showed why the ability to deliver public services is also a form of political responsibility.

Venezuelans abroad and those displaced within the country remind us that the unit of history is not simply the nation-state. Family networks, human rights, labor, environmental damage, health care, and territorial claims cross boundaries drawn by constitutions and treaties. In these connections, the experiences of Indigenous communities, workers, women, migrants, and political dissenters remain essential rather than supplementary.

A future historian may have answers unavailable on 16 November 2026. The U.S. criminal trial might proceed, change, or be resolved in unexpected ways. Venezuela might achieve a verifiable election, preserve an extended interim arrangement, or experience renewed political conflict. Courts might rule on the Essequibo controversy; reconstruction might succeed or fail. This chapter does not turn any of those possibilities into a fact.

The final lesson of the series is a discipline of historical explanation: locate power, examine its legal justification, trace its material effects, recover the experiences of those subject to it, compare conflicting accounts, and acknowledge the limits of the record. Latin American history does not end at the page boundary of November 2026. The people of the region remain its agents, and the historical consequences of their decisions remain open.
