Madero, Huerta, Villa, Zapata, and the Revolutionary Wars

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A President Murdered, a Revolution Divided

On the night of 22 February 1913, Francisco I. Madero and José María Pino Suárez, the elected president and vice president of Mexico, were taken from their confinement in Mexico City and shot. The movement that had overthrown Porfirio Díaz less than two years earlier had reached a point at which its own president was killed by officers of the army he had left standing.

The two men had been arrested on 18 February, forced to resign on the 19th, and killed on the 22nd, in the last days of a ten-day military revolt in the capital that Mexicans remember as the Decena Trágica, the Tragic Ten Days. Their deaths did not end the Revolution. They opened its largest phase. Within weeks a governor in the north had refused to recognize the new regime, and within a year armies in Chihuahua, Sonora, Coahuila, and Morelos were converging on Mexico City. This chapter asks how a rebellion that began in 1910 as a campaign to remove an aging dictator and restore competitive politics became a series of wars among revolutionaries.

The answer lies in the fact that "the Revolution" was never one movement. Chapter 71 showed how the Porfiriato's modernization, political closure, land concentration, and succession crisis produced widespread grievance. Those grievances were not the same everywhere. Middle-class reformers wanted free elections and an end to reelection. Ranchers and muleteers in Chihuahua resented the political domination of a few families. Villagers in Morelos faced sugar estates that had absorbed their fields and water. Workers wanted wages and organization. Landowners, army officers, foreign investors, and the Catholic Church wanted order and, in many cases, protection of what they held. Díaz's fall united these groups only negatively. What replaced him had to answer questions on which they disagreed.

The chapter follows five phases. Division I covers the uprising of 1910–1911, the capture of Ciudad Juárez, and the negotiated settlement that removed Díaz while leaving the federal army and much of the state apparatus intact. Division II examines Madero's presidency, the agrarian rebellion of Emiliano Zapata, the northern revolt of Pascual Orozco, and the coup of February 1913. Division III follows Victoriano Huerta's regime and the Constitutionalist war that destroyed it under Venustiano Carranza's political leadership and the military leadership of Francisco Villa and Álvaro Obregón. Division IV explains why the victors split, through the Convention of Aguascalientes and the Villa–Zapata alliance. Division V traces the civil war of 1915, the social life of a country at war, the confrontation with the United States, and the Constitutionalists' rise to dominance.

Evidence is unusually abundant and unusually partisan. The Revolution generated manifestos and plans, which tell historians what leaders chose to promise, not what followers wanted. It produced newspapers, stenographic records of meetings, diplomatic cables, military dispatches, photographs, and, soon afterward, memoirs and official histories written by victors to legitimate their rule. Villagers, women, and ordinary soldiers appear mostly in petitions, court and agrarian records, oral histories collected decades later, and the corridos that carried the war into popular memory. The chapter distinguishes documents from commemoration, and it separates what a source claims from what can be established.

Two cautions govern the whole chapter. First, no leader here is treated as a representative of "the people." Madero, Zapata, Villa, Carranza, Obregón, and Huerta each spoke for particular constituencies and each used coercion. Violence came from federal troops, rebel armies, and local strongmen alike. Second, victory did not equal settlement. When the Constitutionalists had defeated Villa's main armies and gained U.S. recognition by late 1915, the questions of land, labor, church and state, and the authority of the national government were still open. The chapter ends at the threshold of the Constituent Congress at Querétaro, whose work belongs to Chapter 73.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the national antireelectionist goals of the 1910 uprising from the local land, labor, regional, and personal grievances that moved different participants.
  2. Explain why the May 1911 Treaties of Ciudad Juárez removed Díaz but left the federal army and administrative apparatus largely intact.
  3. Analyze the constraints and choices that shaped Madero's presidency without reducing his failure to naïveté or weakness.
  4. Compare the Plan of Ayala and the Plan de la Empacadora as expressions of different rural and regional revolts against Madero.
  5. Reconstruct the Decena Trágica, the roles of Huerta, Félix Díaz, and Ambassador Henry Lane Wilson, and the murder of Madero and Pino Suárez.
  6. Differentiate the February 1913 coup, the assassination of Madero, and the subsequent Huerta government.
  7. Trace the development of Constitutionalism from the narrow Plan of Guadalupe of March 1913 to the broader reform commitments of 1914–1915.
  8. Evaluate the military organization, logistics, and railroad use that allowed revolutionary armies to defeat the federal army.
  9. Assess why the Convention of Aguascalientes failed to produce revolutionary unity, comparing Carrancista, Villista, and Zapatista claims to legitimacy.
  10. Contrast Zapatista restitution of village lands with other agrarian policies, and distinguish Villa from Zapata as leaders with different bases.
  11. Interpret the experiences of women, workers, civilians, and refugees as part of revolutionary warfare.
  12. Explain why Constitutionalist military victory in 1915–1916 did not by itself resolve the Revolution's central demands.

Historical Context

Chapter 71 described the regime that Díaz had built since 1876: a government of formal constitutional liberalism and personal rule, sustained by a loyal but aging army, a rural police, a network of appointed governors and jefes políticos, foreign capital, and a political elite that included the group called the científicos. It also traced the pressure points: the concentration of land and water in estates, the loss of communal village property under nineteenth-century liberal land laws, labor conflicts such as Cananea and Río Blanco, tensions between northern elites and rising local rivals, and the question of who would succeed an eighty-year-old president. Madero's book on the presidential succession and his 1910 campaign, discussed there, produced the Plan of San Luis Potosí and its call to arms. This chapter begins with that call.

Geography shaped what followed. Mexico in 1910 had roughly fifteen million people, most of them rural. The north, from Chihuahua and Coahuila to Sonora, was thinly populated, tied by railroads to the United States, and marked by ranching, mining, and commercial agriculture. Its frontier society had armed militias, muleteers, cowboys, miners, and small landholders, some of whom had lost military colony privileges or local autonomy under Díaz. Central and southern Mexico held dense peasant populations, many in Indigenous or mestizo villages, alongside sugar and henequen estates. Morelos, just south of the capital, was the most intensely commercialized sugar region, and its villages faced the most direct pressure on their fields and water. These differences produced different revolutions in different places.

The institutions that would decide the outcome were uneven. The federal army was a professional force with a college-trained officer corps, large in paper strength, and often poorly led and supplied. Irregular units, the rurales, supplemented it. Railroads, built largely in the Porfirian era, tied the north to the center and later became the arteries of revolutionary war. The Church retained influence in education and rural society. Foreign interests held much of mining, oil, railways, and banking, and diplomats in the capital, notably the U.S. ambassador, took an active part in politics. The state's fiscal base, tied to customs and export taxes, would prove vulnerable once war interrupted trade.

Historians have long argued about how to name what happened between 1910 and 1920. Some emphasize a political revolution in 1910–1911 that was transformed by social rebellion; others stress that regional and local revolts, with different aims, were folded into a national narrative afterward. Scholars such as Alan Knight, Friedrich Katz, and John Womack have shown how deeply regional variation mattered, while others have debated how far peasant demands drove events. This chapter follows the regional approach, while remaining attentive to the national political questions that bound the conflicts together.

Section 72.1

The Revolutionary Uprising of 1910 and the Emergence of Regional Armed Movements

The Plan of San Luis Potosí, issued by Madero from Texas in October 1910, called on Mexicans to rise on 20 November at six in the evening. The call was ambitious and the response was thin. Madero had been jailed in San Luis Potosí during the summer election, escaped to San Antonio, and tried to coordinate a rebellion through intermediaries, exiles, and small conspiratorial circles. The authorities uncovered part of the plot. In Puebla, the antireelectionist Aquiles Serdán was killed in a police raid on his house on 18 November, two days before the appointed date, an episode in which his sister Carmen Serdán, who had helped organize the conspiracy, became a symbol of women's participation. When Madero crossed into Coahuila around 20 November he found almost no force waiting for him and withdrew back across the border.

What kept the movement alive was not the capital or Madero's own plans but regional conditions that made local rebellion plausible. In Chihuahua, the movement found its strongest early base. The state had been dominated for years by the Terrazas-Creel family network, whose landholdings were vast and whose control of politics frustrated rival ranchers, merchants, and communities. Abraham González, a Maderista organizer in the state, drew together muleteers, small ranchers, former militia members, and villagers from districts such as Guerrero in the western mountains, where communities that had once held military colony privileges resented encroachment on their lands and autonomy. Pascual Orozco, a muleteer and small merchant, and Francisco Villa, a rural bandit-turned-recruit whose earlier life remains partly obscured by legend, emerged among the local leaders who raised forces in the winter of 1910–1911.

Elsewhere the uprising spread unevenly. In Coahuila and Sonora, smaller bands formed, with Sonora's rebel activity deepened by local resentment among Yaqui and Mayo communities and by rivalries among commercial and ranching elites. In the south, local leaders in Guerrero and Puebla took up arms. In Morelos, where the sugar estates had been absorbing village land for decades, villagers turned toward rebellion in early 1911 when local leaders aligned themselves with the Maderista cause. Emiliano Zapata, a horse trainer and farmer who had been elected to a village leadership position in Anenecuilco in 1909 and had led its defense of land titles, became the principal military leader of the Morelos rebels. He joined the Maderista movement because Madero's plan contained a provision, Article 3, promising restitution of lands taken from small proprietors by fraudulent surveys and legal manipulation.

The point to hold on to is that national and local objectives overlapped but were not identical. The Plan of San Luis Potosí was a political document: it declared the 1910 elections void, called for "effective suffrage" and no reelection, named Madero provisional president, and included a limited promise on land restitution. Madero believed that political reform would open the way for other changes. Many of his followers saw the rebellion differently. For Chihuahuan ranchers, it was about removing a regional oligarchy; for Morelos villagers, about regaining fields and water; for others, about wages, local office, or the opportunity that war offered. Spanish phrases like sufragio efectivo, no reelección described a cause, not the aims of every person carrying a rifle. That gap between program and participants runs through the entire chapter.

Section 72.2

The Capture of Ciudad Juárez, the Treaties of May 1911, and Díaz's Resignation

By early 1911 the rebellion had expanded across much of the north and parts of the center and south, though the federal army still held the major cities. Díaz's government, slow to grasp the extent of the movement, sent troops by rail and attempted concessions, including promises of reform and changes of cabinet. The army, however, was stretched across a large territory, and the rebels' ability to retreat into mountainous country and re-emerge made them hard to destroy. In Chihuahua, the Maderistas defeated federal columns at several points and by spring had isolated the border city of Ciudad Juárez, across the river from El Paso, Texas.

Madero returned to Mexico in February and established a headquarters near Juárez. A commercial and railroad hub, the city was valuable for customs revenue and for its access to arms and supplies across the U.S. border. Madero, wary of the risks of a bloody battle that might provoke U.S. intervention, and hoping for a negotiated settlement, hesitated to order an attack. Orozco and Villa favored action, but the precise trigger remains disputed: historical reconstructions differ over whether the assault began through deliberate disregard of Madero's instructions, confusion at the front, or a response to federal fire. During 8–10 May 1911 revolutionary troops assaulted the city, and on 10 May the federal commander surrendered. Whatever initiated the fighting, the field commanders exercised substantial operational initiative, and their victory transformed the rebellion's politics by demonstrating that the federal army could be beaten and that regional commanders could influence events independently of the nominal national leader. The tension between Madero and his fighters, visible here, would reappear.

Negotiations followed. Díaz's representatives and Madero's group reached agreements on 21 May 1911, known as the Treaties of Ciudad Juárez. The recitals recorded the anticipated resignations of Díaz and Vice President Ramón Corral and the expected interim succession of Foreign Secretary Francisco León de la Barra, who would call elections; the agreement's operative provision formally ended hostilities and provided for gradual disbandment of revolutionary forces as peace was restored. The treaties did not dissolve the federal army, replace the governors, restructure the judiciary, or adopt an agrarian program. Díaz and Corral both submitted resignations dated 25 May 1911; Corral, writing from Paris, specifically requested that his resignation be accepted together with the president's. The former president left Mexico from Veracruz in the last days of May. The old order's figurehead had gone; many of its institutions remained.

Contemporaries and later historians have disagreed about the wisdom of this settlement. Madero, a believer in legal continuity and in avoiding a prolonged civil war, had reasons to prefer a negotiated transfer: the revolutionary forces were irregular and uncoordinated, the country's economy and foreign relations were exposed, and a prolonged war could invite intervention. Critics, including some of the revolutionary commanders, argued that leaving the army and bureaucracy untouched left the revolution vulnerable. The decision to retain the federal army, in particular, shaped what came later, since its officer corps, loyal to the old order or to its own institutional interests, would be the instrument of the February 1913 coup. Whether a sweeping purge was feasible in May 1911 remains a counterfactual question that cannot be settled, but the choice to negotiate rather than dismantle the old state was a decision with consequences, not an inevitability.

Section 72.3

The Provisional Government, Revolutionary Demobilization, and the Limits of the Settlement

From late May to early November 1911, Mexico was governed by León de la Barra, a Porfirian diplomat acceptable to both sides in the negotiations. His government was a coalition of convenience. Its cabinet included Maderistas and Porfirians, and its real power rested on the federal army, which remained under its established command, and on the governors and officials who still held local office. Madero spent these months organizing his supporters into a political party, traveling the country, and preparing for the election. In effect, the revolution's leader was a candidate while the old state administered the transition.

Demobilization was the most delicate task. The armies that had fought Díaz were not unified formations; they were local forces raised by regional leaders, whose men expected pay, recognition, and some form of reward. Some units were incorporated into the rural police and given official status. Others were paid off and sent home. Orozco, who had expected a significant post and was offered the command of the rurales in Chihuahua rather than a larger role, harbored grievances that would feed his later rebellion. Villa accepted a discharge, took up a butcher's business in Chihuahua, and kept a distance from politics. The reintegration of thousands of armed men into civilian life, without a settled program of reform, left a latent source of instability.

The central crisis occurred in Morelos. Zapata's forces had taken Cuautla in mid-May 1911 and were prepared to lay down arms only on conditions: removal of unpopular officials, and above all, a settlement of the land question. Madero met Zapata in June and in subsequent months, and the two men found themselves talking past each other. Madero, committed to legal procedures, offered to study the land question after taking office and asked for disarmament first. Zapata, who held the force that guaranteed any promise, was unwilling to surrender his arms before seeing action on the lands. The planters of Morelos, who held great political influence, demanded that the interim government restore order, and León de la Barra responded by ordering federal troops into the state, commanded in part by Victoriano Huerta, in August 1911. Madero, trying to hold the competing demands together, called for a halt to the campaign, but the pattern was set: federal military pressure on a village-based force whose confidence in Madero was eroding.

The interim period thus showed the underlying structure of the problem. The settlement had preserved the army and administration, and those institutions behaved as they had before, pressing on rebels who had not been disarmed to their satisfaction. Madero was attempting to persuade former rebels that the political victory justified patience, while the old elites, using the press and their positions, portrayed disorder in Morelos as evidence that the revolution had unleashed anarchy. Both sides could point to evidence that supported their fears. This was not a failure of communication alone; it was a conflict between two views of how change should occur, one proceeding through legal and electoral processes and the other through the immediate restoration of land.

Section 72.4

Madero's Election, the Restoration of Constitutional Politics, and the Expectations of Revolution

The presidential election was held in October 1911. Madero ran as the candidate of the Partido Constitucional Progresista, a party formed by his supporters from the earlier antireelectionist movement. José María Pino Suárez, a Yucatecan lawyer and journalist, was the vice-presidential candidate, a choice that displaced Francisco Vázquez Gómez, who had been prominent in the revolutionary junta and who felt slighted. Madero won by an overwhelming margin, and the election was widely regarded at the time as one of the most open in recent Mexican history, though the party apparatus, local officials, and the limited literate electorate still shaped the outcome. Madero took office on 6 November 1911, six months after Díaz's resignation, not immediately after it.

The new government's first months were marked by genuine political freedom. The press, long constrained, now attacked the president, his family, and his policies with little restraint. Independent unions began to organize, and the Casa del Obrero Mundial, an anarcho-syndicalist center of workers, was founded in 1912. Congress, elected in a competitive environment, contained a significant opposition. New parties, including Catholic ones, entered politics. For a generation of liberal reformers and many urban middle-class Mexicans, the restoration of constitutional politics seemed to fulfill the promise of 1910.

But the constituencies that had made the revolution expected quite different things. Liberal reformers expected honest elections and the rule of law. Rural communities in Morelos and elsewhere expected the return of their lands. Former rebels, including those in the north, expected recognition and in some cases office. Workers wanted wages, hours, and the right to organize. Provincial elites who had joined the movement hoped to replace Porfirian officials with themselves. Landowners, merchants, and the Church hoped that the revolution would end quickly and without cost to their property. Foreign investors, including mining, oil, and railway interests, watched warily to see whether the new government would honor concessions and maintain order. The new president needed to satisfy these actors, whose goals sometimes conflicted directly.

The coalition that had overthrown Díaz was thus a coalition of opposition, not of program. Its members agreed that Díaz must go; they did not agree what would take his place. This is the essential point of Division I. The victory of 1911 resolved the immediate crisis of the presidency, but it left unsettled the questions of land, labor, the army, regional power, and the relation of the state to foreign capital. The remainder of Madero's presidency tested whether a government committed to constitutional politics could address those questions without the old state's coercive machinery or the revolutionaries' armed pressure, and whether it could do so before either abandoned it.

Historical Vocabulary
Maderismo, Plan de Ayala, Restitution, and Revolutionary Junta

Maderismo refers to the political movement around Francisco Madero: liberal, constitutionalist, and focused on free elections and no reelection. It was not a social program, though many supporters read social hopes into it. Restitution meant the return of lands, water, and woodlands that villages claimed had been taken from them, in contrast to distribution of land that had never belonged to the recipients. The Plan de Ayala (28 November 1911) made restitution, and a limited expropriation of large estates, central; it is treated in §72.6. A revolutionary junta was a council of leaders that claimed to speak for the movement, such as the junta in San Antonio, Texas, in 1910, and the juntas that Zapatistas and others proposed to choose a provisional president.

These terms matter because the word "revolution" concealed different projects. Using one vocabulary for all of them leads students to assume that every rebel wanted the same thing.

Division II · Sections 72.5–72.8 · 1911–1913

Madero's Presidency and the Collapse of Constitutional Government

Central question: Why was Madero unable to consolidate a durable constitutional government after defeating the dictatorship?

Section 72.5

Madero's Liberal Presidency: Political Reform, Opposition, and Institutional Constraints

Madero governed as a liberal constitutionalist who believed that the main task of the revolution had been accomplished by opening political competition. He respected press freedom even when newspapers mocked him, allowed Congress to function as a real forum of opposition, left the judiciary largely in place, and tolerated strikes and union organizing more than his predecessors had. He established a Department of Labor within the Ministry of Development in late 1911 to mediate disputes, a modest but real step in recognizing labor as a matter of public policy. He also created an agrarian commission to study land questions, though its work was slow and its results small. These measures reflected his conviction that reform should come through law, study, and the gradual strengthening of institutions.

The difficulty was that the institutions he inherited were not his. The federal army kept its officers and structure. Many governors, judges, and local officials owed their positions to the Porfirian system and were not committed to the new order. Madero replaced some, but he left many in place, partly because replacing them all would have required political resources and loyal personnel he did not have, and partly because he distrusted measures that looked like the arbitrary power he had criticized under Díaz. His own movement was divided. The Maderista Congress included followers loyal to him, others who soon criticized him, and groups, such as the Catholic party that entered the legislature, with different aims. Family members, including his brother Gustavo Madero, played active political roles that opponents attacked as favoritism.

Opposition came from several directions at once. Conservatives resented the loss of order and the new political voices. Landowners and business groups worried about unrest and about government interference with property, and some foreign interests were uneasy, particularly oil companies disturbed by a 1912 tax on petroleum. The press, free to attack, amplified every setback. On the other side, revolutionaries who expected social change grew disillusioned, as the president's reliance on legal procedure seemed to delay it. Early challenges came from the Vázquez Gómez brothers, who proclaimed a rebellion in late 1911, and from General Bernardo Reyes, whose attempted uprising in December 1911 failed. Madero survived these challenges, but each showed the thinness of his coalition.

Economic conditions added to the strain. The revolution had disrupted mining, railroads, and agriculture in some regions, and uncertainty reduced investment. Government revenues, tied to customs and export taxes, did not grow quickly enough to finance both an army large enough to maintain order and the reforms that revolutionaries expected. Madero's government pursued a conservative fiscal policy, avoiding large borrowing and large new commitments. That choice protected the government's credit but limited its ability to pay for land purchases, public works, or the expansion of schools that might have built support.

The difference between Madero's program and the demands of other revolutionaries is central. Madero believed that democracy was the precondition of social improvement. Zapata and others believed that land was the precondition of democracy, because villagers who lacked fields could not exercise political independence. Neither view was frivolous. The question was whether Madero could build enough institutional strength, in the army, administration, and party, to carry out reform through legal channels before his opponents on the right and left acted against him. The debate below explores how historians have answered it.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Could Madero Have Preserved Constitutional Government While Satisfying Revolutionary Social Demands?

The question: Was Madero's fall the result of structural conditions that no president could have overcome, or of political choices that a different strategy might have avoided?

Interpretation A — Structural limits: Historians who emphasize structure, among them Alan Knight in his comprehensive study of the Revolution, stress that Madero faced a federal army with its own corporate interests, a landowning class with substantial influence, a press and political class unaccustomed to restraint, a fiscal base too narrow for large reform, and revolutionaries with incompatible demands. On this view, any president attempting liberal reform from within the inherited state would have faced similar pressures, and Madero's conciliatory style was a plausible response to a situation in which he lacked the means to coerce his opponents.
Interpretation B — Political choices: Other interpretations, including older ones that emphasized Madero's idealism, argue that he made decisions that increased his vulnerability. He kept the federal army intact, dismissed revolutionary units, treated Zapata's demands as a problem of order rather than reform, retained officials hostile to him, and delayed agrarian measures. On this view, a more active program of land reform, a reorganization of the army, and a stronger alliance with rural communities might have given him a base to withstand the conservative conspiracy. Critics of this view reply that such measures could themselves have provoked an earlier coup.

Evidence: Both views draw on Madero's correspondence and speeches, congressional debates, newspapers, diplomatic dispatches, army records, and the accounts of participants, many written after his death with the benefit of hindsight. Counterfactual claims cannot be tested directly.

Why it matters: The question affects how the Revolution's subsequent radicalization is understood: as the consequence of a failed liberal experiment, or as a process driven by forces that the liberal experiment could not have contained. It also bears on how historians evaluate constitutionalism as a strategy in a society with deep inequalities.

Student task: Write one paragraph defending each interpretation, then identify what kind of evidence (for example, army records or correspondence with Zapata) would most help decide between them.

Section 72.6

Emiliano Zapata, the Plan of Ayala, and the Agrarian Revolution in Morelos

Zapata's break with Madero came from the failure of negotiation, not from rejection of constitutional politics as such. During the summer and autumn of 1911 Zapata and Madero met several times. Zapata agreed in principle to the dissolution of his forces, but he insisted on a settlement of land claims and the replacement of officials hostile to the villages. Federal military operations in Morelos, the burning and occupation of villages in some districts, and the persistence of planter influence over state politics convinced Zapata and his advisers that Madero could not or would not act. After Madero's inauguration, Zapata, with his closest collaborators, declared that he would not trust promises that depended on a government composed in part of the people who had dispossessed the villages.

The result was the Plan of Ayala, proclaimed on 28 November 1911 in the village of Ayoxustla in Puebla and named for the Morelos village of Villa de Ayala. Its principal drafter was Otilio Montaño, a rural schoolteacher and Zapatista adviser, working with Zapata. The plan accused Madero of betraying the revolution, repudiated him as president, and recognized Pascual Orozco as chief of the revolution, with Zapata taking that role if Orozco declined. Its most important provisions concerned land. Villages and individuals who held titles to lands, woods, and waters usurped by landowners, científicos, or local bosses would reclaim them by presenting their titles and defending them with arms if necessary. A further article called for expropriation, with compensation, of a third of the land of large estates, so that villages could receive fields. Estates whose owners opposed the plan were to be confiscated outright, with part of the proceeds used to compensate war victims.

Students should notice what the plan did and did not claim. It did not reject elections or constitutional government. It demanded that the revolution deliver on promises, most of all on restitution, which Madero's own plan had included in modest form. It distinguished between restitution of land that villages could show they once held, expropriation of part of the great estates, and confiscation of the property of enemies. These were different policies with different legal and social logics, and later movements would adopt and combine them in different ways. The plan was also a proclamation of authority: it claimed that the revolutionary chiefs, not the Madero government, had the right to determine what the Revolution meant.

The social foundation of Zapatismo was the village. In Morelos, communities defended land and water rights through local officers, councils of elders, and collective memory. They kept written titles, some going back to colonial grants, in village archives, and treated those documents as foundations of their legitimacy. Zapata himself had been elected to a position of local responsibility before the Revolution and had led the effort to protect Anenecuilco's titles. The Ejército Libertador del Sur, the Liberation Army of the South, drew its soldiers from these communities, and its commanders, many of them local figures, answered to village expectations. Its strength was local knowledge, popular support in its territory, and the ability to disperse into the villages. Its limit was that its strategic vision centered on Morelos and the restoration of local control more than on conquering the national state.

Federal reprisals strengthened the movement. In 1912 the government sent troops under General Juvencio Robles to Morelos, and the campaign involved forced relocation of villagers and burning of settlements, practices that drove more people to Zapata's ranks. Madero's government, dependent on the army that carried out these policies, was thus responsible for tactics that deepened the rural rebellion. The conflict in Morelos did not end under Madero; it continued, and the Plan of Ayala became the program around which agrarian demands in central and southern Mexico would later coalesce.

Section 72.7

Pascual Orozco's Rebellion, Federal Military Campaigns, and Northern Disillusionment, 1912

While Zapata fought in the south, the northern part of the Maderista coalition fractured. Orozco, the hero of Ciudad Juárez, had been disappointed by Madero's treatment of him after 1911. He had been offered a lesser role than the one he believed he had earned, and he saw Madero's government in Chihuahua as favoring the established elite rather than the rural people who had fought. He had also come under the influence of the Vázquez Gómez brothers, who had their own grievances against Madero, and of members of the Chihuahuan oligarchy who saw an opportunity to undermine the government. In March 1912 Orozco rebelled.

His program, the Plan de la Empacadora, was issued on 25 March 1912. It denounced Madero's government, and it included a broader social program than Madero's own: provisions touching on labor conditions, restrictions on foreign and railway influence, the abolition of the jefes políticos, municipal autonomy, and land measures focused on national and idle lands. Scholars have noted both its genuine social content and the role of conservative backers, including members of the Terrazas-Creel network who financed the rebellion in hopes of weakening the Madero government. The rebellion thus drew from multiple sources: popular discontent among former Maderista fighters, regional elite opposition, and the ambitions of an individual commander.

The government's response relied on the federal army. In the spring, Orozco's forces defeated federal troops at Rellano, an episode that ended with the suicide of the federal commander, General José González Salas. Madero then entrusted the campaign to General Victoriano Huerta, an experienced and politically ambitious Porfirian officer. Huerta organized a more methodical advance up the northern railroad and defeated Orozco at several points, including Conejos and Bachimba in the summer of 1912. Orozco's forces broke up, and he eventually fled to the United States, though some of his men continued irregular resistance.

Villa played a revealing part in the campaign. After a period of civilian life, he raised a force and joined the federal side, putting his cavalry under Huerta's command. The relationship was uneasy. In June 1912 Huerta accused Villa of insubordination and theft of a horse and had him arrested, and he was, according to the accounts of participants, nearly executed before Madero intervened. Villa was transferred to prison in Mexico City, from which he escaped in December 1912 and fled to the United States. The episode soured him on the army's high command, deepened his personal hostility to Huerta, and demonstrated how the federal officers regarded revolutionary volunteers as subordinates, not partners.

The Orozco rebellion shows why opposition to Madero cannot be reduced to a single ideology. It was partly social, in the demands of the Empacadora plan; partly regional and elite, in the support of Chihuahuan conservatives; partly personal, in the grievances of former commanders. It also strengthened the federal army and Huerta in particular, since the defeat of Orozco proved that the army was indispensable to the government. In the process Madero became dependent on the institution that would eventually remove him.

Section 72.8

The Decena Trágica: The February 1913 Coup and the Assassination of Madero

By early 1913 Madero faced conspiracies among military officers and conservative politicians. Félix Díaz, nephew of Porfirio Díaz, had attempted an uprising in Veracruz in October 1912, failed, and been imprisoned. General Bernardo Reyes, held in a military prison after his failed December 1911 revolt, remained a figure around whom discontented officers rallied. A circle of officers, politicians, and businessmen planned a coup, and rumors circulated in the capital. Warnings reached Madero, who dismissed them or trusted that Huerta and other generals remained loyal.

The revolt began before dawn on 9 February 1913. Rebel soldiers freed Reyes and Díaz from their prisons, and the combined forces moved toward the National Palace. The plan collapsed almost at once: Reyes was killed at the palace gate when the guard fired on the column, and the commander of the palace garrison, General Lauro Villar, was wounded. The rebels, led now by Díaz, withdrew to the Ciudadela, a large arsenal in the city. Madero appointed Huerta commander of the loyal forces. For ten days, 9 to 19 February, artillery and rifle fire were exchanged across a densely populated urban area, killing many civilians, damaging buildings, and halting daily life. Estimates of casualties vary but run into the thousands among soldiers and civilians.

The fighting was a stalemate that Huerta chose to prolong. As commander of the government forces, he was in a position to attack the Ciudadela decisively or to leave it alone, and the evidence indicates that he used the interval to negotiate. Gustavo Madero, the president's brother and political lieutenant, arrested Huerta on suspicion, and Madero, persuaded by Huerta's assurances, released him. On 17 February, Huerta went further and arranged the arrest of Gustavo Madero, who was held and killed in the Ciudadela the next day. Huerta, meanwhile, concluded his agreement with Félix Díaz.

That agreement, the Pacto de la Embajada, was negotiated on 18 February in the U.S. Embassy, whose ambassador, Henry Lane Wilson, had been openly hostile to Madero and had urged him to resign. Wilson presented himself as a mediator concerned about foreign lives and property and used the embassy as a meeting place for the conspirators. The pact provided that Huerta would become provisional president and that Díaz's group would have a voice in the new government. Wilson's role was real but should not be exaggerated into a claim that the United States engineered the coup: the plot was Mexican, the decisive actors were Mexican generals and politicians, and Wilson acted on his own initiative, in conflict with instructions from Washington to remain neutral. Responsibility lay with the conspirators. Wilson's conduct, nonetheless, gave them cover and legitimacy.

On 18 February officers loyal to Huerta, under General Aureliano Blanquet, arrested Madero and Pino Suárez at the National Palace. They were induced to sign resignations on 19 February, after promises, later unfulfilled, that their lives would be spared and that they could leave the country. The resignations were routed through a constitutional maneuver. Pedro Lascuráin, the foreign secretary, was next in the line of succession, and he assumed the presidency for a very short time (reports say under an hour), long enough to appoint Huerta as interior secretary and then resign, leaving Huerta as president under the constitutional succession rules. The legalism was a façade over a military coup.

Madero and Pino Suárez were held in the palace for three days. On the night of 22 February they were removed in cars to be transferred to the penitentiary and shot on the way, at the prison's walls, by officers acting under Huerta's government. The official story claimed that they had been killed in an attempted rescue, an account almost universally rejected. The murder transformed Madero, whose reputation had been battered by months of press criticism, into a martyr. It also turned a coup into a moral crisis for any political figure considering cooperation with the new regime.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The February 1913 Coup

Long-term causes

Survival of the Porfirian federal army and administration after the 1911 settlement; the concentration of landed and commercial elites' opposition; unresolved agrarian demands; a fiscally weak government; a political culture accustomed to military arbitration.

Short-term causes

Orozco's rebellion, which made Huerta and the army indispensable; Zapatista war in Morelos; press and congressional hostility to Madero; the plot of Reyes and Félix Díaz; the open hostility of Ambassador Henry Lane Wilson.

Event or transformation

The Decena Trágica, 9–19 February 1913: a military revolt turned into a coup when Huerta, the government's commander, joined the rebels and arrested the president.

Immediate effects

Resignation under duress of Madero and Pino Suárez; Huerta's assumption of the presidency through a legalistic succession; the murder of both men on 22 February; the death of Gustavo Madero.

Long-term consequences

Rejection of the new regime by Coahuila, Sonora, Chihuahua, and Morelos; the outbreak of the Constitutionalist war; a revolution that now meant armed struggle against a military government.

Historical complexity

The coup combined military conspiracy, institutional weakness, personal ambition, and diplomatic interference. No single cause, and no single actor, explains it, and it was neither inevitable nor an unrelated accident.
Historical Vocabulary
Decena Trágica, Huertismo, Golpe de Estado, and Constitutionalism

The Decena Trágica ("Tragic Ten Days") names the military revolt and fighting in Mexico City from 9 to 19 February 1913. Huertismo refers to the regime and supporters of Victoriano Huerta; the word was used by opponents and, later, by historians, and implies military authoritarianism more than a coherent ideology. A golpe de estado is a sudden seizure of government, usually by military or political insiders; the February 1913 events were a coup in this sense even though they were dressed in a legal succession. Constitutionalism, in this period, was the claim of Carranza and his supporters to defend the legal constitutional order of 1857 against a regime that had violated it.

These terms matter because "coup" and "revolution" are not synonyms. Huerta's takeover was a coup against a revolutionary government; the Constitutionalist movement was a revolutionary reaction to it, and both claimed legality.

Division III · Sections 72.9–72.12 · 1913–1914

Huerta, Constitutionalism, and the War against the Federal Army

Central question: Why was the revolutionary movement able to defeat Huerta militarily, and why did victory make its internal divisions increasingly difficult to contain?

Section 72.9

Victoriano Huerta's Government: Military Rule, Political Repression, and Counterrevolution

It is important to separate three things that are often run together: the coup of February 1913, the murder of Madero and Pino Suárez, and the government that Huerta then led. The coup was a conspiracy among officers and politicians. The assassinations were a decision, never fully documented, taken by people close to Huerta, and the question of how directly he ordered them has been debated, though his government's responsibility is not seriously in doubt. The Huerta regime was a distinct project, an attempt to restore order through the army, and its fortunes depended on whether it could gain recognition at home and abroad.

Initially the regime had broad support among groups that had been uneasy with Madero. Many landowners, merchants, Porfirian politicians, and Church-linked groups welcomed an end to what they saw as disorder. Most state governors recognized Huerta, and the governments of several European powers, notably Britain, were prepared to deal with him, partly out of concern for investments in oil and railways. Félix Díaz was sent abroad on a diplomatic mission, removing a rival. Huerta's cabinet mixed Porfirian figures and conservatives. Some former Maderistas and some rebels, including Orozco and his followers, rallied to him, and Orozco's irregulars were placed at the regime's disposal against northern enemies. The government's hope was to pacify the country through a mixture of force and bargaining.

The regime's reliance on the army deepened. Huerta set out to enlarge the federal forces substantially, and the available estimates suggest they grew several-fold during 1913–1914, though the numbers on paper exceeded those who could be armed, paid, and kept in the field. Recruitment depended heavily on the leva, forced conscription that swept up laborers, prisoners, and the poor, and that produced soldiers of uncertain loyalty. Officers were drawn increasingly from the regular army, and the government used loans, paper issues, and forced contributions to pay for it, putting pressure on an economy already damaged by war.

Repression took violent forms. Abraham González, the Maderista governor of Chihuahua, was arrested and killed in March 1913. Opponents were jailed or disappeared. The most notable case was Belisario Domínguez, a physician and senator from Chiapas, who in a Senate speech in September 1913 denounced Huerta as a usurper and called on Congress to remove him. He was abducted and murdered in early October. When many deputies protested, Huerta dissolved Congress on 10 October 1913 and arrested scores of legislators. Elections held on 26 October were widely regarded as a farce, and the regime's constitutional veneer fell away. The press faced closures and censorship.

By late 1913 Huerta ruled as a military dictator facing open rebellion in much of the country. His regime held the capital, the central states, and the railway network, but its authority beyond them was contested, its finances were strained, and the international recognition it wanted was withheld by the United States. The paradox of the regime was that the more it relied on force to impose order, the more it generated resistance, and the more it needed to expand its army and its taxes.

Section 72.10

Regional Resistance, Revolutionary Financing, and the International Crisis of Huerta's Regime

Opposition to Huerta reorganized in the same regions that had backed Madero, along with new ones. In Coahuila, Governor Venustiano Carranza and the state legislature refused to recognize the new government. In Sonora, the legislature, with the support of local officials and military leaders, took a similar stance. In Chihuahua, after the murder of Abraham González, Villa crossed the border from Texas in March 1913 with a handful of men and began raising forces. In Morelos, Zapata rejected Huerta's overtures and continued his war. The opposition was not a single organization; it was a set of regional movements that found a common enemy, with Carranza providing a banner of legality to the northern ones.

Money, arms, and supplies determined what each force could do. Revolutionary armies in the north depended on proximity to the U.S. border, which allowed purchase of rifles and ammunition, and on control of towns where customs duties, taxes on cattle and mining, and forced loans could be collected. Villa's forces seized cattle from estates and sold them across the border; they also took control of mines and haciendas, confiscated properties of Huerta's supporters, and issued paper money. In Sonora, Constitutionalist leaders taxed mining companies and commerce. Zapatista Morelos relied more on local food production, village support, captured weapons, and small-scale arms purchases. Huerta's government, by contrast, drew on customs revenue from ports, loans, and the forced extraction of resources from the territory it held.

The international dimension became critical in 1913–1914. Woodrow Wilson, inaugurated in March 1913, was not the same as his Ambassador in Mexico, Henry Lane Wilson, to whom he was unrelated, and the new president disapproved of the way Huerta had come to power. He refused to recognize the regime, recalled the ambassador, and sent personal emissaries to assess the situation. In February 1914 he lifted the arms embargo that had affected the Constitutionalists, allowing them to buy weapons in the United States, while continuing to press for Huerta's departure. Britain and other European powers, concerned with oil and investments, were more willing to deal with Huerta, but the U.S. position weighed heavily on the regime's credit and standing.

The crisis escalated in April 1914. On 9 April, a small party of American sailors was arrested in Tampico by Huerta's local forces, then quickly released, and the U.S. commander demanded a formal apology and a salute to the flag. When Huerta refused to satisfy the demand fully, and with news that a German ship carrying arms for Huerta was approaching Veracruz, Wilson ordered U.S. forces to occupy the port. They landed on 21 April 1914 and fought Mexican defenders, including naval cadets and local civilians, with 19 U.S. military deaths and many more Mexican casualties. U.S. Navy historical research cites Veracruz hospital records listing 126 Mexican dead and 195 wounded, many civilians, while cautioning that the actual totals were likely higher. The occupation lasted until November. Mexican reactions were hostile across factions, and Carranza, who wished to preserve the claim of sovereignty, protested sharply even though the occupation hurt Huerta. An international mediation by Argentina, Brazil, and Chile (the ABC powers) at Niagara Falls in the summer produced talk of a negotiated settlement that Carranza rejected as interference.

Veracruz shows how external events interacted with internal ones. The occupation deprived Huerta of customs revenue and arms shipments through the port, and it hastened his fall, but it did not cause it. Mexican armies had been winning battles in the north for months. The episode also reinforced a theme that later chapters will follow: Mexican leaders of all factions insisted on national sovereignty even as they sought arms, recognition, and markets from the United States, and they were prepared to resist a power that they could not defeat.

Section 72.11

Venustiano Carranza, the Plan of Guadalupe, and the Constitutionalist Movement

Venustiano Carranza was a landowner and veteran politician from Coahuila, who had served in the Porfirian Senate, entered Madero's government as governor, and carried the prestige of an established politician rather than a charismatic rebel. When news of the coup arrived, he asked the Coahuila legislature to refuse recognition of Huerta and obtained authority to raise forces. His reasoning was legal: the constitutional government had been overthrown, and the proper response was to defend the constitution. With a small force he withdrew from Saltillo and gathered other officers.

The Plan of Guadalupe, signed on 26 March 1913 at the Hacienda de Guadalupe in Coahuila, was short and narrow. It declared that Huerta was not to be recognized as president, refused to recognize the legislative and judicial powers that had acquiesced to him, and extended the refusal to governors who recognized the regime. It organized an army called Constitutionalist and named Carranza its First Chief, the Primer Jefe. It provided that when the army occupied the capital, the First Chief would assume the interim presidency and call elections. The plan contained no program of land reform, labor legislation, or social change. Its aim was the restoration of constitutional order. The narrowness was deliberate: it was meant to unite as many opponents of Huerta as possible, including those of conservative leanings who objected to the coup but not to the social order.

Students must keep the stages of Constitutionalism distinct. The Plan of Guadalupe of March 1913 was a political document. Carranza's social program emerged later, through additions made in December 1914, through the agrarian decree of January 1915, labor and other legislation in 1915, and the constitutional congress of 1916–1917, as the movement faced competition from Villa and Zapata and needed popular support. It would be an error to read the later reform legislation back into the first plan, and it would also be an error to treat the later reforms as insincere; both opportunism and conviction played a part.

The Constitutionalist leadership was a coalition. In Sonora, where the state legislature and local elites backed the movement, Álvaro Obregón, a successful farmer and local official who had fought against Orozco in 1912, quickly became the leading military figure. Other Sonorans, including Plutarco Elías Calles, Adolfo de la Huerta, Salvador Alvarado, and Benjamín Hill, rose within the movement. In the northeast, Pablo González, a former Maderista officer, commanded forces in Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas. In Chihuahua and Durango, Villa and others raised armies that formally accepted Carranza's leadership but retained their own organization and loyalties. Carranza traveled to Sonora in 1914, establishing his authority in the northwest.

Behind the common banner lay diverse causes. Many soldiers joined for local reasons: to avenge a murdered governor, to resist the leva, to claim land, to earn pay and plunder, to follow a trusted commander. The leadership was largely middle class, composed of ranchers, small businessmen, professionals, and officers rather than peasants, though it recruited peasants and workers in large numbers. The distinction between the leaders' goals and the diverse motives of the troops is essential to understanding why the coalition would fracture once Huerta was defeated.

Section 72.12

The Northern Campaigns, Villa's División del Norte, Obregón's Advances, and the Defeat of Huerta

Revolutionary armies in 1913–1914 were not simply bands of mounted fighters. Recruitment combined volunteers drawn by local loyalties, former federal soldiers and defectors, miners, railroad workers, ranch hands, and, increasingly, men attracted by pay and booty. Horses and mules were essential, and cavalry gave northern armies mobility across large distances. But railroads were the arteries of war. Armies moved troops, horses, artillery, and supplies by train, repaired track under fire, and used locomotives and rolling stock as military assets. Ammunition supply, which depended on purchase across the border and on captured stocks, determined how long an army could fight. Taxation of towns, mines, and haciendas, and the printing of paper money, paid for it, and commanders ran what amounted to regional war economies.

Villa's rise was extraordinary. Crossing the border in March 1913, he raised a small force and within months commanded thousands. In September 1913, after a successful campaign in the Laguna region, his forces were consolidated as the División del Norte. He captured Torreón, a railway junction of great strategic value, on 1 October 1913. In November, he seized Ciudad Juárez by a ruse: his men took a train carrying them into the city under the pretext of being a federal supply movement. A federal counterattack was defeated at Tierra Blanca, and in December Villa took Chihuahua City. As governor of Chihuahua, he confiscated estates of prominent landowners, used revenues to finance the army, and issued currency. The División's organization included cavalry, artillery, medical trains, telegraph, and railway units, and the presence of Felipe Ángeles, a professional artillery officer who joined Villa in early 1914, improved the use of artillery.

Obregón's campaigns in the northwest and west were different in style. Sonoran forces, drawing on local resources, moved down the Pacific coast, defeating federal columns at Santa Rosa and other engagements in 1913–1914, and pressed on to Sinaloa and Jalisco. Obregón relied on careful planning, combined arms, and the effective use of defense and counterattack. In July 1914 his army defeated federal forces at Orendáin, near Guadalajara, and entered that city, cutting off the western approach to the capital. In the northeast, Pablo González's army took Monterrey and Tampico, capturing the oil port and cutting federal supply lines. Zapata's forces in the south meanwhile tied down federal units and moved toward Mexico City from Morelos and Guerrero.

The decisive blow came at Zacatecas. After Villa retook Torreón in early April 1914, he moved toward the mining city of Zacatecas, a fortified point on the line to the capital. Carranza, concerned about the growing independence of Villa, ordered him to send troops to another sector. The dispute reflected rivalries within the coalition and led to a temporary breach, resolved by negotiation that preserved the appearance of unity. On 23 June 1914 Villa's forces, with massed artillery under Ángeles and coordinated assault, stormed the heights surrounding Zacatecas and routed the federal garrison in one of the bloodiest battles of the war. The federal army lost much of its northern strength, and the road to central Mexico lay open.

Huerta's position collapsed in the following weeks. His finances were exhausted, the army was demoralized, and the United States and domestic opponents continued to press. He resigned on 15 July 1914 and left the country. Francisco Carbajal, a jurist, was named interim president to negotiate the transfer of authority. Huerta's fall was not the end of the federal army's existence or of the revolution's political problems. The surrender of the capital and the formal disbanding of the army came in August, and, as the following division shows, the victors were divided over what came next. The war had been won by an alliance whose members distrusted one another, and with Huerta gone, the glue that held them together weakened.

Section 72.13

The Treaties of Teoloyucan, the Dissolution of the Federal Army, and the Revolutionary Succession Crisis

The fall of Huerta on 15 July 1914 and the entry of revolutionary forces into Mexico City were separate events, and the interval between them was occupied by negotiation. Carbajal's interim government tried to arrange a transfer that would preserve some guarantees for the army and for the capital's population. Carranza, who insisted on unconditional surrender, refused to accept compromises on the army's future. Obregón, whose forces were advancing from the west, took the lead in the final arrangements, and Pablo González's army approached from the northeast.

The two agreements signed on 13 August 1914 at Teoloyucan, north of the capital, were distinct instruments. In the first, Álvaro Obregón and Eduardo N. Iturbide, governor of the Federal District, agreed on the handover of Mexico City, including the transfer of police authority and guarantees for inhabitants. The second specified the evacuation, disarmament, and dissolution of the federal army and navy; it was signed on the Constitutionalist side by Obregón and Lucio Blanco and on the federal side by General Gustavo A. Salas and Vice Admiral Othón P. Blanco. General José Refugio Velasco exercised senior federal command in the negotiations but must not be substituted for the signatories. These terms dismantled the federal army as a national institution without settling the legitimacy claims of Villa and Zapata. Obregón entered Mexico City on 15 August, and Carranza arrived on 20 August.

The dissolution of the federal army was a major institutional change. For the first time since independence, the army that had served the state as an instrument of order, and which had been the arbiter of Mexican politics for much of the century, ceased to exist as an organization. The officer corps that had made the coup against Madero lost its institutional base, and many of its members went into exile or were absorbed into the new forces. Chapter 73 will take up the creation of a new revolutionary army. At the time, however, the dissolution created a vacuum: the army that had upheld the state was gone, and no unified revolutionary force replaced it.

That distinction is crucial. The old federal army had been destroyed, but the victors were not a single army. They were several, each loyal to its own commanders and territories: the Constitutionalist forces under Obregón and González, the División del Norte under Villa, and the Zapatista armies that had besieged the capital from the south. Carranza claimed authority as First Chief under the Plan of Guadalupe, and the plan provided that he would assume the interim presidency on occupation of the capital. But neither Villa nor Zapata had agreed to submit to his government, and Zapata had never accepted the Plan of Guadalupe at all. Villa, too, had clashed with Carranza over command and strategy.

The succession crisis therefore centered on a question the Plan of Guadalupe had not answered: what was to be done with the armies and the authority of the revolution after victory? Carranza believed that the plan's provisions were sufficient. He would govern as First Chief until order was restored, then call elections. Others held that the revolution required a broader process of deliberation and that the claims of the armies that had won the war needed institutional representation. These positions were not easily reconciled, and the disagreement moved from the capital to a convention in the provinces.

Section 72.14

The Convention of Aguascalientes and the Breakdown of Revolutionary Unity

Tensions between Carranza and Villa grew through the late summer. Villa, whose forces had won the most important battles, resented Carranza's attempts to limit his autonomy and distrusted his political intentions. In September, Villa broke openly with the First Chief. Carranza, for his part, sought to preserve his authority and to avoid a settlement that would subordinate the Constitutionalist leadership to the military commanders. To avoid civil war, Obregón, who had good relations with both sides, undertook to mediate, and Carranza called a convention of military commanders and governors to meet in Mexico City in early October.

Villa and his supporters objected to a meeting in the capital, where Carranza held the advantage, and the venue was moved to Aguascalientes, a central city regarded as neutral ground, where it opened on 10 October 1914. Delegates were military leaders and their representatives from the Constitutionalist and Villista camps, with the Zapatistas, who had not been invited initially, arriving later in the month. On 14 October the Convention declared itself sovereign, claiming authority over the revolutionary movement above any individual leader. Carranza's followers had accepted the convention in the hope that it would affirm his authority, and the sovereignty claim put that in doubt.

The debates were serious. Delegates argued about whether the Convention could supersede the Plan of Guadalupe, about the powers of the First Chief, about the role of civilians, and about the content of reform. When the Zapatista delegation arrived at the end of October, they pressed the adoption of the Plan of Ayala, and the Convention accepted its agrarian principles, a step that broadened the Convention's program beyond Constitutionalism's narrower terms. Delegates also called for both Carranza and Villa to resign, as a way to remove the principal sources of conflict. Carranza responded with conditions that the Convention found unacceptable, and Villa's own stance was ambiguous.

On 1 November 1914, the Convention elected General Eulalio Gutiérrez as provisional president. Gutiérrez, a former Maderista and Constitutionalist governor of San Luis Potosí, was seen as a compromise figure. Carranza refused to recognize the choice and in the following days declined to hand over power, leaving Mexico City for Veracruz in late November. The Convention then declared Carranza in rebellion and named Villa to command the forces against him. The attempt to settle the divisions of the revolution by negotiation had failed. Two governments now claimed legitimacy: the Convention's, backed by Villa and Zapata, and Carranza's, based on the Plan of Guadalupe and controlling Veracruz and the Gulf coast, and on the commanders who remained loyal, notably Obregón, who broke with the Convention.

Competing claims to legitimacy shaped the conflict. Carranza invoked legal continuity, the Plan of Guadalupe, and his role as the leader of the movement against Huerta. The Convention invoked collective sovereignty, the right of the revolution's armed representatives to decide its political direction. Villa claimed to represent the people who had fought; Zapata spoke for the villages. None of these claims was frivolous, and none was purely instrumental. They reflected fundamentally different views of what a revolutionary government should be, and the ambiguity of the term "revolution" meant that each could appeal to it.

Section 72.15

Francisco Villa, the División del Norte, and the Villa–Zapata Alliance

Villa's strength in 1914 rested on a northern constituency very different from Zapata's. His army drew on cowboys, miners, railroad workers, small ranchers, and the unemployed of a region closely tied to the U.S. economy, and it was sustained by confiscated estates and mining and customs revenues. It was a mobile army with a professional core, and with Ángeles directing artillery it had become capable of conventional warfare. Villa's policies in Chihuahua showed both ambition and improvisation. He expropriated estates of the Terrazas family and other Huerta supporters and placed them under military administration, using proceeds to pay soldiers, care for widows and orphans, and feed cities. He did not carry out a systematic land distribution, and he postponed plans to divide estates among soldiers until the war ended.

Villa's relationship with Ángeles reveals the complexity of his movement. Ángeles, a professional officer who had been loyal to Madero and who held a more politically moderate and civilian-oriented view than many Villistas, urged a policy of conciliation and reform. Others around Villa, including former bandits and ambitious commanders, were less constrained. Villa himself was capable of both generosity and brutality, and his forces committed executions and killings of prisoners and civilians, as the other factions did. Historians, including Friedrich Katz, have noted that his political views were more flexible than the hagiography or the demonization surrounding him suggest.

The alliance with Zapata was one of necessity and convenience. Both distrusted Carranza, both had been marginalized by the Constitutionalist leadership, and both headed armies that had fought for the revolution. They had few ideological ties: Villa was a northern military figure with a regional base, Zapata a village leader with a program centered on land. Villa's northern army had never occupied a village-based agrarian society, and Zapata had no experience in the large-scale mobile warfare that Villa's army practiced. They met on 4 December 1914 at Xochimilco, a town south of Mexico City. A stenographic record of their conversation survives and has been published; it shows two men who spoke bluntly, expressed mutual suspicion of politicians and Carranza, and affirmed their alliance, though it should be consulted in a verified edition rather than in the many retellings.

On 6 December 1914, their forces entered Mexico City together in a large parade. The Convention's government, led by Gutiérrez, was installed. The symbolic power of the occasion was great: the two best-known popular commanders of the revolution were present in the capital. But the entry of armies into a city is not the same as governing it. The Villista and Zapatista forces were not integrated, their commanders disagreed about strategy, and the city experienced requisitions, violence, and killings of political opponents. Villista officers executed prisoners and killed David Berlanga, a Convention delegate, and Gutiérrez, increasingly alarmed, left the capital in January 1915, taking part of his government with him.

The alliance thus proved politically significant but institutionally fragile. It lacked a unified command, a common program, and a coherent administration. Villa's strategic priorities lay in the north and the center; Zapata's lay in Morelos, and Zapata's forces did not follow Villa's armies north. The alliance gave the anti-Carranza coalition a claim to represent the popular revolution, but it left Carranza free to consolidate in Veracruz, take control of the Gulf coast revenues and the oil region, and regroup his forces under Obregón.

Section 72.16

Emiliano Zapata and the Agrarian Revolution in Morelos, 1914–1915

While Villa's armies marched, Zapata's followers carried out their program in Morelos. After Huerta's fall and the withdrawal of federal forces, the Ejército Libertador del Sur controlled most of the state. Villages reclaimed fields and water, often by seizing land from estates whose owners had fled, and by presenting colonial and later titles as proof of ownership. Local councils, led by village elders and Zapatista officers, supervised the process. The distribution of land followed local histories and titles, not a single model, which led to disputes between neighboring communities and between villages and soldiers.

The organization of Zapatismo was a combination of military and village institutions. Commanders, many of them from local communities, had authority over territory and troops but depended on the loyalty of villages that fed and housed their men. Soldiers often returned to their fields between campaigns, and the army's strength fluctuated with the agricultural calendar. Zapata himself, based at his headquarters in the state, held a position of moral authority as the defender of village rights, but he was dependent on his commanders and on the advice of intellectuals and secretaries. Manuel Palafox, a secretary who rose to a prominent role in agrarian policy, helped to formulate administrative procedures, and agrarian commissions were established to examine land claims and oversee the distribution of fields.

Zapatista agrarian policy deserves careful distinction from other programs. Restitution meant returning land to villages that could show they had held it. It differed from the expropriation and subdivision of large estates for distribution to those without land, which the Plan of Ayala also contemplated in part, and from confiscation of the property of enemies. In practice Zapatistas combined these, but their emphasis was the community and its titles, not individual smallholding on the model of later reform. Some sugar estates were seized and operated under the control of Zapatista officers, and an agricultural school was established, though the war interrupted these efforts. On 26 October 1915 at Cuernavaca, the Executive Council of the Sovereign Revolutionary Convention promulgated an agrarian law implementing the Plan of Ayala; Manuel Palafox and other Convention authorities participated. Some versions carry a 22 October drafting or adoption notation, which should not be confused with the date of issuance. The law provided for restitution and granting of lands, woods, and waters to communities and reflected an institutional Convention initiative rather than a unilateral personal decree by Zapata.

Zapatismo's strengths were its local roots and its ability to survive. Because it rested on village institutions and a rural population that supported it, federal and later Constitutionalist armies found it difficult to destroy, and it commanded remarkable loyalty in Morelos and neighboring areas of Guerrero, Puebla, and the Federal District. Its limitations were also real. It did not develop a national political organization or a strategy for taking and holding state power, and it distrusted outsiders and politicians, including allies. Its economy was strained by war and the dislocation of the sugar industry, and the demands of supplying an army weighed on the villages it claimed to defend.

The distinction between rural social revolution and national military strategy explains much of what followed. Zapata was committed to securing the transformation in Morelos and to preventing a government hostile to the villages from taking power in the capital. He did not aspire to rule Mexico himself. That commitment made Zapatismo a formidable defensive force and a powerful moral symbol, but it left the national field to others. In 1915 and 1916 Constitutionalist forces would return to Morelos in strength, and the Zapatista movement would be forced to defend what it had built under mounting pressure.

Historical Vocabulary
División del Norte, Ejército Libertador del Sur, and Convencionismo

The División del Norte (Division of the North) was Villa's army, formed in September 1913 and organized around cavalry, artillery, railway transport, and medical services. The Ejército Libertador del Sur (Liberation Army of the South) was Zapata's force in Morelos and neighboring regions, rooted in village communities. Convencionismo refers to the alignment of forces and political leaders that supported the Convention of Aguascalientes against Carranza, principally Villistas and Zapatistas; the term hides substantial differences between them.

These terms matter because they identify armies with distinct organizations and aims. Treating Villa and Zapata as interchangeable "peasant leaders" obscures that one commanded a mobile northern army with a mixed constituency and the other led a village-based movement concerned with land restitution in a particular region.

Section 72.17

Álvaro Obregón, the Battles of Celaya, and the Defeat of Villa's Main Armies, 1915

Carranza used the winter of 1914–1915 to build a position. From Veracruz he controlled the port's customs revenues and the nearby oil region's taxes, issued paper money, and consolidated command. He also broadened his program. In December 1914 he issued additions to the Plan of Guadalupe promising social and economic reforms, including agrarian, labor, and fiscal legislation. On 6 January 1915 he decreed an agrarian law, largely drafted by the lawyer Luis Cabrera, that declared illegal the alienation of village lands under earlier laws, authorized restitution to villages that had been dispossessed and grants to those that could not prove title, and created agrarian commissions to implement it. The measure was designed in part to compete with the Zapatistas for rural support.

Villa, meanwhile, issued a general agrarian law at León, Guanajuato, on 24 May 1915 under powers claimed through the Convention. The statute envisaged reducing great estates by subdivision, indemnifying owners, and creating smaller holdings through state-level mechanisms; its ambitious provisions had limited opportunity for enforcement amid military defeats. Villa and Carranza thus offered different answers to the land question. Carranza's decree emphasized restitution and grants administered by a national commission, with the state as arbiter. Villa's law envisioned breaking up large estates and creating family-sized farms. Neither was fully implemented during the war, and the comparison below shows how they differed from each other and from Zapata's program.

Restitution restored communal claims on the basis of historical titles; subdivision created new private holdings; and grants offered land to those without title. The decisive military encounters took place in the Bajío, the fertile plains of Guanajuato. Obregón, commanding Carranza's principal field army, marched from Veracruz and the capital toward the region, aware that Villa would seek battle. At Celaya, he positioned his troops behind trenches, barbed wire, and irrigation ditches, with machine guns and artillery covering the approaches. In the first battle, on 6–7 April 1915, Villa's cavalry charged repeatedly against the defenses and was repulsed with heavy losses. In the second, on 13–15 April, Villa attacked again with larger forces and was again defeated, losing thousands of men, horses, and artillery.

Subsequent battles confirmed the result. At León and Trinidad in late April through early June, Villa again attempted to break Obregón's lines, and Obregón lost his right arm to an artillery shell in early June. But the División del Norte suffered further defeats and fell back. By midsummer it was no longer a conventional field army capable of facing Obregón's forces. Villa retreated north, and in November 1915, an attack on Agua Prieta in Sonora was defeated, aided by Constitutionalist use of U.S. railroads to move reinforcements and by electric searchlights. Many Villista soldiers deserted or dispersed, and the movement reverted largely to guerrilla warfare in Chihuahua.

The reasons for Constitutionalist success were several and should not be reduced to technology. Obregón's tactics, entrenched defense, combined arms, and counterattack, were well suited to meeting Villa's cavalry charges. Machine guns and barbed wire contributed, but so did the fact that Villa had chosen to attack prepared positions, perhaps overconfident from earlier victories, and that Ángeles's advice favoring caution was not always followed. Strategic and economic factors mattered as well. Carranza controlled the revenues of the Gulf ports and oil region, which allowed the purchase of arms and the payment of troops. Constitutionalists retained access to more consistent supply, and they recruited urban workers and others through political appeals and, as the next section shows, labor alliances. Villa's economy, dependent on northern resources and a paper currency that lost value, was less sustainable. Finally, Villa's alliance with Zapata brought little military cooperation and exposed the Convention to the charge of lacking a coherent program.

Section 72.18

Revolutionary Society: Soldaderas, Workers, Families, Refugees, and Everyday Life during War

Revolutionary armies were social institutions, not only military ones. Soldiers' families followed the armies, and the soldaderas, women who accompanied troops, performed essential work: cooking, foraging, carrying supplies, nursing the wounded, burying the dead, and often fighting. They were not uniformly volunteers; some followed because they or their families had been forcibly conscripted, others to stay with husbands and sons, others by choice. Women also served as officers and fighters in some units. Among documented cases, Amelio Robles, who lived as a man and rose to the rank of colonel in the Zapatista forces, is one; others, such as Petra Herrera, are the subject of historical debate over the evidence. Such women were later marginalized in official commemoration.

Women's roles extended beyond the army. Dolores Jiménez y Muro, a teacher and writer, had been associated with antireelectionist and Zapatista politics. Carmen Serdán had acted in Puebla in 1910. Hermila Galindo, who served as Carranza's secretary, wrote and spoke on women's rights and became an advocate for suffrage. Elena Arizmendi helped to establish a neutral Red Cross organization, the Cruz Blanca, in 1911. Women worked as nurses, journalists, teachers, and organizers, and in towns and villages they kept households and farms running while men were away. Women's work in markets and as small merchants sustained urban food supplies, and their petitions and protests, particularly over food shortages, are part of the record.

Workers entered the war through the Casa del Obrero Mundial, the anarcho-syndicalist center in Mexico City. In February 1915, after the Constitutionalists reoccupied the capital, Carranza's government and the Casa signed an agreement in Veracruz: the Casa's leaders agreed to support the Constitutionalists, and the government promised to favor workers' demands. Several thousand workers were organized into Red Battalions and fought against Villista and Zapatista forces. The decision reflected workers' distrust of peasant armies and of the Convention, and the Constitutionalists' hope of mobilizing urban labor. The alliance was temporary. By 1916, the government, facing strikes amid inflation, moved to suppress the Casa, and a general strike that summer was crushed.

Civilians bore heavy costs. Armies requisitioned food, horses, and money, and forced loans fell on merchants and propertied families. Competing paper currencies issued by the factions, whose value collapsed, produced inflation and made trade difficult. In Mexico City in 1915, food shortages, hoarding, and disease caused severe hardship and some deaths, including from typhus, and the capital changed hands several times. Rail lines were cut and repaired, farms abandoned, mines and sugar mills disrupted, schools and municipal governments suspended. Villages were burned or sacked, and civilians, caught between armies, were killed in reprisals, whatever the faction. Some fled to cities or across the border, where growing numbers of refugees settled in the United States, a migration that would continue. The total human cost is uncertain: estimates of deaths from violence, hunger, and disease between 1910 and 1920 range widely, often from several hundred thousand to over a million, and population loss also reflects emigration and lower birth rates.

Experiences varied by place and class. Villages in Morelos faced repeated military occupation and, at times, burning and relocation; northern ranching communities experienced requisitions and conscription; cities faced shortages and inflation; elite families sometimes left for Veracruz, Havana, or the United States, and many working-class families had no such options. These differences are part of why the same events, such as a battle or a change of government, meant different things for different people. The features below illustrate patterns of experience through composite portraits based on the kinds of lives that sources document, not on named individuals.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Patterns of Wartime Experience

These are composite illustrations built from patterns recorded in memoirs, oral histories, petitions, and scholarship. They are not accounts of particular individuals.

A soldadera. A woman following a regiment might carry the household's cooking equipment and children on foot or by rail, often riding on freight car roofs. She would prepare meals from whatever could be bought or taken, nurse the sick and wounded, and, after battle, collect weapons and ammunition from the field. Some women fought; others were exposed to sexual violence and abandonment. Their labor allowed armies to move, yet pay and pensions rarely recognized it.

A rural household. In a village that changed hands among armies, a family might hide seed corn and animals, feed whichever soldiers arrived, and cope with forced loans or confiscation. Men might serve in a local force or hide from the leva; women and children tended fields. Planting and harvest followed the war's rhythm, and in some regions land claims were pursued through restitution and the committees that decided them.

An urban worker. A textile or railway worker in Mexico City faced wages paid in rapidly depreciating paper, rising food prices, and unemployment as factories lacked fuel and raw materials. Some turned to the Casa del Obrero Mundial; some, in 1915, joined the Red Battalions; others sought bread in the markets or in public distributions. Workers' hopes for organization and higher wages ran up against a government that valued their support in war and restricted their strikes afterward.

A displaced family. Families fleeing fighting or hunger left farms and small shops, selling possessions to buy passage on trains or on foot. Some went to larger cities, others to border towns such as El Paso, where refugees and exiles formed communities. The loss of homes and records complicated later claims to land and status, and the experiences of refugees were recorded in letters, border-town newspapers, and later oral histories.

The silences in official sources about these experiences are themselves evidence: armies and governments recorded troop movements and decrees, while the lives of the people who fed, nursed, fled, and survived appear mostly in fragments.

Historical Vocabulary
Primer Jefe, Soldadera, Red Battalions, and Revolutionary Faction

The Primer Jefe ("First Chief") was Carranza's title under the Plan of Guadalupe, designating him head of the Constitutionalist Army and, upon victory, interim head of the executive. A soldadera was a woman who accompanied a revolutionary or federal force, performing logistical, medical, and sometimes combat roles; the word covers many different situations, from voluntary participation to coercion. The Red Battalions were units of workers from the Casa del Obrero Mundial who served the Constitutionalists in 1915 after an agreement with Carranza's government. A revolutionary faction is a group of leaders, armies, and followers pursuing a distinct political program or claim to authority within the broader upheaval; historians prefer it to "side" because it recognizes that the Revolution contained many, shifting groupings.

These terms matter because they point to social actors that narratives of leaders and battles tend to leave out, and to the problem of naming a conflict without implying a unity it lacked.

Section 72.19

The Mexican Revolution and the United States, 1914–1916: Recognition, Columbus, and the Punitive Expedition

The civil war unfolded in the shadow of a powerful neighbor whose policy shifted with events. The Veracruz occupation of 1914, discussed above, lasted until November, when U.S. forces withdrew and handed the port to Carranza, who regained its customs revenues just as the factional war began. Woodrow Wilson, who hoped to see a constitutional government emerge and who distrusted all of Mexico's military leaders, at first dealt with Carranza and Villa in turn. He sent emissaries to meet them, and during much of 1914–1915 he treated Villa with sympathy, in part because Villa's forces were seen as more cooperative and because Carranza insisted on strict Mexican sovereignty.

As the war shifted in Carranza's favor, U.S. policy shifted too. After the defeats at Celaya and León, Washington consulted with Latin American governments, and in October 1915 recognized Carranza's government as the de facto authority in Mexico. The decision, which also allowed Carranza to buy arms in the United States, and the use of U.S. railroads to move Constitutionalist troops at Agua Prieta, deepened Villa's sense of betrayal. Carranza's government had won recognition without accepting any conditions about foreign property or the treatment of foreigners, and it continued to defend Mexican sovereignty against U.S. demands.

On 9 March 1916, Villa's forces raided Columbus, New Mexico, a border town with a U.S. Army garrison, killing eighteen Americans—ten civilians and eight U.S. soldiers, as counted by the National Park Service—and burning part of the town before being driven off, with a large number of Villistas killed. The raid followed an incident in January 1916 in which Villista troops had killed American mining engineers at Santa Isabel, Chihuahua. Historians have debated Villa's motives. Explanations include anger at the U.S. recognition of Carranza, a desire to provoke a U.S. intervention that would discredit Carranza, the need for supplies, and conflicts with local merchants, and the evidence does not settle the question. What is clear is that the raid was a violent attack on a U.S. town and that it provoked a strong reaction.

Wilson ordered a Punitive Expedition under Brigadier General John J. Pershing to enter Mexico in pursuit of Villa. It crossed the border in mid-March 1916 and eventually numbered about ten thousand troops, using trucks and airplanes for the first time in U.S. military operations of this kind. Carranza, who had not authorized the entry, protested the violation of Mexican sovereignty, negotiated the terms of the operation, and refused to allow the Americans to use Mexican railroads. In April, a clash at Parral with local people and Mexican troops led to casualties, and in June at Carrizal, U.S. and Carrancista forces fought a battle that killed and captured soldiers on both sides and brought the countries to the brink of war. Further negotiation prevented it.

The expedition failed to capture Villa, who evaded it in the north with the help of sympathetic communities. Its presence became an irritation to Mexican nationalism and a diplomatic burden for Wilson, who by early 1917 was focused on the European war. U.S. troops withdrew in February 1917. For Carranza, the episode strengthened his reputation as a defender of sovereignty and gave him a nationalist argument against both foreign intervention and rivals. For northern civilians, it brought armed troops, requisitions, and fear. In the wider story, it shows that U.S. policy shaped the conditions of the war without determining its course: Mexican factions, not U.S. officials, decided the major battles, and Carranza's resistance to U.S. pressure set limits on what Washington could achieve.

Section 72.20

The Constitutionalists' Ascendancy and the Unresolved Revolutionary Settlement, 1916–1917

By 1916 the Constitutionalists held decisive advantages. They controlled the major cities, including Mexico City, and the principal ports and railroads; they commanded the largest and best-equipped armies under experienced leaders; they drew revenues from customs, oil, and other taxes; and they enjoyed U.S. recognition, which opened access to credit and arms. Villa's armies had been broken as conventional forces, and the Convention had disintegrated. Carranza had built a government that, whatever its weaknesses, could claim to be the successor of the revolution. Obregón, the victor of Celaya, was the army's foremost general and a rising political figure, though his relations with Carranza were beginning to show strain.

Yet the Revolution's armed conflicts were not over. Villista guerrillas remained active in Chihuahua and Durango, and Villa's presence continued to disrupt the north. Zapatista forces in Morelos continued to fight after the Constitutionalists' campaigns of 1916 captured major towns and attempted to pacify the state, and the Zapatistas, though pressed, retained local support. Orozquistas, Felicistas, and other anti-Carranza groups operated in various regions. Regional caudillos and military commanders, including some Constitutionalist officers, wielded local power that the central government could not easily control. The war's ending would take years.

Policy moved with these pressures. Carranza's government issued decrees on land, labor, and local government, partly to compete with its rivals. It also moved against workers who challenged it, as in the 1916 strike. Within Constitutionalism, tensions were growing between moderates around Carranza, who favored a limited reform within the constitutional framework of 1857, and radicals, many of them military men and politicians linked to Obregón, who argued for stronger measures on land, labor, and the Church. The First Chief wanted a stable government with strong presidential authority; others wanted the Revolution's promises written into law.

These tensions made a new legal framework necessary. The Constitution of 1857, though it remained the nominal law, had been suspended in practice by years of war and decree, and it contained no provisions on the social questions the revolution had raised. Carranza's government had governed by decrees under the authority of the First Chief, a basis whose legitimacy depended on the circumstances of war. A constituent congress would give the victorious movement a legal foundation, allow competing views to be debated within a formal structure, and respond to demands for reform. In September 1916 Carranza issued a decree modifying the Plan of Guadalupe and calling for a congress. Delegates were elected in October, excluding those who had supported the Convention or Huerta, and the congress opened in the city of Querétaro on 1 December 1916.

The new constitution was promulgated on 5 February 1917. Its articles on education, land and subsoil resources, labor, and the Church, and the debates that produced them, belong to Chapter 73, as do Carranza's presidency and the problems of implementation. For this chapter, the point is that military victory had not settled the revolution's central questions. Who would own the land? What rights would workers and villages have? How strong would the presidency be, and what would become of the generals who had won the war? What would be the relationship between the state and foreign capital and the Church? Those questions remained open, and so did the violence: Zapata would be assassinated in April 1919, and Villa would live until 1923, when he too was murdered. The Revolution had not ended; it had entered a new phase in which the contest moved increasingly from battlefield to constitutional and political institutions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Revolutionary Goals in 1910, 1913, and 1916

What Changed

  • 1910: The central aim was to remove Díaz and restore competitive politics, with a limited promise of land restitution.
  • 1913: After the coup, the aim was to restore constitutional order against a military government, with the Plan of Guadalupe still silent on social reform.
  • 1916: Land, labor, education, and church-state questions had entered the programs of the leading faction, and the aim had become to build a new constitutional framework.
  • The federal army was destroyed and replaced by revolutionary armies; violence escalated from a short rebellion to a multi-year civil war.

What Stayed the Same

  • Regional bases and local grievances continued to shape participation.
  • Claims to constitutional legitimacy remained central to political argument.
  • The agrarian question, first raised in 1910, remained unresolved.
  • Foreign powers, especially the United States, continued to influence the conflict without controlling it.

Who experienced change most directly: Villagers in Morelos, northern communities, workers in the capital, and soldiers' families, whose lives were most altered by war and displacement. Greater continuity: Large landowners in regions that avoided heavy fighting, and many foreign investors, retained much of their position. Why the pattern matters: The revolution's goals widened under the pressure of war and competition among factions, and that widening explains why the constitutional settlement of 1917 had to address questions that the movement's first plans had not contained.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Mexican Revolution from the uprising against Díaz in 1910 to the threshold of the Constituent Congress at Querétaro in 1916–1917. Its central argument is that a movement to overthrow a dictator and restore competitive politics became a succession of conflicts among revolutionary movements with different constituencies, armies, and programs, and that Constitutionalist military victory did not resolve the questions those movements raised.

The uprising of November 1910 was uneven, kept alive by regional conditions in Chihuahua, Morelos, and elsewhere. National antireelectionist goals and local grievances over land, politics, and autonomy overlapped without being identical. The capture of Ciudad Juárez in May 1911, undertaken against Madero's initial caution, led to the Treaties of Ciudad Juárez, which removed Díaz but left the federal army and administration largely intact.

The interim government of León de la Barra and Madero's election in October 1911 restored constitutional politics, but the demobilization of rebels and the campaigns in Morelos showed that the coalition that had defeated Díaz lacked agreement on what should follow. Madero governed as a liberal committed to legal change, and he faced a surviving army, hostile elites, a narrow fiscal base, and revolutionaries who wanted immediate social change.

Zapata's Plan of Ayala of November 1911 broke with Madero over land, not over civilian government, and made restitution and limited expropriation central to a village-based movement. Orozco's Plan de la Empacadora of March 1912 expressed a different northern disillusionment, with both popular and elite backers. Huerta's defeat of Orozco made the army indispensable to a government that it would soon overthrow.

The Decena Trágica of February 1913 was a military coup in which Huerta, commander of the government's forces, joined the rebels, with the hostile ambassador Henry Lane Wilson providing a stage but not the cause. The murder of Madero and Pino Suárez on 22 February removed the moral center of the 1910 movement. Huerta's government must be distinguished from the coup that produced it: it was a military dictatorship that dissolved Congress, killed opponents, and ruled by conscription and force.

Carranza's Plan of Guadalupe of March 1913 was a narrow constitutionalist rejection of Huerta, silent on social reform. The war against Huerta was won by regional armies, notably Villa's División del Norte and Obregón's western forces, using railroads, border supplies, local taxation, and increasingly professional organization. Woodrow Wilson's refusal to recognize Huerta, the arms embargo's lifting, and the occupation of Veracruz affected the regime's finances but did not decide the war. Zacatecas in June 1914 broke the federal army, and Huerta resigned on 15 July.

The Teoloyucan agreements of August 1914 dissolved the federal army, a major institutional change, but did not create a unified revolutionary command. The Convention of Aguascalientes tried and failed to settle the question of who would govern, ending with two rival governments and competing claims to legitimacy: Carranza's constitutional legality and the Convention's collective sovereignty. The Villa–Zapata alliance was politically significant but institutionally fragile, uniting a mobile northern army and a village-based movement with different goals.

Zapatista Morelos carried out a rural social revolution based on village institutions and restitution, a program different from Villa's expropriations and from Carranza's January 1915 decree. In 1915 Obregón's entrenched defense at Celaya and León broke the División del Norte, aided by Gulf-coast revenues, an alliance with urban workers in the Red Battalions, and Villa's own strategic errors. Carranza's agrarian decree and Villa's May 1915 law reveal competing social programs.

Warfare was a social process. Soldaderas, nurses, teachers, organizers, workers, and refugees sustained and suffered the war; civilians endured requisitions, inflation, hunger, and violence from all factions. U.S. relations shifted from Veracruz to recognition of Carranza in October 1915, Villa's raid on Columbus in March 1916, and the Punitive Expedition, which failed to capture Villa and reinforced Carranza's nationalism.

By 1916 the Constitutionalists held decisive advantages, but Villista and Zapatista resistance continued, tensions between moderates and radicals sharpened, and the agrarian, labor, church-state, and regional questions remained open. Those questions made a new constitutional framework necessary, and the Congress at Querétaro opened on 1 December 1916. Students should also take away a method: plans, decrees, and memoirs record what leaders chose to claim, and the experience of villagers, women, and workers must be recovered from other, fragmentary evidence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Mexican Revolution was a sequence of conflicts, not a single struggle with a single aim. The 1910 uprising removed a dictator through a settlement that spared the army and administration; the years that followed tested whether constitutional politics could address land, labor, and regional power without the coercive machinery of the old state. Madero's government failed for reasons that included its own choices and the constraints it faced, and its overthrow transformed a political revolution into a war.

The chapter also shows how different the movements were. Zapata's village-centered agrarian revolution, Villa's northern military coalition, and Carranza's constitutionalism overlapped in their opposition to Huerta but diverged in their institutions and priorities. Their failure to agree at Aguascalientes was not mainly a clash of personalities; it reflected incompatible conceptions of legitimacy and of what the state should do. Military victory by the Constitutionalists, built on organization, finance, alliances with workers, and the mistakes of their opponents, ended one conflict without resolving the questions that had caused it.

The war's social history belongs at the center of its meaning. Women, workers, villagers, and refugees sustained and endured the fighting, and the Revolution's later memory often obscured their roles. The United States influenced the conflict through diplomacy, recognition, arms policy, and intervention, but Mexican factions determined the main outcomes, and Carranza's defense of sovereignty became a lasting theme of revolutionary nationalism.

Chapter 73 takes up what the victors tried to do with their victory: the Constitution of 1917, with its provisions on land, labor, education, and the Church, and the problems of building a state from a revolution that was not over. Students should carry forward the question this chapter leaves open, namely how a government born of one faction's victory could claim to embody the aims of movements it had defeated or absorbed, and how the demands of villages and workers would be translated into law and, eventually, into the revolutionary state examined in Chapter 74.
