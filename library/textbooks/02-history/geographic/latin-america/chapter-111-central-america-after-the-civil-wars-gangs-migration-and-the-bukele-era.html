Chapter Opening: Peace Without Safety

The wars ended, and the killing did not. In the Salvadoran capital and the Honduran north coast, former war zones and refugee neighborhoods became the ground for a new kind of violence, organized not by armies but by street gangs, and answered, thirty years after the peace accords, by one of the most sweeping security campaigns in the modern hemisphere.

On 16 January 1992, representatives of the Salvadoran government and the Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front (FMLN) signed the Chapultepec accords, ending a civil war that Chapter 91 examined in detail. Guatemala followed in December 1996. Both settlements promised demobilized armies, civilian police, and electoral politics. Yet within a generation the Northern Triangle of Central America, comprising Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras, ranked among the most violent regions on earth outside declared war. In 2015, El Salvador's officially recorded homicide rate was roughly 100 per 100,000 inhabitants, a figure that reported estimates place between about 103 and 106. A decade later, the same government reported a rate of 1.3, and UNHCR cited it as a fall from 18.1 in 2021.

This chapter asks how both of those facts can belong to one history. It begins with the postwar settlements and the unfinished work of building police, courts, and economies in societies that had been militarized and displaced. It follows the transnational origins of Mara Salvatrucha (MS-13) and Barrio 18 in Los Angeles, the effects of United States deportation policy, and the growth of neighborhood-based criminal organizations that taxed shops, buses, and households through extortion. It then examines the failures and partial successes of earlier security strategies: the Mano Dura policies of 2003 and after, the 2012 gang truce, and the violence of 2015. Migration, including asylum seeking and displacement, runs through the story as both a consequence of insecurity and a response to poverty and family need. Migrants must not be confused with criminals, and the chapter keeps the two subjects distinct.

The center of gravity is the presidency of Nayib Bukele in El Salvador. Elected in 2019, he presented himself as a break with the two parties that had dominated postwar politics. After a surge of killings on 26 March 2022, his government obtained a state of exception from the Legislative Assembly that suspended several constitutional protections and that continued to be extended into 2026. Government figures reported tens of thousands of arrests, and an unprecedented drop in recorded homicides followed. Public safety in many neighborhoods changed visibly. At the same time, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, United Nations bodies, and non-governmental monitors documented arbitrary detention, deaths in custody, and limits on judicial review, while constitutional changes in 2025 removed barriers to indefinite presidential reelection. The chapter treats both bodies of evidence as history. Neither the victims of gangs nor the people swept into detention without a meaningful hearing can be written out of the account.

The chapter then widens the lens. Honduras and Guatemala did not follow the Salvadoran path, and their institutions, gangs, and political conditions differed. Honduras used a state of exception from December 2022 until January 2026, and its government changed course after a new administration took office. Guatemala declared a thirty-day state of siege in January 2026 after prison riots and coordinated attacks that killed police officers. Costa Rica and Nicaragua serve selectively as comparators. The Salvadoran model became politically influential across the region, and whether it can be transferred, and at what institutional cost, is an open question in 2026.

Evidence is unusually contested here. Homicide figures come from police and security ministries whose definitions can change. Gang membership estimates come from governments and researchers with different methods. Arrest and detention data are sometimes sealed or not disclosed. Reports by the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, and Salvadoran organizations are based on testimony and case files, while government statements emphasize results. Gang organizations leave little documentary record of their own, and gang self-presentation should never be taken at face value. The chapter therefore attributes numbers, gives reporting dates, and separates alleged membership, arrest, charge, and conviction.

The chapter's boundaries are firm. It does not retell the Salvadoran and Guatemalan civil wars, which belong to Chapter 91, and it does not survey all Latin American organized crime, which is the task of Chapter 112, including Tren de Aragua, Brazil's criminal organizations, and Ecuador. It builds on the democratization of Chapter 94 and the social movements of Chapter 110. Its time frame runs from about 1990 to mid-November 2026, and events of 2025 and 2026 are marked where outcomes remain unresolved.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the peace settlements in El Salvador and Guatemala did not produce public security, and why Honduras requires separate analysis.
  2. Trace the origins of MS-13 and Barrio 18 in Los Angeles and distinguish their histories.
  3. Assess the role of United States deportation in gang expansion without reducing it to a single cause.
  4. Analyze gang organization, territorial control, and the economics of extortion.
  5. Distinguish gang membership, coerced association, kinship, and residence in a gang-controlled area.
  6. Compare Mano Dura, the 2012 truce, and the state of exception as security strategies.
  7. Distinguish asylum seekers, refugees, internally displaced persons, deportees, and other migrants.
  8. Explain Nayib Bukele's rise and the legal structure of the 2022 state of exception.
  9. Evaluate official homicide statistics, including their definitions, limits, and independent corroboration.
  10. Interpret human-rights findings on due process, detention, and deaths in custody against government claims.
  11. Contextualize constitutional change in El Salvador and the emergency measures in Honduras and Guatemala.
  12. Separate measurable results, unresolved allegations, legal standards, and outcomes not yet established by November 2026.

Historical Context

Chapter 91 showed how the Salvadoran and Guatemalan conflicts ended through United Nations-mediated negotiations. In El Salvador the 1992 accords reduced the army, dissolved the old security forces, created a new National Civil Police, and turned the FMLN into a political party. A Truth Commission reported in 1993 and attributed most documented abuses to state forces and allied death squads, but a general amnesty passed days later shielded perpetrators until the Constitutional Chamber of the Supreme Court struck it down in 2016. In Guatemala, the 1996 settlement ended thirty-six years of conflict, but its provisions on police, justice, and land were implemented unevenly. Honduras had no comparable civil war. It had instead served in the 1980s as a base for United States-backed operations against Nicaraguan and Salvadoran insurgents, and its military retained influence in politics and security.

The postwar economies were structurally fragile. Land and wealth remained highly concentrated. Rural families had been displaced into cities, and many had fled to the United States, where Salvadoran, Guatemalan, and Honduran communities grew in Los Angeles, Washington, and elsewhere. Remittances became a pillar of household and national economies. Cities such as San Salvador, Guatemala City, Tegucigalpa, and San Pedro Sula expanded quickly, often with poorly serviced informal neighborhoods. Weapons were abundant after the wars. Police forces were new or weak, courts were slow, and prisons were overcrowded. Honduran politics were further shaken by Hurricane Mitch in 1998 and by the 2009 coup.

Terminology requires care. The Spanish word mara is a regional term for street gangs, and pandilla is also used. "Gang" in this chapter refers to neighborhood-based organizations whose members identify with a group such as MS-13 or Barrio 18. It does not imply that every young person in a poor neighborhood belonged to one. Gangs are not the same as the drug-trafficking organizations discussed in Chapters 97 to 104, although the two interact. And the widely used phrase "Northern Triangle" is a convenient grouping of three countries with distinct institutions, rather than a unit that behaved uniformly.

Historians also face a political context in which security claims are politically charged. A government that reports a falling homicide rate is also making an argument about its own legitimacy. Human-rights organizations that document abuses are also making arguments about law. Both are legitimate evidence, and both require scrutiny. This chapter reads each against the others and keeps the dates and definitions of every number visible.

From Civil War to Postwar Gang Societies

Section 111.1

The Northern Triangle after the Civil Wars

The Salvadoran peace was a negotiated transformation of the state. The army was cut and purged, the former security forces were abolished, and the new National Civil Police (PNC) was meant to recruit civilians, including former insurgents, under a new doctrine. The FMLN entered electoral politics and competed with ARENA, which governed until 2009. These were real achievements. Yet the reconstruction was thin. The police were built quickly, the judiciary and prosecutors remained weak, and the amnesty of 1993 left the old questions of accountability unresolved. In Guatemala, the 1996 settlement left a smaller military role but a justice system that struggled to prosecute powerful actors, a weakness that later fed the work and the closing of the UN-backed anti-impunity commission (CICIG, 2007–2019).

Social conditions compounded institutional weakness. Rural land distribution had been a cause of conflict and was only partly addressed. Cities absorbed displaced families, and informal neighborhoods lacked services, schools, and formal jobs. Young people who had grown up in wartime or in migrant households found few routes into stable employment. Firearms were abundant. Remittances from relatives abroad sustained consumption but also tied households to migration and to the labor markets of the United States. None of these conditions produced gangs by themselves, but they formed the setting in which gangs could recruit and survive.

Honduras must be treated separately. It had no insurgency on the Salvadoran scale and no peace accord with a truth commission, though its 1980s security forces committed serious abuses, as Chapter 90 noted in the context of the Contra war. After the Cold War it demobilized less dramatically, and military institutions retained considerable influence. Honduran politics, dominated by the Liberal and National parties, were disrupted by Hurricane Mitch (1998) and by the coup of 2009 that removed President Manuel Zelaya. Police corruption, a weak prosecutorial system, and the intersection of drug-trafficking routes with local politics gave Honduras its own security trajectory. By the early 2010s San Pedro Sula had a reputation as one of the most violent cities in the world, and the Honduran national homicide rate peaked in that period. Honduras is therefore not an example of postwar violence in the narrow sense. It is a case of insecurity produced by a different mix of state weakness, geography, and criminal markets.

The difference between negotiating the end of a war and building an everyday monopoly on legitimate coercion is central to the postwar story. In El Salvador, the accords defined what the armed forces could no longer do, but civilian authorities still had to recruit police, investigate ordinary crime, protect witnesses, and sustain courts through annual budgets. Former combatants faced reintegration questions that were simultaneously political, economic, and personal. A community could welcome the absence of army offensives while finding that theft, land disputes, and threats were still difficult to report. The dismantling of an abusive security institution could be indispensable for peace without automatically producing a competent replacement. That is an institutional problem, not an argument that military repression had been preferable.

The Guatemalan settlement was complicated by the history of massacres against Indigenous communities and by the unequal distribution of land and political influence. Its commitments to Indigenous rights, socioeconomic reform, civilian power, and demobilization were broad, but the resources and coalitions needed to implement them were contested. The 1999 referendum that rejected proposed constitutional changes demonstrated that an agreement among negotiating parties did not guarantee popular support for every subsequent reform. Police and courts confronted organized networks whose members could draw on private wealth, personal connections, and remnants of wartime clandestine structures. The later creation of CICIG showed that the problem of impunity became a defining postwar political question.

It would be misleading, however, to say that all violence in the three countries flowed mechanically from the wars. Honduras's security challenges were affected by drug-transit geography, cross-border trade, corruption, and urban development, but also by decisions of civilian governments and security commanders after 1990. In all three countries, rural households experienced crop shocks and changing export opportunities differently from urban families. Young adults differed by class, gender, education, and locality. The postwar environment supplied vulnerabilities and opportunities, not an inevitable trajectory toward membership in a mara. The task for historians is to identify the intervening mechanisms: why a neighborhood became governable by a gang, why a witness remained silent, or why an officer feared or accepted illicit influence. These causal steps distinguish serious institutional history from an undifferentiated story of poverty producing crime.

The Chapultepec Accords and the UN Truth Commission Report

Creators: Government of El Salvador, the FMLN, and UN mediators; the UN-appointed Commission on the Truth for El Salvador · Dates: accords signed 16 January 1992 in Mexico City; commission report issued March 1993 (UN document S/25500) · Type: negotiated treaty-like agreement and official investigative report

Direct documentary records: Record 1: United Nations · Record 3: United Nations

Context: The accords ended the war and set out reforms to the armed forces, police, judiciary, and electoral system, with UN verification. The Truth Commission, a creation of the negotiations, investigated serious acts of violence and published names and findings.

Paraphrase: The accords commit the state to a civilian-led police force separate from the military, a reduced army, and judicial reform. The commission's report concludes that most documented abuses were committed by state agents and death squads linked to them, and it recommends removing implicated officers and reforming the justice system.

Analysis: These documents establish what the peace was supposed to build. They are essential for judging how far postwar institutions met their promises, and for understanding why justice and police reform became unfinished business. They say little about the economic problems that followed or about the gangs that grew after the war, and the commission's mandate did not cover postwar violence.

  1. What institutional promises did the accords make about police and justice?
  2. Who created each document, and under what international supervision?
  3. What can the documents tell historians about state violence before 1992?
  4. What can they not tell us about postwar gang violence?
Section 111.2

Los Angeles, MS-13, and Barrio 18: The Origins of the Maras

Both principal Central American gangs began in Los Angeles, but they began differently. Barrio 18, or the 18th Street gang, emerged in the 1960s in the Rampart neighborhood among Mexican-American youth. It was later one of the few large Los Angeles gangs to admit immigrants of different origins, and Salvadorans, Guatemalans, and Hondurans joined in the 1980s and 1990s. Mara Salvatrucha, by contrast, formed in the 1980s among young Salvadoran refugees in Los Angeles neighborhoods such as Pico-Union, partly as protection against established gangs that targeted newcomers. Researchers generally describe the early MS-13 as a local youth association that became more violent and more formally structured through conflict with rivals and through contact with the prison system.

The backdrop was migration caused by war and poverty. Hundreds of thousands of Salvadorans, many of them without legal status or with temporary protection, settled in Los Angeles during the 1980s. Their children grew up in crowded, poorly served neighborhoods, in school systems and labor markets that offered them little, and in a city where Mexican-American gangs already controlled territory. Gang membership supplied identity, protection, and a sense of belonging. This pattern, a youth subculture formed under pressure of migration and urban marginality, is the basis of the interpretation advanced by scholars of migration and gangs, and it explains why the gangs are best understood as transnational phenomena rather than as imports or inventions of one country.

The two gangs developed distinct identities. MS-13 later aligned itself with the Sureño network associated with the Mexican Mafia prison gang, while Barrio 18 had its own relations with that network, and the two became bitter enemies. They used distinct signs, tattoos, and codes, and their rivalry spread with them. Students should avoid treating them as interchangeable or as a single "Central American gang." In El Salvador, Barrio 18 itself divided in the mid-2000s into two factions, the Sureños and the Revolucionarios, with the second more involved in later truce negotiations. The Los Angeles experience also shows that the label "gang member" can cover people with very different degrees of commitment, a point that matters later when the chapter turns to detention.

A migration-centered history should begin with the people who did not join gangs. Salvadoran refugees often depended on relatives, churches, labor organizers, community clinics, and legal advocates in Los Angeles. Many settled into work and family life while facing language barriers, economic insecurity, and uncertain legal status. The development of MS-13 occurred within this much larger community and involved a minority of its youth. It is analytically wrong to transfer attributes of a criminal organization to Salvadoran migrants generally. The fact that a gang emerged in an immigrant setting does not make migration itself a criminal cause. The same distinction applies to subsequent deportation and to people later suspected of gang association in Central America.

The organization names themselves can mislead. A person using the label MS-13 in a Los Angeles neighborhood during the 1980s did not necessarily belong to the same command hierarchy, engage in the same offenses, or face the same obligations as a Salvadoran clique member decades later. Over time, prison relationships, intra-gang discipline, local rivalry, and the opportunities generated by extortion reshaped the groups. In El Salvador, the division of Barrio 18 into rival factions also demonstrates how the national environment altered a transplanted identity. Historical comparisons should therefore use precise places and dates rather than projecting the structure of the 2010s backward onto the 1980s.

Researchers face difficult evidence problems in reconstructing gang origins. Accounts from former members are valuable for social identity and recollection, but they may compress events, elevate the importance of particular leaders, or reflect incentives to conceal past behavior. Police intelligence can clarify prosecutions and links among suspects while overlooking informal boundaries between occasional participants and committed members. Newspaper reports often foreground especially violent incidents. The most reliable historical explanation triangulates such materials with neighborhood histories, migration studies, court records, and interviews with people who remained outside the gangs. This method also helps explain why two organizations with shared Los Angeles geography became institutionally and culturally distinct.

Historical Vocabulary
Mara and Clica

A mara is a Central American term for a street gang, probably from slang associated with groups of friends. A clica (or clika) is a local cell or clique of a larger gang, typically tied to a specific neighborhood, with its own leader and rules.

The terms matter because Central American gangs usually worked through neighborhood cliques with varying degrees of coordination, not through a single centralized command. "Mara" should not be read as a synonym for organized crime in general. It names a specific kind of neighborhood-rooted organization whose relationship to larger criminal markets varied by place and time.

Section 111.3

Deportation, Return Migration, and the Spread of Gang Networks

In 1996 the United States enacted laws, notably the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act, that expanded the categories of offenses for which noncitizens could be removed and limited relief from deportation. Over the following years, large numbers of people who had been convicted of crimes, as well as many who had not, were deported to Central America. Many had spent most of their lives in the United States and had little family or work in the countries to which they were sent. Some were gang members. Others had left gangs, or had been only marginally connected. Governments in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras received them with little capacity for reintegration and often without detailed criminal records.

The effect on the region was real but must be stated carefully. Deportation moved the culture, symbols, and organizational models of Los Angeles gangs to Central America, helped link local groups to U.S.-born ones, and brought experienced members into poor neighborhoods. Researchers on the maras disagree about how large this effect was. Some scholars argue that deported members were a decisive catalyst. Others emphasize that local gangs already existed, that many recruits were born and raised in Central America, and that domestic factors, including police repression, prison overcrowding, and local criminal markets, shaped how the gangs grew. A third position treats deportation and domestic conditions as interacting rather than competing causes.

Deportation also had human consequences beyond gang history. Families were separated, children left in the United States or raised by relatives, and remittance flows interrupted. Return migrants often faced stigma and suspicion simply because they were deportees, and a tattoo or deportation record could lead to police harassment. The danger of treating deportation history as proof of gang membership appears again in the Salvadoran detentions of the 2020s.

United States immigration policy changed through more than one statute and administrative practice. The 1996 legislation expanded removal grounds and narrowed relief for many noncitizens, but subsequent enforcement priorities, policing partnerships, court rulings, and detention capacity determined how these provisions operated. Deportation statistics count administrative events: a removal order may follow a conviction, a visa violation, or another legal ground. They do not automatically describe gang affiliation. Because national aggregates obscure neighborhoods and individual histories, they cannot by themselves establish how many gang participants arrived in a particular Central American city or whether they formed the local cliques later observed there.

Reintegration was also unequal. A deported adult who had relatives, a trade, and knowledge of local language and institutions might rebuild a livelihood; someone sent to a country left as a young child could confront unfamiliar streets, employment exclusion, and suspicion. Some young people were placed in densely populated districts where resident gangs already contested territory. Others experienced harassment from gangs precisely because their U.S. background suggested resources or contacts. Women deportees confronted distinct caregiving and employment obstacles. Families on both sides of the border had to decide whether to support an arriving relative, finance another journey, or relocate within the country. Such choices linked law in Washington to household economics in San Salvador or Tegucigalpa.

A careful causal model has at least three stages. First, migration and U.S. street and prison environments shaped a group of individuals and organizational practices. Second, deportation and return carried some of those practices into Central American neighborhoods. Third, local policing, political decisions, prison structures, markets, and gang rivalry selected which practices endured and expanded. Each stage can be documented differently, and none proves that every deportee was a participant. The model matters for policy: if reintegration opportunities and credible institutions alter the third stage, the influence of deportation need not be constant across countries or decades. Policies that stereotype all returnees may instead make reintegration harder and worsen the vulnerabilities they purport to solve.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Deportation Produce Central America's Gang Crisis?

The question: Did the return of deportees from Los Angeles create the Central American gangs, or did domestic postwar conditions determine their growth?

Interpretation A — Deportation as catalyst: Deportation transferred gang identities, rivalries, and organizational experience to societies without the capacity to absorb returnees. Gang names, symbols, and feuds came from Los Angeles, and large-scale removals coincided with rapid gang growth.
Interpretation B — Domestic conditions as determinants: Weak police, corrupt or slow courts, inequality, informal urban settlement, abundant weapons, and punitive policies created the conditions in which gangs could recruit. Most members were local youths, and territorial violence grew from local rivalries and from prisons.

Evidence: Both sides use gang histories, interviews with members, deportation statistics, and comparisons across countries. Differences among El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala, and comparisons with countries that received fewer deportees, are central, but data on membership and on deportees' later paths are weak.

Why it matters: The question affects blame and policy. A mainly deportation-driven account puts weight on U.S. immigration policy. A domestic-conditions account points to state-building and social policy. Most historians combine both, treating deportation as one contributing channel interacting with local conditions.

Student task: Write a paragraph defending a combined interpretation, and identify one dataset that would test it.

Section 111.4

Territorial Control, Gang Organization, and Rivalry

The gangs' power rested on territory. A neighborhood or street, held by a clica, was a space where outsiders were watched, rivals were killed, and residents were expected to comply. Boundaries were enforced with violence and sometimes marked with graffiti. Moving between neighborhoods could be dangerous, particularly for young men, because a person's address could be read as allegiance to a rival gang. Territorial control meant that some residents could not use certain buses, visit relatives, or attend certain schools.

Organization varied. In MS-13, local clicas were connected to regional and national structures that, in El Salvador, included a national leadership body known as the ranfla, which coordinated disputes and set rules, though researchers differ on how much control it exercised over local cells. Barrio 18's division in El Salvador produced two rival factions. Leaders were frequently imprisoned, and the prisons became organizational centers. Prison confinement of members from the same gang in the same facilities, a result of earlier policy, made it easier to communicate and to discipline members. Discipline within the gangs was severe and included killing members who broke rules or tried to leave.

Scholars disagree about whether the maras were mainly street gangs, whose economic base was extortion and small-scale retail drug sales, or more centralized criminal enterprises linked to large-scale trafficking. The evidence suggests that their economic activity centered on local extortion rather than on controlling major drug routes, which were more often managed by other organizations. The distinction matters for policy: a locally rooted organization that taxes a neighborhood requires different responses from an international trafficking network. Membership estimates, which governments sometimes set in the tens of thousands, are difficult to verify, since being a "member" involved varying degrees of participation and many estimates derived from police lists. This chapter treats such figures as estimates from stated sources, not as measurements.

Territory did not mean control of every block at every hour. A clica might maintain lookouts at road entrances, know which families operated shops, and punish people suspected of passing information to rivals, while state officers could still enter intermittently. Control was often selective and relational: a gang could compel payment from a bus owner without determining every household decision. Some members moved between cities or prisons; others occupied local positions for years. This local variation limits claims that either major gang possessed a single, fully centralized command over all its namesakes. Networks coordinated across some boundaries, but their practical authority depended on people, geography, and enforcement.

Prisons transformed these relationships. Confining members of rival gangs together, or segregating them in ways that strengthened collective identity, could give influential prisoners opportunities to negotiate, discipline affiliates, and communicate with neighborhood groups. Corrupt custodial arrangements sometimes allowed prohibited communications or economic transactions. At the same time, researchers must avoid assuming that all commands attributed to imprisoned leaders were followed everywhere. Different clica leaders might accept orders, bargain for exceptions, or act independently when the expected benefits and risks diverged. The prison consequently became both an arena of state coercion and a political site within criminal networks.

Gang participation had a temporal dimension too. People entered at different ages, became inactive, attempted to leave, or were coerced into service. Some roles involved violence, while others included collecting payments, carrying messages, or maintaining social relationships. Law-enforcement files may categorize these together even when their seriousness and legal consequences vary. Families of members might be targeted by rivals, but family relationship is not evidence of personal guilt. Historians should distinguish a gang's self-presentation as an omnipotent territorial authority from the uneven, negotiated, and sometimes fragile reality of its control. This distinction becomes decisive when assessing why large-scale arrests affected whole districts and whether the collapse of a neighborhood clique necessarily eliminated all forms of illicit commerce.

Extortion, Violence, Migration, and Early Security Responses

Section 111.5

The Economics of Extortion and Neighborhood Control

The characteristic income of the maras was renta, a regular payment demanded from small businesses, market vendors, bus and taxi operators, and sometimes from households. Collection was usually local. A clica identified shops and routes within its territory and sent members to demand payments. The amounts could be modest per business, but multiplied across a neighborhood and across months they supplied a stable income. The threat behind the demand was credible because the gang killed those who refused, and because police and courts rarely protected victims.

Extortion shaped the economy of poor areas. Some businesses closed or relocated. Others raised prices, reduced hours, or reduced the range of goods they sold. Transport operators, who were highly exposed because their routes crossed territories, suffered numerous killings, and bus owners sometimes suspended service in dangerous areas. Investors avoided neighborhoods where payments were required, and small enterprise growth stalled. Victims often did not report, both from fear and from distrust of police, so official figures on extortion undercount it and reports differ by source.

Honduras has stood out for the importance of extortion to businesses and transport workers. Reports on the Honduran transport sector document repeated killings of drivers and fare collectors and long-running extortion of bus companies, and the practice survived the state of exception discussed in Section 111.17. Extortion also existed in Guatemala, where MS-13 and Barrio 18 extort bus routes and businesses in Guatemala City and elsewhere. A comparison of El Salvador with Honduras and Guatemala therefore needs to ask about extortion separately from homicide, since the two can change at different speeds.

Extortion was more than a criminal revenue stream: it was a form of coercive local taxation without accountability or public benefit. For a bus operator, regular payments could be demanded separately by rival cliques along a single route. The operator might respond by passing costs to passengers, shortening service, limiting routes after dark, or leaving the market altogether. For a food vendor, an apparently modest weekly charge could destroy a thin profit margin. For an employer, a threat against workers could deter investment regardless of whether a violent attack occurred. The economic costs thus included lost production, abandoned storefronts, higher prices, and the depletion of trust necessary for ordinary trade.

The system depended on information. The collector needed to know which household received remittances, which workshop had recently gained customers, and when a delivery vehicle would arrive. Such knowledge came from proximity and intimidation rather than sophisticated technology. Gangs could exploit local disputes or offer selective, coercive protection against rivals while causing the fear from which they claimed to protect residents. Some businesses paid one faction and were later threatened by another; others could negotiate temporary arrangements through intermediaries. These practices varied across neighborhoods and organizations. A uniform national figure for extortion rarely captures what happened at the level of a family enterprise.

Measuring the offense is especially difficult. Victims may decline to file complaints because they fear retaliation or expect a corrupt or ineffective response. Changes in police recording practices can alter recorded totals independently of underlying victimization. Surveys can reveal broader patterns, but question wording and fear of disclosure matter. Payments may also be disguised as gifts, wages, or services, making financial tracing difficult. For this reason a fall in reported homicide is not a proxy measure for extortion, and an increase in complaints may reflect improved willingness to report rather than worsening crime. A full assessment of anti-gang policy requires independent victimization surveys, business evidence, prosecution records, and local observations read together over comparable time periods.

Historical Vocabulary
Renta

Renta (literally "rent") is the Central American term for protection or extortion payments demanded by gangs. It resembles a tax but was collected under threat and without legal authority.

The word's ordinary meaning shows how extortion was normalized in daily vocabulary. Historians use the term to analyze gangs as quasi-governing organizations that extracted resources from neighborhoods. They should not, however, treat renta as a legitimate form of governance or imply that residents consented to it.

Section 111.6

Violence, Youth Recruitment, Women, and Community Life

Gang violence fell most heavily on poor communities. Homicide victims were mostly young men, but threats, sexual violence, forced recruitment, and disappearance touched women, girls, boys, and families. Gangs recruited adolescents, sometimes by persuasion, sometimes by pressure, and sometimes by coercion in which refusing carried a risk of death. Young people also joined for reasons of friendship, belonging, protection, or income. Leaving was often dangerous or impossible.

Students should distinguish four situations that policy and public language often blur: active membership in a gang, coerced association such as being forced to carry messages or keep a house, kinship with a member, and mere residence in a gang-controlled neighborhood. A relative of a gang member could be pressured to help, and a person who lived in a gang area could be forced to pay renta, to remain silent, or to allow the gang to use a space. Treating all of these as equivalent to membership leads to errors that later detention policy would reproduce.

Women's experiences were varied. Some women were gang members. Others were relatives, partners, or victims. Women and girls were targets of sexual violence, forced relationships with gang members, and femicide, and reports from Honduras and Guatemala have repeatedly documented femicide as a major form of violence. Women also sustained families when men were killed, imprisoned, or migrated, and women organized in churches, mothers' groups, and community associations. Schools, churches, and neighborhood associations adapted: some negotiated access with gang members, some closed or relocated, and some became sites of mediation or risk. Community responses ranged from silence for safety to organized resistance and quiet emigration.

To reconstruct community life, historians must avoid reducing every resident to a victim or a perpetrator. Families continued to attend schools, worship, celebrate, maintain markets, and create voluntary organizations in places that outsiders described almost exclusively through violence. Their choices often involved competing risks. A parent might move a child to a distant school to avoid a contested boundary while losing work hours and paying new transport costs. A teacher might negotiate safe passage for students without endorsing the criminal group controlling an entrance. A church or youth center might provide temporary refuge but lack the resources to make it permanent. Ordinary continuity was itself a form of social labor under insecurity.

Gender affected the risks and the available strategies. Young men could face pressure to join a clica or be treated as rival participants because of their neighborhood, dress, or companions. Girls and women could experience coerced relationships, threats of sexual violence, or demands to carry messages, while some also participated in criminal organizations as actors with different degrees of agency. A mother seeking help for a detained son had a different relationship to police authority than a shopkeeper seeking protection from extortion. Accounts must preserve such distinctions and distinguish reported incidents from prevalence estimates. The vulnerability of minors raises additional questions about schooling, child protection, and how state systems determine culpability.

Displacement could be almost invisible in national migration tables. A family might abandon a home after threats and move only a few kilometers, stay with relatives, or rotate among temporary residences. The movement would alter school attendance, income, and social networks without necessarily crossing a border or being registered as official displacement. Landlords and employers might refuse residents from stigmatized neighborhoods. Neighborhood associations sometimes lost their most active members, which further weakened collective capacity. Where community organizing endured, it could support witnesses, document abuses, and advocate services, but participants might become targets. This history explains why counting killings alone gives only one dimension of insecurity and why safety must ultimately be measured in the everyday freedoms available to residents.

Section 111.7

Mano Dura, Militarized Policing, and the Limits of Earlier Strategies

In July 2003, President Francisco Flores of El Salvador announced Mano Dura ("firm hand"), authorizing police and soldiers to sweep neighborhoods and arrest people for gang markers such as tattoos or hand signs, and the Assembly passed a law that criminalized gang membership. Courts struck down the law in 2004 on constitutional grounds, and the government replaced it with Súper Mano Dura under President Antonio Saca. Honduras adopted anti-gang provisions in the same year. The results were disappointing: many arrested youths were released for lack of evidence, others were jailed alongside rival and affiliated members, and prisons overcrowded.

Researchers argue that these policies had unintended effects. Detention concentrated gang members in prisons, where they organized more effectively. Mass arrests pushed gangs to hide markers such as tattoos, changing their visibility without ending their activity. And the policies fed distrust between communities and police. The Salvadoran state also experimented with other approaches, including social programs and, later, a gang-reintegration plan, but support was uneven and funding limited.

In March 2012, Salvadoran media reported that the government of Mauricio Funes, the first FMLN president, had facilitated a truce between MS-13 and the two Barrio 18 factions, with intermediaries including a former guerrilla and a Catholic bishop. Homicides fell sharply in the following months, and the arrangement included transfers of imprisoned leaders to less restrictive prisons. The government denied that it had negotiated, describing its role as facilitating a gang-initiated process, while critics argued that it had bargained with criminal organizations. The truce weakened by 2013 as political support and public trust eroded, and a new government took a harder line. By 2015, killings reached extreme levels. The record rate of that year was the culmination of the failure of the truce and of the renewed confrontation, and not a sudden development without antecedents.

The early punitive programs rested on a theory that the visible markers of gang identity could be used to disrupt criminal networks before serious offenses occurred. Police deployed in heavily stigmatized neighborhoods and made arrests based on appearance, associations, or alleged organizational membership. Courts repeatedly confronted questions about whether these criteria met constitutional requirements for individualized proof. Even when members committed serious crimes, a strategy that classified young men collectively risked weakening trust among witnesses and non-gang residents. Officers might be pressured to demonstrate success through numbers of arrests rather than the quality of investigations or reductions in particular victimizations.

The prison consequences were especially significant. Large inflows of detainees strained food, sanitation, medical care, and separation of prisoners by security risk. Concentration of gang-affiliated prisoners could strengthen internal leadership and communication. After release, a person might return to the same neighborhood with fewer employment opportunities and a stronger social attachment to imprisoned peers. The lesson is not that imprisonment has no deterrent effect: properly investigated convictions for violent offenses can protect victims. It is that indiscriminate detention may impose costs on innocent or marginally involved people while failing to dismantle the recruitment and extortion mechanisms that sustain organizations.

The 2012 truce was a different experiment. Mediators and gang leaders claimed they could reduce homicide through negotiated restraint; officials differed in their public acknowledgment of the contacts. A sharp fall in killings followed, but disputes remained over whether the arrangement involved transfers, prison privileges, or tacit political concessions, and over whether ordinary people gained freedom from extortion. The truce carried a legitimacy problem: victims were rarely positioned as decision makers, and gang representatives might gain recognition without delivering durable nonviolence. When political support eroded and enforcement resumed, violence rose again. Historians should neither dismiss the immediate reduction in killings nor treat it as proof that secret bargaining was sustainable. Evaluating the truce requires separate evidence on murders, extortion, institutional conduct, and the credibility of any agreements.

Section 111.8

Violence, Internal Displacement, Asylum, and Migration

Migration from Central America had many causes, and violence was only one. Poverty, lack of employment, family reunification, climate-related shocks in the agricultural "Dry Corridor," natural disasters, and political crises all drove departures. For many families, migration was a long-standing strategy, supported by networks of relatives abroad. Gang threats, however, were a significant cause for some people, who fled forced recruitment, extortion, or direct threats. Reports by UN agencies describe internal displacement within El Salvador and Honduras, with families moving between neighborhoods or regions before, or instead of, leaving the country.

Terminology matters. An asylum seeker has applied for protection and awaits a decision. A refugee has been recognized as needing international protection. An internally displaced person has been forced to flee but has not crossed an international border. A deportee has been removed by a state. Many people fit more than one category over time. Those who traveled north faced dangerous routes through Mexico, exposure to kidnapping and extortion by criminal groups, and changing U.S. policies, including restrictions on asylum that varied between administrations. Child and family migration surged in the mid-2010s and again later in the decade, and the "caravans" of 2018 and after brought new public attention.

Remittances sent home formed a large share of national income: roughly a fifth to a quarter or more of gross domestic product in El Salvador and Honduras in recent years, and a smaller but significant share in Guatemala, though exact figures vary by year and source. Communities left behind experienced both money and absence: children raised by grandparents, labor shortages, and dependence on foreign earnings. Migration must not be treated as synonymous with criminality. The overwhelming majority of migrants were workers and families, and the confusion of the two categories in public debate has had real consequences for policy and for migrants' safety.

Migration histories are organized around stages rather than a single motive. A household could first relocate inside its country because of a threat, then move to a neighboring state for work, and only later seek protection farther north. A family might pursue reunification with relatives in the United States while also avoiding gang recruitment at home. Drought, falling farm income, and the rising cost of food could change the ability to remain in a district already affected by extortion. These interacting pressures make the phrase "economic migrant versus refugee" too crude for many individual histories: an asylum determination is a legal process evaluating specific forms of persecution, while a person's lived reasons for moving can be multiple.

The legal vocabulary also matters. An internally displaced person has not crossed an international border; a refugee must satisfy a relevant legal definition, while an asylum seeker awaits a determination. A deportee is someone removed under an immigration system, not someone found guilty of a violent offense. A transit migrant may be exposed to crime and exploitation in countries other than the one of origin. Men, women, adolescents, and families faced different risks in transit, including robbery, extortion, disappearance, and exploitation by both criminal actors and abusive officials. The 2010s movement of unaccompanied minors made child protection especially prominent, but it did not mean that the majority of Central American youths were linked to gangs.

Remittances complicate any simple assessment of migration's effects. Money sent from abroad can fund food, schooling, housing, and medical care, but dependence on overseas income may expose families to shocks in foreign labor markets and to extortionists who infer that a household can pay. When parents migrate, caregiving arrangements change, often placing additional responsibilities on grandparents or other relatives. Return after deportation may then produce new burdens for the same households. A historically complete account examines migration not merely as a border-security debate but as a restructuring of families, labor, civic participation, and community authority across the isthmus.

The Rise of Bukele and the Salvadoran Security Transformation

Section 111.9

Nayib Bukele's Rise and the Territorial Control Plan, 2019–2021

For three decades after the peace, Salvadoran politics alternated between ARENA and the FMLN. By the late 2010s, both were tarnished by corruption cases, by the failure of their security policies, and by economic stagnation. Nayib Bukele, the son of a prominent businessman, won the mayoralty of Nuevo Cuscatlán in 2012 and of San Salvador in 2015 as an FMLN candidate, was expelled from the party in 2017, and ran for president in 2019 under the banner of a small party, GANA, after the electoral authorities blocked his own movement's registration. He won in February 2019 with a majority in the first round, ending the two-party order. His movement, Nuevas Ideas, soon became the dominant political force, and in February 2021 it won a legislative supermajority.

His security program began with the Territorial Control Plan, launched in June 2019. It deployed police and soldiers into gang-affected municipalities, increased prison restrictions, and used social-media communication to present security as a visible success. Homicides were already falling when Bukele took office, and they continued to fall in 2019 and 2020, a trend that scholars attribute to a mix of earlier policies, gang strategy, and new deployments. Images of shirtless, tightly packed prisoners during the COVID-19 lockdown in April 2020 signaled a harsher prison regime.

Allegations that the government negotiated with gang leaders have been central to the debate. Salvadoran journalists, notably at El Faro, reported in 2020 on contacts between officials and gang leaders, and in December 2021 the U.S. Treasury sanctioned two Salvadoran officials, citing negotiations with gangs. The government has denied that it made any deal. These claims remain disputed, and the chapter treats them as allegations supported by reporting and by a U.S. sanction decision, but not as judicial findings. Meanwhile Bukele's relations with the other branches of government changed. In February 2020 he brought soldiers into the Assembly during a budget dispute, and in May 2021 the new Assembly removed the Constitutional Chamber's justices and the attorney general, replacing them with allies. The Supreme Court then ruled in September 2021 that presidential reelection for a single consecutive term was permitted, a decision that opened the way to his 2024 candidacy.

Bukele's rise cannot be understood solely as an advertising success. For many voters, the ARENA–FMLN political settlement had permitted peaceful electoral alternation but had failed to deliver credible personal security and had been damaged by high-profile corruption investigations. Local government gave Bukele visibility as an administrator and communicator, while disputes with the FMLN leadership helped him present himself as independent of the postwar establishment. Digital media allowed him to address citizens directly and frame legislative disagreement as obstruction. Yet the same campaign drew on actual frustration with extortion, limited job opportunities, and the costs of older security policies. Public demand for a break was genuine even when specific proposed remedies were debatable.

The Territorial Control Plan predated the emergency and therefore matters for causal inference. Expanded police and military deployment, prison restrictions, and surveillance in selected areas may have influenced criminal activity before March 2022. Claims about particular stages of the plan should be tested against published budgets, operational descriptions, and independently observed local outcomes. An evaluation that begins only in March 2022 can attribute earlier trends to a later policy. Conversely, a decline recorded before the emergency does not explain why the post-2022 collapse in homicide figures was so dramatic. A serious chronology examines both periods without assuming the same mechanism operated throughout.

Allegations of contacts with gang leaders demand unusually careful wording. United States Treasury sanctions and investigative journalism identify alleged negotiations and concessions by named officials; Salvadoran authorities deny an improper arrangement. Sanctions are administrative legal acts with stated evidentiary bases, not criminal convictions of everyone named. Journalistic accounts may offer credible documents or testimony without resolving every disputed episode. Students should ask what each source actually establishes: a meeting, a payment, a transfer, a stated promise, or an inference about homicide trends. The integrity of later security claims cannot be tested by repeating either the government's denials or its critics' strongest allegations as settled fact. The competing evidence should be placed alongside the shift in control of the legislature and courts after 2021.

Section 111.10

March 2022: The Gang Killings and the State of Exception

On Saturday, 26 March 2022, the Salvadoran police counted about sixty killings, the deadliest day in decades, which authorities attributed to gangs. The next day the Legislative Assembly, on the president's request, approved a state of exception for thirty days. It has been extended repeatedly, in monthly renewals, and remained in effect as of the 16 November 2026 cutoff, under Decree 689 of 21 October extending it through 26 November 2026. Decree 333 initially suspended constitutional protections under Articles 7(1), 12(2), 13(2), and 24, relating to association, defense guarantees, limits on administrative detention, and privacy of communications; later renewals, including the August 2022 extension, no longer included Article 7(1). Related statutory measures changed criminal procedure and penalties. Accompanying legislative changes lengthened sentences, created new offenses relating to gang symbols and "illicit groups," allowed collective hearings, and extended criminal responsibility to minors from twelve years of age in some circumstances.

The government justified the measures as necessary to break the gangs' command structures, arguing that ordinary procedures could not cope with the scale of the threat. It also pointed to widespread public exhaustion with gang rule. Why the March 2022 surge happened has been disputed. Government critics and journalists suggested it was linked to a breakdown in an informal understanding between the government and the gangs, and the government denied any such arrangement. This remains unresolved. What is not disputed is that the state of exception produced an immediate and large-scale police and military response.

The exception differs from the ordinary use of emergency powers because of its duration. Constitutional provisions on suspension of rights typically presuppose a time-limited response to an emergency. Monthly renewals for more than four years raised the question that the Inter-American Commission and others pressed: when does an emergency measure become a permanent regime? Domestic courts offered little check. The legal conditions of the exception and its practical operation are the subject of the next sections.

Legal chronology is essential. Legislative Decree 333, issued on 27 March 2022, initially suspended constitutional guarantees identified as Article 7, first paragraph, Article 12, second paragraph, Article 13, second paragraph, and Article 24. They concern association, defense-related rights, the permissible period of administrative detention, and correspondence or communications. Yet the suspension did not remain identical in every later renewal. By an August 2022 extension, the text continued suspensions under Articles 12(2), 13(2), and 24 without continuing Article 7(1). To say indefinitely that freedom of association was suspended under every decree would therefore misstate the evolving statutory record. Students should compare the original decree with a specific renewal rather than infer continuous legal effects from a shorthand description.

It is equally important to separate a formally suspended guarantee from other powers created in related legislation. Rules on gang-related offenses, criminal procedure, restrictions on public communication, juvenile liability, and group hearings arose through different measures that should be identified individually. The constitutional question concerns not only whether the Assembly could declare an emergency, but also whether repeated renewals remained justified by a particular, time-limited disturbance and whether effective judicial remedies continued to operate. The presence of an electoral majority explains political authorization but does not by itself settle compliance with the constitution or international human-rights obligations.

The latest renewal available before the chapter cutoff is concrete: Legislative Decree 689, adopted on 21 October 2026, extended the regime from 28 October through 26 November 2026. Thus, as of 16 November, the emergency was in force; its next renewal was a future decision. Government representatives cited restored order and territorial control as reasons for continuation. Critics argued that exceptional powers had become normalized despite the severe decline in recorded killings. Both positions are observable political arguments, but evaluating them requires investigating the continuing need for suspension, the regularity of criminal proceedings, and whether particular restrictions are proportionate. This distinction between what the law says, what authorities do, and whether those actions are justified should guide the remaining sections.

Historical Vocabulary
State of exception

A state of exception (régimen de excepción) is a legal regime in which a government temporarily suspends certain constitutional rights in response to an emergency. International human-rights law permits such suspensions only under strict conditions: necessity, proportionality, time limits, and non-derogable rights that cannot be suspended, such as protection from torture.

The term is distinct from a state of siege (estado de sitio), a related but separately defined mechanism used in Guatemala in 2026. Readers should not equate the two or assume that either measure means the same thing in different countries. Each is defined by its own constitution and statutes.

Section 111.11

Mass Arrests, Prison Expansion, and CECOT

Under the exception, police and soldiers carried out sweeps, checkpoints, and raids. The government reported more than 91,500 arrests according to government data cited by the IACHR on 21 April 2026, a cumulative figure that does not tell how many remained detained at any date. Monitors noted that the Attorney General's Office and the judiciary reported they did not hold detailed statistics on arrests, releases, and case outcomes, which limits independent verification. The government has stated that a portion of those arrested were released after review. Reports by human-rights organizations documented arrests based on anonymous tips, tattoos, appearance, or residence, and found that people with no clear gang connection were detained.

Detention ran through pretrial processes in which large numbers of defendants were tried together in collective hearings, sometimes with public defenders handling many cases. Pretrial detention could last far longer than the originally allowed period, since the law extended time limits. The prison population, already large, rose to levels that monitors described as among the highest incarceration rates in the world. In January 2023 the government opened CECOT, the Center for Terrorism Confinement, a facility in Tecoluca with a stated capacity of 40,000, designed to hold gang leaders and members in conditions of strict isolation. The government presented the prison as a way to dismantle command structures and to deprive gangs of communication.

It is important to separate detention from conviction. A person arrested under the exception was a detainee, and in many cases a defendant awaiting trial, not necessarily a convicted offender. Critics questioned the evidence on which some detentions rested. Families of detainees often had difficulty learning where relatives were held, what charges applied, or whether they were alive, and they organized to seek information, sometimes at risk to themselves. The documentation of these experiences is largely from non-governmental groups, since the government restricted access to prisons and to some records. Equally, the victims of gangs, whose relatives had been killed or extorted, were among the strongest supporters of the policy, and their testimony is part of the historical record too.

The prison system illustrates both the logistical ambition and the evidentiary problems of the crackdown. CECOT, built near Tecoluca and inaugurated in 2023, was presented as a means of isolating influential gang prisoners and preventing orders from reaching neighborhoods. Its scale, controlled movement, severe restrictions, and carefully produced publicity distinguished it from earlier crowded prisons. Yet incarceration capacity does not answer the threshold question of how individuals were identified, whether evidence was tested, or what happened to people later found not guilty. A prison's physical capacity, its population on a given date, and the cumulative number of people sent through it are separate statistical concepts.

Mass criminal proceedings also place pressure on ordinary legal safeguards. Individualized proof can be difficult in cases involving clandestine organizations, but collective proceedings must still permit defendants to know and challenge the evidence against them. A person accused of extortion, murder, or exercising leadership presents a different evidentiary case from someone arrested because of appearance or neighborhood proximity. Legal counsel requires meaningful time, records, and access to clients; a nominal appointment is not equivalent to an effective defense. Family members need reliable systems for tracing detention locations and medical status. The combination of large arrest numbers and restrictions on case information makes independent oversight particularly important.

The accumulation of prisoners creates further questions about health, nutrition, disease control, and responsibility for deaths in custody. Every reported death should be investigated by competent authorities, but a list compiled by monitors does not automatically prove the cause of each death or criminal responsibility for it. Conversely, the absence of an official finding does not establish that an allegation is false when independent access is restricted. The government emphasizes that incarceration removed violent actors from neighborhoods; independent investigators emphasize the need for due process, appropriate medical care, and transparent case review. These are not rival facts about one measurable indicator. They concern distinct obligations of the state, all of which remain relevant when evaluating whether a security program is institutionally sustainable.

Section 111.12

Falling Homicides and the Recovery of Public Space

The decline in recorded homicides is the best-documented outcome of the Bukele period. The Prosecutor’s Office online table reports 496 homicides in 2022 (the Security Cabinet’s January 2026 retrospective gives 495), a rate near 7.8 per 100,000, about 154 in 2023, 114 in 2024 (about 1.9), and 82 in 2025, a rate of 1.3 announced by the Security Cabinet in January 2026. UNHCR's 2025 global report cites a fall from 18.1 per 100,000 in 2021 to 1.3 in 2025, and notes that overall reported crime fell by 52 percent between 2024 and 2025. For comparison, the rate in 2015 is commonly reported at 103 to 106, among the highest ever recorded outside war. InSight Crime listed El Salvador in 2026 as having the lowest homicide rate in Latin America for 2025.

These facts are consistent across independent compilations, though most rest on Salvadoran government data. Careful analysis therefore asks about classification. In 2019 the National Police announced it would stop counting some deaths, such as those occurring in confrontations between security forces and alleged gang members and bodies found in clandestine graves, which lowered the reported rate. Missing-person reports and discoveries of bodies raise additional questions about whether all violent deaths appear in homicide counts, and some organizations have reported higher numbers of missing persons. Access to official data is also limited. These problems do not erase the decline, since the fall from the previous decade is too large to be explained by classification alone, but they argue against treating every figure as independently verified.

The decline in killings was accompanied by reduced gang territorial control. Residents in many neighborhoods report moving more freely, using public transport, and keeping businesses open later, and surveys have shown high public approval of the government's security policy. Extortion appears to have fallen as well, according to government statements and UN reporting, although it is difficult to measure for the same reasons that earlier made it difficult. At the same time, some residents continue to fear arbitrary arrest, a fear that Section 111.15 examines. The chapter therefore treats the recovery of public space as a genuine and widely experienced change, and not as proof that the accompanying detention practices were lawful or necessary in the form used.

Official series illustrate the need for transparent provenance. The Salvadoran Prosecutor's Office online homicide table assigns 496 to 2022, while a January 2026 security-ministry retrospective series gives 495. Both figures are attributed to Salvadoran authorities, and the single-case difference should be identified rather than silently resolved. These counts also belong to different publication contexts, and the table labels used to categorize killings matter. The 2023 and 2024 published figures of 154 and 114, and the 2025 figure of 82, are useful observations, but should not be treated as independently generated by UNHCR or news organizations when those bodies reproduce the Salvadoran government's underlying data.

Historical measurement requires three separate questions. First, what change occurred in the recorded number of intentional homicides, using a defined population denominator for rates? Second, what changed in coverage, exclusion rules, and access to underlying records? Third, which specific measures may have contributed to the observed pattern? Even a very large decline cannot distinguish on its own among incapacitation through detention, previous security operations, deterrence, localized bargaining, improved reporting, or other mechanisms. The timing of the decline before and after March 2022 must be examined alongside the scope and pace of arrests and the available evidence on gangs' neighborhood activity. The strongest conclusion is that recorded killings fell extraordinarily sharply, while the precise distribution of causal responsibility remains contested.

Improvement in perceived safety is a separate domain of evidence. Residents' accounts of reopened streets and reduced fear deserve serious treatment; so do accounts by families who fear detention without individualized evidence. A national poll may demonstrate high aggregate approval but obscure differences among rural residents, former gang-controlled neighborhoods, victims of crime, and relatives of detainees. Extortion figures may also suffer from underreporting, especially when victims assume that reporting could trigger consequences. An outcome evaluation therefore needs separate measures of homicide, missing persons, extortion, school attendance, movement, business activity, and procedural fairness. No single favorable number is a substitute for this wider profile, although the decline in recorded homicide remains an undeniable and historically important result of the period.

Human Rights, Political Institutions, and the Costs of Security

Section 111.13

Due Process, Arbitrary Detention, and Human-Rights Investigations

The documentation of the state of exception comes from several kinds of institutions. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, an organ of the Organization of American States, monitored the regime and, on 21 April 2026, called on El Salvador to restore the full validity of the suspended rights and guarantees and to respect human rights in measures against crime. International and Salvadoran non-governmental organizations, including Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, and Cristosal, published case-based reports. In March 2026 an independent International Group of Experts (GIPES) presented findings to the IACHR, which asserted that a pattern of arbitrary detention, forced disappearance, torture, and deaths in custody might amount to crimes against humanity and recommended international investigation. Amnesty International released a report in 2026 reaching a similar assessment of possible crimes against humanity.

These bodies reported arrests without warrants, detentions without adequate evidence, lack of effective access to counsel, mass hearings, and deaths in custody. They also reported abuse of detainees, including children and other vulnerable persons. Some documented cases involved people with no known gang ties, and in some cases legal proceedings eventually ended in release. The GIPES report cited 540 cases of forced disappearance through February 2025 and 403 deaths in custody through August 2025, and other monitors gave different figures. Because the government restricted data, these counts are uncertain and rely on case reports.

The government rejected these findings. Bukele criticized the GIPES report, saying it demanded release of all arrested gang members, a characterization that the report's supporters disputed. Officials described the allegations as politically motivated and pointed to the improvement in public safety. The state also described individual deaths in custody as natural or unrelated to mistreatment, while monitors said that autopsies and investigations were insufficient. Pressure on civil society grew: in 2025 the Assembly passed a law requiring foreign-funded organizations to register and pay a heavy tax on foreign funding, a prominent human-rights lawyer, Ruth López, was arrested in May 2025, and Cristosal moved its operations out of the country. Journalists from El Faro had left earlier, citing threats. These developments affect the evidence: the documentation of abuses has become harder to produce inside the country precisely when it is most contested.

A fair historical reading holds two things together. The gangs killed and extorted thousands, and there is credible evidence that many Salvadorans experienced the exception as relief. There is also substantial evidence from multiple independent bodies that the regime has detained many people arbitrarily and that detainees have died in state custody. Neither fact cancels the other, and the weight historians will ultimately give to each depends on information not yet available, such as full judicial records and independent access to prisons.

The evidentiary status of each human-rights finding needs precision. An international commission's report may establish a pattern of complaints and identify violations using its investigative standards; it does not have the same authority as a criminal judgment against a named officer. A nongovernmental organization's interviews can expose cases omitted from official datasets, while sampling and access limitations make statistical extrapolation uncertain. The GIPES figure of 403 deaths in custody covered cases reported through August 2025, and its 540 disappearance cases were documented through February 2025. Neither should be represented as a complete November 2026 count. Later organizational updates should be listed separately, with their own dates, methods, and definitions, not added together without checking for overlap.

The distinction between arbitrary detention and lawful prosecution is especially important. A person can be arrested on reasonable evidence and later acquitted; that does not establish that the original detention was necessarily arbitrary. Conversely, a conviction does not automatically validate every earlier search or detention procedure. Investigators need to examine the stated grounds for arrest, access to counsel, duration without meaningful review, evidence used at hearings, and the ability to correct mistakes. In cases involving death in custody, independent medical investigation, preservation of records, and family notification are central. An allegation of torture requires its own evidentiary analysis rather than inference from imprisonment alone. These distinctions protect the historical account against both official denial of all abuses and unsupported generalization from particular cases.

Constraints on information are themselves historically significant. The 2025 Foreign Agents Law changed the legal environment for civil-society organizations relying on overseas funding; the May 2025 arrest of the lawyer Ruth López became a prominent case for regional rights bodies. As organizations relocate or change their work in response to legal pressure, the supply of independent testimony and case records can decline. The result is not merely a political controversy; it also changes what later historians will be able to verify. It is therefore essential to preserve documentary dates, identify the issuing organization, explain the method used, and avoid turning the absence of accessible state records into a definitive finding about every detainee. The problem is one of public accountability and historical evidence at the same time.

Section 111.14

Democratic Institutions, Presidential Power, and Constitutional Change

Security policy and institutional centralization overlapped in El Salvador. The removal of the Constitutional Chamber and the attorney general in May 2021, the 2021 court ruling on reelection, and the legislature's repeated renewal of the exception gave the executive a degree of control that earlier postwar presidents lacked. In February 2024, Bukele was reelected with roughly 85 percent of the valid votes, in an election criticized by constitutional scholars because the constitution had long barred immediate presidential reelection. Opposition parties won few seats in the Assembly that year, which also reduced the number of seats and changed the way votes were converted to seats, changes critics said favored the ruling party.

On 31 July 2025, the Legislative Assembly, voting 57 to 3, approved constitutional amendments permitting indefinite presidential reelection, extending the presidential term from five to six years, eliminating the runoff, and advancing the next presidential election so that the current term would end on 1 June 2027 instead of 2029. The reform also removed earlier provisions that had penalized promoting reelection and had barred recently serving presidents from running again. Bukele had said in 2024 that he did not think a constitutional reform would be necessary. Supporters argued that the change reduced the costs of frequent campaigns and let voters decide on a leader's performance. Critics, including opposition legislators and many constitutional scholars, argued that the reform ended the principle of alternation in power that had been part of Salvadoran constitutions since 1841, and that the process violated the constitution's own amendment rules. In July 2026, the ruling party ratified Bukele's candidacy for the presidential election of February 2027, so a third consecutive term was possible but not yet decided at the time of this chapter's cutoff.

Public approval is part of the explanation, not a footnote. Polls repeatedly showed very high support for Bukele, and many Salvadorans regarded the elimination of gang control as a historic gain that outweighed institutional concerns. Democratic theory does not make popular support irrelevant, and it does not make it conclusive either: institutions such as independent courts and a free press exist to protect rights when majorities or officeholders would prefer not to. The historical question is whether security gains and institutional constraints can coexist, which El Salvador had not settled by November 2026.

The institutional transformation preceded the indefinite-reelection vote. When the newly elected Legislative Assembly removed senior judges and the attorney general in May 2021, the balance between elected majorities and constitutionally protected offices changed. The subsequent Constitutional Chamber interpretation permitting an incumbent to seek immediate reelection was issued by judges appointed after those removals. The debate therefore involves more than a single presidential election: it concerns the sequence through which bodies responsible for checking executive power were reconstituted, and the legal interpretations that followed. Students should distinguish a government winning elections from an institutional framework that allows competitors, journalists, and courts to contest its decisions fairly.

The July 2025 constitutional amendment also had a timing effect. By shortening the ongoing term's endpoint and synchronizing the next presidential contest with legislative and local elections in 2027, the reform changed the incentives of parties and candidates. Removal of a runoff could affect how fragmented opposition votes translate into a winner; six-year terms alter how frequently voters review a presidency; and indefinite reelection changes the prospect of alternation. Each mechanism should be analyzed separately rather than summarized merely as "more power." Supporters argued that voters should be free to retain an effective leader; critics challenged both the substance of the revisions and the procedure by which they were adopted.

The governing party's July 2026 nomination of Bukele for the 2027 election was a nomination, not an electoral victory. At the chapter's cutoff, the election had not happened. The historian should avoid projecting later outcomes and instead examine the political conditions visible at the time: public approval, legislative dominance, restricted institutional counterweights, opposition capacity, and the continuing exception. Such analysis can recognize political popularity without equating it with unconstrained authority to suspend rights. It also avoids a simplistic contrast between democracy and security by asking exactly which political and legal safeguards were modified, how they were justified, and what opportunities remained for independent review.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Lasting Security or Institutional Risk?

The question: Did the Bukele strategy create durable security, or does its reliance on emergency powers present a long-term institutional risk?

Interpretation A — A durable security gain: Gang territorial control has been broken, killings have fallen to historic lows, extortion has declined, and public space has been recovered. Earlier, softer strategies failed, and the scale of gang rule justified exceptional measures. Public approval reflects lived improvement.
Interpretation B — A fragile gain with institutional risk: The gains rest on suspended rights and mass detention, with documented arbitrary arrests and deaths in custody. Without independent courts, data access, and a free press, errors cannot be corrected and abuse can continue. Concentration of power creates risk if future officials use the same tools differently.

Evidence: Dated homicide and crime statistics, victimization surveys, polling, detention and release data (where available), monitor reports, court records, and comparison with other countries. Key evidence, such as independent measures of detainee numbers and case outcomes, remains unavailable.

Why it matters: The answer affects how other countries assess and borrow the model, and how historians distinguish short-term results from long-term outcomes. The two interpretations are not strict opposites: evidence can support a real security improvement and a real institutional risk at once.

Section 111.15

Everyday Life after Gang Territorial Control

For many residents of formerly gang-controlled neighborhoods, the most visible change has been mobility. Bus routes that had been dangerous operate more normally. Markets and small shops stay open, and residents report that they can visit relatives or attend events across former boundaries. Women report greater safety in public spaces in many areas, and families describe sending children to school without fear of recruitment. Tourism has grown, and the government has invested in downtown San Salvador and public works. Surveys cited in public reporting have shown very high approval for the security policy.

The same residents may live with new uncertainty. Many families know someone who was detained, and in poor neighborhoods in particular, young men with tattoos or past police records worry about arrest. Families of detainees describe spending savings on lawyers, traveling to courts and prisons for information, and receiving little. Small businesses may no longer pay extortion, yet some report lower sales or continuing economic hardship. Former gang members who left before the exception, and people who left gangs years earlier, face the difficulty of proving it. Reintegration programs have been limited. Rebuilding trust within neighborhoods, which had developed forms of silence and fear over decades, is a slow process.

Accounts of this period require care in attribution. A resident's testimony reflects one household's circumstances. Public opinion polls show broad support but are conducted in a climate where criticism of the government can carry costs, and monitors caution about reading polls as a measure of how every group experiences the policy. The chapter therefore avoids describing "the Salvadoran experience" as uniform.

The meaning of safer public space becomes clearer at the scale of individual routines. A market vendor who previously paid renta might now plan a week without setting aside a payment to a collector. A bus passenger may cross a boundary once avoided because of rival gang control, while a relative can travel to visit another neighborhood without the same risk of being questioned by armed lookouts. Shops may extend hours and workers may accept jobs farther from home. These experiences are meaningful even when they cannot be reduced to a precise monetary gain. They also shape political legitimacy because citizens encounter the consequences of a security policy repeatedly in everyday life, not only during elections.

The same scale reveals costs that national security announcements rarely capture. Relatives of people taken into custody may spend savings traveling to government offices, searching prison lists, paying for legal help, or arranging care for children whose parent has disappeared from the household. A child may be safer on the route to school while also losing a caregiver to detention. Neighbors may become reluctant to discuss arrests publicly, making the social fabric difficult to study through ordinary surveys. Those experiences do not erase the safety gains reported by other residents. They show why a single national narrative is insufficient to describe an unevenly distributed policy.

Researchers should gather accounts from people with different relationships to the state: victims of gang violence, former merchants under extortion, teachers, public defenders, detainees' families, neighborhood volunteers, and health workers. Interviewees may withhold information for fear of gangs, police, or political retaliation. Testimony should be recorded with attention to chronology, consent, and risks to participants. Patterns across independent accounts are more informative than one exceptionally moving story presented as representative. This methodological caution is necessary for both positive and negative claims. The study of everyday life explains why security and civil liberty are not abstract, competing slogans but overlapping conditions affecting work, family relationships, mobility, and trust.

Everyday Life and Social History

Buses, Markets, and Waiting Rooms

Two daily scenes, drawn from patterns in monitoring and press reports, illustrate the coexistence of change and uncertainty. On public transport, a rider can cross neighborhoods that were once closed to people from outside. Section 111.16

CECOT, U.S. Deportations, and International Security Cooperation

In 2025, El Salvador became a participant in United States deportation policy. In February 2025 the U.S. State Department designated MS-13, together with several Mexican cartels and Tren de Aragua, as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, and designated Barrio 18 effective 24 September 2025 under a separate Federal Register notice. A Foreign Terrorist Organization designation is a U.S. legal measure under immigration law that carries consequences such as criminal liability for material support and immigration inadmissibility. It is not a universal scholarly classification, and it does not substitute for historical analysis of what these groups are or how they developed.

In March 2025 the United States flew several hundred people, most of them Venezuelans accused by U.S. officials of belonging to Tren de Aragua (a subject of Chapter 112), to CECOT, under an arrangement with the Salvadoran government that included payment. The U.S. administration invoked the Alien Enemies Act for some removals, and U.S. courts heard challenges over due process. Many of the men said they had no gang affiliation, and lawyers and families argued that tattoos or other evidence were insufficient. The men were held without access to outside lawyers and, according to monitors, without charges in Salvadoran courts. In July 2025, 252 Venezuelans were released from CECOT and returned to Venezuela in a prisoner exchange for ten people held there. Rights groups reported abuses against some of the detainees, which the Salvadoran government denied. As of April 2026, monitors reported that the number of Salvadorans transferred in the same operations was not publicly disclosed, and that at least thirty-six remained detained.

This bounded case study shows how criminal allegations, proven organizational links, detention status, and verified abuses must be kept apart. U.S. officials alleged gang membership. Detainees and their lawyers disputed it. Judicial determinations were limited. Reports of mistreatment came from monitors and former detainees, while the government rejected them. The episode also illustrates the new geography of security cooperation: a small state housing detainees for a larger one, in a relationship with economic and political benefits for both governments, and with Salvadoran leadership acting by its own calculation and not merely as an instrument of U.S. policy.

The international dimension requires separating several decisions taken by different governments. In February 2025 the United States designated MS-13 as a Foreign Terrorist Organization; the U.S. designation of Barrio 18 became effective on 24 September 2025 under a distinct Federal Register notice. These designations have specified consequences under United States law and were not judgments by an international court that all persons suspected of contact with those organizations were guilty of particular crimes. A designation concerns an organization; the evidentiary basis for labeling an individual a member or proving a separate offense remains a different legal question.

The transfer of Venezuelan nationals to CECOT in March 2025 tested those distinctions. The U.S. administration asserted gang-related and immigration grounds for removals, while lawyers and relatives disputed classifications and contested access to hearings. El Salvador agreed to hold people sent from outside its ordinary domestic criminal process. On 18 July 2025, 252 Venezuelans returned as part of a negotiated exchange, providing a defined end point for that cohort's Salvadoran detention, though not resolving each allegation or legal claim. The historian must distinguish immigration removal, national-security designation, foreign custody, prisoner exchange, and criminal conviction. They are different legal acts with distinct procedures and evidentiary thresholds.

This cooperation also demonstrates agency on both sides. United States officials sought removal capacity and the demonstration of a tougher migration policy; the Salvadoran government sought diplomatic influence, financial or political benefits, and international recognition of its detention system. Governments can cooperate while exercising different legal responsibilities. Human-rights questions follow people across borders: which state authorized detention, which had custody, what information families received, and which courts could review a transfer? A documentary study should compare the texts of public agreements, congressional testimony, legal filings, and released government statements rather than rely only on dramatic prison images. The cases connect Central American gang history to global debates over outsourced detention without requiring this chapter to retell the entire history of Venezuelan criminal networks reserved for Chapter 112.

Regional Comparison, Migration, and the Future of Security

Section 111.17

Honduras: Gang Suppression, Extortion, and the Limits of Emergency Rule

Honduras began its anti-gang policies early. A 2003 reform of the penal code under President Ricardo Maduro criminalized gang membership, and its enforcement, like that of Mano Dura, filled prisons without ending gang activity. Prisons became sites of catastrophe: the 2012 fire at the Comayagua penitentiary killed some 360 inmates, and in June 2023 a massacre at the women's prison at Támara killed dozens of women, an episode the government attributed to gang members. The police were repeatedly reformed and purged with limited durable success, and links between criminal groups and politicians were exposed in U.S. courts. Former president Juan Orlando Hernández was convicted in a U.S. federal court in 2024 on drug-trafficking and weapons charges and sentenced to a long prison term, and was pardoned by President Trump in December 2025. The sequence shows the entanglement of state power and trafficking in Honduras, and also the way United States policy has moved between prosecuting and forgiving Honduran officials.

In December 2022, President Xiomara Castro declared a state of exception, inspired in part by the Salvadoran example, and it was extended repeatedly in forty-five-day periods until it covered much of the country. Government figures and independent compilations showed falling homicide rates: preliminary national figures reported in early 2026 suggested a 2025 rate as low as 23.2 per 100,000, but the interinstitutional UNAH National Violence Observatory bulletin published in August 2026 documented 2,633 killings in 2025, or 26.2 per 100,000, compared with 2,690 in 2024. These different series should not be mixed without indicating their methodology and publication dates. The measure expired on 26 January 2026, the final day of Xiomara Castro’s administration. National Party president Nasry Asfura took office the following day, 27 January, and did not immediately restore the expired emergency. The United Nations human rights office reported that security forces committed killings, disappearances, and torture in 2025 under the exception, and that the regime had lost its temporary character after many extensions.

The Honduran experience shows why homicide reduction does not necessarily mean extortion reduction. U.S. security assessments and Honduran observers noted that the state of exception did not appear to curb extortion, particularly of the transport sector, and that new groups emerged. Kidnappings recorded by the authorities rose from 8 in 2023 to 24 in 2025. Tegucigalpa and San Pedro Sula, where homicide rates had been very high, saw marked declines. Yet impunity remained high, institutions were weak, and the police and prosecutors lacked capacity. The Asfura government said it would address extortion and violence without emergency powers, and its record was not established by the time of this chapter's cutoff.

Honduras's history demonstrates why a similar legal instrument need not produce the same measured outcomes. The country contains several distinct criminal environments: urban neighborhoods where extortionists target transport, cities affected by organized drug markets, rural districts with contested land, and border corridors linking commerce to illicit transit. Police units and prosecutors differ in resources and credibility across those spaces. A decree allowing exceptional search and detention powers cannot itself create reliable investigators, protected witnesses, or courts capable of pursuing major corruption cases. Local conditions also influence whether armed groups can retreat, fragment, or exploit the absence of effective public services.

The administration of Xiomara Castro introduced an emergency in December 2022 and renewed it in multiple municipalities. Rights monitors reported concern over abuses, while authorities emphasized policing results. The measure expired on 26 January 2026, the last day of her administration; Nasry Asfura took office on 27 January. The chronology matters because the incoming government did not cause an expiration that had occurred before inauguration, though it subsequently determined whether and how to use extraordinary powers. In assessing the period, students should compare the legal breadth of each Honduran order with actual changes in extortion, homicide, prosecution, and public trust rather than equate the existence of an emergency with effective gang suppression.

Quantitative comparisons also require particular care. Early 2026 accounts circulated preliminary figures suggesting a 2025 homicide rate near 23.2 per 100,000, but the interinstitutional National Violence Observatory of UNAH presented its 2025 bulletin in August 2026 with 2,633 homicides and a rate of 26.2. The 2024 comparable count was 2,690. A lower number may come from a preliminary police series, a different population estimate, or a narrower case definition; it should not be placed alongside a later consolidated figure without explanation. The broader conclusion is a decline from the earlier extreme levels but continuing serious violence and extortion. It is not evidence of a Salvadoran-style transformation, and it is not a reason to dismiss real improvements documented in particular Honduran communities.

Everyday Life and Social History

Drivers, Fare Collectors, and the Route as a Tax Base

In Honduran cities, bus and taxi workers have long been among the most exposed to extortion. A route is a fixed, predictable, and valuable asset, and each stop on it can be taxed. Drivers and fare collectors, often paid by the day, face demands to pay or to stop operating. When violence occurs, it can close a route for weeks, leaving commuters to pay more for less reliable alternatives.

Studies of the sector have recorded many killings of transport workers over the years, though reported figures vary by source. The state of exception did not eliminate these pressures, which is one reason scholars treat homicide counts and extortion burdens as separate measures. Workers' associations pressed for protection and, at times, suspended service as a form of protest.

Section 111.18

Guatemala: Gangs, State Institutions, and the 2026 Security Emergency

Guatemala's gangs operated within a country with a larger population, a deeply unequal society, a large Indigenous population, and a history of institutional impunity. MS-13 and Barrio 18 extort bus routes and businesses, particularly in the capital and its surrounding areas, and the country also hosts drug-trafficking networks with links to Mexican groups. Police and prosecutorial capacity was limited. The UN-backed CICIG investigated corruption and organized crime from 2007 until the government ended its mandate in 2019, and later disputes over the prosecutor's office and the contested path to the 2024 inauguration of President Bernardo Arévalo reflected the unresolved conflict over who controls the justice system. The 2017 fire at a state-run children's home, in which forty-one girls died, remains a symbol of institutional failure.

Prisons have been a particular weakness. Gang leaders held in overcrowded facilities retain the ability to order actions outside. In late 2025, reports described the escape of about twenty Barrio 18 leaders from a prison, which led to the removal of security officials. On 17 January 2026, inmates seized control of three prisons and took dozens of hostages, according to reports of about forty-six, after officials limited privileges of gang leaders including Aldo Dupié, the imprisoned leader of Barrio 18. On 18 January, security forces retook the facilities, and gang members in and around Guatemala City attacked police officers in what authorities described as retaliation. At least seven officers were killed that day, and the reported death toll reached eleven officers by 31 January 2026. The Interior Minister blamed gangs, and President Arévalo declared a thirty-day state of siege, stating that the state would not yield to criminals.

The state of siege differs from the Salvadoran exception in legal basis and duration. It was declared under Guatemala's constitutional framework for a defined period, required congressional approval though it could take effect before the vote, expanded the operational role of police and army, and allowed measures such as arrests without a judicial order for the period. Arévalo said it should not alter normal life for most Guatemalans, and schools were temporarily closed. Guatemala also notified the United Nations of the derogation, as required for states party to the relevant human-rights treaty. The state of siege ended on 16 February 2026. A separate state of prevention entered into force on 17 February under Governmental Decree 2-2026, as confirmed by an official United Nations treaty notification. The durable effects of these measures had not been established by the chapter cutoff. Guatemala's homicide rate, about 17 per 100,000 in 2025 according to police-based and research compilations, rose slightly from the previous year, so there was no Salvadoran-style decline.

Guatemala's institutional setting differs further. Its peace process concerned the relationship between the state and Indigenous populations, the legacy of wartime intelligence networks, and reform of deeply unequal public institutions. The anti-impunity commission CICIG helped investigate corruption and illicit political networks before its mandate ended in 2019, demonstrating both the potential and fragility of external support for the rule of law. Gang extortion is serious, particularly for urban transport and small business, but it coexists with other criminal and political networks that cannot all be explained through the history of MS-13 and Barrio 18. A response aimed narrowly at street gangs therefore addresses only part of the national security problem.

The January 2026 crisis followed prison disturbances involving hostage-taking and coordinated attacks on police. Initial reporting listed fewer fatalities, but by 31 January the reported toll of police officers who died had risen to eleven. On 18 January President Bernardo Arévalo declared a thirty-day state of siege, and the government later described joint police-military operations and measures directed at prison control. The measure ended on 16 February 2026. From 17 February, Guatemala entered a different fifteen-day state of prevention under Governmental Decree 2-2026, which was separately notified to the United Nations under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. The later state's legal basis and restrictions must not be collapsed into the earlier state of siege.

This sequence permits an unusually clear comparative study of time limits. One government declared an emergency after a specific wave of attacks and moved to a different legal instrument at the end of thirty days; another, El Salvador, continued renewing an emergency begun in 2022. That distinction does not by itself prove which country protected citizens better. It does permit students to compare duration, oversight, official justification, detainee safeguards, and the conditions for returning to ordinary law. Guatemala's own records claim operational successes, but longer-term trends require comparable figures on homicides, extortion, prison control, and case disposition. The bounded emergency is a documented event; its durable effectiveness remains a historical question rather than an established conclusion at the 2026 cutoff.

Section 111.19

Migration, Regional Security, and Competing Policy Models

By the mid-2020s, the Salvadoran model had become a reference point. Politicians in several countries praised it, and some proposed similar measures. In Costa Rica, a country with a long democratic tradition, homicide rates rose to record levels in the 2020s, driven largely by drug trafficking, and Laura Fernández Delgado, declared elected by the Tribunal Supremo de Elecciones on 3 March 2026 and inaugurated on 8 May, campaigned on tougher security proposals, including measures likened by commentators to the Salvadoran approach. Campaign proposals must be distinguished from executive actions actually taken. Nicaragua, with a different political system under the Ortega–Murillo government and generally lower reported homicide rates, offered a contrast: low reported violence combined with severe restrictions on political freedom. These comparisons show that low homicide and political liberty do not always vary together.

Whether the Salvadoran approach is transferable depends on conditions. El Salvador is small, has a compact territory, a unified command over police and military, and a legislature aligned with the president. Gang control there was concentrated in particular neighborhoods and prisons, and the government had public support. Honduras has larger territory, weaker institutions, and gangs less dominant relative to other criminal groups. Guatemala is larger, has weaker state presence in rural areas, and has a more fragmented political system with institutions that, whatever their flaws, are not controlled by one party. Administrative capacity, legitimacy, and the structure of criminal organizations all differ.

Alternatives exist. Policies emphasizing community policing, violence-interruption programs, investigation of finances, prison reform, and social investment have shown mixed results in various countries, and reduction of homicide through such methods is typically slower. Rehabilitation and reintegration initiatives have been small in scale across the region. Migration policy intersects with all of these. In 2025 and 2026, tighter U.S. asylum and deportation policies, agreements to receive third-country nationals, and changing conditions in Mexico altered the routes and numbers of migrants, while remittances remained a major source of income and, in the Honduran case, a source of dependence on U.S. goodwill. Return migration, with deportees arriving in countries that had limited reintegration programs, once again raised the question of whether deportation would feed insecurity or whether conditions had changed.

Political borrowing often consists of adopting the most visible element of a foreign policy while ignoring the institutions that made its outcomes possible. Regional candidates praised Bukele's popularity, prison construction, military deployment, or reduced homicide statistics, but these were not necessarily equivalent proposals. A government could advocate larger prisons without changing judicial procedure; another could intensify policing while retaining ordinary constitutional safeguards. Comparison must separate the tools being borrowed from the promised results and determine whether the underlying crime problem is similar. Gang territorial control, corruption within customs agencies, transnational trafficking, and rural land conflict do not respond identically to the same intervention.

Costa Rica provides a revealing contrast. Its long history of competitive elections and civilian policing did not insulate it from rising homicides linked in part to drug-trade disputes. Laura Fernández Delgado was formally declared elected president by the electoral tribunal on 3 March 2026 and began her term on 8 May. Her election supplied a new political context for security proposals, but a platform position must not be reported as an enacted reform unless a dated government record documents implementation. Nicaragua presents a different problem: a government can claim order while tightly restricting independent journalism, civic organizations, and political opposition. Both cases show why apparently simple regional rankings can conceal large differences in transparency and the meaning of public security.

The United States also shaped the menu of regional choices through deportation, asylum processing, border enforcement, funding, sanctions, and diplomatic agreements. Stronger border controls can change migration routes and expose travelers to new risks without resolving the violence or economic conditions that influenced departure. Community-based interventions, rehabilitation, educational opportunities, and witness protection require longer observation than a short-term arrest campaign. Students should therefore compare policies along several dimensions: public safety, legal safeguards, administrative feasibility, financial cost, trust, and durability. The regional question is not whether leaders use the same rhetoric; it is whether an intervention changes measured victimization while preserving reliable institutions capable of correcting its mistakes.

Historical Vocabulary
Foreign Terrorist Organization

A Foreign Terrorist Organization (FTO) is a designation made by the U.S. Secretary of State under U.S. immigration law for foreign groups found to engage in terrorist activity that threatens U.S. nationals or security. Designation makes it a crime to provide material support to the group and has consequences for immigration and financial transactions.

In 2025 the United States designated MS-13 and later Barrio 18. The designation is a legal tool of one government. It is not a scholarly classification, and many criminologists describe these groups as street gangs and criminal enterprises rather than political-terrorist organizations. The term should be used as a description of a legal status.

Section 111.20

Historical Synthesis: Security, Liberty, and the Postwar State through 2026

The postwar history of the Northern Triangle shows how a peace settlement can end a war without building the institutions that provide ordinary safety. Weak police, slow courts, unequal economies, abundant weapons, mass migration, and the transnational circulation of gang culture produced neighborhood-based criminal organizations that taxed and governed poor communities by force. Deportation moved gang members and practices across borders, but local conditions determined how the gangs grew. Punitive policies and negotiation each failed to produce durable results before 2019.

The Salvadoran transformation after 2022 is measurable in some respects. Recorded homicides fell to a rate of 1.3 per 100,000, according to government figures reported by UNHCR and others, and public life in many neighborhoods changed. Other claims remain allegations or are unsettled. These include the number of people wrongfully detained, the causes of many deaths in custody, the existence and nature of earlier dealings with gangs, and the independent verification of crime statistics. Legal standards are also contested: the IACHR and independent experts have found the regime incompatible with human-rights obligations, while the government rejects that view. And some outcomes could not be established by 16 November 2026: whether Bukele will win a third term in February 2027, whether the exception will end, how many detainees will be tried, convicted, or released, and whether homicide stays low without emergency powers.

The regional comparison argues against both easy imitation and easy dismissal. Honduras reduced homicides under emergency rule without ending extortion, and then abandoned the measure. Guatemala used a thirty-day state of siege after a specific crisis, ending it on 16 February 2026 and replacing it with a separate state of prevention beginning the next day. Each state's institutions, gangs, and public support differ. The central question that remains is whether extraordinary security powers can produce durable safety while preserving accountability and equal citizenship. The evidence of 2026 does not settle it, and it will be answered by what happens after the emergency, not during it. Chapter 112 widens the frame to the transnational criminal networks, including Tren de Aragua, Brazilian organizations, and Ecuador's security crisis, that increasingly operate beyond the borders of any one country.

The chronology gives no single turning point that explains the whole chapter. Peace agreements reduced military confrontation but did not guarantee ordinary safety; migration reshaped families and gang networks but did not mechanically turn migrants into criminals; punitive policing altered prisons but did not end extortion; the 2012 truce reduced reported killings for a time but did not create durable institutional confidence. Bukele's administration then produced an unusually large and widely experienced security transformation whose official figures were accompanied by serious documentation of rights restrictions. These outcomes must be placed in relation to one another rather than organized as a simple rise-and-fall narrative.

The unresolved questions are concrete. How many people remained detained without individualized adjudication at the cutoff? How reliably were homicides, disappearances, and extortion counted? Could independent judges review arrests and provide remedies? Did safer neighborhoods translate into lasting opportunities for youths and businesses? What political alternatives remained available to voters under constitutional changes allowing indefinite presidential reelection? These questions cannot be answered by a single election result, headline homicide rate, or human-rights report. They require longitudinal evidence on people, institutions, and local economies.

As of 16 November 2026, El Salvador's Decree 689 continued the emergency through 26 November; the 2027 presidential election had not taken place. Honduras's exception had expired under the outgoing Castro administration in January 2026; its successor's long-term security record was not established. Guatemala's January state of siege had concluded and been replaced by a different, shorter legal measure in February; its lasting impact remained uncertain. These distinct endpoints should not be described as a single regionwide pattern. The history is still open, but the documented chronology is not unknowable. The chapter closes by asking which forms of public authority can defend residents from criminal coercion while remaining accountable to law, evidence, and the people most affected. Chapter 112 will examine broader organizations and criminal economies that cannot be understood solely through the politics of these three postwar states.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Central America's transition from civil war to electoral politics coincided with the growth of gangs, extortion, migration, and, in El Salvador, a security transformation that combined measurable gains with serious documented costs.

The peace settlements of 1992 and 1996 demobilized armies and created new police and electoral systems, but left weak courts, concentrated wealth, and unresolved accountability. Honduras, without a comparable civil war, had its own pathway of police weakness, trafficking, and political crisis.

MS-13 and Barrio 18 began in Los Angeles, in different circumstances: Barrio 18 in a Mexican-American gang of the 1960s that later admitted Central Americans, and MS-13 among Salvadoran refugee youth in the 1980s. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

U.S. deportation after 1996 moved members and practices to Central America. Historians disagree on how much this explains. The best-supported view treats deportation as one channel interacting with domestic conditions such as weak institutions, prisons, and local economies.

The gangs organized through neighborhood cliques, controlled territory, and drew income chiefly from extortion (renta) of businesses, transport, and households. Extortion shaped local economies and was especially entrenched in Honduras and Guatemala. Membership figures are estimates, and gang membership, coercion, kinship, and residence must be distinguished.

Mano Dura policies from 2003 filled prisons and strengthened gang organization. The 2012 truce reduced killings for a time but was politically contested and collapsed, and 2015 brought near-record violence. Violence, poverty, family reunification, and climate shaped migration, which must not be confused with criminality.

Nayib Bukele's 2019 election ended the two-party order. After the killings of 26 March 2022, the state of exception suspended rights, enabled mass arrests, and led to CECOT. Recorded homicides fell from 18.1 per 100,000 in 2021 to 1.3 in 2025, according to figures cited by UNHCR, with caveats about definitions and data access.

The IACHR, GIPES, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, and Salvadoran groups documented arbitrary detention, forced disappearances, and deaths in custody. The government rejected these findings. Constitutional changes in July 2025 allowed indefinite reelection, and in July 2026 Bukele's party ratified his candidacy for 2027.

El Salvador became part of U.S. security policy through the 2025 detention of deportees at CECOT, including 252 Venezuelans later returned. The U.S. FTO designations of MS-13 and Barrio 18 are legal measures and not historical conclusions.

Honduras ended its exception in January 2026 after reducing homicides but not extortion. Guatemala declared a thirty-day state of siege after the January 2026 prison riots and attacks on police. These cases show that the Salvadoran model is not easily transferable.

The chapter's main lesson is that security results and institutional costs must be evaluated together and with dated evidence. Whether extraordinary powers can yield durable safety with accountability remained unresolved in November 2026. Chapter 112 turns to wider transnational criminal networks.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what happens after a peace settlement succeeds on paper. Armies were demobilized and elections held, yet the institutions that give people ordinary safety were slow to form. The gangs that filled the gap were products of migration, war, deportation, and local weakness, and their history is also a history of the United States and Central America as a single social field.

It also shows a security transformation of unusual scale and the difficulty of judging it. A large and measurable fall in recorded killings, a recovery of public space, and wide popular support sit beside documented arbitrary detention, deaths in custody, and constitutional change that removed limits on presidential tenure. Historians do not have to choose one body of evidence over the other. They have to keep each dated, attributed, and open to correction.

The chapter connects to earlier themes of the textbook: the unfinished justice of Chapter 91, the transitions of Chapter 94, and the social movements of Chapter 110. It also leads forward. Chapter 112 examines organized crime that crosses borders and no longer fits the traditional cartel model, including Tren de Aragua, Brazil's organizations, and Ecuador's crisis, and the questions raised here about emergency powers, prisons, and migration will recur there. The final judgment on the Salvadoran model, and on Central America's other responses, belongs to the years after 2026.
