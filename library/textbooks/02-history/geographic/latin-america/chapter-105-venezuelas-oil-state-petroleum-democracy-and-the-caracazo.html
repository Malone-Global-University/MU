Venezuela's Oil State: Petroleum, Democracy, and the Caracazo

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Democracy Built on Oil Meets Its First Great Rupture

On Monday, 27 February 1989, a dispute over bus fares at a terminal in Guarenas, a town east of Caracas, set off the most lethal civil disturbance in modern Venezuelan history. The anger that spread through the country in the following days was about fares and prices, but it was also about something larger: the collapse of a bargain, struck over half a century, in which petroleum wealth was supposed to guarantee both prosperity and democratic stability.

This chapter explains how that bargain was made and how it came apart. Its subject is the Venezuelan petroleum state: a government whose power, budget, and political legitimacy came to rest on the export of crude oil, and a democratic system, usually called the Punto Fijo system after a 1958 agreement among political parties, whose stability depended on distributing oil income. The story begins in the early twentieth century, when a poor, rural, dictator-ruled country with an export economy of coffee and cacao discovered that the Maracaibo basin held some of the richest oilfields in the world. It ends in 1989, when a government elected on memories of oil prosperity imposed an austerity program in an economy that had been weakening for more than a decade.

The chapter's central argument is that oil was neither simply a blessing nor simply a curse. Petroleum revenue allowed the Venezuelan state to build roads, schools, hospitals, universities, steel mills, and hydroelectric dams, to pay off foreign debt, and to finance a competitive party democracy at a time when much of South America was falling under military rule. The same revenue encouraged imports over domestic production, concentrated power in the executive, rewarded political loyalty with public jobs and contracts, and led many Venezuelans to expect that the state could always deliver more. Foreign companies, Venezuelan politicians, military officers, technocrats, labor unions, and urban migrants each pursued their own interests within that system, and the outcomes were never determined by the oil alone.

Three themes organize the chapter. The first is sovereignty: how Venezuelan governments gradually claimed a larger share of oil income, from the concession laws of the Gómez dictatorship, through the 1943 Hydrocarbons Law and the 1948 profit-sharing arrangement, to the founding of OPEC in 1960 and nationalization in 1975–1976. The second is democracy: how a country with a long history of caudillo and military rule, and a brutal dictatorship as recently as 1958, produced a durable two-party system, and what that system excluded. The third is distribution: who benefited from oil income, who did not, and why a system built on abundance generated deep inequality and a precarious fiscal position when prices fell and debts came due.

Venezuela's history in these decades should not be read as the history of a single raw material alone. Venezuelans were also coffee growers and peasants displaced from the land, Andean migrants to the oil towns, Caribbean and European workers, women who gained the vote in the 1940s, students who organized against dictatorship, union members, soldiers, exiles, and the residents of the hillside barrios that grew around every major city. Indigenous peoples such as the Barí, Wayuu, and Añú of the Maracaibo region and the peoples of the Orinoco and Guayana lived through oil exploration and its consequences, and Afro-Venezuelan communities, descended from the enslaved Africans of the colonial plantation economy, remained central to the country's culture and labor force. The chapter attends to these groups where the evidence allows.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually contested. Historians use concession contracts, laws and decrees, congressional debates, company records, central bank and ministry statistics, diplomatic correspondence, party programs, newspapers, memoirs, union records, and, for the Caracazo, human-rights investigations and judicial findings. Each category has limits. Company and government figures served institutional purposes, party histories were written by participants, and the casualty figures for the Caracazo vary enormously depending on who counted and when. The chapter's source windows invite students to read these documents critically, and it marks where numbers are approximate or disputed.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It follows the Venezuelan oil state from 1914 to 1989 and treats the Caracazo as its culmination. It does not narrate the 1992 military rebellion, Hugo Chávez's imprisonment, the 1998 election, or the Bolivarian Constitution; those belong to Chapter 106, and the oil policies of the Chávez era belong to Chapter 107. Students should resist reading the Caracazo as a prologue that made later events inevitable. The task here is to explain a system's rise and the crisis of its legitimacy, and to leave open what Venezuelans did next.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Venezuela's pre-petroleum economy and political history shaped the way oil development unfolded after 1914.
  2. Analyze the concession system under Juan Vicente Gómez and the relationship between foreign capital, oil revenue, and central state power.
  3. Distinguish the 1943 Hydrocarbons Law from the 1948 profit-sharing arrangement and assess what each changed.
  4. Trace the movement from the 1945 civil-military overthrow through the Trienio, the 1948 coup, and the Pérez Jiménez dictatorship to the 1958 democratic restoration.
  5. Evaluate the Pact of Punto Fijo as a mechanism for preserving civilian rule, including who it included and who it excluded.
  6. Explain Venezuela's role in founding OPEC and the ideas of Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo about petroleum policy.
  7. Define the rentier state and assess how oil income strengthened government while shaping incentives for production, politics, and consumption.
  8. Compare the 1975–1976 nationalization and the creation of PDVSA with earlier forms of national control over petroleum, and interpret competing assessments of PDVSA's autonomy.
  9. Connect the 1973 oil shock, rising public spending, and external borrowing to the currency crisis of February 1983.
  10. Reconstruct the events of the Caracazo from sources of different types, distinguishing official figures from later human-rights and judicial findings.
  11. Assess why 1989 was a rupture in Venezuelan political legitimacy while explaining why the later rise of Chávez was not historically inevitable.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XXIV's larger account of oil, democracy, and collapse.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did oil, rather than coffee or manufacturing, become the foundation of the Venezuelan state, and what did the dictator Gómez's regime contribute to that outcome?
  2. In what ways did Venezuelan governments gain greater control over petroleum between 1943 and 1976, and why did each step stop short of full national control until 1975?
  3. How did a country with a history of military rule produce a stable civilian democracy after 1958, and how much of that stability depended on oil income?
  4. What does the concept of a rentier state explain about Venezuelan politics and economy, and what does it leave out?
  5. Was the nationalization of 1975–1976 a rupture with foreign control, a continuation of earlier trends, or both?
  6. Why did the boom of the 1970s leave Venezuela vulnerable to the crisis of the 1980s?
  7. How can historians reconstruct the Caracazo when official figures, press reports, human-rights investigations, and court judgments differ?
  8. Why did a political system that had survived insurgency and military conspiracy lose legitimacy so rapidly after 1983?
  9. How should historians weigh long-term structural pressures against contingent political choices in explaining 1989?

Historical Context

Earlier chapters have shown how Spanish colonial rule, the wars of independence, and republican caudillismo shaped northern South America. Venezuela had been a peripheral colony, organized as the Captaincy General of Venezuela in 1777, and its export economy rested on cacao, later coffee and hides, produced on plantations worked by enslaved Africans and on estates and ranches in the interior. The independence wars of 1810–1823 devastated its population and economy, and Simón Bolívar and many other leaders of Spanish American independence came from Venezuela (Chapters 53 and 60). After Venezuela left Gran Colombia in 1830, the country was governed by a succession of caudillos, endured the Federal War of 1859–1863, and abolished slavery in 1854. Through the late nineteenth century, state authority was weak and regional strongmen contested national power.

The decades before oil were a period of economic stagnation and political violence. Antonio Guzmán Blanco, who dominated Venezuelan politics from 1870 to 1888, built a measure of centralized authority and modernized Caracas, but the country remained poor and overwhelmingly rural. Cipriano Castro, an Andean general from Táchira, took power in 1899 and in 1902–1903 faced a naval blockade by Britain, Germany, and Italy over unpaid claims, an episode that illustrated the vulnerability of a debtor state with a narrow export base. Venezuela's population in the early twentieth century was under three million, with most people living in rural areas and in small towns, and Caracas was a modest city. The legal tradition inherited from Spanish rule, and affirmed in Bolívar's 1829 decree on mines, held that subsoil resources belonged to the state rather than to surface landowners. That principle, unusual compared with the legal regime of the United States, became decisive once petroleum was found.

Oil entered a country with a very particular political economy. State revenue depended on customs duties on imports and on taxes related to a few exports, and a small number of landowning, commercial, and military families dominated politics. Foreign capital had already entered through asphalt, railways, and other concessions. When international companies, anticipating the demand created by the automobile and by navies converting from coal, began exploring Venezuela in the first decade of the twentieth century, they dealt with a government that had little administrative capacity but unquestioned authority to grant concessions. This is the context for the formation of the petroleum state.

Modern scholarship has shifted away from two older narratives. One portrays Venezuelan oil history as a simple story of foreign exploitation followed by national redemption; the other treats oil abundance as a natural cause of distorted development. Historians such as Fernando Coronil, Terry Lynn Karl, Miguel Tinker Salas, and Bernard Mommer have instead emphasized the interaction among international markets, company strategies, Venezuelan state-building, and political choices. This chapter follows that approach: oil mattered enormously, but through institutions and decisions that Venezuelans and foreigners made and that could have been made differently.

Section 105.1

Venezuela before Oil: Agriculture, Landownership, and the First Commercial Wells

In 1900 Venezuela exported coffee, cacao, hides, and a few other agricultural goods, and its fiscal health rose and fell with their prices. Coffee, grown in the Andes and the central mountains on estates and smallholdings, accounted for the largest share of export earnings. Cattle ranching dominated the vast plains of the Llanos, and cacao was a specialty of the coastal valleys, including the Afro-Venezuelan communities of Barlovento, where the descendants of enslaved plantation workers remained numerous. Landownership was concentrated, rural labor was often bound by debt and dependence, and most Venezuelans were poor, illiterate, and exposed to endemic disease, especially malaria. Regional inequalities were stark: the Andes, the coast, the Llanos, Guayana, and the Maracaibo region were linked by few roads, and travel between them was slow.

Petroleum was known long before it was exploited commercially. Indigenous peoples of the Maracaibo region had long used surface seeps of tar and oil, and Spanish colonists noted them. In 1878 a Venezuelan company, the Petrolia del Táchira, began small-scale production in the Andes, and asphalt from Lake Guanoco in eastern Venezuela was exported by foreign concessionaires in the late nineteenth century. These were minor activities. The decisive development came in the first decade of the twentieth century, when American and British geologists, working for companies seeking new sources of supply for a world increasingly dependent on liquid fuel, surveyed the Maracaibo basin and the eastern plains and concluded that the geology was promising.

The geography of Lake Maracaibo shaped everything that followed. The lake is a large, shallow body of water in the northwest, connected to the Gulf of Venezuela by a narrow channel, and its basin is bordered by swampy lowlands and by the Perijá and Andean mountains. Oil lay under the eastern shore and later under the lake itself, and the heat, insects, and tropical diseases of the region made it a difficult place to work. Control of the lake's outlet and its sandbars affected the size of tankers that could enter, and as a result a great deal of Venezuelan crude was refined not in Venezuela but in the Dutch islands of Aruba and Curaçao, close to the coast. The geography thus tied the Venezuelan industry to a Caribbean and Atlantic network of refineries, tankers, and markets.

Commercial production began in 1914 with the Zumaque No. 1 well at Mene Grande, in the state of Zulia, drilled by a subsidiary of Royal Dutch Shell. Exports followed in 1917. The first years were modest, and the outbreak of the First World War initially interrupted activity, but the war also demonstrated the strategic value of oil for navies, armies, and industry. Over the following decade, petroleum moved from marginal to central in the Venezuelan economy. By the mid-1920s it had overtaken coffee as the country's leading export, and by the 1930s it dominated. Students should note how rapid this change was: within roughly a generation, an agrarian export economy became a single-commodity export economy based on a resource that Venezuelans themselves did not yet have the capital or technology to extract.

The legal framework that governed this transformation was therefore crucial. Because the subsoil belonged to the state, the government decided who could explore and on what terms, and it did so by granting concessions to companies. That arrangement gave Venezuelan governments a legal claim to a share of the proceeds that landowners in some other oil-producing countries did not enjoy. It also meant that control over concessions became a source of enormous political power and personal enrichment, as the next section shows.

Section 105.2

Juan Vicente Gómez, Foreign Oil Concessions, and Authoritarian Modernization

Juan Vicente Gómez, an Andean landowner and general from Táchira, seized power in December 1908 while his superior, President Cipriano Castro, was abroad for medical treatment. He ruled until his death in December 1935, sometimes directly and sometimes through presidents he controlled. His regime accomplished what no Venezuelan government of the nineteenth century had managed: it built a national army with modern weapons and communications, defeated or co-opted the regional caudillos, and established a durable central authority. It did so through a combination of patronage, surveillance, and repression. Opponents were imprisoned in fortresses such as La Rotunda in Caracas or driven into exile, and the press was controlled.

Oil revenue and Gómez's centralization reinforced one another. The regime's early finances depended on customs duties, but as oil production grew, the government collected taxes, royalties, and fees from the companies and gained the means to pay soldiers, officials, and contractors without depending on regional notables. Gómez retired the country's foreign debt by 1930, a notable achievement for a state that had been blockaded over debts only a generation earlier. He built roads, including links between the Andes and the capital, and the army could now move quickly across the territory. Oil income did not create the Gómez dictatorship, but it made the dictatorship more secure and more capable than any predecessor.

The concession system was shaped by the interests of the dictator and his circle. Gómez granted large concessions to relatives, officials, and friends, who often sold their rights to foreign companies, collecting substantial profits as intermediaries. Royal Dutch Shell, affiliates of Standard Oil of New Jersey (notably Creole), and Gulf, among other firms, acquired the rights that became the basis of the industry. The sequence of petroleum laws between 1920 and 1922 shows the pressures involved: the Minister of Development, Gumersindo Torres, drafted a law in 1920 intended to give the state a stronger position, but the companies objected, their governments' diplomats supported them, and revisions in the following two years produced terms more favorable to the investors. The episode shows both the limits of a weak state facing wealthy corporations and the presence of Venezuelan officials who tried to defend the national interest.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession and royalty

A concession was a grant by the Venezuelan state allowing a company to explore for and produce petroleum in a defined area for a defined period. The company paid the state various charges, including a royalty, a share of production or its value owed to the owner of the subsoil, along with taxes and fees.

The concession system placed ownership of the oil in the state but operations in private hands. It was the basis of Venezuelan oil policy until nationalization. The terms of concessions, how long they lasted, how royalties were calculated, and what taxes applied, were the main arena of petroleum politics for half a century.

The Gómez era also produced the generation that later built Venezuelan democracy. In February 1928 university students in Caracas protested the dictatorship during Carnival week, and a short-lived military revolt followed in April. The Generation of 1928, which included Rómulo Betancourt, Jóvito Villalba, and Raúl Leoni, was crushed, and many of its members spent years in exile, where they read widely, debated socialism and nationalism, and thought about how to reshape Venezuela. When Gómez died in 1935 they returned, and within a decade they dominated national politics. The petroleum state of the 1920s and 1930s therefore left a double legacy: a strong executive financed by foreign companies, and a cohort of politicians determined to turn that financing to democratic and national ends.

Primary Source Window

The Hydrocarbons Law of 1922 and the Concession System

Creators: The Venezuelan executive and Congress under Gómez · Dates: Laws of 1920, revised in 1921 and 1922 · Type: Legal text and administrative regulation

Context: Venezuela's early petroleum laws governed how concessions were granted, how large they could be, and what taxes and royalties the companies owed. They were issued under an authoritarian regime in which Gómez retained final authority and in which foreign companies lobbied intensively.

Paraphrase of the law's character: The 1922 text reaffirms that hydrocarbons belong to the state and may be exploited only under concessions, sets out procedures for exploration and exploitation permits, and fixes the charges due from concessionaires, on terms more favorable to the companies than the 1920 draft had proposed.

Analysis: The law shows that the principle of state ownership was settled early, but that the practical terms were the product of negotiation under unequal conditions. It cannot reveal the private arrangements through which concessions were distributed to insiders, which must be reconstructed from other records. Students should read the law as the outcome of political pressure, not as an abstract statement of national policy.

Section 105.3

The Petroleum Boom of 1922: Workers, Migration, and Urban Transformation

On 14 December 1922 the Los Barrosos No. 2 well, drilled by a Shell subsidiary in the La Rosa field near Cabimas on the eastern shore of Lake Maracaibo, blew out of control. For roughly nine days it spewed enormous quantities of oil, estimated at around one hundred thousand barrels per day, before it was brought under control. The blowout was a disaster for the crews and the surrounding land, and it was also an advertisement. It showed observers around the world that the Maracaibo basin could yield spectacular volumes, and investment poured in. Production, which had been only a few million barrels in the early 1920s, rose past one hundred million barrels per year by the end of the decade, and by 1928 Venezuela was the world's second-largest producer after the United States and its largest exporter.

Oil changed where and how Venezuelans lived. Towns such as Cabimas, Lagunillas, and Maracaibo itself expanded rapidly, and new settlements appeared around the fields. Work in the industry was divided by skill, nationality, and race. Skilled and managerial positions went disproportionately to Americans, British, and Dutch employees, many of whom lived in company camps with housing, schools, clubs, and medical services that the surrounding Venezuelan communities lacked. Skilled workers also came from the English-speaking Caribbean, especially Trinidad, bringing experience from older oil fields. Venezuelan workers, many of them migrants from rural areas of Zulia, the Andes, and the Llanos, filled less skilled jobs and lived in more crowded housing, often outside the camps. The segregation of space and privilege created resentments that later fed labor and nationalist politics.

The oil frontier also affected Indigenous peoples. In the Perijá mountains and the Catatumbo region the Barí, known to outsiders then as the Motilones, resisted the incursion of exploration crews into their territory, and violence between the Barí and company employees, sometimes with the support of Venezuelan authorities and missionaries, continued for decades. The Wayuu of the Guajira, the Añú who lived on the lake itself, and other peoples saw their lands and waters transformed by drilling, roads, and migration. The sources documenting these encounters are largely company and missionary records, which give the perspective of outsiders; Indigenous testimony is much scarcer.

The unequal effects of the boom were visible in the economy as a whole. Oil revenue and spending pushed up the value of the bolívar, making imports cheap and domestic agriculture less competitive. Rural people left the land for the oil towns and for Caracas, and coffee and cacao, once the pillars of the economy, declined relative to oil. Venezuela became increasingly dependent on imported food and manufactured goods, paid for with oil dollars. The pattern, later given the name "Dutch disease" by economists, was visible by the 1930s, though contemporaries did not use the term.

Workers began to organize. After Gómez's death in 1935, oil workers formed unions and in December 1936 launched a major strike that lasted several weeks, demanding better wages, housing, and recognition. The government of Eleazar López Contreras, Gómez's successor, ended the strike, but the episode introduced organized labor as a political force in the oil regions. A labor law in 1936 gave legal structure to labor relations. The oil camps and towns, with their combination of foreign managers, Caribbean and Venezuelan workers, and company-controlled services, were laboratories in which Venezuelans learned that the foreign-owned industry that financed their state also depended on their own labor.

Section 105.4

Petroleum Nationalism, the 1943 Hydrocarbons Law, and the 1948 Fifty–Fifty Arrangement

After Gómez's death, Venezuelan politicians and intellectuals debated what the country should do with its new wealth. In 1936 the writer and politician Arturo Uslar Pietri published an influential newspaper essay, whose title became a slogan: "sembrar el petróleo," to sow the oil. His argument was that Venezuela should use oil income to build a productive economy, in agriculture, education, and infrastructure, so that the country would not be impoverished when the oil ran out or lost value. The phrase became a durable theme, invoked by governments of very different orientations, and it also became a measure of failure, since critics later argued that Venezuela had spent rather than sown.

Petroleum nationalism also drew energy from outside. In March 1938 the Mexican government of Lázaro Cárdenas expropriated the foreign oil companies operating in Mexico (Chapter 74), and Venezuelans watched the aftermath closely. Mexico's experience encouraged those who wanted a larger share of oil income, but it also suggested the risks of confrontation: Mexico faced a company boycott and diplomatic pressure. Venezuelan leaders in the late 1930s and early 1940s chose a more gradual path. The government of Eleazar López Contreras (1936–1941) sought to increase the state's take, and under his successor, General Isaías Medina Angarita, the government negotiated a comprehensive revision.

The result was the Hydrocarbons Law of 1943, preceded by an income-tax reform in 1942. The law consolidated existing concession arrangements, set the royalty paid by the companies at a uniform rate, and imposed requirements related to refining and operations within the country. In exchange, the companies received a clear legal basis and extension of their concession terms, which they valued highly. Medina's government presented the law as a milestone that increased state revenue and established the principle that the nation should obtain a fair share. The opposition, in particular the young Acción Democrática, criticized the law for extending concessions and leaving the companies in control, though many observers recognized that the revenue increase was significant. Students should see the 1943 law as a bargain, increasing national revenue while preserving the concession system, and not as nationalization.

The change that followed the October 1945 revolution built on the 1943 law. The new government imposed an extraordinary tax on company profits in 1945 and, under the Minister of Development, Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, pursued a policy of granting no new concessions. The distinction from 1943 is important. The 1943 law consolidated the concession regime and set the royalty, while the 1948 change altered the tax structure so that the state received a share of profit comparable to the companies'. It was also a step beyond what most oil-producing countries then obtained. The Venezuelan model influenced the later arrangements in the Middle East, notably the Saudi Arabian agreement with Aramco in 1950.

The debate about what to do with the money continued. Pérez Alfonzo and Betancourt, among others, argued that oil income should be invested in education, health, agriculture, and industry. Their position was an early statement of what became the central premise of the Venezuelan developmental state: the government would capture a larger share of oil revenue and spend it on modernization. Whether that premise could work, and what it would do to the rest of the economy and politics, is the subject of the remaining sections.

The chronology of the 1948 reform is especially revealing. Legislative approval on 11 November, followed by the law dated 12 November, occurred less than two weeks before the overthrow of President Rómulo Gallegos on 24 November. The fiscal formula thus belonged to the democratic Trienio, but it outlived that government. Its durability shows that demands for a greater national share of petroleum profits were not confined to one party or political regime. The 1943 law had reconciled company contracts with improved state claims; the 1948 income-tax instrument operated on profits and was meant to correct a different imbalance. Division II — Democracy, Dictatorship, and the Punto Fijo Settlement, 1945–1963Sections 105.5–105.8
Section 105.5

The October Revolution of 1945, Acción Democrática, and the Trienio

By 1945 Venezuela had a restricted political system. Gómez's successors, López Contreras and Medina Angarita, had permitted a limited opening, and political parties, including the Communist Party and the social-democratic Acción Democrática (AD), operated with varying degrees of tolerance. But the presidency was chosen indirectly by a Congress controlled by the government, and most Venezuelans, including all women and illiterate adults, could not vote for national officials. In 1945 Medina's attempt to arrange the succession broke down. His first choice for a successor, Diógenes Escalante, became incapacitated by illness, and the government's substitute candidate was unacceptable to AD and to a group of young army officers.

On 18 October 1945, a civil-military coup overthrew Medina. The plotters were an alliance of AD leaders, led by Rómulo Betancourt, and officers belonging to a secret military organization, including Marcos Pérez Jiménez and Carlos Delgado Chalbaud. It is accurate to call this a coup, since officers used force to remove a constitutional government, and it is also accurate to call it a revolution in its consequences, since it led to a fundamental change in who could take part in politics. The Revolutionary Government Junta, presided over by Betancourt, governed for the next three years, a period called the Trienio.

The Junta and its successor expanded political participation on a scale unprecedented in Venezuelan history. A new electoral law gave the vote to all adults over eighteen, including women and illiterates, and direct elections were held in 1946 for a constituent assembly and in December 1947 for the presidency. The 1947 Constitution established direct, universal, and secret suffrage. In the presidential election, the novelist Rómulo Gallegos, AD's candidate, won by a very large margin. The Trienio also expanded education, supported union organization, launched an agrarian reform program, and pursued the increased share of oil revenue described above. Women entered politics as voters, candidates, and legislators, building on organizations that had campaigned for suffrage since the late 1930s.

The Trienio was also a period of intense conflict. AD dominated electoral politics and was accused by opponents of sectarianism and of using the state to advance the party. COPEI, the Christian-democratic party founded in 1946 by Rafael Caldera and others, and the Catholic Church opposed AD's education policies, and landowners, businesses, and elements of the military viewed the reforms with alarm. Some officers, who had helped bring AD to power, resented being excluded from decisions. On 24 November 1948 the military overthrew Gallegos. The new junta, led by Delgado Chalbaud with Pérez Jiménez and Luis Felipe Llovera Páez, banned AD and the labor confederation associated with it. The coup showed how fragile civilian government was and how the military could act when the elite felt threatened. The memory of the Trienio, and of its sectarianism and its overthrow, deeply affected the generation of leaders who rebuilt democracy in 1958.

Section 105.6

Marcos Pérez Jiménez: Dictatorship, Petroleum Wealth, and State-Led Development

The military government that took power in 1948 was at first a collective junta. In November 1950 Delgado Chalbaud was kidnapped and killed, and Pérez Jiménez, the most powerful figure in the army, gradually dominated the regime. In November 1952 the junta held elections for a constituent assembly, expecting the government-aligned party to win. The opposition party Unión Republicana Democrática (URD), led by Jóvito Villalba, instead appeared to be winning, and the government stopped the count and installed Pérez Jiménez as provisional president. A constituent assembly in 1953 wrote a new constitution and confirmed him as president. The regime was thus a military dictatorship installed after the suppression of an electoral result, and it ruled until January 1958.

The regime's ideology, the "New National Ideal," emphasized order, material progress, and the transformation of the physical environment. Oil revenue, which grew with increased production, financed an ambitious program of public works. The government built highways, including the road from Caracas to the coast, large public housing blocks in Caracas, government and military buildings, hotels, and the new campus of the Central University of Venezuela designed by Carlos Raúl Villanueva. Steel and hydroelectric projects in the Guayana region were planned, and the regime encouraged immigration from Spain, Italy, and Portugal, which added several hundred thousand people to the population and workforce. The Caracas that many Venezuelans remember from this period, with its new highways and towers, was largely the work of these years.

The costs were substantial. The regime relied on the political police, the Seguridad Nacional, led by Pedro Estrada, which monitored, arrested, and tortured opponents. Political parties were banned or restricted, unions were controlled, newspapers were censored, and prisoners were held in places such as the Guasina camp in the Orinoco delta. AD and the Communist Party operated clandestinely at great risk, and many leaders went into exile. Corruption was widespread, and contractors close to the regime profited. In 1956–1957 the government granted new oil concessions, reversing the policy of no new concessions, and collected very large payments, which financed more spending and produced a short-term fiscal windfall. Critics, including the Catholic hierarchy, which in 1957 issued a pastoral letter pointing to the poverty of many workers despite the regime's wealth, argued that oil money was building monuments while ordinary people lacked adequate housing, wages, and health care.

The Pérez Jiménez era shows that oil revenue could support very different political regimes. The same resource financed the reformist Trienio and the military dictatorship, and its distribution depended on who controlled the state. It also left a lasting lesson for the opposition: authoritarian government might deliver roads and buildings, but it could not secure legitimacy. In December 1957 the regime held a plebiscite on the continuation of Pérez Jiménez's rule, with a single question and no opposition campaign. The announced result was overwhelmingly favorable, and almost no one inside or outside Venezuela believed it.

Section 105.7

The Overthrow of Pérez Jiménez and the Pact of Punto Fijo, 1958

The regime collapsed within weeks of the plebiscite. Underground opposition had been building for years. In 1957 a clandestine Patriotic Junta, bringing together representatives of AD, COPEI, URD, and the Communist Party, began coordinating resistance, and its members included the journalist Fabricio Ojeda. Business groups, the Church, and many officers had grown disillusioned. On 1 January 1958 a rebellion by air force units failed, but it revealed the regime's weakness. In the following weeks, strikes and demonstrations spread, and on 21 January a general strike paralyzed Caracas. On 23 January 1958 Pérez Jiménez flew out of the country, and a junta of military officers, soon joined by civilians and headed by Rear Admiral Wolfgang Larrazábal, took over. The date, 23 January, became a symbol of democratic restoration, and a Caracas housing development built under the dictatorship was renamed for it.

The central question for the transitional period was how to prevent a repetition of 1948. The leaders of the three principal parties had mostly spent the dictatorship in exile or underground, and their experience had convinced them that interparty conflict had helped doom the Trienio. Betancourt, Caldera, and Villalba, with their parties, negotiated a series of agreements. Early in 1958 business and labor leaders agreed on a framework for industrial relations. Then, on 31 October 1958, Betancourt for AD, Caldera for COPEI, and Villalba for URD signed the Pact of Punto Fijo, named for the house in Caracas where the discussions took place. The signatories agreed to respect the results of the coming election, to defend the constitutional order against military or other threats, and to share governmental positions through a coalition regardless of who won. They also agreed on a common minimum program of government.

Historical Vocabulary
Puntofijismo

Puntofijismo is the name given, mostly in retrospect and often critically, to the political system that developed after the 1958 pact: a competitive party democracy dominated by AD and COPEI, in which major decisions were negotiated among party leaders and the state distributed oil income through party and union networks.

The term is analytical and contested. Supporters see the system as the achievement that preserved civilian government, while critics call it partidocracia, rule by party machines. Students should note that the pact itself was a short document about electoral conduct and coalition, and that the system's later features developed over three decades.

The Pact of Punto Fijo, like many elite settlements, included some and excluded others. It bound the three largest non-communist parties, and the exclusion of the Communist Party, which had played a significant role in the resistance, was intentional. Whether the exclusion was demanded by the other parties, by business interests, by concern about the Cold War and the United States, or by a combination, is debated. The pact also relied on the military's acquiescence and on businesses and the Church; it did not include the broader population in its negotiation. In the election of December 1958, held under the transitional junta, Betancourt won the presidency with about half the vote, ahead of Larrazábal, who was supported by URD and the Communists, and Caldera.

Displacement of the dictatorship did not immediately settle who possessed legitimate authority. The provisional government was a military-civilian coalition; soldiers retained leverage, and organized parties had to translate an emergency alliance into rules for ordinary competition. The January victory therefore presented two simultaneous tasks. One was to ensure that the armed forces would accept civilian electoral outcomes. The other was to persuade parties with incompatible social constituencies to protect procedures they might later lose under. Party leaders drew on the memory of 1948, when intense polarization and military intervention had destroyed an experiment in representative government. They also faced a country transformed since the last competitive election: oil revenues had expanded cities, wage employment, and expectations of public services, giving the parties a wider electorate and a larger set of claims to negotiate.

The Punto Fijo agreement was not simply an allocation of ministries. Its provisions linked recognition of election results, defense of constitutional authority against armed interruption, and participation in a national-unity cabinet. A minimum common program supplied an additional basis for cooperation. This architecture made political defeat less threatening to established parties, because an opposition organization could remain inside the constitutional system and reasonably expect another election. Yet a pact negotiated by leaders was not a substitute for social inclusion. The Communist Party's exclusion, despite its contribution to the anti-dictatorship struggle, made the new settlement vulnerable to a critique of restricted pluralism. Nor were impoverished neighborhoods, Indigenous communities, or rural laborers represented directly at the negotiating table. The pact created opportunities for stability while setting boundaries around who could shape it.

Later descriptions sometimes collapse the 1958 settlement, the two-party dominance of subsequent decades, and the clientelist practices associated with puntofijismo into one uninterrupted phenomenon. They should be separated analytically. URD was a signatory but eventually withdrew from the governing coalition; AD and COPEI developed into the principal electoral competitors. The durability of elections after 1958 depended on institutions and political choices, including successive transfers of authority, not on permanent adherence to every original coalition arrangement. Understanding that distinction prepares students to assess both the democratic accomplishment and the eventual legitimacy crisis without assuming either was predetermined.

Section 105.8

Rómulo Betancourt, the 1961 Constitution, and the Defense of Democracy

Betancourt took office in February 1959 with a coalition government and an agenda that included moderate reform and, above all, survival. He faced threats from several directions. Conservative military officers conspired against the government, and the dictator of the Dominican Republic, Rafael Trujillo, supported plots against Betancourt, whose government had condemned him; in June 1960 a car bomb in Caracas, attributed to Trujillo's agents, injured Betancourt. The Betancourt Doctrine, which refused diplomatic recognition to governments that came to power by coup, expressed his determination to isolate dictatorships in the hemisphere. The government also passed an agrarian reform law in 1960, aiming to redistribute land to peasants, and it expanded education and public works with oil income.

The more difficult challenge came from the left. The Cuban Revolution of 1959 inspired many young Venezuelans, and in 1960 AD's youth wing split away, forming the Movement of the Revolutionary Left (MIR). The Communist Party and MIR concluded that armed struggle could overthrow what they regarded as a bourgeois government, and in 1962–1963 the Armed Forces of National Liberation (FALN) began guerrilla and urban attacks. In 1962 military uprisings at Carúpano and Puerto Cabello, in which leftist officers cooperated with civilians, were suppressed at heavy cost. The government banned the Communist Party and MIR, imprisoned deputies, and used the army against rural guerrillas. In 1963 authorities found a large arms cache on the Paraguaná coast that they linked to Cuba. The insurgency never threatened to overthrow the government, but it intensified the polarization and encouraged the army to take part in counterinsurgency, a pattern that would recur throughout Latin America (Chapter 82).

Betancourt's government also created the constitutional framework of the democratic period. The Constitution of 1961, promulgated on 23 January, the anniversary of the dictator's fall, established a presidential system with a bicameral Congress, a five-year term, direct election, constitutional guarantees that the president could suspend under defined conditions, and a prominent role for the state in the economy and in social welfare. It gave the executive considerable power and prohibited immediate presidential reelection. The suspension of guarantees, a tool used during the insurgency, would reappear in 1989.

The government's survival was itself a historical achievement. In December 1963, despite guerrilla threats to disrupt the vote, turnout was very high, and Raúl Leoni of AD won the presidency. In March 1964, Betancourt handed the presidency to Leoni, the first transfer from one elected civilian president to another in Venezuelan history. For the signatories of Punto Fijo, this was the proof that the pact worked. For the excluded left, it confirmed the concern that the system rested on restriction. Both conclusions have evidence behind them, and the tension between them runs through the next sections.

Betancourt remains a figure of intense and divided assessment. Admirers see a democratic statesman who defended civilian rule against threats from both the right and the left. Critics emphasize the repression of the left, the use of emergency powers, and the construction of an AD-dominated party and union apparatus that would later become a vehicle for patronage. A balanced reading recognizes that he faced real threats and that the methods used to counter them shaped the system's character.

Division III — Petroleum Democracy, OPEC, and the Developmental State, 1960–1975Sections 105.9–105.12
Section 105.9

Acción Democrática, COPEI, and the Institutionalization of Party Democracy

Between the early 1960s and the late 1980s, Venezuelan politics was dominated by two parties. Acción Democrática, with its base among workers, peasants, teachers, and the urban poor, and COPEI, with its base among Catholics, middle-class professionals, and parts of the Andes, alternated in the presidency, and between them they took the great majority of votes. Smaller parties, including URD, the Movement toward Socialism (MAS), which split from the Communist Party in 1971, and various regional and personalist groups, existed but rarely competed for national power. In the 1973 election, the two main parties together won roughly eighty percent or more of the vote, and turnout was extremely high. By the standards of Latin America in the 1970s, a decade in which military governments took power in Chile, Uruguay, and Argentina, this was a remarkably stable democracy.

The institutions that sustained it were partly formal and partly informal. Formally, there were competitive elections, a Congress, an independent press, and a constitution. Informally, party organizations penetrated the labor movement, professional associations, universities, and the state bureaucracy. The Confederation of Venezuelan Workers (CTV) was dominated by AD and COPEI, and public employment and contracts were often distributed through party channels. Business interests, represented by the national federation Fedecámaras, negotiated directly with governments over tariffs, credit, and exchange rates. Political scientists have called this arrangement "partyarchy" or partidocracia, and the scholar Michael Coppedge emphasized how party leaders controlled nominations and the careers of legislators. The system produced consensus on major issues and also made it difficult for outsiders to participate.

The mechanism that tied the system together was the distribution of oil income. Because the state collected a large share of oil revenue, governments could fund programs for many constituencies at once, including subsidized credit for businesses, public jobs, price controls on staples, subsidized gasoline, and expanded education. Competition between AD and COPEI therefore often took the form of competing promises to distribute more effectively rather than conflicts over taxation or sacrifice. This is one of the central insights of research on petroleum democracies: when the state does not need to tax its citizens heavily, the bargain between rulers and ruled can rest on distribution rather than on representation in exchange for taxes.

Elections tested the system. In 1968, after AD split, Rafael Caldera won the presidency by a margin of less than one percentage point, and AD's candidate conceded. Caldera took office in March 1969, the first time an opposition candidate won the presidency and took power peacefully in Venezuelan history. Caldera also pursued a policy of "pacification," legalizing the Communist Party and negotiating the reintegration of guerrillas, and by the early 1970s the insurgency had largely ended. Students should recognize the significance of these transfers. They did not mean that every grievance was addressed, but they demonstrated that the losers of an election would accept the result, and this was not a common feature of the region's politics.

Venezuela's stable democracy also had an international dimension. In the 1970s the country received political exiles from the military regimes of the Southern Cone, and its governments supported democratic movements elsewhere in the hemisphere. The prestige of the democratic experiment, funded by oil, gave Venezuela an influence in international affairs out of proportion to its population, a point that Carlos Andrés Pérez would exploit in the mid-1970s.

Section 105.10

Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, the Founding of OPEC, and Petroleum Sovereignty

Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, a lawyer who had worked on oil policy since the 1940s, served as Betancourt's Minister of Mines and Hydrocarbons from 1959 to 1963. He believed that the international oil companies controlled the market and that producing countries could gain only by acting together. In the late 1950s the major companies lowered posted prices, the reference prices on which taxes and royalties were calculated, without consulting the governments whose revenues depended on them. A price cut in 1959 and another in August 1960 alarmed producers, since each reduction directly reduced their income.

Pérez Alfonzo built contacts with officials from the Middle East. He attended the Arab Oil Congress in Cairo in 1959 as an observer and developed a relationship with Abdullah Tariki of Saudi Arabia, and the two explored ideas for cooperation, including the regulation of production to support prices, a model Pérez Alfonzo drew in part from the way Texas regulated its own output. The Venezuelan government, meanwhile, created the Venezuelan Petroleum Corporation (CVP) in 1960 as a state company, and while it was small, it gave the state a presence in the industry. In September 1960, delegations from Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela met in Baghdad and agreed to form the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries. They demanded that the companies maintain prices and consult producers before changing them, and they resolved to create a permanent organization.

Pérez Alfonzo's contribution was intellectual as well as diplomatic. He argued that oil revenue was a depleting asset that should be used with discipline, and he pressed for conservation, production controls, and a larger state role. He thus embodied two sides of the Venezuelan experience: a founder of the institution that would claim greater producer power, and an early critic of the dependence that power could entrench.

The international environment was also shaped by the policies of the United States. In 1959 the Eisenhower administration imposed mandatory limits on oil imports, in part to protect domestic producers, and while the rules gave Western Hemisphere suppliers such as Venezuela a favorable position relative to the Middle East, they also showed how decisions in Washington affected Venezuelan interests. Venezuelan governments negotiated with the United States as a sovereign producer and not simply as a dependent supplier, but the market, controlled by a small number of multinational firms, remained difficult to influence. Real change came later. In December 1970, under Caldera, an amendment to the income tax law authorized the executive to set reference prices for tax purposes, a step toward unilateral control. In 1971, Venezuela adopted a law governing the reversion of concession assets to the state when the concessions expired and reserved the natural gas industry for the state. These measures prepared the ground for nationalization.

Section 105.11

Oil Revenue, Education, Urbanization, and the Unequal Distribution of Prosperity

The postwar decades brought changes in living conditions that were among the fastest in Venezuelan history. Campaigns against malaria, led by the physician Arnoldo Gabaldón beginning in the 1930s, and expanded health services and sanitation reduced mortality and raised life expectancy. The population grew from about five million in 1950 to more than fifteen million by 1980. Education expanded at every level: primary enrollment rose, secondary schools and technical institutes multiplied, and universities grew rapidly, with the Central University of Venezuela becoming a major center of intellectual and political life. In 1974 the government created a scholarship program that sent thousands of Venezuelans abroad for graduate study. Highways, electricity, telephones, and piped water reached more of the country, and a sizable middle class of professionals, public employees, and business owners appeared.

Venezuela also became an overwhelmingly urban society. About half the population lived in cities in 1950, and by the 1980s more than eighty percent did. Caracas, Maracaibo, Valencia, Maracay, and Barquisimeto grew rapidly, as did the new industrial city of Ciudad Guayana. Many migrants from the countryside and from Colombia, whose citizens came in large numbers, settled in barrios, informal neighborhoods built on hillsides and the peripheries of cities, often by the residents themselves. Over time, the state extended services to the barrios, but their residents, frequently working in the informal economy or in low-paid jobs, remained poorer and more exposed to economic shocks than the middle class. Afro-Venezuelans and people of Indigenous descent were disproportionately represented among the poor, though Venezuelan national ideology, which celebrated racial mixture, often obscured racial inequality.

Historical Vocabulary
Rentier state

A rentier state is a government that obtains a large share of its revenue from external rents, such as payments from the sale of a natural resource, rather than from taxing the productive activity of its own citizens. The concept, developed by the economist Hossein Mahdavy in analyzing oil states of the Middle East, has been applied to Venezuela by scholars including Terry Lynn Karl and Fernando Coronil.

The idea suggests that such states can fund public spending without negotiating taxes with their citizens, may favor distribution over production, and can become dependent on commodity prices. It is a useful analytical model, not a law of nature, and scholars debate how far it explains Venezuelan outcomes compared with decisions by particular governments.

Oil revenue gave Venezuelan governments a distinctive relationship with their economy. The state was the owner of the resource and the main recipient of its income, so it was also the principal source of investment, credit, and employment. The bolívar was kept strong, and the government maintained a fixed exchange rate of about 4.30 per dollar from the early 1960s until 1983, which made imports cheap and benefited consumers and importers, but reduced the competitiveness of agriculture and manufacturing. Gasoline was subsidized so heavily that Venezuelans paid some of the lowest prices in the world. Governments protected industries with tariffs and subsidies, creating a manufacturing sector that served the domestic market but struggled to export. As a result, the incentive for many businesses and individuals was to seek access to state resources, through contracts, credit, or licenses, rather than to compete in world markets.

This was the pattern that Pérez Alfonzo and others criticized, and it is what the rentier state concept tries to capture. Revenue strengthened government, but it also lowered the pressure to develop other productive sectors, since the state could meet many demands from oil income. At the same time, it would be a mistake to see Venezuelans as passive recipients. Workers organized for higher wages, businesses lobbied, and politicians competed for votes, and these actors shaped how the revenue was spent. The unequal outcomes, in which a prosperous middle class coexisted with large barrios, reflected political choices as well as economic structure. A popular phrase of the 1970s, "ta' barato, dame dos," attributed to Venezuelan shoppers abroad ("it's cheap, give me two"), captured the consumer boom and its irony, though students should treat it as popular lore, not a statistical source.

Section 105.12

The 1973 Oil Shock, Carlos Andrés Pérez, and the Gran Venezuela Project

In October 1973, during the Arab-Israeli war, Arab members of OPEC imposed an embargo on shipments to the United States and some other countries and the organization raised prices sharply. Within months the price of crude oil roughly quadrupled, from around three dollars per barrel to around eleven or twelve. Venezuela, as a member of OPEC and a major exporter, was a large beneficiary. Government oil revenue, which had been growing, rose dramatically, giving the incoming government an unprecedented sum to spend. The oil shock was a global event with consequences across the world economy, and for Venezuela it came just as a new president took office.

Carlos Andrés Pérez, a former Interior Minister under Betancourt known for his energy and personal campaign style, won the December 1973 election for AD with nearly half the votes and took office in March 1974. He promised a "Gran Venezuela," a country that would use its resources to become a modern industrial nation. His government created the Venezuelan Investment Fund to manage part of the windfall, obtained special economic powers from Congress, and launched ambitious projects. Steel, aluminum, and hydroelectric development in Guayana expanded, with the Guri dam on the Caroní River becoming a major source of power. The government invested in petrochemicals, built the Caracas Metro, expanded the scholarship program, and increased public employment and wages.

The project had real accomplishments and real risks. Many of the investments produced lasting infrastructure and industrial capacity. Imports surged, inflation rose, and projects frequently cost more and took longer than planned. The Venezuelan government also borrowed abroad, in part because it believed that its oil revenues guaranteed repayment.

Pérez also pursued an active foreign policy, presenting Venezuela as a leader of developing countries. He supported the founding of the Latin American Economic System (SELA) in 1975, took part in North-South discussions, and aided governments and movements in Central America and the Caribbean that he judged democratic or anti-dictatorial. These actions reflected the confidence that oil wealth and stable democracy gave Venezuelan leaders in the mid-1970s. They also fed the belief, among many Venezuelans, that the country was destined for prosperity.

The international price shock did not operate through a single policy decision. Arab petroleum exporters used embargoes against selected countries while posted prices and market expectations changed across the broader oil economy. Venezuela benefited from the resulting price increase, but was not a participant in the Arab embargo. The distinction matters because it preserves agency: Venezuelan leaders had helped build OPEC to strengthen producer-country bargaining, yet they faced a market transformation in which Middle Eastern events were decisive. Price receipts appeared in state accounts faster than the capacity to evaluate, contract, and supervise major projects could develop. That mismatch made competent administration a central political issue rather than a technical afterthought.

The Investment Fund and the heavy-industrial projects of Guayana represented competing visions of how to make oil income productive. One vision emphasized accumulating financial assets for future generations; another prioritized immediate industrialization, transport systems, electricity, and skilled employment. Neither was inherently irrational. But different projects had different time horizons and foreign-exchange requirements, and many depended on continued public support long before they could earn independent revenue. The government was simultaneously financing ambitious construction, raising wages, acquiring assets, and extending the state's reach into sectors that had previously depended on private firms. Its international borrowing reflected not only confidence in petroleum but also a decision to accelerate the pace of development.

Section 105.13

Petroleum Nationalization: The 1975 Law and January 1, 1976 Transfer

By the early 1970s a consensus had formed that the concessions, due to expire in the early 1980s, would not be renewed in their existing form. The 1971 reversion law had fixed the principle that the concessionaires' assets would pass to the state at expiration, and the new revenue gave the government the means to contemplate an earlier takeover. In 1974 Pérez appointed a presidential commission to study the question, and it consulted political parties, business, labor, the military, and universities. Several outcomes were possible: waiting for the concessions to end, negotiating a partial or phased transfer, or nationalizing immediately. The question of timing was not trivial. Advocates of immediate action argued that Venezuela needed to take control while prices were high and the politics were favorable; others warned of the loss of technical expertise and of access to markets.

Congress approved the Organic Law that Reserves to the State the Industry and Commerce of Hydrocarbons, which Pérez signed on 29 August 1975, and nationalization took effect on 1 January 1976. The companies were compensated, in an amount of around one billion dollars based on the net book value of their assets, in cash and bonds. Venezuela did not simply seize the industry; it paid, and it also entered into technical-assistance and marketing arrangements with the former concessionaires, which gave the companies fees and long-term supply relations. The law reserved to the state the exploration, production, refining, transport, and commerce of hydrocarbons, and it provided in its Article 5 that the state companies could, in special cases and with the approval of Congress, enter into association agreements with private companies. That article, little noticed at the time, later became the basis for the partial opening of the industry to foreign participation in the 1990s.

Interpretations of nationalization differ. Supporters, including Pérez and most of the political establishment, presented it as the culmination of a century-long struggle for national control of the country's patrimony. The left, including MAS and the Communists, criticized the generous compensation and the continuing ties to foreign companies. Pérez Alfonzo, who had long called for a larger state role, was skeptical of how much nationalization would solve if the underlying pattern of dependence remained. The companies, for their part, adjusted, since they retained marketing relations and technical roles, though they lost control of reserves. The transfer was orderly and peaceful, in contrast with the confrontations in Mexico in 1938 or elsewhere, and this shaped how Venezuelans remembered it: a national achievement accomplished by negotiation and consensus.

Nationalization required more than a vote establishing public ownership. Officials had to determine which concessionary assets would transfer, how compensation would be calculated, whether export contracts could continue, and which personnel could keep the industry operating after the legal change. Venezuela possessed experienced technicians and managers, but many specialists had worked inside the structures of multinational companies. The transition demanded arrangements for records, procurement, maintenance, refining, shipping, and access to markets abroad. That practical work complicates narratives of a simple overnight break with foreigners: the legal title changed decisively, while international transactions and some technical relationships continued.

The law, published in Official Gazette Extraordinary No. 1,769 on 29 August 1975, is particularly useful for interpreting the political settlement. Its first article reserved oil activities and their commerce to the state and provided for the termination of existing concessions at the end of 1975. At the same time, provisions allowed operational arrangements and, in special circumstances, associations with private entities under state control. Contemporaries disputed whether these exceptions protected commercial flexibility or created loopholes that might undermine sovereignty. A student reading only the ceremonial nationalization speeches could miss this conflict about the legal form of national control.

The transfer also raised questions about whose interests the newly national industry should serve. For many workers, Venezuelan ownership promised recognition, training, and job security. For planners, it supplied a means to organize production with national development in mind. For finance officials, it meant that failures of oil-company investment or profitability could become public fiscal problems. These constituencies did not necessarily agree about production targets, reinvestment, wages, or how much revenue should be transferred immediately into the treasury. Nationalization resolved ownership but opened a lasting debate over governance.

Section 105.14

PDVSA and the Institutional Structure of Venezuela's National Oil Industry

The nationalized industry was organized around Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A. (PDVSA), created by decree on 30 August 1975 and operating from 1 January 1976. PDVSA was a holding company wholly owned by the state, which supervised a group of operating subsidiaries, most of them successors to the former foreign concessionaires. Initially the former companies were converted into separate state subsidiaries, and in 1978 they were consolidated into a small number of operating companies, including Lagoven, Maraven, and Corpoven, which had been the successors to Creole, Shell, and the state company CVP and other firms. Research and technological services were concentrated in an institute, Intevep, created in 1979, and a petrochemical company, Pequiven, operated separately.

The choice of this structure reflected a deliberate decision to preserve the industry's technical competence. Venezuelan managers and engineers, many of whom had worked for the foreign companies for decades, took over leadership, and PDVSA's first president was General Rafael Alfonzo Ravard, a military officer with prior experience in public enterprise. The company developed a strong corporate culture, with merit-based recruitment, relatively high salaries, professional training, and substantial autonomy over investment and operations. Its executives, based in Caracas, communicated frequently with the international industry, and PDVSA developed an international presence, acquiring refining and distribution assets abroad, including a stake in a German company in 1983 and, in 1986, half of the U.S. refiner CITGO.

The institutional design contained a tension. The state owned PDVSA, and the government appointed its board and had the final say over budgets and policy through the Ministry of Energy and Mines. But the logic of the design was to insulate management from day-to-day political interference, so that professionals rather than party appointees ran the company. Defenders argued that this was what made PDVSA efficient and shielded it from the patronage that affected other state firms. Critics, including some on the left and some economists, argued that PDVSA had become a "state within the state," pursuing its own corporate interests, favoring expansion and international investment, and escaping democratic accountability. The question of how much autonomy a state oil company should have, and who should control the revenue, would become a major political conflict in the following decades (Chapters 106–107).

PDVSA's legal creation and the start of its operations were two separate events. The holding company was established in August 1975 to supervise the transfer and to coordinate state oil enterprises. When nationalization took effect on 1 January 1976, production continued through operating subsidiaries largely formed from the former concessionary companies. These subsidiaries initially retained different engineering cultures, personnel systems, and regional production portfolios. The organizational change was therefore neither a complete replacement of the industry's workforce nor merely a change in the sign on company buildings. It combined continuity of expertise with a newly defined public owner.

Corporate integration unfolded over several years. Contemporary institutional histories describe the consolidation of numerous initial operators to seven in 1977, four in 1978, and three by 1986: Corpoven, Lagoven, and Maraven. These reorganizations aimed to reduce duplication and coordinate procurement, planning, finance, and production, but they also generated questions about where operational decisions should be made. A holding company could improve national oversight while preserving technical specialization in producing subsidiaries. It could also establish bureaucratic distance between the people managing fields and the elected representatives formally responsible for state property. Neither outcome followed automatically from nationalization.

The governance relationship between PDVSA and the Ministry of Energy and Mines was central. The ministry claimed authority to define public policy and capture resources for the national budget; company managers argued that predictable investment programs, maintenance, and technical decisions required continuity beyond election cycles. Strong corporate financial performance could reinforce arguments for autonomy, but it did not answer whether reinvestment should take priority over hospitals, public housing, or fiscal stabilization. Different social groups judged success using different metrics. The national company's early emphasis on maintaining production and export credibility was understandable in an industry dependent on international contracts and specialized equipment, yet it could also be criticized as too close to the priorities of the pre-nationalization firms.

Students should distinguish independence from private ownership. PDVSA belonged to the Venezuelan state even when managers exercised professional discretion. Questions about board appointments, disclosure, audits, and dividend transfers were therefore questions of public accountability, not simply commercial efficiency. The company's eventual diversification and international refining arrangements, developed further in the 1980s, reflected the search for stable outlets for Venezuelan crude. Their longer consequences belong to later chapters. Here the essential point is that petroleum sovereignty required an institutional technology of corporate government whose design could influence national policy for decades.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Nationalization a Rupture or a Continuity?

The question: Did the 1975–1976 nationalization fundamentally change who controlled Venezuelan oil, or did it largely continue earlier patterns, with a state company replacing foreign firms while operations, markets, and managers stayed much the same?

Interpretation A — A decisive change in sovereignty: Supporters emphasize that ownership of operations, reserves, and revenue passed to the state, that the companies lost control of decisions on investment and production, and that Venezuela gained the capacity to pursue its own energy policy. On this view, the transfer completed a long process and created a national industry.
Interpretation B — Substantial continuity: Others emphasize that managers and technology remained largely the same, that former owners kept marketing and technical roles, that compensation was generous, and that PDVSA's autonomy and international orientation resembled a multinational company. On this view, the change was real but narrower than the rhetoric suggested, and the new structure carried over earlier patterns.

Evidence: Both views draw on the 1975 law and its technical-assistance agreements, PDVSA's organization and reports, production and revenue statistics, and the testimony of participants. They differ in how much weight they give to legal ownership compared with managerial practice.

Why it matters: The answer affects how later conflicts over PDVSA are understood. If nationalization was a decisive rupture, later disputes appear as struggles over how to use a national asset. If it left a corporate culture largely insulated from politics, later political efforts to control the company appear as a redefinition of the original settlement. The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine them.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, and identify two additional kinds of evidence that would help decide how much real control changed.

Section 105.15

The Rentier State: Public Spending, Development Projects, Debt, and Inequality

Nationalization increased the amount of oil income under government control and arrived as prices climbed again after the Iranian Revolution in 1979. In the second half of the 1970s and into the early 1980s, the Venezuelan state was among the largest spenders in Latin America. The Fifth National Plan, associated with Pérez, committed funds to steel, aluminum, electricity, petrochemicals, and other heavy industries. A large number of public enterprises grew, often under the Venezuelan Corporation of Guayana and other state holding bodies. The government also expanded social spending, subsidized food, kept gasoline, electricity, and public transport cheap, and raised public-sector wages.

Spending connected to the structure of the economy. Because the bolívar was strong, imports of food, consumer goods, and machinery were inexpensive, and domestic agriculture and manufacturing struggled to compete. Import dependence rose, and the economy became less diversified, not more. Large projects often took years to complete and sometimes failed to reach their expected output, and some of the plants built in these years operated below capacity or needed continuing subsidies. Public companies also borrowed abroad, frequently on their own account and without close coordination with the central government, as international banks, flush with deposits from oil exporters, lent eagerly to Latin American governments. External debt grew from low levels in the early 1970s to tens of billions of dollars by 1983, a rise concentrated in the public sector.

Corruption controversies accompanied the boom. The scale of contracts, the weak oversight of public enterprises, and the close connections between politicians and business created many opportunities for abuse. After an investigation that intensified in 1979, Congress in May 1980 held former president Pérez politically responsible in the Sierra Nevada refrigerated-vessel procurement controversy, without a criminal conviction. This was a notable legislative rebuke of a former president that illustrated both the concern about public spending and the willingness of the system to police itself, even if imperfectly. The administration of Luis Herrera Campíns of COPEI, who won the 1978 election and took office in 1979, criticized what he called the mortgage left by his predecessor, yet his government also borrowed heavily and continued many policies.

The distribution of benefits was unequal. Middle-class professionals, public employees, unionized workers in the oil and heavy industries, and business owners with state connections were the principal beneficiaries. Millions of poor Venezuelans gained from expanded education, health services, and subsidies, but many remained in precarious employment, and the barrios continued to grow. The pattern of petroleum-funded growth increased expectations of continued improvement, so when conditions worsened, the sense of disappointment was acute.

The Sierra Nevada refrigerated-vessel controversy illustrates the importance of precise institutional language. Investigation and criticism expanded in 1979 after Pérez left the presidency. In May 1980, Congress concluded that the former president bore political responsibility in the procurement controversy, while not establishing his criminal guilt. The episode should be read as a dispute over procurement, legislative scrutiny, and the reputation of an oil-funded executive, rather than proof that every public investment followed the same pattern. Equally, fiscal choices cannot be understood apart from the expectations voters had formed during the high-price years.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: The Venezuelan Oil State, 1914–1983

What Changed

  • Venezuela moved from an agrarian export economy to dependence on petroleum
  • The state's share of oil income rose from modest royalties to roughly half of profits by 1948 and then to full ownership of operations in 1976
  • Politics moved from caudillo and military rule to a competitive party democracy, interrupted by dictatorship from 1948 to 1958
  • A rural population became overwhelmingly urban

What Stayed the Same

  • State ownership of the subsoil, a principle inherited from Spanish colonial law, remained the legal basis
  • Government revenue and the economy depended on a single export
  • The executive remained powerful, and access to state resources remained the key to political and economic advancement
  • Inequality persisted despite rising average incomes

Who experienced change most directly: Migrants who moved from the countryside to oil towns and cities, oil workers, and the new middle class. Greater continuity: The rural poor, the barrio residents who remained in informal work, and Indigenous peoples in the oil and Guayana regions. Why the pattern matters: It shows that institutional change, such as nationalization and democracy, coexisted with persistent economic dependence, and it helps explain why the crisis of the 1980s struck at the system's foundations.

Section 105.16

The Debt Crisis, Capital Flight, and Black Friday, February 1983

The conditions that supported the boom reversed around 1981–1982. Oil prices, which had peaked around 1980, softened as demand weakened in the recession in industrial countries and as conservation and non-OPEC supply increased. Interest rates in the United States rose sharply, increasing the cost of servicing floating-rate loans, and in August 1982 Mexico's announcement that it could not meet its debt payments shook confidence in all Latin American borrowers (Chapter 93). Venezuela's government faced falling revenue, rising debt service, and a growing deficit. Much of the external debt was short-term, so refinancing was urgent.

Venezuelans responded to uncertainty by moving money out of the country. Business owners, investors, and many middle-class households converted bolívars to dollars, deposited them abroad, or bought foreign assets, and in the early 1980s capital flight drained foreign-exchange reserves by amounts that analysts have estimated in the billions of dollars, though the totals vary widely by method of calculation. The government, committed to the fixed rate that had held since the early 1960s, sold dollars to meet the demand, and its reserves fell. By early 1983 it was clear that the exchange rate could not be sustained.

On Friday, 18 February 1983, the government of Luis Herrera Campíns suspended the sale of foreign currency and announced a new exchange system, which became known as Viernes Negro, Black Friday. Exchange controls were introduced, and a special agency, the Office of Differential Exchange Rates (RECADI), allocated dollars at preferential rates for approved imports and debt payments, with other transactions at less favorable rates. The bolívar, which had been worth 4.30 to the dollar for nearly two decades, lost much of its value in the following months and years; by the end of the decade the official rate for most transactions was well above ten bolívars per dollar. The measure was a response to a real crisis, but its effect on public expectations was profound.

Viernes Negro was a psychological rupture as much as a financial one. For a generation, Venezuelans had assumed that the bolívar was stable, that travel abroad and imported goods were within reach of the middle class, and that the government could solve economic problems with oil revenue. The devaluation made foreign debts, imports, and travel suddenly more expensive, and it exposed the vulnerability of an economy dependent on a single export. The multiple exchange rates created opportunities for corruption, since access to cheap dollars was a valuable privilege that could be distributed through connections. It was a major reason why the 1980s are often described as a "lost decade" in Venezuela, as elsewhere in Latin America.

Currency stability was an important part of Venezuela's social contract, but stability did not mean an identical nominal rate for two decades. The exchange-rate system established in 1964 used a rate around 4.45 bolívares to the dollar; the rate moved modestly and stood at about 4.30 for much of the 1970s and into early 1983. The interruption on Viernes Negro was therefore experienced not simply as an adjustment of an administrative number but as the collapse of a familiar measure of national prosperity. Importers, savers, and households with overseas obligations encountered the change differently. Multiple official rates and controls created new incentives for lobbying and unequal access to foreign exchange.

That exchange-rate crisis also shows why the language of an overnight collapse needs qualification. Public indebtedness, capital flight, and fiscal pressure had accumulated over years, while the reserves and institutional capacity that might have eased adjustment were unevenly deployed. The international debt crisis increased the cost of refinancing, but domestic spending choices and the continuing concentration of exports in petroleum magnified vulnerability. Devaluation did not end government control over the foreign-exchange market; rather, the controls were reorganized. Later corruption controversies around the exchange-allocation agency RECADI became one of the ways Venezuelans understood the distributional consequences of scarcity.

Historical Comparison

Historical Comparison: Boom and Bust in Venezuela and Mexico

Venezuela: A democratic government, wealthy from petroleum, borrowed and spent in the 1970s, held a fixed exchange rate until 1983, and entered the 1980s with falling revenue and large debts.

Mexico: Under a dominant-party regime, a new oil boom in the late 1970s encouraged heavy borrowing, and in August 1982 the country announced it could not service its debt, an event often treated as the start of the regional debt crisis (Chapter 93).

What the comparison shows: Different political systems experienced similar financial patterns when commodity booms coincided with abundant international credit and rising interest rates. The cases suggest that the vulnerability was not unique to Venezuelan institutions, but they differ in the political consequences: Venezuela's two-party system absorbed the first shocks, while its legitimacy eroded over the following years.

Section 105.17

Jaime Lusinchi, Economic Adjustment, and the Erosion of Public Confidence

Jaime Lusinchi of Acción Democrática won the December 1983 election with a majority of the vote and governed from 1984 to 1989. He inherited an economy in recession, an external debt that had to be renegotiated, and an exchange-control system that was already generating controversy. His government chose to manage the crisis through negotiation with creditors, rather than a comprehensive structural adjustment of the kind that international lenders were then urging on Latin American governments. On 26 February 1986 it signed a refinancing agreement with foreign banks covering approximately US$21.2 billion of existing public-sector external bank debt, rescheduling maturities rather than providing US$21.2 billion in new credit. Falling oil prices quickly forced discussion of renegotiation. It also kept multiple exchange rates in place, using RECADI to allocate dollars at favorable rates for debt service and for priority imports.

The period was one of declining living standards. Oil prices collapsed again in 1986 as Saudi Arabia increased production, and Venezuelan export earnings fell sharply. Real wages declined, unemployment and informal work grew, and inflation, which had been low by Latin American standards, rose. Average income per person, which had peaked in the late 1970s, fell substantially over the following decade, and studies using various methods found that poverty increased markedly. The government tried to protect incomes through price controls and subsidies, but shortages and black markets accompanied the controls, and the gap between official and market exchange rates invited abuse.

Corruption allegations eroded confidence. The RECADI system became the subject of widespread accusations that officials and businesspeople had obtained preferential dollars through false import documents or political connections, with estimates of the sums involved in the billions of dollars. Investigations and prosecutions followed in the years after Lusinchi left office, though accountability remained limited. The personal conduct of the president and the influence of his close associates, including his private secretary Blanca Ibáñez, became public scandals. Students should distinguish between the documented existence of irregularities in the exchange system, which was well established in subsequent investigations, and the larger claims made in political debate, which varied in their evidence.

The government also faced questions about human rights. In October 1988, in the border village of El Amparo in Apure state, a military and police unit killed fourteen fishermen, and the authorities initially described them as guerrillas. Survivors and human-rights groups challenged that account, and the case became a symbol of impunity. The human-rights organization Provea, founded in 1988, began systematically documenting such cases. These events complicated the image of Venezuela as an exemplary democracy that its leaders had cultivated since the 1960s.

At the same time, party leaders recognized that the system required reform. In 1984 Lusinchi created a presidential commission on state reform, COPRE, which proposed changes in the political system, including the direct election of governors and mayors, who had previously been appointed by the president. The reforms were enacted by the end of the decade and the first direct elections were scheduled for December 1989. They illustrate that elements within the system were aware of its loss of credibility, but the reforms were slow and were overtaken by events. By the late 1980s, opinion surveys and the rising abstention rate indicated that confidence in AD and COPEI was declining.

Section 105.18

Carlos Andrés Pérez Returns: The 1988 Election and the 1989 Economic Reform Package

Carlos Andrés Pérez, who had left office in 1979, was permitted to run again after the constitutionally required ten-year interval. His campaign for the December 1988 election evoked memories of the first Pérez presidency, remembered by many as a time of abundance, and he criticized the international financial institutions in terms that suggested he would resist their demands. He defeated Eduardo Fernández of COPEI with a little over half the vote, while abstention rose to roughly one in five voters, high by Venezuelan standards. Voters had returned to a leader they associated with prosperity, and they expected a restoration of the earlier boom.

Pérez took office on 2 February 1989 in a lavish ceremony attended by numerous heads of state. Within days his government announced that it would adopt a program of economic adjustment, which he called the "Great Turn," El Gran Viraje. The reasons were fiscal and external: reserves were low, debt service was heavy, the exchange-rate system was unsustainable, and the government needed financing. His economic team, drawn largely from technocrats trained in economics and business, negotiated with the International Monetary Fund, whose support also opened access to other lenders. The program was announced in mid-February and was formalized in an arrangement with the IMF early in 1989.

Several features of the package mattered for what followed. Gasoline prices were to rise in stages, and the government authorized an increase of about thirty percent in public-transport fares. The elimination of price controls on most goods, together with the devaluation caused by floating the exchange rate, would quickly raise the cost of imports and food. Within the governing party, leaders who had not been consulted were uneasy, and the labor confederation and the left criticized the plan sharply.

Economists have debated the program's merits. Its defenders argued that the old model was exhausted and that adjustment was unavoidable, and that the government had little choice given the debt and reserves. Critics argued that the speed and the way it was introduced, with little preparation of public opinion and with the sacrifice falling heavily on the poor, courted disaster. This chapter does not resolve that debate. What matters for the history is the situation at the end of February 1989: a population whose incomes had been falling for years, a president who had promised the opposite, and a set of price increases about to reach the streets.

The financial chronology is essential. Pérez's government announced its adjustment measures on 16 February 1989, before the unrest began. The International Monetary Fund did not approve the full three-year Extended Fund Facility before the Caracazo: a first-credit-tranche purchase was authorized on 23 March, followed by approval of the extended arrangement on 23 June. These dates matter because opposition to the price and transport changes developed amid public announcements and expectations rather than after the completion of an international lending agreement. The distinction also prevents a presidential policy decision from being inaccurately represented as the automatic implementation of a contract already signed.

There was a second chronological tension between policy and daily life. Changes to gasoline pricing and transport fares reached households through buses and commuting expenditures, while many promised compensatory measures depended on administrative machinery that could not immediately reach every affected family. Students should not infer that all drivers, transit passengers, or demonstrators shared one political interpretation. For some, the immediate grievance was the fare demanded for a trip to work or school; for others, the announcement signaled that an elected government had abandoned the promise of an oil-financed standard of living. These experiences offer a social explanation of how a technically framed macroeconomic program could provoke a rapid crisis of legitimacy.

Section 105.19

The Caracazo, February 27–March 1989: Protest, Repression, and Civilian Deaths

The protests began on the morning of Monday, 27 February 1989, in Guarenas, a town about forty kilometers east of Caracas whose residents commute to the capital. Passengers at the bus terminal found that drivers were charging fares well above the authorized increase, and angry confrontations escalated. Crowds blocked streets, buses were attacked or burned, and shops were looted. The unrest spread within hours to the neighboring town of Guatire and to Caracas, where bus terminals, such as Nuevo Circo, and the poorer eastern and western districts, including Petare and Catia, saw similar disturbances. By afternoon and evening, looting of supermarkets, appliance stores, and other commercial establishments was widespread in the capital, and it spread to other cities, including Valencia, Maracay, Barquisimeto, Maracaibo, Mérida, and Ciudad Guayana.

The early response was disorganized. Police forces, which were small and unprepared, were quickly overwhelmed in many places, and there were reports that some police stayed away or were themselves involved. The government, which had not anticipated a disturbance on this scale, faced a rapidly escalating situation on the first night. Looting in some areas included not just luxury goods but food, and the sources give evidence of both opportunistic theft and desperate need. Many participants were young people from the barrios, but accounts also record people of other backgrounds taking part in the looting.

On 28 February the government issued Decree 49, suspending several constitutional guarantees, including freedom of movement, personal liberty, and inviolability of the home, and imposed a curfew. The measure invoked the emergency powers permitted by the 1961 Constitution. The army and the National Guard were deployed in large numbers in Caracas and other cities, and security forces conducted house-to-house searches and used firearms, including in dense barrio neighborhoods. In the following days, hospitals and morgues in Caracas received large numbers of dead and wounded, and many victims were killed in their homes or on streets by gunfire, including, according to later testimony and judicial findings, people who had not participated in looting. Order was largely restored by early March, and guarantees were restored in stages over the following weeks.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: What Was the Caracazo?

The question: Was the Caracazo a spontaneous outburst of looting and disorder set off by fare increases, a political protest against austerity and the political system, or a combination whose meaning is better explained by deeper social conditions?

Interpretation A — A reaction to austerity and political exclusion: Scholars such as Margarita López Maya have emphasized the context of declining incomes, the sense of betrayal by the president's reversal, and the weak channels through which popular grievances could be expressed. On this view, the looting and protests were a form of popular political action, even if unorganized.
Interpretation B — Disorder and opportunism, within a longer economic crisis: Others stress that the events involved widespread looting by participants with varied motives, that no political organization led them, and that the immediate trigger was a fare dispute, so that characterizing them as a political uprising goes beyond the evidence. Some analyses add that the speed of the spread reflected the weakness of the police rather than a coordinated movement.

Evidence: Both views draw on press accounts, survey data, testimony, and the circumstances of the first hours. They differ in how they interpret motives that participants rarely articulated at the time.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how the event is placed in Venezuelan history: as the first great popular repudiation of the system, or as a violent episode whose political meaning was assigned afterward. These views overlap, since the protests may have been unorganized and still reflect political grievances, and the separate question of state violence is not affected by how the protesters' motives are characterized.

Student task: Identify what evidence would show whether participants understood their actions as protest against the program, and explain why the justification of lethal force would not depend on that answer.

Section 105.20

The Crisis of Punto Fijo Democracy and the Unfinished Dilemma of the Petroleum State

The Caracazo dramatized several tensions that had been building for decades. First, resource dependence: a state financed by a single export had little cushion when prices fell and debt rose, and the adjustment forced by the crisis fell on a population whose expectations had been shaped by abundance. Second, democratic stability: the party system had preserved civilian government and peaceful alternation, a real achievement, but its reliance on distribution made it vulnerable when resources contracted. Third, development: the aspiration to sow the oil had produced real infrastructure, education, and industry, but not a diversified economy. Fourth, patronage and inequality: benefits flowed through party and union networks and left many Venezuelans at the margins. Fifth, legitimacy: the use of lethal force against civilians, and the failure to investigate it, damaged the credibility of a system that claimed to embody democratic values.

It is accurate to call 1989 a rupture. The scale of the violence and of the public anger had no precedent in the democratic period, and the political class understood this. The Pérez government continued its adjustment program, and the economy initially recovered, but social discontent and political division persisted. Within the military, a portion of younger officers, who had been deployed in the streets and were also affected by declining pay and perceptions of corruption, became increasingly critical of civilian leadership. Within the parties, the distance between leaders and voters widened, and abstention grew. The system continued to function, but its authority was impaired.

It would nonetheless be a mistake to read the Caracazo as the first step in an inevitable path to a particular future. Historical outcomes depend on choices and contingencies, and Venezuelans in 1989 faced several possible directions: reform of the parties and institutions, a successful economic recovery, continued decay, or a challenge from outside the established system. The decentralization reforms were about to produce the first direct elections of governors and mayors, some of whom came from outside the two major parties. Reformist groups within and outside the parties proposed ways to renew democracy. That some of these possibilities did not prevail, and that a military challenge came three years later, was the result of events and decisions that Chapter 106 examines. Students should treat the later history as an open question in 1989, not as a conclusion already contained in it.

The dilemma of the petroleum state remained unresolved. Venezuela's institutions, economy, and politics were still organized around the collection and distribution of oil income. The nationalized industry, PDVSA, continued to produce and to expand its international operations, and the debates about how to use its revenue, how autonomous the company should be, and how to diversify the economy were unsettled. Those debates would shape the choices of the 1990s and the next decades. The chapter has traced how a democracy was built on oil, how it delivered substantial achievements and distributed them unequally, and how in 1989 its foundations were visibly strained. In February 1992, a group of army officers would attempt to take power by force, an event that Chapter 106 takes up.

Everyday Life and Social History

Camps, Barrios, and Fare Lines: Living in the Petroleum State

In the oil camps of the 1920s and 1930s, the geography of daily life expressed inequality. Foreign managers lived in company housing with amenities, while Venezuelan and Caribbean workers lived in crowded quarters outside the camps, and women worked as domestic servants, laundresses, cooks, and market sellers in the surrounding towns. Families from the Andes and the Llanos adapted to the heat and the cash economy of the oil region, and Indigenous communities such as the Barí, Wayuu, and Añú saw their territory and fishing waters change.

In the postwar decades, millions moved to cities. In barrios, families built homes in stages as money and materials allowed, organized neighborhood committees to obtain water, electricity, and schools, and relied on informal trade, domestic work, and construction. Women were often the organizers of these efforts and the managers of household budgets, and when prices rose in the 1980s they adjusted by cutting meals, buying smaller quantities, and taking additional work. Commuters from towns such as Guarenas spent hours on buses each day, so that fare increases were felt immediately in household budgets. The silence of many sources about this daily life, which appears mostly in oral histories, neighborhood studies, and the testimony of victims' families, is itself a reminder of whose experience is recorded.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the rise and crisis of Venezuela's petroleum democracy from the first commercial well in 1914 to the Caracazo of 1989. Its central argument is that oil financed both achievement and vulnerability: it enabled a modernizing state and a competitive party system, and it fostered dependence on a single export, an expectation that the state could provide, and a distribution of benefits that left many behind.

Venezuela entered the oil era as a poor, rural country governed by caudillos, with an export economy of coffee and cacao and a legal tradition that placed the subsoil in the hands of the state. Commercial production began in 1914, and after the Los Barrosos blowout of 1922, production expanded so quickly that by the late 1920s Venezuela was the world's second-largest producer and oil had become its leading export.

Under the dictator Juan Vicente Gómez, the concession system tied foreign capital to the central state. Revenue strengthened the dictatorship and paid off the foreign debt, while concessions were distributed to insiders and sold to foreign companies. The oil camps and towns created new forms of work, migration, segregation, and labor organization, and Indigenous peoples, notably the Barí, bore the costs of the oil frontier.

After Gómez, the question of national control and use of oil moved to the center of politics. The 1943 Hydrocarbons Law consolidated and raised the state's share while extending concessions, and the 1948 amendment produced the fifty–fifty arrangement. Arturo Uslar Pietri's call to sow the oil framed a developmental aspiration that governments would invoke for decades.

The political history was turbulent. The civil-military revolution of October 1945 and the Trienio introduced universal suffrage and mass politics, but the coup of November 1948 ended the experiment. The dictatorship of Pérez Jiménez used oil wealth to build roads and monuments and repressed opponents, and it fell in January 1958 after a broad coalition of civilians and officers turned against it.

The Pact of Punto Fijo, signed in October 1958 by AD, COPEI, and URD, and the Constitution of 1961 established the framework of a competitive party democracy. Betancourt's government survived conspiracies from the right and insurgency from the left, and the transfers of power to Leoni in 1964 and to Caldera in 1969 demonstrated that elections could change governments peacefully. The system excluded the Communist Party and relied on the distribution of oil income through party, union, and business networks.

In the international arena, Venezuela helped found OPEC in 1960 under the influence of Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, who also warned about the dangers of oil dependence. Measures in 1970 and 1971 expanded state control, and the price shock of 1973 brought an unprecedented flow of revenue to Carlos Andrés Pérez, whose Gran Venezuela program invested heavily in industry and infrastructure while spending, importing, and borrowing at a rapid pace.

Nationalization took effect on 1 January 1976 under a law that compensated the companies and created PDVSA, a state holding company with a professional management and substantial autonomy. Historians debate whether the change was a decisive rupture or contained significant continuities, and the tension between corporate autonomy and democratic accountability would become a major political issue.

The rentier state shaped the economy and politics. A strong bolívar and fixed exchange rate made imports cheap and discouraged domestic production, subsidies and public enterprises distributed income widely but unequally, and foreign borrowing grew. When prices fell and interest rates rose in the early 1980s, capital flight and falling reserves forced the government to suspend the sale of dollars on 18 February 1983, the Viernes Negro that ended nearly two decades of exchange-rate stability.

The 1980s were years of declining incomes, debt negotiation, exchange controls, corruption scandals, and violations of human rights that eroded public confidence in AD and COPEI. Carlos Andrés Pérez, elected in 1988 on memories of the boom, announced an adjustment program within days of taking office that raised fuel and transport costs and removed price controls.

On 27 February 1989 a fare dispute in Guarenas set off protests and looting that spread across Caracas and other cities. The government suspended constitutional guarantees and deployed the military, and security forces killed many civilians. The death toll is disputed, ranging from the official figure of a few hundred to estimates of a thousand or more, and later investigations and the Inter-American Court established state responsibility in specific cases. The Caracazo was a rupture in the legitimacy of the Punto Fijo system, but the rise of a new political order was not inevitable. The unresolved dilemmas of the petroleum state, and the open question of what would follow, lead to Chapter 106.

Key Terms

Petroleum state

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown how one resource reshaped a country without determining its fate. Oil gave Venezuelan governments the means to build institutions and to broaden prosperity, and it shaped the habits and expectations of politics. The achievements were real, including a durable civilian democracy in a region of dictatorships, but they depended on revenue that no government controlled and on a distribution that left many outside its benefits.

The chapter also shows the importance of sovereignty as a historical idea. From concessions under a dictator to nationalization under a democratic government, Venezuelans debated who should own, control, and benefit from the country's patrimony. That debate did not end in 1976. The questions of PDVSA's autonomy, the use of oil income, and the diversification of the economy remained open, and they connect Venezuela to other resource-exporting societies in Latin America, a theme Chapter 108 explores comparatively.

The Caracazo reveals a further continuity: the tension between democratic legitimacy and the use of force. A system that prided itself on peaceful alternation responded to unrest with a military deployment that killed civilians, and the struggle of victims' families to obtain truth and justice became part of the country's democratic history. That struggle, and the disputed memory of the event, continue to shape how Venezuelans understand their past.

Chapter 106 turns to the political consequences of the decay of the Punto Fijo system: the 1992 military rebellion, the imprisonment and rise of Hugo Chávez, the election of 1998, and the Bolivarian Constitution. Students should carry forward the view that these developments arose from choices and contingencies in a system under strain, and that the dilemmas of the petroleum state, left unresolved in 1989, remained at the center of Venezuelan politics.
