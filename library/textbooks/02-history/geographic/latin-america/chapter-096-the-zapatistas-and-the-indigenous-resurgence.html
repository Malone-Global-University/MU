The Zapatistas and the Indigenous Resurgence

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Rebellion Prepared for Eleven Years

The uprising that astonished Mexico on January 1, 1994 was not the beginning of the Zapatista story. It was the moment a movement prepared in secrecy since November 17, 1983 became visible to the world, in the hour that Mexico's government was celebrating its entry into a North American free-trade bloc.

In the early hours of New Year's Day 1994, men and women wearing ski masks and carrying a mixture of modern and improvised weapons entered San Cristóbal de las Casas and several other towns in the southern Mexican state of Chiapas. They proclaimed a war against the federal army and the national government, and they took the name of Emiliano Zapata, the agrarian leader of the Mexican Revolution. They called themselves the Zapatista Army of National Liberation, or EZLN (Ejército Zapatista de Liberación Nacional). Most of them were Indigenous: Tseltal, Tsotsil, Ch'ol, Tojolabal, and members of other communities. The date was chosen with care. That day the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), described in Chapter 95 as the centerpiece of Mexican economic reform, entered into force.

The timing invites a simple explanation: the Zapatistas rose against NAFTA. That explanation is partial. The EZLN had been founded more than a decade earlier, and its roots reach back further still, into decades of Indigenous organizing, church-based community work, struggles over land, and disputes about political representation in Chiapas. The rebellion of 1994 was the public appearance of a movement that had been preparing, and the reforms of the early 1990s intensified grievances more than they created them. This chapter therefore separates the preparation of the rebellion from its public appearance, a distinction on which the whole argument depends.

The chapter's purpose, in the language of the Authoritative Outline, is to examine the EZLN, Chiapas, Indigenous rights, globalization, land conflict, transnational activism, constitutional reform, and new models of political organization. It follows three transformations. First, Indigenous communities moved from exclusion to organized political demands about land, public services, cultural recognition, and representation. Second, the EZLN moved from armed rebellion to negotiation with the Mexican state, culminating in the San Andrés Accords of 1996 and a constitutional reform in 2001 that the movement judged inadequate. Third, Zapatista communities moved from protest to the construction of autonomous institutions of governance, education, health, and collective work, while confronting significant internal and external constraints.

Four categories must be kept distinct throughout. The EZLN was a clandestine politico-military organization with a command structure. Its civilian support bases were Indigenous communities that affiliated with it and organized autonomous institutions. The broader Mexican Indigenous movement, including the National Indigenous Congress and countless community and regional organizations, overlapped with Zapatismo but was not controlled by it. And other Indigenous movements across Latin America, in Ecuador, Bolivia, Guatemala, and elsewhere, developed along their own paths. The Zapatistas anchor this chapter, but they were not the origin or the representative of every Indigenous movement in the region. Residents of Chiapas also belonged to many other organizations, parties, churches, and communities, and some opposed the EZLN.

Evidence requires special care. The EZLN produced a large body of communiqués, declarations, letters, and interviews, preserved in its own archives, and these are evidence of what the movement proclaimed, not neutral descriptions of events. Government laws and decrees are evidence of what the state enacted. Reports by the Mexican National Human Rights Commission, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, and United Nations bodies were gathered under their own methodologies and limits. Journalists, anthropologists, and Indigenous women and men have left testimonies and studies that complicate every official narrative. No single type of source decides the disputed questions of this chapter, and the sections below identify which kind of source supports which claim.

Finally, the chapter's boundaries matter. Chapter 94 treated the broad return to democracy and Chapter 95 treated neoliberal reform and NAFTA as economic policy. Chapter 96 examines their consequences for Indigenous communities and the political movements that responded. Colombia's armed conflict and organized crime belong to Chapters 97 to 100, and Chapter 108 will take up Bolivia's and Ecuador's later governments. The principal chronological scope runs from about 1980 to 2006, with background on earlier organizing and a bounded epilogue on the reorganization of Zapatista governance announced in November 2023.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the historical conditions of land, poverty, and unequal development in Chiapas that preceded the uprising, distinguishing the Highlands, northern zone, and Lacandon Jungle.
  2. Trace the roles of Indigenous congresses, Catholic pastoral networks, and peasant organizations in preparing the ground for Zapatismo.
  3. Distinguish the EZLN as an armed organization from its civilian support bases, the wider Mexican Indigenous movement, and Indigenous movements elsewhere in Latin America.
  4. Analyze why the 1992 amendment of Article 27 and the entry into force of NAFTA intensified existing grievances without being the sole cause of rebellion.
  5. Reconstruct the events of January 1 to 12, 1994, separating documented events from contested casualty figures and political claims.
  6. Interpret the First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle and the Revolutionary Women's Law as evidence of what the movement proclaimed and who its audiences were.
  7. Evaluate the San Andrés Accords, the COCOPA proposal, and the 2001 constitutional reform, explaining what was agreed, what was enacted, and why the EZLN judged the reform inadequate.
  8. Reconstruct the Acteal massacre of December 22, 1997 and distinguish Las Abejas, paramilitary groups, state institutions, and the EZLN.
  9. Explain the development of autonomous municipalities, caracoles, and Good Government Councils, and the principle of mandar obedeciendo.
  10. Assess the transnational dimension of Zapatismo and distinguish the movement's own ideas from outside supporters' interpretations.
  11. Compare Zapatista strategies with those of CONAIE in Ecuador, Bolivian lowland Indigenous movements, and Guatemala's Maya rights movement.
  12. Evaluate the movement's achievements and limits, including the 2023 reorganization of its governing structures.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is it misleading to treat the 1994 uprising as only a reaction to NAFTA, and what evidence supports a longer chronology?
  2. How did Catholic pastoral work, agrarian organizations, and clandestine revolutionary networks interact in Chiapas, and where did they disagree?
  3. Why did a rebellion of limited military strength produce national and international political consequences far greater than its armed forces?
  4. What is the difference between a declared ceasefire, a negotiated agreement, and an implemented settlement, and how does this distinction explain the history of 1994 to 2001?
  5. How should historians weigh the claims of the EZLN, the Mexican government, human-rights investigators, and local communities when they conflict, as at Acteal?
  6. In what ways did debates about gender and rights occur within Zapatista communities, not only between those communities and the state?
  7. Does local autonomous government offer stronger self-determination than representation within existing state institutions, and what did Indigenous movements themselves argue?
  8. What did Indigenous movements in Mexico, Ecuador, Bolivia, and Guatemala share, and why did their strategies diverge?
  9. How should the Zapatista legacy be assessed when it cannot be measured only by military victory, territorial control, or constitutional text?

Historical Context

Chiapas lies in southeastern Mexico on the border with Guatemala. It is a state of great ecological variety, with Pacific coastal plains, the central depression around the capital Tuxtla Gutiérrez, the cool forested Highlands (Los Altos) around San Cristóbal de las Casas, northern uplands, and the humid Lacandon Jungle in the east. It is also among Mexico's poorest states, though it produces hydroelectric power, petroleum, coffee, and cattle that benefit regions far beyond it. It was part of the Maya world described in Chapters 9 and 12 and a colonial frontier of the Kingdom of Guatemala (Chapters 34 and 40). Chiapas formally joined Mexico in September 1824 after a contested political decision, while the Federal Republic of Central America still existed; the status of Soconusco and the border remained disputed. The federation itself disintegrated mainly in the late 1830s and early 1840s (see Chapters 57 and 62).

Indigenous peoples have lived in Chiapas continuously through all these changes. Tseltal and Tsotsil speakers are the largest groups, followed by Ch'ol, Tojolabal, Zoque, and smaller Mayan-language communities such as Mam and Lacandón (Lacandon Maya). Indigenous Chiapas also includes communities displaced or formed by migration, including from Guatemala in the 1980s. Chapter 4 explained why these linguistic labels are not identical to political units, and the same caution applies here. A Tseltal community in the jungle, a Tsotsil village in the Highlands, and an Indigenous resident of a town may have shared language but very different land, religion, and politics.

The twentieth-century background came from earlier chapters. The Mexican Revolution of 1910 to 1920 (Chapters 71 to 73) promised land reform, and Article 27 of the Constitution of 1917 made the nation the original owner of land and enabled redistribution through the ejido, a form of communal grant. Cárdenas expanded the program in the 1930s (Chapter 74). Chiapas, however, felt the Revolution unevenly: large landowners, including cattle and coffee estate holders, retained power in many regions, and agrarian reform there proceeded slowly, often through bureaucratic procedure that left petitioners waiting for years. The PRI-dominated political system (Chapters 74 and 94) governed through corporatist organizations and local intermediaries, and Indigenous communities often experienced the state through officials, caciques, and party structures rather than through accountable representation.

Three more developments from Volume V frame the chapter. The debt crisis of the 1980s (Chapter 93) led Mexico to adopt austerity, and rural support and credit shrank. Chapter 94 traced a slow and contested democratization in which the PRI's monopoly eroded, though in Chiapas local power remained heavily entrenched. Chapter 95 described the market reforms of Carlos Salinas de Gortari (1988 to 1994), including the amendment of Article 27 and NAFTA. Finally, 1992, the quincentenary of Columbus's voyage (Chapter 27), became an occasion for Indigenous movements across the hemisphere to contest celebratory narratives of conquest. Rigoberta Menchú's Nobel Peace Prize that year (Chapter 91) symbolized a new international visibility for Indigenous rights. Zapatismo emerged in a climate where Indigenous claims were gaining international resonance.

Historians have revised older accounts. The EZLN was not an isolated peasant jacquerie nor a conspiracy imposed by outside revolutionaries on passive communities, though both views were advanced at the time. Scholars of Chiapas, including Thomas Benjamin, Neil Harvey, and Shannan L. Mattiace, show a process in which long-standing Indigenous organizing and external revolutionary networks met and changed each other. This chapter follows that interactive account, while acknowledging that its details, especially the early clandestine years, are known mainly through the movement's own narratives and scholarly interviews rather than documentary archives.

Section 96.1

Chiapas: Land, Poverty, Indigenous Communities, and Unequal Development

The history of land in Chiapas begins with colonial conquest. After the Spanish and their allies subdued the Maya and other peoples of the region in the sixteenth century (Chapter 34), the Crown granted tribute and labor through encomienda, and Indigenous communities retained a measure of land through corporate village holdings, as elsewhere in New Spain (Chapters 33 and 40). Dominican friars, among them Bartolomé de las Casas, who served as bishop of Chiapas in the 1540s, defended Indigenous rights in language that later Chiapas activists would claim. San Cristóbal de las Casas, formerly Ciudad Real, became a Spanish-ruled town surrounded by Indigenous villages, and the division between a mestizo or ladino town and Indigenous countryside defined local social relations for centuries.

Nineteenth-century liberalism (Chapters 58 and 63) weakened corporate village landholding and encouraged private estates, and the export boom (Chapters 68 and 69) increased pressure. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, coffee estates on the Pacific slope and cattle ranches expanded, and highland Indigenous men were drawn or coerced into seasonal labor on lowland plantations, often through debt. Historians describe the persistence of coercive labor arrangements in Chiapas long after abolition elsewhere. The Mexican Revolution reached the state as conflict among landowning factions more than as a mass agrarian movement. As a result, post-revolutionary land reform in Chiapas began later and met stronger resistance than in central Mexico.

Post-1930s agrarian reform was real but uneven. Many ejidos were created, but procedures were slow, officials were often allied with landowners, and many applicants waited years or decades for resolution. Large estates, ranches, and sometimes illegally enlarged holdings survived. Indigenous communities were often pushed to marginal land. In the second half of the century, population growth and land scarcity in the Highlands led thousands of families to migrate into the eastern Lacandon Jungle, where federal policy encouraged colonization as a safety valve for land pressure. Settlers cleared forest, organized new communities with Tseltal, Ch'ol, Tojolabal, Tsotsil, and mestizo residents, and sometimes applied for ejido recognition, sometimes against competing claims.

Those competing claims were intense. A presidential decree in 1972 recognized a large area of the jungle as the property of a small group of Lacandón families, a decision that overlapped with settlers' occupation and with logging and conservation interests. The result was a prolonged conflict over legal status, eviction threats, and relocation. The Lacandon Jungle also lay near the Guatemalan border, a military and refugee frontier in the 1980s when thousands of Guatemalans fled army counterinsurgency (Chapter 91) into Chiapas. The region therefore combined settler communities with insecure land title, a strong army presence, and weak civil institutions.

Students should distinguish the three regions. In the Highlands, predominantly Tsotsil and Tseltal communities such as Chamula, Zinacantán, and Chenalhó had long histories of local authority, religious cargo systems, and political relationships with the ruling party; intermediaries in San Cristóbal controlled commerce and political access. In the northern zone, Ch'ol communities and others lived amid ranching and coffee and developed their own agrarian organizations and later became a site of paramilitary violence. In the Lacandon Jungle, newer settlements formed by migration produced a particular political culture marked by collective organization, shared hardship, and recent memory of the settlement itself. The Guatemalan border region added its own dynamics.

Indicators of inequality were stark and remain contested only in detail. By national standards, Chiapas had low levels of schooling, high illiteracy, limited access to piped water, electricity, and health clinics, and high infant and maternal mortality, especially in Indigenous municipalities. Meanwhile, the state's dams supplied a large share of Mexico's electricity, and its oil and agricultural exports left little local benefit. Many Indigenous households lacked services that were available in the capital city, and there were few secondary schools or hospitals in remote regions. The contrast between resource extraction and local poverty became part of the political language of the 1990s. The statistical study below invites students to examine this evidence carefully.

The unequal development of Chiapas had political as well as economic dimensions. Municipal governments often depended on state and federal transfers, local leaders were connected to the PRI machinery, and Indigenous people were underrepresented in positions of decision-making. Evangelical conversion, religious expulsions in Chamula and other highland communities, and political conflict over who controlled community offices divided Indigenous communities themselves. This internal diversity matters. It means that "the Indigenous communities of Chiapas" did not act with one voice, and that Zapatismo grew within, and sometimes against, other Indigenous institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Ejido and Territorial Rights

The ejido was a form of landholding created by Mexican agrarian reform after the Revolution. The state granted land to a community of petitioners, who as ejidatarios held rights to individual plots (parcelary rights), to common-use lands, and to house lots. Before 1992, ordinary alienation of ejido plots to outsiders was heavily restricted and the state retained supervisory powers; the legal treatment of leases and security interests also differed from the rules created in 1992. A distinct form, the comunidad agraria, recognized the older corporate landholding of Indigenous villages.

The ejido is not the same as territorial rights in the sense Indigenous movements later used, namely collective claims over land, natural resources, and habitat as a basis of cultural survival and self-government. The ejido was a state-granted legal form, while territory was claimed as a historic Indigenous right. The difference matters in §96.10 and §96.11, where the legal language of land became a central dispute.

Section 96.2

Indigenous Organization, Catholic Networks, and Agrarian Mobilization

The Catholic Church in the Diocese of San Cristóbal de las Casas changed profoundly under Bishop Samuel Ruiz García, who took office in 1960. Influenced by the Second Vatican Council (1962 to 1965) and the Latin American bishops' conferences at Medellín (1968) and Puebla (1979), discussed in Chapter 88, Ruiz and his pastoral teams moved from conventional evangelization toward what they called an incarnated, Indigenous-language church. They learned Tseltal, Tsotsil, Ch'ol, and Tojolabal, trained lay catechists and tuhuneles (service officers) from the communities, and encouraged communities to read scripture in light of their own situation. This pastoral approach, informed by liberation theology, did not simply instruct communities to rebel. It created networks of trained community leaders with experience of public speaking, collective decision-making, and organization.

The key event was the Indigenous Congress held in San Cristóbal de las Casas in October 1974, convened at the request of the state governor to mark the five hundredth anniversary of the birth of Bartolomé de las Casas. Church workers organized preparatory community meetings, and more than a thousand Indigenous delegates from Tseltal, Tsotsil, Ch'ol, and Tojolabal communities attended. Instead of hearing speeches, delegates discussed land, commerce, education, and health, and their resolutions denounced exploitation and called for change. The Congress is widely seen as a landmark: for many participants it was the first time they had addressed Indigenous peoples of other language groups on shared problems, and it generated lasting networks.

The 1970s and 1980s saw a proliferation of organizations. Independent peasant groups such as CIOAC (Independent Confederation of Agricultural Workers and Peasants), the Emiliano Zapata Peasant Organization (OCEZ), and regional cooperative and credit unions emerged. In 1988, the Union of Unions (Unión de Uniones) and later the Independent Regional Peasant Association (ARIC), formed from cooperatives, sought to bypass intermediaries in coffee and credit. Land occupations became widespread, and landowners and local authorities often responded with eviction, arrest, or violence, and in some cases with private armed groups sometimes called guardias blancas (white guards). In 1992, delegations of Chol people from the northern zone, the Xi'Nich march, traveled to Mexico City to press land claims, an example of a regional movement using national media.

These organizations disagreed. Some believed in negotiating with the government and using legal procedure, others in direct action, and others in building cooperatives. Some were affiliated with political parties, and rivalries over leadership, resources, and strategy were intense. Revolutionary organizations, Marxist-inspired groups of Mexican origin among them, entered communities as well, sometimes cooperating with the pastoral networks and sometimes competing. The diocese itself was divided, with some clergy and lay Catholics criticizing Ruiz and the church's involvement in politics. After the uprising, Ruiz would be accused by opponents of fomenting rebellion, a charge he rejected, stating that he supported peaceful change and mediation. Historians of the region generally find that the church's networks provided organizational capacity and a vocabulary of dignity and justice, but that the decision to take up arms was taken by others.

Women's participation in this phase is documented only unevenly. Women were catechists and cooperative members, participated in community assemblies, and ran household economies under conditions of scarcity. Women's cooperatives in weaving and bakeries emerged in the 1980s, and some women gained skills in Spanish and organization. These developments created a base for the later demands of the Revolutionary Women's Law (§96.13). However, community decision-making remained predominantly male, and women's access to land and office was limited by custom and by the structure of ejido membership.

The evidence for this period includes church records and pastoral publications, the proceedings of the 1974 Congress, peasant organizations' documents, newspaper reports, government agrarian files, and ethnographic and oral-history interviews. The pastoral sources reflect the church's perspective, the agrarian files reflect the state's, and most surviving testimonies were recorded after 1994, when memory was shaped by the uprising. Students should read each with attention to the moment it was produced.

Section 96.3

The Formation of the EZLN, 1983–1993

On November 17, 1983, a small group established the Zapatista Army of National Liberation in the Lacandon Jungle. The founders belonged to the National Liberation Forces (Fuerzas de Liberación Nacional, FLN), a clandestine revolutionary organization that had formed in northern Mexico in 1969 and drew on Marxist-Leninist and Latin American guerrilla traditions, including the influence of the Cuban Revolution described in Chapters 80 to 82. The EZLN's own accounts, and scholars' interviews with participants, describe a small initial group of about half a dozen, some Indigenous and some mestizo, who entered the jungle with the aim of preparing a revolutionary insurgency. The early years were marked by isolation, hardship, and limited contact with local communities.

The movement's character changed through contact with the communities it entered. According to the EZLN's own later accounts, the guerrillas' initial vanguardist ideas encountered the political culture of Indigenous settlements, with their assemblies, collective work, concept of community service, and distrust of outsiders. Over the following years, the group learned local languages, shared communal work, and gradually recruited members, including young Tseltal, Tsotsil, Ch'ol, and Tojolabal men and women. The Indigenous composition of the organization grew until Indigenous members formed the large majority and its political leadership. In 1993, the movement created the Clandestine Revolutionary Indigenous Committee–General Command (CCRI-CG), composed of Indigenous representatives who, according to the EZLN, voted in community consultations in early 1993 to authorize a declaration of war. This internal decision-making is known principally through the movement's own testimony and should be treated as such, though independent scholars have found it broadly consistent with community accounts.

The role of the spokesman who became known as Subcomandante Marcos requires care. Marcos, later revealed by the government to be a former university lecturer, was the movement's best-known public voice from 1994, writing communiqués in a literary style that mixed political analysis, humor, and parable. He described himself as a spokesperson, not a leader, and the Indigenous commanders insisted that command rested with the CCRI. Marcos's prominence nonetheless shaped how the movement was perceived, and some scholars and Indigenous critics argue that international attention to him overshadowed Indigenous voices. Others note that his role as a translator between communities and an outside audience was real. In 2014 the EZLN announced that the persona of "Marcos" would be retired, with the name Subcomandante Galeano adopted, and Subcomandante Moisés became the chief spokesperson. The movement cannot be reduced to one personality.

The relationship between the guerrillas and local organizations was complicated. The EZLN recruited from communities that also belonged to ARIC, church networks, and agrarian groups, and tensions arose, including disputes over whether armed struggle was necessary or wise. Some communities and organizations broke with the movement or opposed it, and the relationship between those who joined the EZLN and those who chose other paths would later be a source of division, including violent division, in several regions. The expansion of the EZLN's clandestine network in 1992 and 1993, as land reform closed and repression of independent organizations continued, accelerated recruitment. A premature clash with the army in May 1993, in which soldiers found an EZLN camp, alerted the military to a guerrilla presence, though the army and government apparently underestimated its scale.

Scholars debate how to weigh the contributions of its sources. Some stress the external revolutionary origin, others the communities' agency, and most today stress their combination. The EZLN's own historians emphasize that the movement became "Indigenous" by being transformed by the communities, and that its demands came from them. Government and critical accounts in the 1990s sometimes emphasized outside manipulation. Because the early years rest largely on movement testimony, interviews, and later scholarship, students should treat claims about internal decision-making and numbers with appropriate caution, and note that clandestine organizations leave few documents.

Section 96.4

Neoliberal Reform, Article 27, NAFTA, and the Road to Rebellion

Chapter 95 explained the economic logic of Salinas's reforms. This section examines their political meaning for rural Indigenous communities. In November 1991, President Salinas proposed amending Article 27, and the constitutional amendment was published on January 6, 1992; its implementing Agrarian Law followed on February 26, 1992. The changes ended the state's general constitutional obligation to distribute land and recognized the legal personality of ejidos. The implementing Agrarian Law of February 26, 1992 distinguished leases and other use agreements (Article 79), transfers of parcelary rights under specified conditions (Article 80), assembly-authorized conversion of parcels to full private title (Articles 81–83), and security interests in usufruct rights (Article 46). These were not blanket permissions to sell or mortgage all ejido land; common-use land, in particular, retained different protections and legal rules. A federal program (PROCEDE) was created to survey and certify ejido plots. The government argued that these changes would end legal uncertainty, attract investment, and give peasants property rights. Critics argued that they would permit concentration of land and weaken communal tenure.

For communities in Chiapas, the political meaning was immediate. Many had pending land petitions, some for decades, and for them the end of redistribution meant that the legal route to land was closed. Those who had hoped that organization and patience would bring resolution now faced a state that said no further land would be distributed. The reform did not itself take land from anyone, and its actual effects on land sales were more limited than critics had predicted. But its symbolic and procedural meaning was significant: the Revolution's promise of land was officially ended just as the Revolution's seventy-fifth anniversary approached. The EZLN later cited the reform as one of the reasons for taking up arms, calling it a death sentence for the peasantry.

Other economic changes mattered. The international coffee agreement collapsed in 1989, and prices fell sharply, hitting small producers in Chiapas who had relied on coffee. The state agency for coffee marketing was dismantled, subsidies and credit declined, and the retreat of the state left many households exposed. NAFTA, negotiated between 1990 and 1992 and signed in December 1992, raised fears that cheap imported maize from the United States would undercut small farmers. These fears were widely shared in rural Mexico and have been debated by economists, with research since the 1990s finding that effects varied by region and crop and that other factors, including migration and government programs, also mattered. What is clear is that in 1993 many rural communities expected harm.

Political repression and election politics added to the pressure. Chiapas's governors in the early 1990s were criticized for their treatment of independent organizations, and local elections were regularly contested. The government had launched the National Solidarity Program (PRONASOL) in December 1988, soon after Salinas took office; the program continued distributing anti-poverty funds to communities in the early 1990s, in part to build support for the reforms. Critics noted that its distribution could be used to reward loyal groups. Meanwhile, Indigenous organizations that sought legal recourse found it blocked, and some leaders concluded that peaceful means had been exhausted. Other leaders drew the opposite conclusion and continued to organize peacefully, and their choice should not be forgotten.

In sum, the reforms of 1992 and the approach of NAFTA were accelerants, not first causes. The EZLN had been organizing for nine years, and its decision to rise in 1994 reflected an internal timeline as well as external events. The movement's choice of January 1 was symbolic: it declared that NAFTA was a death sentence for Indigenous peoples. Historians therefore describe the uprising as the result of three converging processes: long-term exclusion from land and political power, a decade of organizing that produced both peaceful and armed movements, and a moment of neoliberal reform that closed hopes for redistribution and gave the rebels a timely stage.

Section 96.5

January 1, 1994: The Zapatista Uprising

In the early morning of January 1, 1994, EZLN forces moved simultaneously on several towns of eastern and highland Chiapas. They occupied the municipal seats of San Cristóbal de las Casas, Ocosingo, Las Margaritas, Altamirano, and, according to most accounts, Oxchuc, Huixtán, and Chanal. In San Cristóbal, masked fighters took the municipal palace, from whose balcony a spokesperson read the movement's declaration to townspeople and tourists, and they left the city within a day or two. Other units attacked the federal army's barracks at Rancho Nuevo, near San Cristóbal, with limited success. The rebels also freed prisoners in some towns and took documents from municipal offices. They distributed copies of a declaration of war, and the movement's newspaper circulated the same day.

The government's response combined military force and political hesitation. Army units moved into the region, and in the first days there was heavy fighting in several places. The most violent confrontation took place in Ocosingo, where rebels occupied the town and army troops arrived on January 2 and 3, with fighting in the market and surrounding streets. Human-rights organizations later documented the deaths of civilians and the summary execution of captured combatants in Ocosingo, and the army also used aircraft and artillery against rebel positions in the hills outside San Cristóbal in the first week. The EZLN withdrew to the jungle. These events produced significant media coverage in Mexico and abroad, including photographs of dead combatants, and public outcry.

Casualty figures remain uncertain and contested. Mexico's National Human Rights Commission and other bodies reported fatalities in the low hundreds, a commonly cited figure being around 145, and other sources, including some human-rights groups and later estimates, suggest higher totals when the missing and unrecorded dead are included. The dead included rebels, soldiers, police, and civilians, and the numbers in each category are disputed. Students should treat any single figure as an estimate, note who produced it, and distinguish documented deaths from claims. Some killings, such as the execution of captured individuals, were subsequently investigated by authorities, though with limited accountability.

The coincidence with NAFTA was deliberate, and the EZLN framed the uprising as a response to an agreement it said condemned Indigenous peoples. The government of Salinas, which had hoped to present Mexico as a stable emerging market on January 1, found its image shaken. Initial official statements sought to minimize the uprising or attribute it to outside agitators, including foreign guerrillas, and the press debated the rebels' composition. Investigators and journalists soon established that most combatants were local Indigenous people, and that their leadership included Indigenous commanders. The government's early narrative lost credibility, though some officials continued to emphasize the role of non-Indigenous organizers.

The uprising's limits were as important as its drama. The EZLN did not hold the towns it entered, did not defeat the army, and did not spark the broader national insurrection that its declaration invoked. Its fighters were few relative to the army's resources, and its weaponry was limited. Yet its declaration, its masked spokesperson, and its Indigenous composition proved politically potent. Many Mexicans who rejected armed rebellion nonetheless found its grievances persuasive, and public opinion demanded a halt to the fighting and negotiation. Within days, the question was no longer whether the rebels could win but how the state would respond to a movement that had made visible conditions many Mexicans had ignored.

Section 96.6

The First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle

The declaration read in San Cristóbal and distributed on January 1 had been printed in the EZLN's newspaper, El Despertador Mexicano, dated December 1993. Signed by the CCRI-CG, it was the movement's founding public text. It opened with the words that became the uprising's slogan, "Today we say enough" (¡Hoy decimos ¡Basta!), and it spoke in the first person plural of a people that, it said, had been denied everything for five hundred years: a reference that linked the uprising to Indigenous resistance since conquest (Chapters 33 and 47) and to the quincentenary of 1992.

The text states its demands in a list that has become one of the best-known statements of Indigenous Mexican grievance: work, land, housing, food, health, education, independence, freedom, democracy, justice, and peace. This list combined material necessities with political rights, and it did not mention Indigenous autonomy as such. That omission is significant: the demand for autonomy and collective rights emerged more clearly after 1994, through dialogue with other Indigenous organizations. In January 1994, the EZLN's language was national, revolutionary, and social rather than specifically ethnic, though the text's authors identified themselves as the product of a long history of Indigenous suffering.

The declaration's invocation of the Mexican Revolution and of Zapata was deliberate. It presented the EZLN as heir to the revolutionaries of 1910, as a continuation of an unfinished revolution betrayed by a dictatorial regime. It appealed to the Constitution, citing the article that holds that national sovereignty resides essentially and originally in the people and that the people have the inalienable right to alter or modify their form of government. In this way, the document justified rebellion in terms of Mexico's own constitutional tradition. It also ordered its forces to advance toward the capital, defeating the federal army, and to respect civilian populations and prisoners, appealing to the laws of war and to the Geneva Conventions.

Reading the declaration requires attention to audience. It addressed the Mexican people as a whole, calling on the other branches of government and on citizens to support the rebellion, and it was written in a register of revolutionary proclamation common to Latin American insurgencies. It also addressed international observers and the army. It was not a legal program: it did not specify institutions or policies. Later communiqués, some in a notably different literary and ironic tone, elaborated a more complex political language that included the critique of neoliberalism, the defense of Indigenous dignity, and the claim that the movement did not seek to seize power.

Historians read the document as evidence of how the movement chose to present itself at the moment of public emergence. It does not tell us how many Indigenous communities supported the war, how the decision was reached, or what ordinary members expected. Within weeks, the EZLN's emphasis shifted. It declared that it was ready for dialogue, abandoned the call for an advance on the capital, and increasingly stressed Indigenous rights and democratization. This shift from armed demands to political demands is a central theme of the chapter.

Section 96.7

The Twelve Days, the Ceasefire, and the Turn toward Dialogue

The fighting lasted twelve days, from January 1 to January 12, 1994, though it was intense mainly in the first four or five. As the army regained control of towns and began operations in the Lacandon region, television and press images, together with human-rights reports, galvanized public opinion. Civil organizations, students, church groups, and intellectuals in Mexico City and other cities held demonstrations demanding an end to hostilities. Many of those marching did not support armed struggle and did not necessarily agree with the EZLN, but they opposed the army's use of force against an Indigenous population and supported negotiation. This civilian antiwar mobilization was one of the most important elements of the crisis.

The government faced pressure at home and abroad. Salinas had staked his presidency on modernization and on NAFTA, and the image of an army bombing Indigenous communities clashed with that narrative. Foreign investors and governments, human-rights groups, and the press watched closely. In early January, Salinas replaced the interior secretary and appointed Manuel Camacho Solís, a prominent politician who had been Mexico City's mayor, as Commissioner for Peace and Reconciliation in Chiapas. On January 12, the president announced a unilateral ceasefire and offered an amnesty and negotiation, and the EZLN soon declared its own willingness to cease offensive operations. A federal amnesty law followed later in January.

A declared ceasefire is not a settlement. The ceasefire halted large-scale combat but did not resolve the conflict, and the army remained deployed in the region, the EZLN retained its arms, and its relations with local authorities and landowners remained tense. Throughout 1994 and beyond, there were violations, accusations, and incidents. The ceasefire created a space in which dialogue could begin, but whether that dialogue would yield agreement, and whether any agreement would be implemented, remained uncertain. Distinguishing the ceasefire from peace is essential to understanding the later crises.

Why did the government choose negotiation? Historians offer several explanations. The uprising's popular sympathy and the antiwar protests made a continued military offensive politically costly, especially in an election year, since presidential elections were scheduled for August 1994. International attention mattered, and the government wished to protect NAFTA and its image. The army, too, faced the difficulty of conducting counterinsurgency against an elusive force in difficult terrain, and some officers preferred to avoid a prolonged conflict. Finally, the government hoped that negotiation would divide the rebels from their supporters and bring them into institutional politics. These motives could coexist.

The EZLN, for its part, made a strategic choice. It recognized that it could not defeat the army and that the wider public sympathy it had earned was its greatest asset, and it chose to use negotiation to publicize its demands and to challenge the legitimacy of the government. The ceasefire thus began a new phase in which the movement's political capital depended on remaining visible, on retaining the support of civil society, and on avoiding the isolation that had defeated many Latin American guerrilla movements (Chapters 82 and 91). It was this shift, rather than military success, that gave the Zapatistas lasting significance.

Section 96.8

A National Debate: The Government, Opposition, Churches, and Civil Society

Dialogues between the government and the EZLN opened in the cathedral of San Cristóbal de las Casas in late February 1994, with Bishop Samuel Ruiz serving as mediator and Camacho Solís representing the government. The setting was symbolic: the church, not a government building, hosted the talks, and EZLN commanders emerged from the jungle to attend while hundreds of journalists and observers watched. The government presented a written response to the EZLN's demands, offering commitments on services, development, and some political reforms. The EZLN, in turn, said it would consult its communities before replying.

Political events intervened. On March 23, 1994, Luis Donaldo Colosio, the PRI's presidential candidate, was assassinated in Tijuana, deepening a crisis of confidence in the political system. Camacho Solís, who had hoped to be a candidate himself, resigned as commissioner in June. In the middle of these events, the EZLN consulted its communities, and, on June 10, it published the Second Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, rejecting the government's offer. The reasons it cited included that the offer did not address national democratization and that it was not accompanied by changes in the political system. Instead, the Second Declaration called on Mexican civil society to form a national movement for democracy, and it led to the National Democratic Convention, convened in August 1994 in a jungle site that the EZLN named Aguascalientes, after the convention of revolutionaries in 1914.

The presidential election of August 21, 1994 took place in this atmosphere. The PRI's Ernesto Zedillo won, after a campaign in which the uprising, the assassination, and demands for electoral reform were prominent themes (Chapter 94). In Chiapas, the contested election for state governor, in which opposition candidate Amado Avendaño claimed fraud, produced a parallel "rebel government" and further division. The EZLN did not endorse any party and did not participate in the election, though many supporters voted. At the end of 1994, as the peso crisis shook Mexico's economy (Chapter 93), the EZLN's announcement on December 19 of its extension into dozens of municipalities, which it declared autonomous, again altered the situation.

Competing public understandings of the uprising were sharply divided. To many Mexicans on the left, as well as to church activists, the rebellion was a legitimate if desperate expression of Indigenous marginalization. To government officials and many conservatives, it was a subversive threat, possibly manipulated by outsiders. Some intellectuals described it as the first "postmodern" or post-Cold War guerrilla movement, noting its use of media and its refusal to seize power, while others criticized it for militarizing Indigenous communities. Many Indigenous people outside the EZLN welcomed the uprising's attention to Indigenous issues while rejecting its methods, and others resented its claim to speak for them. The Catholic Church was itself divided, with Ruiz as mediator under attack from critics who blamed him for the conflict.

Why did the rebellion's political significance exceed the size of its armed forces? Scholars point to several factors. The uprising occurred when Mexico's political system was changing and the government's legitimacy was questioned, so it resonated with demands for democratization. It dramatized the contradictions of an economic model that promised modernization while excluding many rural communities. Its Indigenous composition, its message, and its communication, including the movement's rapid use of faxes, press releases, and later the Internet, reached audiences beyond Mexico. Finally, its willingness to negotiate and to appeal to civil society made it a political actor and not just a military one. Those qualities explain how a movement of a few thousand armed members altered national politics.

Section 96.9

Renewed Confrontation, the 1995 Crisis, and the Search for Peace

Ernesto Zedillo took office on December 1, 1994, and within weeks faced the peso crisis and a deteriorating situation in Chiapas. The EZLN's December moves, including its declaration of autonomous municipalities and its challenge to the state government, alarmed officials. On February 9, 1995, Zedillo announced that the government had identified the leadership of the EZLN, issued arrest warrants, and ordered the army to move into rebel areas. The government named the man it said was Subcomandante Marcos, a former university teacher, a revelation intended to portray the movement as the project of urban outsiders. The army moved into positions including Guadalupe Tepeyac, the site of the Aguascalientes meeting center, and rebels withdrew deeper into the jungle without major fighting.

The offensive provoked a political backlash. Protests took place in Mexico City and elsewhere, and legislators, church leaders, and civil organizations urged the government to return to dialogue. Within weeks, Zedillo backed down from the arrest campaign. The Congress passed, and on March 11, 1995 the president promulgated, the Law for Dialogue, Reconciliation and a Dignified Peace in Chiapas. It provided a legal framework for negotiation, suspended arrest warrants against EZLN members who took part in dialogue, and created a Commission of Concord and Pacification (COCOPA) composed of legislators from the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate and a representative of the Chiapas state legislature. Alongside COCOPA, the EZLN and the government accepted the National Intermediation Commission (CONAI), headed by Bishop Ruiz, as mediator.

These institutions are important because they show how the conflict became institutionalized. COCOPA represented the legislative branch and parties, giving the process a link to Congress, while CONAI represented civil society and the church. The government named its own delegation, and the EZLN named a delegation of commanders and advisers. Negotiations moved from the cathedral to San Andrés Larráinzar (Sakamch'en de los Pobres), a Tsotsil highland municipality, where, from October 1995, the parties met in a series of working tables. The first table concerned Indigenous rights and culture, and later tables were planned on democracy and justice, welfare and development, and women's rights. The EZLN invited numerous Indigenous organizations and advisers from across Mexico to take part in the tables, expanding the dialogue beyond Chiapas.

Military strategy, state institutions, and local dynamics should be distinguished. At the national level, the government pursued a dual strategy of dialogue and containment, maintaining a large military presence in Chiapas, building roads and camps, and expanding its security forces. At the state level, successive governors and local officials had their own interests, and relations with landowners, local party structures, and rival Indigenous organizations shaped violence. At the local level, conflicts among communities, between supporters and opponents of the EZLN, and over land, religion, and local power escalated, in some areas leading to armed groups and displacement. These conflicts were not simply orchestrated from above or from the EZLN's command, although the question of the extent to which the state tolerated or encouraged certain groups is a matter of debate (§96.12).

By the end of 1995, the dialogue had produced a framework but little concrete resolution, and each side questioned the other's good faith. The EZLN complained that the army remained deployed and that harassment continued. The government complained that the EZLN refused to disarm or to participate in elections. This distrust shaped the entire peace process. It also meant that the first substantive agreement, on Indigenous rights, would be reached amid continuing militarization.

Section 96.10

The San Andrés Accords of February 1996

On February 16, 1996, the EZLN and the federal government signed, at San Andrés Larráinzar, the agreements on the first working table, Indigenous Rights and Culture. They are often called the San Andrés Accords. The text consisted of several documents: a joint declaration setting out principles; proposals the parties agreed to send to national decision-making bodies; and commitments by the federal and state governments to Indigenous peoples. The agreements were negotiated with the participation of advisers and guests from Indigenous organizations throughout Mexico, including participants who were not members of the EZLN, a feature that made the accords a national, not only a Zapatista, Indigenous-rights document.

The parties acknowledged a basic principle: that Indigenous peoples have a right to self-determination, exercised within the framework of national unity. The text affirmed that Indigenous peoples should be able to decide their own forms of internal social, cultural, political, and economic organization, to apply their own normative systems in regulating internal conflicts, to elect their authorities according to their own traditions, to preserve their languages and cultures, and to have access to the use and enjoyment of natural resources in their lands, subject to constitutional limits. It also called for greater political representation, participation in national and state institutions, and recognition of Indigenous communities as entities of public law. These ideas drew on international instruments, notably ILO Convention 169, which Mexico had ratified in 1990.

The accords distinguished what the parties agreed to do from what required legislative action. The federal government committed to promote reforms to the Constitution and to laws recognizing Indigenous rights, to consult Indigenous peoples on development, to promote Indigenous education and media, and to take steps against discrimination. Because constitutional changes require action by Congress and the state legislatures, the accords themselves were not law. They were a negotiated foundation, and the central question was whether Congress would translate them into constitutional text. For the EZLN, the accords were a first step and a test of the government's good faith. For the government, they were a concession that awaited legislative handling.

Several matters remained unresolved. The accords left open how autonomy would be defined in practice, at what level of government, with what territorial extent, and with what relation to municipalities and states. They did not settle questions of land and territory, the control of natural resources, or the status of Indigenous collective rights as against individual rights and the national legal order. The other tables, on democracy and justice, welfare and development, and women's rights, did not reach agreement, and in September 1996 the EZLN suspended its participation, citing the government's failure to comply with the first accord, the continued militarization of the region, and the detention of alleged Zapatistas. The consequence was that the only completed accord became the focus of the entire constitutional struggle.

Section 96.11

The COCOPA Proposal, the 2001 Reform, and Constitutional Dispute

After the accords, COCOPA drafted a constitutional reform proposal translating the San Andrés texts into amendments. The EZLN accepted the COCOPA draft, presented at the end of November 1996, as faithful to the accords. President Zedillo, however, objected, and in December 1996 and January 1997 his government sent back observations and later a counterproposal, arguing that parts of the draft would create separate legal orders, fragment municipalities, and threaten national unity. The EZLN and its allies replied that the government's version reversed what had been agreed. From 1997, the peace process was effectively stalled, and the EZLN refused further dialogue until the COCOPA text was enacted. The National Indigenous Congress, founded in October 1996 with the EZLN's participation, became a national forum for Indigenous organizations defending the COCOPA proposal.

The election of Vicente Fox of the National Action Party (PAN) in July 2000 ended seven decades of PRI presidential rule (Chapter 94) and revived hopes. Fox had promised to resolve the conflict quickly, and on taking office on December 1, 2000, he sent the COCOPA proposal to the Senate. The EZLN responded with conditions: the enactment of the COCOPA text, the release of imprisoned Zapatistas, and the withdrawal of the army from several positions in Chiapas. When the government made partial concessions, the EZLN announced a march to Mexico City to press Congress to approve the reform.

The march, known as the March of the Color of the Earth or the Zapatour, left Chiapas in late February 2001, with a delegation of commanders, Subcomandante Marcos, and a large group of Indigenous delegates, and traveled through a dozen states, holding rallies in Indigenous communities and cities, and attracting heavy press attention. The delegation reached Mexico City on March 11, where it was greeted by a very large crowd in the central plaza. The caravan's route deliberately visited communities of the National Indigenous Congress, giving the march a pan-Indigenous character. On March 28, 2001, after a contentious debate in which some legislators sought to bar the EZLN from the Chamber of Deputies, Comandanta Esther, a Tsotsil woman, addressed the Congress on behalf of the CCRI, speaking in a tribune that no Indigenous woman had occupied in this way before.

Congress did not enact the COCOPA proposal. Instead, in April 2001, the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies approved a modified text, and after approval by a majority of state legislatures it was declared in force in August 2001, amending Article 2 of the Constitution and other provisions. The enacted reform recognized Mexico as a pluricultural nation based originally on its Indigenous peoples, affirmed the right of Indigenous peoples to self-determination and autonomy within the constitutional framework, and listed rights regarding internal social organization, normative systems, language, and the selection of authorities by traditional procedures, subject to constitutional guarantees and respect for human rights and women's participation.

But the differences from the COCOPA proposal were substantial. The enacted text described Indigenous communities as "entities of public interest" rather than entities of public law, which limited their legal standing. It left the definition of autonomy and its practical details to state legislatures, rather than establishing a national framework. It restricted collective access to natural resources and land to what the Constitution and existing laws allowed, narrowing the idea of territory. And it did not provide for the association of municipalities or for the strong political representation envisioned in the accords. The EZLN, the National Indigenous Congress, and numerous Indigenous organizations rejected the reform as a betrayal of the San Andrés Accords, and the EZLN broke contact with the government. Over three hundred municipalities, many of them Indigenous, filed constitutional controversies with the Supreme Court, which in September 2002 declined to rule on the merits, finding that it could not review the amendment procedure.

Defenders of the reform argued that it recognized Indigenous rights for the first time in the Constitution, left room for states to expand them, and sought to preserve national unity and individual rights. Critics replied that it made recognition largely symbolic and that its dependence on state legislation allowed unequal and weak implementation. Some Indigenous leaders accepted the reform as a starting point for litigation, and others rejected it. Subsequent studies of its implementation, including by Indigenous organizations and legal scholars, found uneven state-level legislation and limited effect on community autonomy. The dispute made clear that the Mexican state's legal framework would shape any autonomy: the EZLN responded by building autonomy outside it (§96.14).

Section 96.12

Acteal, Paramilitary Violence, Displacement, and Human Rights

While the dialogue stalled, violence grew in several parts of Chiapas, particularly the northern zone and the highlands. Armed groups, often described as paramilitary because they operated outside formal state structures while allegedly maintaining relations with officials and security forces, emerged in several municipalities. Some, like "Paz y Justicia" in the northern zone and later groups in Chenalhó, claimed to defend communities against the EZLN or to defend ruling-party interests. The roots of the violence lay in disputes over land, local power, party loyalty, religion, and the presence of the army and of Zapatista support bases in the same communities. Thousands of people were displaced from their homes, living in refugee camps or with relatives, and entire communities were divided.

The most notorious episode occurred on December 22, 1997, in Acteal, a community in the municipality of Chenalhó, in the Highlands. Acteal housed displaced families, many belonging to Las Abejas (The Bees), a Tsotsil civil-society organization founded in 1992 that espoused nonviolence and had sympathy with some Zapatista demands but did not belong to the EZLN. On that day, as members gathered in a chapel to pray and fast, armed men attacked them for several hours. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights identified 45 people killed, most of them women and children. The attackers were identified in subsequent investigations as members of a local armed group linked to supporters of the ruling party.

Responsibility has remained disputed. The government prosecuted and convicted dozens of men, and officials presented the massacre as the product of local intercommunity conflict. Human-rights organizations, Las Abejas, journalists, and later scholars argued that state officials and security forces had tolerated or facilitated armed groups, that police were nearby and did not intervene, and that investigations were flawed. The state governor resigned in early 1998, as did the federal interior secretary. In 2009, Mexico's Supreme Court ordered the release of a number of men convicted, citing violations of due process in their trials, and subsequent rulings freed more. Survivors and human-rights groups protested that the releases did not end the question of who was responsible. This history shows the difficulty of reaching a settled judicial account.

It is vital to keep the groups apart. The victims at Acteal were members of Las Abejas, a pacifist organization, not EZLN combatants or Zapatista support bases. The EZLN was a separate organization, and the attackers were local armed men. The conflicts in Chenalhó and nearby municipalities pitted neighbors and kin against one another across party, religious, and organizational lines. To describe the violence as a single war between "the army and the Zapatistas" erases these local divisions, and to blame the EZLN, as some officials did, ignores the evidence. Human-rights investigators from Mexico and abroad, including church-based and independent bodies, documented the displacement and killing, and their findings often differed from official accounts.

The impact on peace prospects was grave. Acteal intensified international criticism of Mexico, brought observers to Chiapas, and led to a further militarization, as the government expanded army positions and its campaign against autonomous municipalities, including the dismantling of some in 1998. The EZLN denounced the massacre as the result of state policy of low-intensity warfare, and the government sought to isolate the movement. The peace process, already stalled over the COCOPA text, did not resume until after the 2000 election. For many Indigenous communities, the experience of Acteal confirmed that rights recognized on paper offered little protection against violence.

Section 96.13

Indigenous Women, the Revolutionary Women's Law, and Political Agency

Women were present in the EZLN from its early years and formed a substantial part of its Indigenous membership, as combatants, commanders, health workers, and support-base participants. Women appeared among the fighters who took San Cristóbal on January 1, 1994, and one of them, Comandanta Ramona, a Tsotsil woman, became an internationally recognized figure. According to EZLN accounts, women commanders and members, among them Susana and Ramona, gathered the demands of women in communities during consultations in 1993, and the result was a text that the movement published in December 1993, the Revolutionary Women's Law. It appeared in the same issue of the EZLN's newspaper that carried the First Declaration, and it is among the most analyzed documents in Zapatista history.

The law's ten provisions addressed political participation, work, reproduction, community office, health, education, marriage, personal safety, military rank, and general rights. They were remarkable for their date and setting: a clandestine army in rural Chiapas, composed overwhelmingly of members of communities with patriarchal customs, declared that women had the right to take part in the struggle at the level their capacity allowed, to work and receive a fair wage, to decide how many children to have and care for, to hold community office, to have priority in health and nutrition, to receive education, to choose their partners and not be forced into marriage, to be free from violence by relatives or strangers, and to hold leadership and military rank. The document also stated that rape and attempted rape would be severely punished.

The relationship between declared equality and everyday practice was complex. Studies by anthropologists and Indigenous women, collected in works such as Dissident Women: Gender and Cultural Politics in Chiapas, describe women who gained new public roles, learned Spanish and organizational skills, and held posts as health promoters, education promoters, council members, and commanders. They also describe persistent obstacles: unequal access to land through ejido membership, the burden of domestic labor, resistance from husbands and community authorities, and violence. Some Indigenous women activists, in Chiapas and elsewhere, argued that "custom" had been used to justify subordination and that Indigenous rights must include women's rights. Others emphasized that Indigenous collective rights and women's rights were not opposed, and that defending Indigenous autonomy did not require accepting every customary practice.

Women's political presence became a distinct dimension of Zapatismo in the late 1990s and 2000s. Comandanta Ramona appeared in Mexico City in October 1996 at the founding of the National Indigenous Congress, despite serious illness, and she died in January 2006. Comandanta Esther's speech to Congress in 2001 was the most visible instance (§96.11). Women's participation in autonomous governments, including as members of Good Government Councils, grew over time, though numbers varied by region and accounts differ. The movement also organized encounters of women, including gatherings that drew Mexican and international participants in the 2010s. Hence the story of Zapatista women is not only one of representation by a few leaders but of changes in communities, whose extent and limits continue to be studied.

Section 96.14

Autonomous Municipalities, Caracoles, and the Good Government Councils

The EZLN began to create autonomous institutions soon after the uprising. In the wake of the 1994 events, communities recovered land from large estates and organized local governments outside official structures. On December 19, 1994, the EZLN announced that it was extending its influence into dozens of municipalities, which it declared autonomous. These "rebel autonomous municipalities" (municipios autónomos rebeldes zapatistas, MAREZ) grouped communities in territories that sometimes overlapped with official municipalities. They created their own councils, courts, and committees for education and health, and did not receive official recognition. The state responded with pressure, and in 1998 the authorities dismantled some of them, though many persisted.

After the failure of constitutional reform in 2001, the EZLN turned inward. In July and August 2003, it announced a reorganization. At a gathering at La Realidad in August 2003, the movement presented the caracoles ("snails" or conch shells), regional centers replacing the earlier meeting places known as Aguascalientes, and the Juntas de Buen Gobierno (Good Government Councils). There were five caracoles, at La Realidad, Oventik, La Garrucha, Morelia, and Roberto Barrios, each with a Council that coordinated several autonomous municipalities. The EZLN described the change as responding to problems of the earlier system: unequal distribution of aid and solidarity resources among regions, conflicts among municipalities and between autonomous and official authorities, and the need to manage the stream of outside visitors and projects.

The Councils were intended to rotate their members, who served without salary, drawn from the autonomous municipalities, and to coordinate projects, mediate disputes, oversee education and health, receive solidarity groups, and monitor outside funding. Members served for short terms, and communities could recall them. The caracoles also became places where visitors registered and learned the rules of Zapatista territory. The movement stressed that the EZLN's military command should not govern civilians: the civilian authorities were autonomous, answerable to communities, and the military structure supported but did not direct them. Observers have questioned how fully this separation held in practice, since many authorities were also members of the EZLN's base and its leaders retained great influence, and the question remains debated.

Historical Vocabulary
Mandar obedeciendo

Mandar obedeciendo ("to lead by obeying" or "governing by obeying") is the Zapatista principle that those who hold authority must follow the will of the community that gave it to them. The movement associated it with a set of principles for civil authorities, including serving rather than being served, representing rather than replacing the community, proposing rather than imposing, and convincing rather than conquering.

The phrase should not be mistaken for a simple description of how every community governed. It is a stated norm, shaped by Indigenous assembly traditions and by the movement's critique of representative politics, and its practice depended on rotating office, unpaid service, and community oversight. It matters because it expresses the movement's alternative to party-based representation, and because later debates ask whether it achieved what it promised.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous Autonomy

Autonomy, in the Indigenous-rights debates of the 1990s, refers to the right of Indigenous peoples to govern internal affairs, apply their own norms, manage land and resources, and preserve their languages and cultures, within or alongside a larger state. It is distinct from independence, which would mean a separate state, and from self-determination, the broader principle of which autonomy is one form. Legal definitions vary from country to country.

In Mexico, the term had at least two meanings. For the San Andrés signers it meant a legal framework to be enacted in the Constitution, applicable to Indigenous communities and municipalities. For the Zapatistas after 2001 it also meant autonomy de facto, built without state recognition. Students should specify which meaning a source uses.

Section 96.15

Governing Everyday Life: Education, Health, Land, and Community Justice

Autonomy was built through everyday institutions. Zapatista education was organized through autonomous schools staffed by community-chosen promoters, who often had limited formal training and received support in the form of food, labor, or modest contributions from the community. The schools used Indigenous languages alongside Spanish, and the curriculum, developed in the movement's education system, stressed local history, agriculture, and community values. Secondary schools were established in the early 2000s, with Oventik serving as a visible center. Supporters have described these schools as a major achievement, and independent studies have noted both the expansion of schooling for children who had had none and challenges, including the lack of official accreditation, the variable preparation of promoters, and limited resources.

Health care followed a similar logic. Community health promoters were trained, often with help from solidarity organizations and volunteer doctors, and autonomous clinics were built in some regions, with a focus on primary care, herbal and traditional medicine, and reproductive health. Women were prominent among promoters. Clinics faced shortages of equipment and medicine, and serious cases required referral to official hospitals, which were distant and sometimes avoided by Zapatista families. Studies of Zapatista health systems describe real improvements in some communities as well as limited capacity for complex care and the dependence on external support.

Land was central. After January 1994, communities occupied land held by large estates and ranches, in some regions redistributing it to families and managing portions collectively. Estimates of the amount recovered vary, and the EZLN and critics give different figures, but scholars agree that tens of thousands of hectares changed hands in the first years. Collective work, trabajo colectivo, included cooperatives for coffee, bees, cattle, crafts, and stores, and communities contributed labor to common projects. The movement refused many government programs, including social-welfare payments, as incompatible with autonomy, and relied on community production, solidarity donations, and the sale of products such as coffee. The economic base remained fragile, as coffee prices were volatile and agricultural land was limited, and young people migrated for wage work in Mexican cities, Cancún, and the United States, a pattern that affected Zapatista communities as well as others.

Community justice relied on assemblies and elected or rotating authorities. Disputes over land, marriage, debt, and offenses were handled by local councils, municipal authorities, and later the Good Government Councils, which aimed to reconcile parties and impose restitution or labor sanctions rather than imprisonment. The movement prohibited alcohol and drug use and the sale of timber and weapons in its territories, rules that expressed both moral and political goals. Observers have noted that these systems offered accessible justice in places where the state was absent, and also that decisions could be shaped by local power, gender hierarchy, and the pressure to conform, and that members who violated community rules or left the movement could face sanctions or conflict.

Constraints were serious. Financial limits shaped what schools and clinics could do. Organizational problems included the burden of unpaid service, which was heavy on families, and the difficulty of coordinating a large and dispersed network. Gender inequalities persisted despite the Women's Law. Security threats from army camps, paramilitary groups, and rival organizations were continual, and conflicts with neighboring non-Zapatista communities over land and resources occasionally turned violent. A well-known example was the killing in May 2014 of a Zapatista teacher, José Luis Solís López, known as "Galeano," at La Realidad, in a dispute with members of another organization, an event that led Subcomandante Marcos to retire his public persona. These facts caution against both idealized and dismissive accounts.

Everyday Life and Social History

Schools, Clinics, Fields, and Assemblies in Zapatista Communities

In a Zapatista community, daily life revolved around maize and bean fields, coffee plots, small livestock, and the collective work assigned by the assembly. Households rotated through service posts. A man or woman chosen as an education promoter might teach children in a shared building while continuing to farm, supported by food from families. A health promoter might walk to homes, deliver babies, and treat common ailments, learning from training courses and from elders who knew medicinal plants. Women prepared food for assemblies and gatherings and, increasingly, took part in meetings and held posts, though the double burden of domestic work and community service was heavy.

Language use was multilayered. Children spoke Tseltal, Tsotsil, Ch'ol, Tojolabal, or another Indigenous language at home and learned Spanish at school, and meetings often required translation between Indigenous languages and Spanish. Local decision-making followed assembly norms in which a matter was discussed until consensus emerged, a slow process that participants prized and critics found burdensome. Meanwhile, families were also affected by migration, remittances, changing agriculture, and the lure of consumer goods. Evidence for this daily life comes from ethnographic fieldwork, testimonies, and movement and solidarity reports, each with limits: visitors saw only parts of community life, and community members spoke to outsiders with caution.

Section 96.16

Zapatismo Goes Global: The Internet, Solidarity, and Anti-Neoliberalism

The Zapatistas' international reach was unusual for a rural insurgency. Almost from the first days, the movement communicated through communiqués sent to Mexican newspapers, notably La Jornada, and copied and circulated by activists via fax and the Internet, then a new medium. Scholars such as Harry Cleaver wrote of a "Zapatista effect" in which electronic networks carried news faster than governments could control it. The importance of the Internet should be kept in proportion: most Zapatista communities had no access to it, and the main communicative effect was to allow supporters in Mexico City, the United States, Europe, and elsewhere to receive and amplify the movement's texts. The movement's communiqués, in a literary and ironic voice, appealed to an audience beyond Mexico and framed local struggles as part of a global one.

Solidarity organizations formed in many countries, including committees in the United States, Italy, Spain, France, and elsewhere. They raised funds, sent delegations and volunteers, and organized "peace camps" and civil observation in Chiapas, where international presence was meant to deter violence. Human-rights organizations documented abuses and pressed governments. Alternative media, including independent radio and later online outlets, carried Zapatista statements. These networks had their own motives, and their interpretations of Zapatismo varied: some saw it as an Indigenous movement, others as a model of anti-capitalist or anarchist politics, others as a postmodern or post-Marxist form of resistance. The movement's own statements stressed Maya roots, community assemblies, and the rejection of seizing state power, and students should keep the movement's ideas separate from supporters' readings.

A landmark event was the Intercontinental Encounter for Humanity and Against Neoliberalism, held in Chiapas from late July to early August 1996. The EZLN invited activists, intellectuals, and organizations from around the world, and several thousand people from dozens of countries attended meetings in the five Aguascalientes sites. A second encounter followed in Spain in 1997. The declarations of the 1996 encounter, including the movement's call for a "world in which many worlds fit," became slogans of the emerging alter-globalization movement. Networks such as Peoples' Global Action, founded in 1998, and the large protests against the World Trade Organization in Seattle in 1999 drew on the movement's language and example, though participants in those protests came from many traditions and cannot be reduced to Zapatista influence.

Within Mexico, the EZLN also used consultations to build civil-society ties. In March 1999, it organized a national consultation on Indigenous rights in which thousands of Zapatista delegates traveled to communities across the country, and organizers reported participation in the low millions; the numbers rest on the organizers' own tallies and should be treated as such. These efforts expanded the movement's reach but also strained its resources and revealed disagreements with other organizations.

Assessing influence requires care. Scholars agree that Zapatismo helped shape the language and tactics of anti-neoliberal activism, strengthened transnational Indigenous-rights networks, and offered a model of using communication to counter state power. They also note that international attention could be fleeting, that solidarity depended on the movement's symbolic appeal, and that the relationship between external supporters and Indigenous communities created tensions, including questions about who spoke for whom. The movement's global profile, in sum, was real but must be separated from its local organizational base.

Section 96.17

Indigenous Resurgence Beyond Chiapas: Ecuador, Bolivia, and Guatemala

The Zapatista uprising occurred amid a hemisphere-wide revival of Indigenous politics. Indigenous peoples had always resisted and negotiated (Chapters 47 and 49), but in the late twentieth century, democratization, the retreat of corporatist states, neoliberal reforms, international legal change, and the 1992 quincentenary created conditions for new national and transnational organizations. Many Latin American constitutions were rewritten in the 1990s to recognize multiethnic or pluricultural nations, including those of Colombia (1991), Mexico (an amendment in 1992), Peru (1993), Bolivia (1994), Ecuador (1998), and Venezuela (1999). The International Labour Organization's Convention 169 (1989) became a key reference. These movements were not directed by the EZLN, and most had their own histories, but they shared concerns with land, recognition, language, and self-determination.

Ecuador. The Confederation of Indigenous Nationalities of Ecuador (CONAIE) was founded in 1986 by uniting highland Kichwa organizations and Amazonian federations. In June 1990, it led a national uprising, el levantamiento, that paralyzed the country with road blockades and marches for several days, demanding land, bilingual education, and recognition of Indigenous nationalities. It was nonviolent and drew on the organized strength of highland communities and Amazonian federations, and it established CONAIE as a national political actor. In 1992, Amazonian Indigenous people marched to Quito demanding land titles. CONAIE and its allies took part in the political crises of the late 1990s, helped bring down President Jamil Mahuad in January 2000 alongside military officers, and entered electoral politics through the Pachakutik movement, founded in the mid-1990s. The Ecuadorian pattern combined mass mobilization with electoral participation (Chapter 108 takes the story forward).

Bolivia. In August 1990, lowland Indigenous peoples of the Beni and neighboring regions, including Moxeño, Chiquitano, Yuracaré, and Chimane communities, began the March for Territory and Dignity, walking from Trinidad to La Paz, a journey of hundreds of kilometers into the Andean highlands. Organized by the Confederation of Indigenous Peoples of the Bolivian East (CIDOB) and supported by highland organizations and church and civic groups, the march won decrees recognizing several Indigenous territories and put lowland demands on the national agenda. Highland Bolivia already had a rich tradition of Aymara and Quechua organizing, including the Katarista movement. A constitutional reform in 1994 recognized Bolivia as multiethnic and pluricultural, and agrarian legislation in 1996 created a category of Original Community Lands (TCOs). Later mobilizations, including the water and gas conflicts of 2000 and 2003, and the rise of Evo Morales, are examined in Chapter 108.

Guatemala. Guatemala's Maya population had suffered massacres and displacement during the civil war (Chapter 91), and the report of the Historical Clarification Commission in 1999 attributed acts of genocide to the state in several regions. Alongside this, a Maya rights movement grew from the 1980s, with organizations devoted to language, education, spirituality, and law, and with leaders such as Rigoberta Menchú. In the peace negotiations between the government and the URNG guerrillas, the Agreement on Identity and Rights of Indigenous Peoples was signed on March 31, 1995. It recognized Guatemala as multiethnic, pluricultural, and multilingual, recognized the Maya, Garifuna, and Xinka peoples, and committed the state to reforms regarding language, spirituality, customary law, land, and participation. Many of its commitments depended on constitutional change, and in a referendum held May 16, 1999, voters rejected the constitutional package across four questions; only about 18.55 percent of registered voters took part, and an approximately 55.25 percent No share was reported in contemporary overall summaries of ballots cast, not of the total electorate, leaving the agreement only partly implemented.

Section 96.18

Different Strategies for Indigenous Political Power

Indigenous movements pursued different strategies, and this diversity is central to understanding the resurgence. One strategy was autonomous self-government, as in Zapatista territories, where communities built institutions outside or alongside the state and refused party politics. A second was participation in political parties and elections. In Ecuador, Pachakutik contested elections and held offices, and in Bolivia, Indigenous and peasant organizations built an electoral instrument that led to Evo Morales's victory in 2005. In Colombia, the 1991 Constitution reserved Senate seats for Indigenous representatives, and in Venezuela the 1999 Constitution reserved seats in the legislature. Scholars such as Donna Lee Van Cott have analyzed how Indigenous parties emerged and the conditions of their success.

A third strategy was mass mobilization: marches, road blockades, and strikes that pressed governments to negotiate. The Ecuadorian levantamiento and the Bolivian march exemplify this. A fourth was constitutional and legal litigation. In Mexico, municipalities challenged the 2001 amendment, and across the hemisphere, communities used national courts and international bodies. In 2001, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ruled in the case of the Mayagna (Sumo) Awas Tingni community against Nicaragua, a landmark finding in favor of collective land rights. A fifth was territorial negotiation with the state, as in Bolivian lowland decrees and TCOs, and a sixth was engagement with international institutions, including the ILO, the United Nations working group on Indigenous populations, and the long process that produced the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples in 2007.

These strategies were not mutually exclusive, and the same organizations sometimes used several. Nor did all Indigenous movements reject elections or seek separate political institutions. Many Indigenous leaders argued that participation in existing institutions was the practical path to influence and that separation risked marginalization. In Oaxaca, Mexico, a state law of 1995 recognized the right of a large majority of municipalities to select authorities by "customary" procedures, an example of autonomy within official institutions. The Zapatistas' refusal of parties was one choice among many. Even within Chiapas, other Indigenous organizations took positions ranging from alliance with parties to cooperation with the state.

Why did strategies diverge? Differences in demography mattered: Indigenous people were a majority in Bolivia and Guatemala, a large minority in Ecuador, and a smaller share nationally in Mexico, though concentrated in particular regions. State responses varied, as did the openness of party systems, the presence of armed conflict, and the strength of national organizations. International ties and funding also shaped movements. And ideologies differed: some leaders emphasized class and peasant identity, others ethnicity and Indigenous nationhood, and many combined them. The result was a spectrum of approaches, which the next debate considers.

Historical Debate II

Does Local Autonomy Offer Stronger Self-Determination than Representation within the State?

The question: Should Indigenous peoples seek power by building autonomous institutions or by participating in the existing institutions of national and state government?

Interpretation A — Autonomy as stronger self-determination: Advocates argue that representative institutions have historically excluded Indigenous people, that parties absorb and co-opt leaders, and that real self-determination requires communities to control their own land, justice, education, and decision-making. Zapatista supporters point to autonomous schools, clinics, and assemblies as proof that communities can govern themselves.
Interpretation B — Participation as the effective path: Others argue that autonomy without legal recognition is vulnerable, that resources and state services reach people mainly through government institutions, and that electoral participation gives access to power, as in Ecuador and Bolivia. They also note that autonomous institutions, like any, can have internal inequalities, including gender hierarchies.

Evidence: Both views rely on case studies: ethnographic work on Zapatista communities, studies of Indigenous parties and municipal governments, comparative scholarship such as Van Cott's and Courtney Jung's, and the statements of Indigenous leaders themselves. The evidence is uneven, since conditions differ between countries and over time.

Why it matters: The answer affects how scholars and governments understand Indigenous rights: as a matter of institutional recognition, or of community-based power outside state structures. Many movements have combined both, and some scholars argue the opposition is overstated.

Section 96.19

The Other Campaign, Mexican Democracy, and Zapatista Reorientation

By the mid-2000s, the EZLN faced a changed Mexico. The PRI had lost the presidency in 2000, the constitutional reform of 2001 had closed the legal path the movement had sought, and the PAN government of Fox had not delivered peace. Autonomy had developed in Chiapas, but national politics seemed to be moving on, with the left-leaning Party of the Democratic Revolution (PRD) gaining strength under Andrés Manuel López Obrador, then mayor of Mexico City and a leading presidential candidate for 2006. In June 2005, after declaring a "red alert" and consulting its communities, the EZLN published its Sixth Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, a long text that reviewed the movement's history, criticized the political class, and proposed a new national initiative.

The Sixth Declaration proposed that the Zapatistas would seek, outside the electoral arena, a national alliance of "the humble and simple" people and organizations of the left, anti-capitalist and from below, to build a new national program of struggle and a new constitution. This initiative became known as the Other Campaign (La Otra Campaña), launched on January 1, 2006. Subcomandante Marcos, now calling himself "Delegate Zero," traveled across Mexico's states, meeting with workers, Indigenous communities, students, women's groups, and social movements. The campaign refused to endorse any candidate or party, and criticized all parties, including the PRD, as part of a political system serving elites.

The campaign unfolded amid the 2006 election. In May, a confrontation between flower vendors and police in San Salvador Atenco, near Mexico City, led to a violent police operation in which two people died and hundreds were detained, with numerous women later reporting sexual abuse by officers. The Other Campaign mobilized in solidarity, and the events became a symbol of state repression. The presidential election in July 2006 was extremely close, and Felipe Calderón of the PAN was declared winner over López Obrador, who contested the result. The EZLN did not support López Obrador's challenge, and its critics on the left blamed its stance for dividing the opposition, while its supporters argued that it had warned against parties' limits. The episode illustrates the tension between national influence and the movement's insistence on independence.

Historians evaluate the Other Campaign in different ways. Some see it as an attempt to broaden Zapatismo beyond Chiapas and to link Indigenous struggles with urban and labor movements, which achieved contact with many groups but did not create a lasting national organization. Others argue that its refusal to cooperate with electoral left forces reduced its national weight, while its defenders respond that its purpose was not electoral. It also revealed a tension between national political aims and local community priorities: the effort of touring and organizing demanded resources and attention at a time when communities faced economic strain and security threats. After 2006, the movement's public presence at the national level diminished, though it continued to hold events, support the National Indigenous Congress, and, in 2017 and 2018, to back an independent presidential bid by Nahua healer María de Jesús Patricio Martínez, which did not qualify for the ballot.

Section 96.20

The Zapatista Legacy: Autonomy, Citizenship, and the Meaning of Democracy

Assessing the Zapatista legacy requires criteria beyond military victory, territorial control, or constitutional text, since the movement achieved none of these in the conventional sense. By other measures, its influence was substantial. It changed the terms of public debate in Mexico about Indigenous peoples, placing Indigenous rights, land, and recognition on the national agenda and contributing to a climate in which the nation was officially described as pluricultural. It showed that Indigenous communities could be political actors with their own voice, challenging paternalist assumptions. It also influenced the language of Indigenous movements abroad, and the movement's critique of neoliberalism shaped alter-globalization activism.

On community self-government, the movement created institutions of education, health, and justice that operated for decades outside state recognition, sustaining a model of mandar obedeciendo that influenced debates about participatory democracy. On women's political participation, the Revolutionary Women's Law and Indigenous women's leadership expanded public roles for women, though with uneven results. On citizenship, the movement argued that democracy should include collective rights and local self-rule alongside individual electoral rights, a claim that fed into scholarly and political debates about multicultural citizenship (Chapter 94). On development, it posed questions about who decides how land and resources are used, questions that returned in Chapter 114's resource politics.

The limits were also real. The EZLN did not secure constitutional recognition of the collective rights it sought, and the 2001 reform left autonomy weakly defined. Poverty in Chiapas remained severe, and autonomous institutions faced shortages of resources. Gender inequality persisted. The movement did not represent all Indigenous communities of Chiapas or Mexico, many of which pursued other strategies, and tensions with other organizations sometimes became violent. Critics, including some former supporters, argued that its leadership remained centralized and that its political isolation limited its effect, while defenders replied that these judgments underestimated the constraints and the achievement of survival without state support.

Historical Debate I

Was Armed Rebellion Necessary to Bring National Attention to Indigenous Demands?

The question: Could Indigenous communities in Chiapas have achieved national attention through peaceful organization, and what alternatives existed?

Interpretation A — Rebellion made the difference: Some scholars and participants argue that decades of peaceful petitioning, legal procedures, and marches, including the Xi'Nich march of 1992, had brought little national response, and that only the uprising forced the state and public to attend. The 1994 rebellion transformed the debate within days.
Interpretation B — Alternatives existed and carried costs: Others point to peaceful movements such as Ecuador's levantamiento and Bolivia's march, which achieved national attention and concessions without armed rebellion, and note that the armed uprising brought militarization, paramilitary violence, and displacement upon communities, many of which had not chosen war. They also argue that attention did not produce the constitutional change sought.

Evidence: The debate rests on comparison across cases, evidence about the responses to earlier peaceful efforts in Chiapas, public-opinion and media records from January 1994, and testimony from community members with differing views. Counterfactuals cannot be tested directly.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians weigh violence and nonviolence in social movements, and it affects how the costs borne by civilian communities are counted. A fair judgment must separate the movement's armed origins from the later political strategies that sustained its influence.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Timing and Consequences of the 1994 Uprising

Long-term causes

Land concentration and slow agrarian reform in Chiapas, exclusion from political power, unequal public services, and decades of community organizing.

Short-term causes

The 1992 amendment of Article 27, falling coffee prices, expectations about NAFTA, repression of independent organizations, and the EZLN's decade of clandestine preparation.

Event or transformation

The armed uprising of January 1, 1994, timed to NAFTA's entry into force.

Immediate effects

Fighting, a ceasefire, national antiwar mobilization, and the opening of negotiations.

Long-term consequences

The San Andrés Accords, an unsatisfying reform in 2001, autonomous institutions, paramilitary violence, transnational solidarity, and a national debate on Indigenous rights.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the uprising, and no single consequence defines its legacy. It was neither merely a reaction to NAFTA nor a purely outside creation.

The concluding judgment, then, should be clear but evidence-based. The Zapatistas did not resolve every demand they advanced, and they did not represent all Indigenous communities. Nevertheless, they transformed the terms of debate about Indigenous rights, democratic participation, collective autonomy, and the relationship between local communities and the modern state, and in doing so they became part of a wider Indigenous resurgence whose other expressions, in the Andes, Amazonia, and Central America, would continue to reshape Latin American politics, as later chapters show.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Zapatista uprising of January 1, 1994 was the public appearance of a movement prepared over eleven years, and that its significance lay less in military success than in how it transformed debates about Indigenous rights, democracy, and autonomy. The EZLN was founded on November 17, 1983, and its emergence cannot be explained as an immediate reaction to NAFTA, though the economic reforms of the early 1990s intensified existing grievances.

Chiapas was shaped by unequal landholding, slow and incomplete agrarian reform, coercive labor in earlier periods, and unequal public services. Its Highlands, northern zone, and Lacandon Jungle had distinct histories, and its Tseltal, Tsotsil, Ch'ol, Tojolabal, and other Indigenous communities were internally diverse. The jungle was settled by migrants whose land titles were disputed, and the Guatemalan border added refugee and military pressures.

Indigenous organization grew through the 1974 Indigenous Congress, the pastoral work of Bishop Samuel Ruiz and catechist networks inspired by liberation theology, and peasant federations and cooperatives. These networks provided organizational capacity and a language of dignity, but participants disagreed over strategy, and the decision to take up arms was made by the EZLN, not the church or the peasant organizations as a whole.

The EZLN began as a small group drawn from a revolutionary organization, and it was transformed through its contact with Indigenous communities, whose political culture and membership shaped it. Its Indigenous command committee, the CCRI-CG, and its spokesperson, Subcomandante Marcos, should not be conflated, and the movement cannot be reduced to one personality. The Revolutionary Women's Law of December 1993 showed that debates about rights occurred within the movement.

The reform of Article 27 in 1992 ended the constitutional obligation to redistribute land, and NAFTA's approach raised fears about rural livelihoods. These were accelerants, not first causes. The uprising of January 1, 1994 seized several towns, produced about twelve days of fighting with contested casualty figures, and was followed by a ceasefire announced on January 12 after a wave of civilian antiwar mobilization. A ceasefire, however, is not a peace settlement.

The First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle presented demands in national and constitutional terms, and the movement's emphasis later shifted toward Indigenous rights, democratization, and appeals to civil society, expressed in the Second Declaration of June 1994. Dialogue under Camacho Solís and Bishop Ruiz produced no agreement in 1994, and the government's February 1995 offensive led to a new legal framework, COCOPA, and CONAI.

The San Andrés Accords of February 16, 1996 were a negotiated foundation recognizing self-determination within national unity, but they were not law. The COCOPA proposal translated them, the Zedillo government objected, and after the 2000 election and the March of the Color of the Earth, with Comandanta Esther's address to Congress on March 28, 2001, Congress enacted a different reform in August 2001. The EZLN and many Indigenous organizations judged it inadequate because it limited legal status, autonomy, and territorial rights.

Violence persisted. At Acteal on December 22, 1997, 45 members of the pacifist organization Las Abejas were killed by armed local men, in a conflict involving paramilitary groups and disputed state responsibility. The victims were not EZLN combatants. After the failure of the legal route, the EZLN built autonomous municipalities, and in 2003 reorganized them as caracoles and Good Government Councils, guided by mandar obedeciendo, while building schools, clinics, cooperatives, and community justice under severe constraints.

Zapatismo also became transnational, through communiqués, the 1996 Intercontinental Encounter, and solidarity networks, though outside supporters' interpretations differed from the movement's own. It formed part of a wider Indigenous resurgence including Ecuador's CONAIE, Bolivia's lowland march, and Guatemala's Maya movement, each with distinct strategies. In 2005 and 2006 the Sixth Declaration and the Other Campaign reoriented the movement toward a national non-electoral initiative, and in November 2023 it dissolved its municipal and Council structures in favor of Local Autonomous Governments.

The concluding judgment is evidence-based. The Zapatistas did not resolve every demand and did not represent all Indigenous communities, but they transformed the terms of debate about Indigenous rights, collective autonomy, democratic participation, and the relationship between communities and the state. Chapter 97 turns to a different dimension of late-twentieth-century Latin America, the cocaine economy and the cartels of Colombia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that a rebellion proclaimed on a single day was the product of decades of land struggle, community organizing, and political exclusion, and that its lasting influence came from what followed: negotiation, a contested constitutional reform, and the slow construction of autonomous institutions. The Zapatistas matter not because they won a war or secured a constitution, but because they changed the terms in which Mexico, and many observers abroad, discussed Indigenous peoples, democracy, and development.

The chapter also shows how Indigenous history continues beyond conquest and independence. Chiapas's land conflicts descend from colonial and republican dispossession, and the Zapatista demand for autonomy echoes earlier negotiations by Indigenous communities with colonial and national states. At the same time, the experiences of Ecuador, Bolivia, and Guatemala demonstrate that there was no single Indigenous strategy: some movements built autonomy, others entered elections, and many did both. Treating Indigenous peoples as political actors with diverse choices is a thread that runs from Volume I through the end of the book.

Finally, the chapter illustrates how contemporary history must be read with care. Movement communiqués, government documents, human-rights reports, and scholarly studies are different kinds of evidence, and the events of 2023 remain too recent for settled judgment. Chapter 97 turns from Indigenous resurgence to the economy of cocaine and the rise of the Medellín and Cali cartels in Colombia, where questions of state capacity, violence, and the limits of authority take a very different but connected form.
