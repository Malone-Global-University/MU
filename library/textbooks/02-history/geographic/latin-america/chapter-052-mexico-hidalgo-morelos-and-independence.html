Mexico: Hidalgo, Morelos, and Independence

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Eleven Years from Dolores to the Zócalo

The Mexico that became independent on 28 September 1821 was not the Mexico that Miguel Hidalgo's followers had tried to create in 1810. A mass insurgency, a constitutional congress, years of guerrilla survival, and a final negotiated coalition each shaped independence, and each meant something different by the word.

In the early hours of 16 September 1810, in the Bajío town of Dolores, a parish priest named Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla called his parishioners to rebellion. Within days, thousands of rural workers, miners, artisans, and villagers had joined him. Within weeks, the movement had taken Guanajuato, one of the great silver centers of the Spanish world, and had marched to the edge of Mexico City. It was the largest popular uprising in the history of New Spain, and in scale and violence it exceeded anything the viceroyalty had experienced since the conquest era. Eleven years later, in September 1821, an army under Agustín de Iturbide, a former royalist commander, entered Mexico City in triumph and an assembly signed an act declaring the Mexican Empire independent. Between those two dates lay a decade of war.

This chapter explains how New Spain moved from the first event to the second. Its central argument is that Mexican independence emerged from changing military struggles, social conflicts, constitutional experiments, and political alliances, and that the movement that achieved independence in 1821 was not identical to the rebellion that began in 1810. The popular insurgency of Hidalgo, the constitutional insurgency of José María Morelos, the long guerrilla survival of regional leaders such as Vicente Guerrero and Guadalupe Victoria, and the negotiated coalition of Iturbide and Guerrero were distinct phases with different leaders, programs, and social bases. Yet the final outcome would have been impossible without what the earlier phases accomplished. They destroyed the confidence of the royal state, broke the habits of colonial obedience, mobilized and militarized much of the population, and placed the idea of an independent Mexico on the agenda.

Chapter 51 explained how the displacement of the Spanish monarchy in 1808 opened a dispute over who could legitimately govern, and how in Mexico City that dispute ended in a peninsular coup against Viceroy Iturrigaray. The rebellion of 1810 grew in the aftermath of that coup, of the crackdown on conspiracies that followed, and of a deep economic and social crisis that had little to do with Napoleon. It is therefore important to see that New Spain's insurgency had roots both in the imperial legitimacy crisis and in conditions peculiar to the colony: harvest failures, land and debt conflicts, fiscal pressure, and the tensions of a society organized by legal and social hierarchies of race, birth, and wealth.

It is equally important to avoid reading the war as a simple contest between Mexicans and Spaniards. Royalist forces consisted overwhelmingly of New Spain's own inhabitants, including many American-born officers and soldiers, and many Mexicans of every social rank supported the monarchy, some out of conviction, some out of fear of social upheaval, and some out of local interest. The insurgents were divided among themselves, among leaders who wanted political autonomy and followers who wanted land, relief from tribute, or revenge. The war was in part a civil war within colonial society, and it left that society fractured as well as free.

The evidence is abundant and contested. Historians rely on royal military reports, viceregal correspondence, the proceedings of trials of insurgent leaders, Inquisition records, insurgent proclamations and newspapers, the Constitution of Cádiz and its local application, parish records, and later memoirs and histories written by participants such as Carlos María de Bustamante and Lucas Alamán, whose political positions shaped their narratives. The pattern of the surviving sources favors literate leaders and officials. Rural insurgents and villagers appear most often through the eyes of those who feared or prosecuted them. Nationalist memory has added further layers, so that episodes such as the exact words of the Grito or the story of the miner known as El Pípila must be distinguished from the documented record.

The chapter follows five phases. The first examines New Spain on the eve of rebellion and the outbreak of the uprising in 1810. The second follows Hidalgo's campaign to its defeat in 1811. The third turns to Morelos and the constitutional insurgency of 1811 to 1815, the most intellectually ambitious phase of the war. The fourth examines royalist counterinsurgency, the survival of regional resistance, the social cost of the war, and the crisis created by the Spanish liberal revolution of 1820. The fifth follows the coalition of Iturbide and Guerrero to the achievement of independence in 1821. The chapter stops in September 1821, leaving Iturbide's empire, the republic, and the state-building struggles that followed to Chapter 59.

Learning Objectives

  2. Trace the conspiracies of Valladolid and Querétaro and the transition from plans for political change to armed rebellion.
  3. Analyze the Grito de Dolores as a historical event and distinguish the documented uprising from later patriotic reconstructions.
  4. Describe the campaign of Hidalgo and Allende from Guanajuato to Calderón, and assess the diverging aims of insurgent leaders and followers.
  5. Evaluate Hidalgo's measures on slavery, tribute, and social restrictions in their documentary context.
  6. Explain the political organization and constitutional ideas of the Zitácuaro Junta and Ignacio López Rayón.
  7. Analyze the military and political significance of Morelos's southern campaigns, including Cuautla, Oaxaca, and Acapulco.
  8. Distinguish the Sentimientos de la Nación, the declaration of independence of 6 November 1813, and the Constitution of Apatzingán.
  9. Assess royalist counterinsurgency and explain why many American-born inhabitants defended Spanish rule.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did the aims of insurgent leaders differ from those of the people who followed them, and how can historians reconstruct the aims of the latter?
  3. Why did Hidalgo's movement succeed so rapidly and collapse so quickly?
  4. What was new about Morelos's movement, and why did it fail militarily while influencing later politics?
  5. Why did so many inhabitants of New Spain fight for the Spanish crown?
  6. How did the insurgency survive after 1815, and what did survival mean?
  7. How did events in Spain in 1820 change the calculations of New Spain's elites?
  8. Was independence in 1821 the fulfillment of the original popular rebellion, or a settlement that redirected it?

Historical Context

On the eve of the rebellion, New Spain was the wealthiest and most populous of Spain's American territories, with a population commonly estimated at about six million, a majority of it Indigenous. It produced roughly half of the silver mined in the Americas, and its mining centers, notably Guanajuato, Zacatecas, and Real del Monte, together with the commercial agriculture and textile production of the Bajío, supported a large urban and rural economy. Mexico City, with a population of perhaps 130,000, was among the largest cities in the Western Hemisphere. The viceroyalty was governed from the capital by a viceroy and audiencia, divided into intendancies created by the Bourbon reforms of the 1780s, and linked to the empire through the port of Veracruz.

The Bourbon reforms described in Chapter 48 had reorganized administration, raised revenue, and expanded the military, including militia units recruited among Americans that would later play a central role in the war. They also reinforced the preference for peninsular Spaniards in the highest offices, a grievance among American-born elites. In 1804 the crown ordered the consolidation of pious funds, requiring the church to call in loans it had made to landowners and merchants. The measure, which remained in force until 1809, drew an estimated ten or more million pesos from New Spain and ruined many borrowers. The war of 1808 to 1814 in Spain brought further demands for contributions and loans from the colony.

The conditions of the previous years had also been hard. Droughts and frosts in 1808 and 1809 damaged the maize harvest, and prices rose sharply in the Bajío and central Mexico, causing hunger among rural workers and urban poor. The memory of the famine of 1785 and 1786 was recent. Mine workers, textile workers, and tenant farmers lived close to subsistence, and their position had been weakened by the concentration of land in large estates and by the pressure on Indigenous village lands.

The crisis of 1808, described in Chapter 51, added a political dimension. The Mexico City cabildo's proposals for a representative government were defeated by a coup in September 1808, which left a legacy of distrust between American-born elites and the peninsular establishment. The new authorities, headed by the audiencia and the viceroys that followed, moved against suspected conspirators. The Cortes of Cádiz, which opened in September 1810, would later offer reforms, but at the time of the rising these were not yet known in New Spain.

One caution applies throughout. The war that began in 1810 is often presented as a struggle for independence from its first day, but in its early weeks the insurgent leaders invoked Ferdinand VII, the Virgin of Guadalupe, and the removal of bad government and of peninsular officials. Independence became an explicit political program only later, and meant different things to different people. Students should resist reading the later outcome back into the earliest phase.

Section 52.1 · Division I — New Spain and the Outbreak of Insurgency, 1808–1810

New Spain on the Eve of Revolution

New Spain was not a uniform country. It was a vast territory stretching from Guatemala's borders to the northern provinces of Texas, New Mexico, and California, divided into regions with different economies and populations. The center and south, from the Valley of Mexico to Oaxaca, held dense Indigenous populations organized in villages with communal lands, municipal governments, and obligations to pay tribute. The Bajío, a fertile belt of plains north of the capital, around Guanajuato, Querétaro, León, and San Miguel, combined silver mines, wheat and maize haciendas, textile workshops called obrajes, and a mixed population in which mestizos and people of mixed ancestry were prominent. The north was a frontier of ranches, presidios, and missions. The Gulf and Pacific coasts included tropical lowlands with large populations of African descent.

The Bajío was the dynamic core of the economy. Guanajuato's mines, including La Valenciana, produced enormous quantities of silver and employed thousands of workers, many of them free wage laborers who received a share of the ore, a custom that mine owners had been trying to reduce. The region's cities were wealthy and densely populated. Its society was also unusually mobile and unsettled, with many people who lacked the protections of Indigenous village membership or the dependent ties of hacienda residence. It would be the heartland of the first insurgency.

The Church was a pervasive presence. The clergy was among the largest landholders and lenders in the viceroyalty, and parish priests were the most important local authorities for much of the rural population. Many parish priests came from American families and had close ties to their parishioners; some, as the careers of Hidalgo and Morelos show, were educated in the colonial colleges where they encountered Enlightenment ideas as well as scholastic theology. The Inquisition remained active, and Church leaders, including most bishops, were firmly royalist.

Politically, the viceroyalty was run by a combination of royal officials, many of them peninsular, and local corporations. The audiencia, which had overthrown Iturrigaray in 1808, controlled the institutional levers in Mexico City, supported by a wealthy peninsular merchant community. American-born elites, including landowners, miners, lawyers, and clergy, occupied lower administrative posts and dominated the cabildos and the militias. The colonial order allowed such elites substantial power and wealth, but it also limited their advancement, and the suppression of the 1808 proposals had demonstrated how forcefully the peninsular establishment would defend its position.

Section 52.2

Social Grievances and the Colonial Order

The social structure of New Spain was organized by legal categories as well as by wealth. At the top stood peninsular Spaniards, known pejoratively as gachupines, and wealthy criollos, Spaniards born in America. Below them were a large intermediate population of mestizos and other people of mixed ancestry, collectively called castas, and at the bottom were Indigenous people, who constituted the majority, and people of African descent, free and enslaved. As Chapter 45 explained, these categories were not rigid biological divisions, and wealth, occupation, and local reputation could alter an individual's standing. They did, however, shape legal obligations. Indigenous people and many castas paid tribute, a head tax, and were subject to specific legal regimes, while Spaniards did not.

Several grievances overlapped. Tribute and other fiscal burdens weighed heavily on the poor, and the demands of the war in Spain increased them. Debt peonage tied many rural workers to haciendas through advances of cash and goods, while the expansion of estates at the expense of village lands produced conflicts that took the form of litigation, boundary disputes, and occasionally violence. In mining centers, wage and ore-sharing disputes created tension between workers and owners. In cities, artisans faced competition from imported goods and fluctuating food prices. Between 1808 and 1810 the combination of poor harvests and high maize prices hit all of these groups at once.

Among elites, the principal grievance was the exclusion of American-born men from the highest offices and the sense that peninsular officials and merchants dominated the colony. The consolidation of pious funds, the fiscal demands of the war, and the coup of 1808 sharpened that sense. Creoles also feared popular disorder, which complicated their relationship to any movement that might mobilize the poor. This tension, between elite desires for autonomy and elite fear of the masses, would shape the entire war.

Historians have debated how to interpret popular participation. Some scholars, including Eric Van Young, have argued that many villagers who joined the insurgency were pursuing local grievances, defending community lands and traditions, and often acted in the name of the king and the Virgin rather than for an independent nation. Others have emphasized that insurgent leaders and ordinary participants shared a broader hostility to peninsular domination and a desire for justice that cannot be reduced to purely local concerns. The evidence suggests that both elements were present, and that motives varied by region, community, and individual. A single explanation of why people rebelled would misrepresent the complexity of the movement.

Section 52.3

Valladolid, Querétaro, and the Conspiracies of 1809–1810

Political organizing in New Spain after 1808 took the form of conspiracies, discussions in private gatherings, and clandestine circulation of ideas. In Valladolid (now Morelia), the capital of the intendancy of Michoacán, a group of creole lawyers, military officers, and clergy, including the militia officer José Mariano Michelena, planned in 1809 to seize power and establish a junta, in the name of Ferdinand VII, that would govern New Spain autonomously. Their plans were discovered in December 1809. The authorities treated the plotters with notable leniency, imprisoning some and releasing others, an indication that officials still regarded the conspirators as misguided notables and not as a mortal threat.

A second and more consequential conspiracy developed in Querétaro and the neighboring towns of the Bajío in 1810. Its leaders included Ignacio Allende, a captain in the Queen's Dragoons of San Miguel, and Juan Aldama, another officer, together with the corregidor of Querétaro, Miguel Domínguez, and his wife, Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez, who hosted meetings. The conspirators drew in militia officers, lawyers, merchants, and priests. They did not have a fully worked-out program. Evidence suggests that they planned to arrest peninsular officials and establish a junta that would govern in Ferdinand VII's name, using militia units and local support, with an uprising scheduled for early December 1810.

Miguel Hidalgo, the parish priest of Dolores, joined the conspiracy through Allende. Born in 1753 on a hacienda in Guanajuato, Hidalgo had studied and taught at the Colegio de San Nicolás in Valladolid, where he had served as rector, and had acquired a reputation as a scholar with wide-ranging interests. In Dolores he promoted local economic projects, including vineyards, silk, and pottery workshops, activities that colonial authorities sometimes restricted. He was older than most of the conspirators, respected in his community, and more familiar with the rural and Indigenous population of the Bajío than the militia officers were. His role in the conspiracy appears to have grown as the plans developed.

The conspirators were betrayed. Around 13 September 1810, authorities in Querétaro learned of the plot, apparently through denunciations, and began arrests. Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez, who was confined to her house, is traditionally remembered for sending a warning to Allende. The details, including the popular story that she tapped on the floor with her foot to signal a messenger, belong largely to later tradition, but the existence of a warning sent through intermediaries is well accepted. Allende reached Dolores in the early hours of 16 September. The conspirators, facing arrest, decided to act immediately. What had been a plan for a coup by notables became a mass uprising because Hidalgo chose to call out his parishioners rather than wait for the militia.

Section 52.4

The Grito de Dolores and the Beginning of Rebellion

On the morning of 16 September 1810, a Sunday, Hidalgo and Allende, with a small group of supporters, released the prisoners from the Dolores jail, arrested the local Spanish officials and residents, and called the people of the town to gather. Hidalgo addressed the crowd, and the event has come to be known as the Grito de Dolores, the "Cry of Dolores." Participants and later chroniclers reported the content differently, and no verified verbatim record exists. Most accounts agree that Hidalgo urged people to rise against bad government and against the peninsular Spaniards, and that the movement invoked Ferdinand VII and religion. Cries of "Viva Fernando VII" and "death to the gachupines" appear in several versions. The famous formulation of "Long live independence" belongs to later retellings. Students should treat the exact words as unknown.

The religious symbolism of the uprising was prominent. Taking a banner depicting the Virgin of Guadalupe from the church at Atotonilco, which they passed on the way to San Miguel, the insurgents marched under an image already venerated across Indigenous and mestizo communities as a patroness of New Spain. The use of the Virgin gave the movement a powerful claim to legitimacy and connected it to popular devotion. It also did not belong exclusively to the insurgents, since royalist forces invoked the Virgin and their commanders attributed victories to her. The conflict over the sacred image shows that religion was a shared language rather than a monopoly of either side.

The recruitment of followers was rapid. As the insurgents moved from Dolores to San Miguel and then to Celaya, thousands of people joined: rural laborers, miners, artisans, villagers, and some militia units. Some came out of loyalty to Hidalgo, some out of anger, some in hope of plunder, and some under pressure. By the time the force reached Guanajuato at the end of September, contemporary estimates placed it in the tens of thousands, though these figures are unreliable. At Celaya in late September, an assembly acclaimed Hidalgo captain general of America and Allende lieutenant general, a command structure that obscured a growing disagreement over strategy and discipline.

The authorities responded quickly. Bishop Manuel Abad y Queipo of Michoacán, who had earlier been sympathetic to American grievances, excommunicated Hidalgo on 24 September, and the Inquisition issued edicts against him. Viceroy Francisco Javier Venegas, who had taken office a few days before the uprising, mobilized forces and offered rewards for the capture of the leaders. The first days thus established the pattern of the war: popular mobilization in the countryside and towns of the Bajío, and the determined organization of royalist forces and ecclesiastical condemnation from Mexico City. The distinction between the event and its celebration matters. Mexico commemorates the Grito each year on the night of 15 September in a ritual that dates from the nineteenth century, and the shift of the date, the invented words, and the staged ceremony are products of later political memory.

Historical Vocabulary
Grito

The Grito (Spanish, "cry" or "shout") refers to Hidalgo's call to rebellion at Dolores on 16 September 1810. The word has become a national symbol, and every year the president of Mexico leads a ceremonial Grito from the National Palace on the night of 15 September.

The ritual should not be confused with the historical event. The exact words Hidalgo used are not known, and different participants and chroniclers recorded different versions. The modern ceremony, with its set phrases listing heroes, was developed later in the nineteenth century. Using the term carefully helps students separate the documented uprising from the national tradition built around it.

Section 52.5 · Division II — Hidalgo and the First Popular Insurgency, 1810–1811

The March through the Bajío and the Taking of Guanajuato

The insurgent force moved through San Miguel and Celaya, where local militias and officials joined or yielded, and reached Guanajuato on 28 September 1810. The city, with its great silver mines, was the richest in the region, and its intendant, Juan Antonio Riaño, had decided to defend it. Riaño withdrew the Spanish and creole residents and the royal treasury into the Alhóndiga de Granaditas, a massive public granary built a few years earlier. He had hoped to hold the building until reinforcements arrived. Hidalgo sent a message asking for surrender, and Riaño refused.

The assault began in the late morning and lasted several hours. Insurgents, including many miners from the Guanajuato mines, attacked the walls and gates under fire from the defenders. Riaño was killed early in the fighting, a blow to the defenders' command. The attackers eventually forced their way into the building. In the aftermath, a large number of the defenders and refugees inside were killed, and the city was looted. Estimates of the dead run to several hundred, and contemporaries described the violence as a massacre. The royalist account, and later conservative histories such as Lucas Alamán's, stressed the horror of this episode and used it to portray the insurgency as a social catastrophe.

Patriotic tradition has added a celebrated figure to the story. According to a narrative that became standard in the nineteenth century, a miner named Juan José Martínez, nicknamed El Pípila, strapped a stone slab to his back to protect himself from musket fire and set fire to the door of the Alhóndiga, enabling the insurgents to break in. Historians have found little contemporary documentation for this account, and the existence and exploits of El Pípila as traditionally described are uncertain. A monument to him now stands over Guanajuato. The episode is a useful example for students of how national memory selects, shapes, and sometimes invents the heroes it needs.

After the fall of the Alhóndiga the insurgents controlled Guanajuato and its mines, and Hidalgo issued proclamations, appointed officials, and began to produce arms and cannon with local resources. The seizure of the royal treasury gave the movement money. For royalist authorities and many elites, the taking of Guanajuato marked the point at which the rebellion appeared as a revolution of the lower orders, directed at property and at the established order, and propertied creoles who might have sympathized with the autonomist aims of the conspiracy began to turn away. This shift was one of the most important consequences of the first weeks of the war.

Section 52.6

Popular Mobilization, Social Conflict, and Revolutionary Authority

The insurgent army was a gathering of very different people with different purposes. Its leaders, Hidalgo and Allende, were an educated priest and a militia officer, and the militia and regular troops that joined them brought some discipline and weapons. Most of the followers, however, were poorly armed men, many with slings, clubs, machetes, and farm tools, accompanied by women and children, and organized largely by village, town, or work group. Mine workers, rural laborers, tenant farmers, and villagers made up the bulk, together with some muleteers, artisans, and small property holders.

The leaders and the followers wanted different things. Allende, a professional officer, wanted a disciplined army and a political settlement with the creole elite, and was alarmed by the plunder and killing of peninsulars. Hidalgo seems to have been more willing to accept popular mobilization as a political force, and he issued measures that appealed to the poor. The followers, as far as the sources reveal, wanted the removal of peninsular officials, relief from tribute and taxes, the return of lands, and sometimes the satisfaction of personal and community scores. Violence was directed especially at European Spaniards, whose property was seized and who were sometimes killed in towns that the insurgents took. The hostility reflected resentment of a privileged group, as well as a view that the peninsulars were the cause of the colony's troubles.

The insurgents' relationship with property was uneasy. Seizure of goods from peninsulars was widespread, and in some places the insurgents also took property from American-born landowners and merchants. Hidalgo and Allende tried to impose discipline, issuing orders against looting and sometimes punishing offenders, but they could not control an army of this size. The disorder frightened creole elites, who feared that a movement they might have joined was turning into a social upheaval. It also gave the royalists a propaganda tool, and Venegas and the Church portrayed the insurgents as bandits, heretics, and enemies of religion and property.

The insurgents' claims to authority were improvised. In the name of Ferdinand VII and of the Virgin, they replaced royal officials with their own appointees, issued proclamations, and collected revenue. Local people sometimes welcomed them, sometimes accepted them because there was no alternative, and sometimes resisted. The insurgent movement was thus a source of authority in the territories it controlled without having an institutional structure to match. That gap between mobilization and organization would shape the rest of Hidalgo's campaign and the efforts of his successors to build a government.

Historical Vocabulary
Gachupín and Criollo

Gachupín (also spelled cachupín) was a derogatory term used in New Spain for peninsular Spaniards, people born in Spain who held office or engaged in trade in America. Criollo (creole) denoted a person of Spanish descent born in the Americas.

The distinction mattered politically because peninsulars were preferred for the highest offices, and resentment of them was widespread. The terms should not be treated as a simple division between "Spaniards" and "Mexicans." Many criollos supported the Crown, many peninsulars had American families and ties, and the insurgents' hostility to gachupines also reflected economic grievances against merchants, officials, and landowners.

Section 52.7

Monte de las Cruces and the Failed Advance on Mexico City

After Guanajuato, the insurgents moved west to Valladolid, which they entered on 17 October without resistance, and then east toward Mexico City. Along the way, the army grew. By late October it was probably the largest force that had ever assembled in New Spain, with estimates running from fifty thousand to eighty thousand, though these numbers, drawn from participants and officials, are rough. Viceroy Venegas had few regular troops to oppose it and ordered what forces he had to the passes west of the capital.

On 30 October 1810 the insurgents met a royalist force of perhaps two or three thousand men under Lieutenant Colonel Torcuato Trujillo at the Monte de las Cruces, a pass in the mountains between Toluca and Mexico City. The royalists fought stubbornly, and their artillery and discipline inflicted heavy losses, but the insurgents, with overwhelming numbers, drove them from the field. The road to the capital lay open. The capital itself was in a state of alarm, with Venegas organizing a defense, the Church holding processions, and many residents preparing to flee.

Hidalgo then took the decision that has been the most debated of the campaign. His advance guard reached Cuajimalpa, within a few miles of Mexico City, and the army halted there. After a pause of a couple of days, in early November, he ordered a retreat toward the northwest. Historians have offered many explanations: that the insurgents lacked ammunition and artillery, that Hidalgo feared a fight in the city and the massacre it might bring, that he was awaiting reinforcements, that he doubted the loyalty or discipline of his men, that he feared the return of Calleja's army from San Luis Potosí, or that he expected the capital to submit without a fight. No source gives the reasoning with certainty, and later accounts, including Hidalgo's own testimony at his trial, were shaped by the circumstances in which they were produced. Students should treat the decision as unresolved.

The retreat was costly. As the army withdrew, desertion increased, and on 7 November a royalist force under Calleja, the commander of the Army of the Center, met the insurgents at Aculco and defeated them. The battle, in which royalist cavalry and artillery routed a disorganized force, was a major reversal. It led to a split. Allende went toward Guanajuato with part of the army, while Hidalgo withdrew to Valladolid and then to Guadalajara. The disagreement between the two men, which had been growing, was now open. Calleja retook Guanajuato on 25 November, and the capture was followed by executions and harsh reprisals.

Section 52.8

Guadalajara: Abolition, Reform, and Revolutionary Government

Hidalgo arrived in Guadalajara in late November 1810 and was received warmly, since the city, in the intendancy of Nueva Galicia, had already been taken by insurgent forces operating in the west. Here the movement attempted something closer to a government. Hidalgo, with the secretary Ignacio López Rayón and others, named ministers, issued decrees, and organized a newspaper, El Despertador Americano, which printed insurgent proclamations and arguments. The city became the headquarters of the insurgency for about six weeks.

The reforms announced there, and earlier, are among the most important in the history of the war. The documentary record shows that Hidalgo and his officials issued measures against slavery over a period of weeks: an order given at Valladolid in October 1810 under the signature of an official, a further announcement in late November, and the better-known decree issued at Guadalajara on 6 December 1810. The Guadalajara decree ordered slaveholders to free their slaves within ten days on pain of death and confiscation of property. It also abolished the payment of tribute by Indigenous people and castas, eliminated the use of stamped paper for legal documents, and removed taxes on certain goods, and a related order ordered that lands rented from Indigenous communities be returned to them. These measures should be seen as a series of related decisions rather than a single act, and the Guadalajara decree should not be presented as the first or only antislavery measure of the insurgency.

The reforms reflect the insurgency's appeal to the poor. Indigenous people and castas, burdened by tribute, were offered relief. The enslaved population of New Spain was small compared with those of the Caribbean or Brazil, concentrated in some coastal areas, sugar estates, and urban households, but the decree signaled an alignment of the movement with the lowest ranks of society. There is little evidence on how many enslaved people were actually freed as a result, and the decree had no way of being enforced except where the insurgents held power. It should be read both as a statement of intent and as a political act.

The Guadalajara period also exposed the movement's contradictions. Insurgent forces arrested peninsular Spaniards in the city and, in the following weeks, killed a number of them, in episodes ordered or tolerated by the leadership. Estimates of the dead run to several hundred, with considerable uncertainty. The killings alienated moderate opinion and gave royalist propaganda further material. Hidalgo's authority was also contested, as Allende, who arrived in Guadalajara in December, disagreed with him over strategy and over the executions. The tension between revolutionary reform and military disorder, between an appeal to the poor and the need to attract elite support, remained unresolved.

Section 52.9

Puente de Calderón and the Destruction of Hidalgo's Army

Royalist forces converged on Guadalajara. Calleja marched from Guanajuato with a well-trained army of several thousand, while the commander of Nueva Galicia, Brigadier José de la Cruz, advanced from the west. Allende wanted to fight a defensive battle. Hidalgo and others favored a confrontation at a favorable position near the Puente de Calderón, a bridge on the road east of Guadalajara. On 17 January 1811 the two armies met. The insurgents, perhaps as many as eighty thousand or more by some estimates, though many were poorly armed, held a strong position and at first pressed the royalists hard. Then a fire, which accounts attribute to a shot that ignited the insurgent ammunition wagon, spread through the insurgents' lines, causing explosions and panic. Calleja's troops took the opportunity to charge, and the insurgent army broke.

The defeat destroyed Hidalgo's army as a fighting force. The insurgent leadership withdrew north, intending to reach the United States border to obtain arms and supplies, or to establish a position in the northern provinces. The retreat divided the leaders. At Zacatecas and Saltillo, command passed from Hidalgo to Allende, a reflection of the military collapse and of Allende's greater authority over the surviving troops. In March 1811, the insurgents were still moving north when a royalist officer who had joined them, Ignacio Elizondo, betrayed the leaders, who were captured at Acatita de Baján on 21 March.

The captured leaders were taken to Chihuahua. Allende, Aldama, and the general Mariano Jiménez were tried by military courts and executed in June 1811. Hidalgo, as a priest, was subjected to an ecclesiastical process, was formally degraded from the priesthood, and was shot on 30 July 1811. A statement attributed to him in which he expressed repentance exists, though its authenticity and the circumstances of its production have been questioned. The heads of Hidalgo, Allende, Aldama, and Jiménez were sent to Guanajuato, where they were hung from the corners of the Alhóndiga, a deliberate royalist message, and remained there for about a decade.

The death of Hidalgo ended the first phase of the war. The insurgency had demonstrated that a mass movement could emerge in New Spain and could defeat royalist forces in the field. It also showed the limits of an improvised army and an improvised program. The royalists had recovered, and the colony was now divided by blood and fear. Yet the insurgency did not end. In many regions, bands of insurgents continued, and new leaders appeared. The question for those who survived was how to transform a revolt into a lasting political and military movement.

Section 52.10 · Division III — Morelos and the Constitutional Insurgency, 1811–1815

Ignacio López Rayón and the Junta of Zitácuaro

After Calderón, Ignacio López Rayón, a lawyer from Michoacán who had been Hidalgo's secretary, took the lead in reorganizing the insurgent cause. He withdrew with a force to the western highlands of Michoacán and, in Zitácuaro, helped establish on 19 August 1811 a body called the Supreme National American Junta. Its members included Rayón, the priest José Sixto Verduzco, and José María Liceaga, and its purpose was to provide the insurgency with a single political and military direction.

The Junta's claim to authority rested on the doctrine of sovereignty that Chapter 51 described, adapted to American circumstances. It declared that it acted in the name of Ferdinand VII and governed on his behalf while he was held in France, a position that allowed it to claim legitimacy without breaking explicitly with the monarchy. In 1812 Rayón drafted a set of constitutional principles, the Elementos constitucionales, which proposed that sovereignty resided in the person of Ferdinand VII but was exercised by a national congress, that Catholicism be the only religion, and that slavery and torture be abolished. The text borrowed from the Cádiz debates and from earlier Hispanic constitutional thought, and it showed that the insurgents were engaged in the same arguments about sovereignty as their opponents.

The Junta's weakness was its lack of a secure territory and its internal disputes. Rayón and the other members quarreled with each other and with insurgent commanders in other regions, who resented their claim to authority. In January 1812 Calleja attacked Zitácuaro, and the Junta fled. The members moved to other locations, and the body's unity began to fray. Whether the commitment to Ferdinand VII was sincere or tactical is debated. For some members it served as a way to attract supporters who might not accept a break with the monarchy, and for others it expressed a real loyalty. The disagreement would be tested by Morelos, who regarded the pretense as unnecessary.

Section 52.11

José María Morelos and the Southern Insurgency

José María Morelos y Pavón was born in 1765 in Valladolid into a modest family of mixed ancestry, and he worked as a muleteer and farm laborer before studying for the priesthood under Hidalgo at the Colegio de San Nicolás. Ordained in 1795, he served as parish priest in Michoacán and in the hot country of the Tierra Caliente. In October 1810 he met Hidalgo at Charo, and the older priest commissioned him to raise an army in the south and to take Acapulco. He left with a handful of men and a few weapons.

Morelos proved to be a gifted military organizer. Operating in the Pacific lowlands and the mountains of what is now Guerrero, a region with a significant population of African descent, as well as Indigenous communities and mestizo ranchers, he built an army by recruiting among the people of the region and by winning over local leaders. Important commanders came from families who joined him: the Bravo family of Chichihualco, including Nicolás Bravo, and the Galeana family, whose member Hermenegildo Galeana became one of his closest lieutenants. A priest, Mariano Matamoros, who joined him in late 1811, became his principal subordinate, and the army included men of African descent and mixed ancestry in positions of command. The southern movement was thus markedly more diverse in its leadership than Hidalgo's had been.

Morelos fought a different kind of war. His army was smaller, better disciplined, and more mobile than Hidalgo's mass host, and it relied on regional bases, supply networks, and the support of local communities. Between 1811 and early 1812 he took a series of towns in the south and the Valley of Cuernavaca, fought off royalist counterattacks, and developed an effective combination of maneuver and strongpoints. The strategy also depended on political choices. He ordered that the castas be treated as equals and that no distinctions of caste be recognized in his army, and he began to articulate a political program that went beyond defense of Ferdinand VII.

Historians have emphasized that Morelos's movement was more than a continuation of Hidalgo's. It sought to establish institutions, collect taxes systematically, create a coherent army, and ultimately build a government with a written constitution. His mixed background and his experience in the south gave him an understanding of the grievances of the people of the region, and the insurgent leadership in the south included people who would later play major roles in independent Mexico. The next sections trace how he tried to turn military success into political structure.

Section 52.12

The Siege of Cuautla, 1812

In early 1812 Morelos moved into the region south of Mexico City and occupied Cuautla, a town in the hot country east of Cuernavaca, with an army of a few thousand. The royalist commander, Calleja, who had been waging a campaign against the insurgents since the fall of Zitácuaro, moved on Cuautla and began a siege on 19 February. Calleja had a larger and better-equipped force, including artillery, and he was determined to capture Morelos and to end the southern insurgency.

The siege lasted until early May. Morelos fortified the town, organized its defense with the Galeana brothers and other commanders, and held off repeated assaults. Civilians took part in the defense, including women who helped carry water, tend the wounded, and prepare food, and the garrison included boys who served as messengers and soldiers. As the siege continued, supplies ran short. Calleja cut off food and water, and disease spread among defenders and civilians. Conditions by April were severe, with hunger and illness widespread, and the garrison and townspeople were reduced to eating whatever they could find. Contemporary accounts describe the suffering, though the numbers of dead are uncertain.

On the night of 2 May 1812, Morelos and his forces broke through the royalist lines and escaped from Cuautla in an operation that cost lives but saved the army. Calleja entered the town and found it nearly empty and devastated. The royalists had won the town but not the army, and Calleja's failure to capture Morelos was a blow to his reputation. For the insurgents, the siege became a symbol of resistance. It showed that an insurgent force could withstand a determined royalist siege for months, and it increased Morelos's prestige. It also showed the human cost of the war, since the siege destroyed much of the town and its economy.

Strategically, Cuautla marked a turning point. Morelos had survived the royalists' principal offensive, and the royal army had shown its limits in a protracted siege. The southern insurgency was now a force that the viceroyalty could not easily destroy. Morelos used the following months to regroup, to resupply, and to plan his most ambitious campaign, the push into Oaxaca.

Section 52.13

Oaxaca, Acapulco, and the Expansion of Insurgent Power

After Cuautla, Morelos took Tehuacán in the highlands of Puebla in the autumn of 1812, and then turned south toward Oaxaca, the capital of a wealthy intendancy and a center of cochineal production. He took the city on 25 November 1812, after a short assault. Oaxaca gave the insurgents control of a large territory with a significant Indigenous population, substantial resources, and valuable trade. The city's capture was one of the major insurgent achievements of the war.

Under Morelos, Oaxaca became a center of insurgent administration. A local Oaxacan, Carlos María de Bustamante, who would later write one of the first histories of the Mexican independence movement, produced the insurgent newspaper Correo del Sur. The insurgents established a mint, collected taxes, and organized supplies, and they appointed officials to run the region. The city was also a site of political discussion, where Morelos and his advisers developed ideas about the constitution of the future government. Insurgent forces held parts of the intendancy for about a year, though many communities remained divided or royalist.

Morelos then returned to the south and, in August 1813, took Acapulco, whose fortress of San Diego had resisted insurgent attacks since 1810. The capture of Acapulco gave the insurgents a Pacific port and removed a symbol of royal power in the south. At its peak, in 1813, the insurgents controlled much of what is now Guerrero and Oaxaca, parts of Michoacán, Puebla, and Veracruz, and the roads between them. They remained unable to capture the major centers of royal power in the Valley of Mexico, Veracruz, or the north.

The expansion exposed the problem of governing. The insurgents had to feed their armies, maintain order, and collect revenue, and these tasks created friction with local communities. Insurgent commanders in different regions acted with considerable independence, and cooperation among them was uneven. The Junta's authority was disputed. Morelos recognized the need for a stronger, more representative political body, one that could unite the insurgents and give them a claim to legitimacy. He therefore called for the assembly of a congress, which met at Chilpancingo.

Section 52.14

The Congress of Chilpancingo and Sentimientos de la Nación

The Congress of Anáhuac, as it was called, opened at Chilpancingo on 13 September 1813. Its members were chosen by a mixture of election and appointment, since many regions could not hold elections, and they included priests, lawyers, and military men, among them Rayón, Verduzco, Liceaga, and Bustamante. Some were representatives of regions that Morelos's army controlled, while others were appointed to represent provinces under royalist control. The Congress was thus a body of limited representativeness, but it was the first assembly of its kind in the insurgent movement.

On 14 September 1813, a document prepared by Morelos was read to the Congress by his secretary. It is known as the Sentimientos de la Nación, "Sentiments of the Nation," and consisted of twenty-three points. It asserted that America was free and independent of Spain and of any other nation, government, or monarchy, and that sovereignty derived from the people and should be exercised by a congress. It declared that the Catholic religion should be the only one permitted. It called for a separation of legislative, executive, and judicial powers, and for the appointment of capable and virtuous legislators. It proclaimed that slavery should be abolished forever, and that the distinctions of caste should be eliminated, so that all would be called Americans. It also proposed the moderation of wealth and poverty, the abolition of tribute and other burdens in favor of lighter and fairer taxation, and the establishment of commemorations on 16 September and on 12 December, the feast of the Virgin of Guadalupe.

The document is the clearest statement of Morelos's political thought, and it differs in important ways from earlier insurgent texts. It dropped the pretense of loyalty to Ferdinand VII, grounded authority in popular sovereignty, and linked independence to a program of social equalization, including the end of slavery and of racial legal distinctions. Its religious exclusivity echoed Cádiz and many insurgent statements, and it reflects the central place of Catholicism in the movement. Historians have discussed whether the text was a fully elaborated constitution or a statement of principles, and whether some of its provisions were added to the version that has come down to us. The text is analyzed in a Primary Source Window later in this chapter.

The Congress also elected Morelos as head of the executive, with the title of Generalissimo. He accepted the post but reportedly insisted on the style of "Servant of the Nation" rather than the grander titles proposed. The Congress represented a new stage in the war, since it provided a claim to national legitimacy that was independent of any monarch, and it gave insurgent leaders the opportunity to speak in the name of a nation they were attempting to constitute. It would have to do so under military pressure, and its fate was bound up with Morelos's military fortunes.

Section 52.15

Independence Declared and the Constitution of Apatzingán

On 6 November 1813 the Congress issued the Solemn Act of the Declaration of Independence of Northern America. It declared that the Americas had recovered the exercise of their usurped sovereignty, that the dependence on the Spanish throne was dissolved forever, and that the nation was free to establish the form of government it judged best. The declaration was a political and legal statement by a besieged insurgent assembly. It was not recognized by Spain, and it did not create an independent state with effective control of territory. It was a claim, a founding assertion that later Mexican governments would invoke.

The Constitution of Apatzingán, formally the Constitutional Decree for the Freedom of Mexican America, was issued on 22 October 1814, after the Congress had been forced to move from Chilpancingo to other locations by the pressure of royalist advances. It contained 242 articles and was among the first constitutional texts produced in Spanish America. It declared that sovereignty resided in the people and was exercised by their representatives in the Congress. It established Catholicism as the state religion and required that no other be tolerated. It defined citizenship, set out the rights of citizens, including equality before the law, property, security, and liberty, and created a system of separated powers. The executive would be held by a collective body of three persons, who alternated in the presidency, with a Supreme Court for the judiciary. The system reflected a fear of concentrated personal power, perhaps in reaction to royal absolutism and perhaps in reaction to the prominence of Morelos himself.

The three documents of 1813 and 1814 should not be confused. The Sentimientos, of September 1813, was a statement of principles by Morelos. The declaration of independence, of November 1813, was a formal act by Congress. The Constitution of Apatzingán, of October 1814, was a detailed framework of government. They share many ideas, but they have different authors, purposes, and legal status. The constitution, in particular, was never effectively applied. Insurgent control was too fragmentary, the war too intense, and the Congress too much in flight to put its provisions into practice. Its significance lies in what it expressed: a vision of a republic with popular sovereignty, separation of powers, and equality, and a model for later constitutional debate.

The insurgent constitutional experiment also showed the limits of the insurgent position. The Congress claimed to speak for a nation that most of its inhabitants had not been consulted about, and many of them were fighting on the other side. The insurgents' authority extended only where their armies marched. And the Congress and the military command were increasingly at odds, since the delegates, wary of military dominance, curtailed Morelos's powers in 1814. The tension between civil authority and military necessity, which would trouble every government in the following decades, was already visible.

Section 52.16

Military Reversals and the Death of Morelos

The insurgents' military fortunes turned in late 1813. Morelos, who had planned to take Valladolid, his native city, advanced with a large force in December. The royalist garrison, commanded by Ciriaco del Llano, with the young officer Agustín de Iturbide as a leading figure, defeated the insurgents on 23 and 24 December in a battle that ended in disorder. The defeat at Valladolid was followed by another at Puruarán in January 1814, where Matamoros was captured and shot on 3 February. Morelos had lost his principal lieutenant and a major part of his army.

The royalist counteroffensive continued under Calleja, who became viceroy in March 1813 and combined military pressure with administrative reorganization. Royalist forces retook Oaxaca in 1814, and the insurgents' hold on the south eroded. The insurgent Congress, driven from place to place, sought safety in the mountains of Michoacán and issued the Constitution of Apatzingán while on the move. Divisions among the insurgent leaders deepened. The Congress, concerned about military leaders' power, restricted Morelos's authority and later assigned him other tasks, and personal and regional rivalries weakened the coordination that the insurgents needed.

In November 1815, Morelos was escorting the Congress toward Tehuacán when a royalist force under Matías Carranco attacked at Temalaca. Morelos ordered the Congress to flee and fought a rearguard action. He was captured on 5 November by a former insurgent who had joined the royalists. He was taken to Mexico City, and was tried by a series of tribunals: a military court, the Inquisition, and an ecclesiastical court. He was found guilty of heresy and treason, formally degraded from the priesthood, and shot at San Cristóbal Ecatepec on 22 December 1815. The surviving Congress, which had been dissolved by one of Morelos's own generals, Manuel Mier y Terán, in November, ceased to exist.

The death of Morelos is often treated as the end of the organized insurgency, and it was the end of its most coherent phase. Yet the struggle did not die with him. His movement had trained commanders, among them Nicolás Bravo, Vicente Guerrero, and Guadalupe Victoria, who continued the war in different regions. His constitutional experiment left a body of ideas, including popular sovereignty, abolition, and legal equality, that would reappear in later Mexican politics. And the royalists, despite their victory, had not restored the colonial order, since the war had changed the balance of power within New Spain in ways that would shape its future.

Section 52.17 · Division IV — Counterinsurgency, Survival, and Political Transformation, 1811–1820

Royalist Counterinsurgency and the Defense of Spanish Rule

The royalist victory over the insurgents was not the work of an army from Spain. Until 1815, the viceroyalty received few reinforcements from the peninsula, which was consumed by its own war against Napoleon. The forces that defeated Hidalgo and Morelos were raised in New Spain and consisted largely of American-born men: the colonial militia and regular regiments, which had been expanded by the Bourbon reforms, and new units raised in response to the insurgency. Their officers included many criollos as well as peninsulars, and the rank and file included mestizos, Indigenous recruits, and men of African descent. Historians such as Christon Archer and Juan Ortiz Escamilla have shown that the royal army was in large part an American institution, and that the war was in significant measure a conflict within colonial society.

Félix María Calleja was its organizing intelligence. A Spanish officer who had served in New Spain since the 1780s, he commanded the Army of the Center, defeated Hidalgo at Aculco and Calderón, besieged Cuautla, and in March 1813 was appointed viceroy. He understood that the war could not be won by battles alone. In 1811 he issued regulations that reorganized the countryside for defense, creating local militias of "patriots," known as realistas, in towns and haciendas, commanded by local property holders, and requiring communities to maintain security. The system put arms in the hands of local elites, and gave many landowners and merchants a stake in the royalist cause. Later historians have argued that it also created a militarized and regionalized power structure that would outlast the colonial state.

Repression was severe. Military justice was summary, and captured insurgents, including commanders, were frequently executed. Towns that had supported the insurgents were sometimes punished by looting or destruction, and civilians suspected of aiding them were imprisoned or fined. Royalist commanders, including Calleja at Guanajuato and Iturbide in the Bajío, became notorious for harshness. The campaign also included the forced concentration of rural populations in some regions to deprive insurgents of support, a tactic that caused hardship and resentment.

At the same time, the royalists offered pardons. Beginning in 1812 and especially under Viceroy Juan Ruiz de Apodaca, who took office in 1816, the government offered amnesty, called indulto, to insurgents who laid down their arms. Many accepted, and the policy contributed to the decline of the insurgency after 1816. It also tells students something about the nature of the war: the viceregal government sought to win back the population as well as defeat the armed bands. The reasons that many American-born inhabitants supported the Crown were varied. Some feared social revolution and the violence of the insurgents. Some had personal and economic ties to the colonial establishment. Some, including the Church hierarchy, were persuaded by religious and political arguments. And some simply followed local leaders or accepted the authority that controlled their region. The war was thus a conflict of several overlapping loyalties rather than a simple clash of nations.

Section 52.18

Guerrero, Guadalupe Victoria, and the Survival of Regional Resistance

After the death of Morelos, the insurgency fragmented into regional movements. There was no longer a central government or a unified army, but there were leaders who held out in particular places, in the mountains and coastal lowlands where royal control was weak. The war continued as a series of guerrilla struggles, local raids, and negotiated truces, and its geographic dispersion made it difficult for the royalists to eliminate.

Vicente Guerrero, born around 1782 in Tixtla, in the south, of a family of mixed Indigenous, African, and Spanish ancestry, had joined Morelos's army and became the most persistent insurgent commander in the mountains of the south. Operating from the Sierra Madre del Sur, he led small, mobile forces that raided royalist positions, avoided major battles, and maintained the support of many local communities, including Afro-descended and Indigenous peasants. Peter Guardino and other historians have studied how the war in this region was sustained by the local population's relationship to the insurgent leader and to the land. Guerrero refused offers of amnesty, including one reportedly delivered by his own father, and became a symbol of insurgent perseverance.

In Veracruz and Puebla, Guadalupe Victoria, born José Miguel Fernández y Félix in Durango, controlled the roads between the capital and the Gulf coast for several years, harassing royal convoys, until a combination of royalist pressure and the amnesty policy reduced his following in 1817. He then withdrew to the mountains, where he is said to have lived for a period in extreme isolation, a story that has become part of his legend. Other leaders, including Nicolás Bravo, Pedro Ascencio, and local chiefs in Michoacán, Guanajuato, and elsewhere, held out or were captured and imprisoned. Women also participated, among them Manuela Medina, who commanded insurgent forces near Taxco, and Gertrudis Bocanegra of Pátzcuaro, who supported the insurgents and was executed in 1817 after her capture.

In April 1817 a Spanish liberal named Francisco Xavier Mina landed on the Gulf coast at Soto la Marina with a few hundred men, many of them foreign volunteers, recruited in Britain and the United States. Mina had fought against Napoleon in Spain and was an opponent of Ferdinand VII's absolutism, and he saw the Mexican insurgency as part of a liberal struggle against the Spanish crown. He marched into the Bajío and joined forces with local insurgents, including Pedro Moreno, who held the fortified position of the Fuerte del Sombrero. The expedition won some victories but was surrounded and defeated by royalist forces, and Mina was captured and shot on 11 November 1817. The episode is a reminder that the Mexican insurgency was part of a wider Atlantic politics of liberalism and anti-absolutism.

By 1818 and 1819 the insurgency had been reduced to scattered strongholds, and the viceroy reported that the colony was largely pacified. The reality was more complicated. Royalist control of the countryside was incomplete, and the government maintained itself through a militarized system that was expensive and unstable. The insurgency was not defeated so much as contained, and when the political conditions changed in 1820, it provided the foundation for a new alignment.

Section 52.19

Society during Eleven Years of War

The war transformed the lives of ordinary people in ways that the battles and constitutions do not capture. Throughout the central and southern regions, populations were displaced as families fled towns and haciendas to escape fighting, requisitions, and reprisals. Villages were burned or abandoned, and people moved to cities, mountains, or other regions. Demographic estimates of the war's toll are uncertain, but historians agree that the losses from combat, executions, disease, hunger, and displacement ran into the hundreds of thousands.

The economy suffered severely. The silver mines of Guanajuato, Zacatecas, and other centers were disrupted by fighting, flooding, the flight of capital and skilled workers, and the destruction of equipment. Silver production fell sharply in the 1810s, and the colony's principal export and source of revenue declined. Agriculture and trade were interrupted by the insecurity of roads, the requisition of animals and grain, and the destruction of estates. Both insurgents and royalists levied forced contributions and loans, imposing heavy burdens on landowners, merchants, and the Church. By 1820, New Spain was poorer than it had been in 1810, and the royal government was heavily indebted.

Women's experience of the war was varied. Some, like Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez, who was imprisoned in a convent for her role in the conspiracy, and Leona Vicario, a wealthy woman of Mexico City who passed information and money to the insurgents, were participants in political networks. Vicario was arrested in 1813, rescued from confinement by insurgent supporters, and joined the insurgent forces, eventually marrying Andrés Quintana Roo, a lawyer and Congress member. Others, among them Manuela Medina and Gertrudis Bocanegra, took more direct roles in armed resistance. Far larger numbers of women worked in the armies as cooks, nurses, and camp followers, ran households alone while men fought, or were victims of violence and displacement. Their stories survive in fragmentary sources.

The Church, as an institution and as a group of individuals, was divided. Bishops and the higher clergy mostly supported the Crown, and the Inquisition and church courts prosecuted insurgent priests. Yet many parish priests, especially in poorer rural parishes, sympathized with or joined the insurgents, and the royalists executed a number of them. Indigenous communities were also divided. Some supported the insurgents in the hope of relief from tribute and in defense of their lands, while others sided with the royalists, in some cases because they viewed the insurgents as a threat or because local rivalries aligned them with the royalist side. In places, Indigenous villages used the war to press local disputes, and the Cádiz Constitution's provisions for town councils gave some communities new tools. The war was thus experienced in many different ways, and its social consequences were deep and uneven.

Section 52.20

The Liberal Revolution of 1820 and the Crisis of Royalist Unity

Events in Spain changed the political situation. As Chapter 51 explained, Ferdinand VII had abolished the Constitution of 1812 in 1814 and restored absolutism. In January 1820, an army officer, Rafael del Riego, led a revolt at Cabezas de San Juan in Andalusia, among troops who had been assembled to be sent to America, and the revolt spread. In March 1820 Ferdinand accepted the Constitution and swore to uphold it. The Cortes was reconvened, and Spain entered a period of liberal government known as the Liberal Triennium, which lasted until 1823.

The restoration of the Constitution reached New Spain in the spring of 1820. Viceroy Apodaca, after some hesitation, swore to it in Mexico City on 31 May. Constitutional ayuntamientos were reestablished, freedom of the press returned, and elections were called for the Cortes and provincial deputations. Mexican deputies were elected to the Spanish Cortes, where they joined American representatives in debates about the future of the empire. The liberal revolution had the effect of reopening the questions of 1810 to 1814 and of giving Americans a legal framework for political action.

The consequences for royalist unity were serious. Many conservative groups in New Spain, including the higher clergy, large landowners, wealthy merchants, and army officers, had supported the Crown because it defended religion, property, and social order. The liberal government in Spain now threatened some of those interests. The Cortes revived anticlerical measures, including the suppression of the Inquisition, restrictions on the religious orders, and the reduction of ecclesiastical privileges, and it proposed reforms that alarmed property holders. For some royalists, the prospect of a liberal Spain controlling New Spain was as threatening as the insurgency had been. At the same time, the liberal reforms offered Americans, including former insurgents, new opportunities for participation, and the amnesty and constitutional freedoms made it easier for them to rejoin political life.

Historians have long discussed whether a group of conservatives, meeting at the Church of La Profesa in Mexico City in 1820, conspired to preserve a monarchical and Catholic order by supporting independence under a Bourbon prince, and whether they chose Iturbide as their instrument. The evidence for an organized conspiracy is thin and has been disputed, and recent work has emphasized the contingent and decentralized character of the movement that followed. What is clear is that the 1820 revolution in Spain made a negotiated independence possible. It created a split between liberal Spain and the conservative groups in New Spain, it legitimized political action, and it encouraged officers and officials in New Spain to consider alternatives. It was an important catalyst, though it did not by itself cause independence, which required a political and military coalition that only formed in 1821.

Section 52.21 · Division V — Iturbide, Guerrero, and the Achievement of Independence, 1820–1821

Iturbide, Guerrero, and the Negotiation of Independence

Agustín de Iturbide was born in Valladolid in 1783 to a Basque father and a creole mother, and he entered the royalist militia as a young man. During the insurgency he rose to the rank of colonel through campaigns in Michoacán and the Bajío, earning a reputation for effectiveness and for harshness. He defeated Morelos at Valladolid in 1813 and was accused by political opponents of corruption and abuses in his command in Guanajuato, charges that led to his removal in 1816. He then retired to Mexico City, where he engaged in business and mixed with conservative circles.

In late 1820, Viceroy Apodaca appointed Iturbide to command the royalist forces in the south, with the mission of eliminating Guerrero and the remaining insurgents in the Sierra Madre del Sur. Iturbide left Mexico City in November, with a force of several thousand, and in his early engagements against Guerrero's men he suffered setbacks. He then changed course. In January 1821 he wrote to Guerrero proposing a negotiation, and the two exchanged letters. Guerrero was at first skeptical, since Iturbide had been one of the insurgency's most effective opponents, but he agreed to talk. In February 1821 the two men met near Acatempan and reached an understanding.

Tradition describes the meeting as a dramatic embrace, the Abrazo de Acatempan, which became a standard scene in Mexican patriotic memory. Historians consider that the two did meet and agree, but the details of the encounter, including the embrace as a symbol of reconciliation between former enemies, come largely from later accounts. What the documents establish is a negotiated cooperation. Guerrero accepted Iturbide's proposal to unite their forces under a political program, and Iturbide brought with him the resources of an experienced royalist army and a network of officers and officials.

The agreement combined interests that had previously been opposed. Guerrero and the surviving insurgents wanted independence and a political settlement that would preserve at least some of their goals, including equal treatment of the castas. Iturbide and the officers and elites who joined him wanted independence under conditions that would preserve religion, property, and social order against both insurgent radicalism and Spanish liberalism. The coalition was a convergence of motives. It was possible because the situation had changed in 1820, and because both sides had tired of war. Its fragility was apparent from the start.

Section 52.22

The Plan of Iguala and the Three Guarantees

On 24 February 1821, in the town of Iguala, Iturbide proclaimed a plan that set out the terms of the new movement. The Plan of Iguala, in 24 articles, declared the independence of New Spain from Spain, established Catholicism as the only religion, and proposed a constitutional monarchy. The crown was to be offered to Ferdinand VII and, if he refused, to other members of the Bourbon dynasty, a provision that reflected a desire for dynastic legitimacy and an expectation that a Spanish prince would accept. A governing junta would rule until the monarch arrived, and a Cortes would draft a constitution. An army was to defend the plan.

The Plan is best known for its three guarantees: religion, independence, and union. The first assured the Church that it would keep its status and privileges. The second declared independence. The third promised equal rights and unity between Europeans and Americans, so that the peninsular Spaniards who remained in New Spain would be protected rather than expelled. The Plan also declared that all inhabitants of New Spain, whether Europeans, Africans, or Indians, were citizens with access to office according to merit, a provision that removed legal distinctions of caste and reflected the influence of Guerrero and the former insurgents. The army that would enforce the plan was named the Army of the Three Guarantees, the Trigarante, and it adopted a flag of three colors, white, green, and red, representing the three guarantees.

Comparison with the earlier insurgent documents shows how the meaning of independence had changed. The Sentimientos of Morelos had proposed a republic grounded in popular sovereignty, with slavery abolished and church monopoly on religion, and it had no place for monarchy. Hidalgo's measures had targeted tribute, slavery, and peninsular dominance. The Plan of Iguala preserved some of their provisions, notably independence, Catholicism, and legal equality of the inhabitants, but it replaced popular sovereignty with a monarchical solution, protected the Church's privileges and the peninsular population, and said nothing about slavery or the redistribution of land. It offered a conservative independence, designed to appeal to royalists and property holders as well as insurgents. Its authors had learned from the war that independence would need elite support to succeed.

The plan was a political masterstroke in its inclusiveness. Royalist officers, creole elites, clergy, and former insurgents could all find something in it, and its promise to protect peninsulars reassured those who feared persecution. It also contained ambiguities, for example about the form of the monarchy and the powers of the Cortes, which allowed different groups to interpret it differently. Those ambiguities would produce conflict after 1821, but in the spring of 1821 they allowed a broad coalition to form.

Historical Vocabulary
Three Guarantees (Tres Garantías)

The Three Guarantees of the Plan of Iguala were religion, independence, and union. They were the principles that the Army of the Three Guarantees, the Trigarante, was formed to defend, and they gave the 1821 movement its name and its flag.

Each guarantee addressed a different constituency. Religion reassured the Church and conservatives, independence appealed to former insurgents and many creoles, and union offered security to peninsular Spaniards and unity to a divided society. The guarantees expressed a compromise that differed from the popular sovereignty and social reforms of the earlier insurgency, and understanding them helps students see why independence in 1821 was achieved by a coalition rather than by the victory of a single movement.

Section 52.23

The Army of the Three Guarantees and the Treaties of Córdoba

The Plan spread quickly. Royalist officers and units in many regions, including officers who had fought the insurgents for a decade, declared for the Plan, and so did former insurgents and local authorities. Historians such as Rodrigo Moreno Gutiérrez have shown that the Army of the Three Guarantees was a composite force, made up of royalist regiments that changed sides, insurgent bands, and local militias, and that its growth owed much to the decisions of regional commanders and civilian officials. In the spring and summer of 1821, province after province went over to the Trigarante, among them Guadalajara, Valladolid, Puebla, and much of the north. Antonio López de Santa Anna, a young royalist officer, joined the movement and helped to take Veracruz's hinterland, and Guadalupe Victoria and Nicolás Bravo associated themselves with it.

The royalist position weakened. Apodaca was removed by officers in July 1821, who judged him unable to contain the movement and replaced him with Francisco Novella, who held Mexico City with a diminishing force. Veracruz, Acapulco, and the capital remained under royalist control, but they were isolated. The viceroyalty's financial and military resources had been depleted by the war, and the Spanish government, preoccupied with its own politics, sent little help. On 30 July 1821, a new Spanish appointee, Juan O'Donojú, a liberal officer, landed at Veracruz. He had been named jefe político superior and captain general of New Spain, not viceroy, a distinction that reflected the constitutional reorganization of Spanish government. He found that the Trigarante controlled most of the territory and that the royalists had little capacity to resist.

O'Donojú opened negotiations with Iturbide and the two met at Córdoba, in Veracruz, on 24 August 1821. They signed the Treaties of Córdoba, which largely adopted the Plan of Iguala. The treaties recognized the independence of the Mexican Empire, as the realm was to be called, and confirmed that it would be a constitutional monarchy. The crown would be offered to Ferdinand VII, then to his brothers and other Bourbon princes in turn, and, if none accepted, to be chosen by the Mexican Cortes. A provisional junta, which would include O'Donojú, would govern until the Cortes met. The treaties also promised the protection of peninsular Spaniards and their property.

The legal status of the treaties must be stated carefully. O'Donojú had no authority from the Spanish government to concede independence, and his action was a recognition of facts on the ground. The Spanish Cortes rejected the treaties in February 1822, and Spain did not recognize Mexican independence until 1836, when a treaty was signed in Madrid. The distinction between the military success of the Trigarante, the formal declaration of independence in Mexico, and recognition by Spain is important. Mexico became independent in practice in 1821 and in international law only over the following years, as other states, beginning with the United States in 1822, recognized it.

Section 52.24

September 1821: Independence and Its Unresolved Contradictions

On 27 September 1821, Iturbide's birthday, the Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City in a ceremonial procession. Novella had evacuated the capital, and the city received the army with festivities. The following day, 28 September, a provisional governing junta, composed of notables including former royalist officials, clergy, and nobles, signed the Act of Independence of the Mexican Empire. The act declared that the Mexican nation, which for three centuries had been subject to Spain, recovered its sovereignty. The junta, with Iturbide as president, appointed a regency of five members, among them Iturbide and O'Donojú, who died on 8 October. The junta set about organizing elections for a constituent Cortes.

The settlement of 1821 was a constitutional-monarchical compromise. It established independence without a republic, it protected the Catholic Church and its privileges, it assured peninsular Spaniards of their rights, and it recognized equality among the inhabitants regardless of caste. It did not abolish slavery, which was small in scale but remained legal. It did not address the problems of land distribution, of the status of Indigenous communities after the end of the colonial legal order, or of the fiscal difficulties of a state whose economy had been devastated. It did not specify who would hold the crown, and its offer to the Spanish royal family was soon rejected. Its provisions on equality were more advanced than those of many contemporary constitutions, and they reflected the participation of Guerrero and the former insurgents, but they were declarations, not programs.

The contradictions in the settlement were visible at once. The coalition included people who had fought each other for ten years and who had different ideas about what independence meant. Former insurgents expected recognition and some reward, while former royalists expected to keep power. Republicans, monarchists, and advocates of a Bourbon prince disagreed about the form of government. The army, enlarged and politicized, was the most powerful institution. Regional leaders, who had joined the movement independently, expected autonomy. The Constituent Congress that met in 1822 would quarrel with Iturbide, who was proclaimed emperor in May 1822, and the empire would collapse within a year. Those events belong to Chapter 59.

The meaning of the independence of 1821 remains debated. It brought the end of Spanish rule after three centuries, and it opened the possibility of a Mexican state under Mexican control. It did so, however, through a settlement that redirected the popular insurgency of 1810 and the constitutional insurgency of 1813 into a more conservative form. Some of the earlier goals, including the abolition of slavery, the end of racial legal distinctions, and the legal equality of Indigenous people and castas, survived in declaratory form. Others, including popular sovereignty and the republic, were postponed. The independent state of 1821 was the product of eleven years of war, social conflict, and political negotiation, and it inherited their legacy, including a militarized politics, regional fragmentation, a depleted economy, and unresolved questions of citizenship and land. The nature of that inheritance would define the first decades of Mexican independence.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Independence the Fulfillment of the Popular Revolution, or a Settlement That Redirected It?

The question: Did the independence of 1821 complete the aims of the movement that began in 1810, or was it a different outcome produced by a different coalition?

Interpretation A — Continuity: Many national histories, and some scholars, emphasize that the insurgency of 1810 to 1815 destroyed the foundations of colonial rule, trained leaders such as Guerrero, Victoria, and Bravo, and made independence possible. On this view, 1821 completed the work begun at Dolores, and the participation of former insurgents in the Trigarante shows the connection.
Interpretation B — Redirection: Other historians, including Timothy Anna in his work on the fall of royal government, stress that independence came through a conservative alliance of royalist officers, clergy, and elites reacting to Spanish liberalism, and that the Plan of Iguala abandoned popular sovereignty and social reform. On this view, 1821 was a settlement that redirected independence toward goals the insurgents had not held.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same documents: insurgent proclamations, royalist reports, the Plan of Iguala, and the actions of commanders. Recent research on the Trigarante army, such as Rodrigo Moreno Gutiérrez's, suggests that regional decisions by officers and officials shaped the outcome. Many scholars hold that both continuity and redirection are present.

Why it matters: The question affects how Mexicans and historians understand the nation's founding, whether as a popular revolution or an elite settlement, and how they interpret the conflicts of the following decades.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation using the three primary sources, and identify what additional evidence might help decide between them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: New Spain before and after the War

What Changed

  • Spanish rule ended, and a new sovereign state was proclaimed
  • The army became the most powerful institution, with regional commanders and militias
  • Legal distinctions of caste were abolished in principle
  • The economy, especially mining, was damaged, and the fiscal system strained
  • Political ideas of popular sovereignty, constitutionalism, and republicanism entered public debate

What Stayed the Same

  • The Catholic Church retained its privileges and exclusive status
  • Large estates and unequal landholding persisted
  • Elite men dominated formal politics
  • Slavery remained legal, though small in scale
  • Indigenous communities continued to face pressures on land and status

Who experienced change most directly: Military officers, former insurgents, peninsular Spaniards, and communities in war zones. Greater continuity: Rural workers, many Indigenous villagers, and women. Why the pattern matters: The political break was sharp, but social and economic structures changed more slowly, which explains why many of the grievances of 1810 persisted after 1821.

Long-term causes

Bourbon fiscal and administrative reform; creole-peninsular rivalry; land, debt, and tribute conflicts; the Bajío's social conditions; Enlightenment and Hispanic constitutional thought.

Everyday Life and Social History

Hunger, Camps, and Households in a Decade of War

For many families, the war began as a food crisis and became a series of displacements. Maize prices that rose in 1809 and 1810 pushed rural households toward the edge of survival, and the insurgent armies, with their wives, children, and camp followers, were in part movements of hungry people. Women ground maize, cooked, nursed the wounded, and managed the supplies that armies could not provide for themselves. When armies passed, villages were required to supply food, animals, and men, and when royalist forces followed they punished communities accused of aiding the rebels.

The war also reshaped households. Men who joined the armies left women to manage land and children, and many families were separated. Funerals, baptisms, and marriages were disrupted, and parish records are incomplete for the war years. In towns that changed hands, residents learned to display whichever symbols the occupying force required. Children served as messengers and soldiers, as in the siege of Cuautla. For most people, the experience of independence was less a matter of constitutional texts than of requisitions, fear, rumor, and the struggle to plant and harvest in the midst of fighting.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how New Spain moved from the rebellion of 1810 to independence in 1821 and has argued that the outcome was produced by distinct phases of struggle rather than by a single continuous movement. The popular insurgency of Hidalgo, the constitutional insurgency of Morelos, the guerrilla survival of regional leaders, and the negotiated coalition of Iturbide and Guerrero each had different leaders, programs, and social bases.

New Spain on the eve of the war was wealthy and unequal. The Bajío combined silver, agriculture, and textiles with a mobile and often precarious population. Tribute, debt, land conflict, and the harvest failures of 1808 to 1810 weighed on the poor. Creole elites resented peninsular preference and the fiscal demands of the Crown, while fearing popular disorder. The 1808 coup in Mexico City had closed one path to autonomy.

The conspiracies of Valladolid and Querétaro planned a junta government by notables. When the Querétaro plot was discovered in September 1810, Hidalgo called his parishioners to arms at Dolores, turning a conspiracy into a mass uprising. The exact words of the Grito are unknown, and the movement invoked Ferdinand VII, the Virgin of Guadalupe, and the removal of bad government and peninsular officials rather than an explicit program of independence.

Hidalgo's army, tens of thousands strong, took Guanajuato, defeated royalists at Monte de las Cruces, and stood before Mexico City, then turned back for reasons that remain debated. Defeats at Aculco and Calderón destroyed it. Its leaders were captured and executed in 1811. The movement had shown the scale of popular discontent and the fears it aroused among elites, and its decrees on slavery, tribute, and social restrictions at Valladolid and Guadalajara signaled an appeal to the poor.

Rayón's Zitácuaro Junta attempted to give the insurgency institutions, but it was Morelos who built a more disciplined army in the south, withstood the siege of Cuautla, took Oaxaca and Acapulco, and convened the Congress of Chilpancingo. His Sentimientos de la Nación, the declaration of independence of November 1813, and the Constitution of Apatzingán of 1814 set out a vision of a sovereign people, separation of powers, abolition of slavery, and the end of caste distinctions, although the constitution was never effectively applied.

Royalist counterinsurgency, directed by Calleja and later Apodaca, relied on American-born officers and soldiers, local militias, harsh repression, and amnesty. The war was in large part a conflict within colonial society. After Morelos's execution in 1815, the insurgency fragmented, surviving through Guerrero in the south, Victoria in Veracruz, and other leaders, and through the expedition of Francisco Xavier Mina in 1817. The war devastated mining, agriculture, and trade and displaced large numbers of people.

The liberal revolution in Spain in 1820 transformed the situation. It restored the Constitution of 1812, alarmed conservative groups in New Spain, and encouraged political realignment. Iturbide, sent to defeat Guerrero, negotiated with him instead, and the Plan of Iguala of February 1821 proclaimed independence, Catholicism, and union, with a constitutional monarchy and legal equality of the inhabitants. The Army of the Three Guarantees grew through defections of royalist units and adhesion of insurgents and local authorities.

The Treaties of Córdoba, signed by Iturbide and O'Donojú in August 1821, recognized independence in practice, but Spain rejected them and recognized Mexico only in 1836. The Trigarante entered Mexico City on 27 September and independence was declared on 28 September 1821. The settlement protected the Church and peninsular Spaniards, offered the crown to Bourbon princes, and affirmed equality in principle, while leaving slavery, land distribution, Indigenous status, fiscal weakness, and the form of government unresolved.

Students should leave with a sense that 1821 both completed and redirected the movement begun in 1810. The insurgencies destroyed the colonial order's confidence and produced commanders, ideas, and a militarized society, but independence was achieved by a coalition whose program differed from those of Hidalgo and Morelos. The contradictions of that settlement, together with the legacies of a decade of war, shaped the empire, republic, and state-building struggles that Chapter 59 will examine.

Key Terms

Grito de Dolores
Hidalgo's call to rebellion on 16 September 1810; its exact words are unknown, and the modern ceremony is a later construction.
Insurgent
A participant in the armed movement against royal authority, with aims that varied by leader and region.
Realista (royalist)
A supporter of Spanish rule, including local militia units, most of whose members were American-born.
Gachupín
A derogatory term for peninsular Spaniards in New Spain.
Criollo
A person of Spanish descent born in the Americas.
Castas
People of mixed or African ancestry, categories that carried legal and fiscal implications.
Tribute
A head tax paid by Indigenous people and many castas, targeted for abolition by insurgents and by the Cortes of Cádiz.
Bajío
The fertile and mineral-rich region of central Mexico around Guanajuato and Querétaro, the first insurgency's heartland.

The public granary in Guanajuato where royalists were besieged and killed in September 1810.
Intendancy
A Bourbon administrative district headed by an intendant.

The Supreme National American Junta of 1811, the insurgents' first attempt at a central government.

The insurgent assembly convened at Chilpancingo in September 1813.

Morelos's 23-point statement of principles presented on 14 September 1813.

The insurgent Congress's formal act of 6 November 1813.
Constitution of Apatzingán
The insurgent constitution of 22 October 1814, never effectively applied.
Popular sovereignty
The principle that political authority derives from the people.
Indulto
A royalist amnesty offered to insurgents who laid down arms.
Guerrilla
Irregular warfare by small mobile forces, used by insurgents after 1815.
Constitution of Cádiz
The 1812 Spanish constitution, restored in 1820, whose provisions shaped politics in New Spain.
Plan of Iguala
Iturbide's 24-article plan of 24 February 1821 proclaiming independence, religion, and union.
Three Guarantees (Trigarante)
Religion, independence, and union, and the name of the army that enforced them.

The agreement of 24 August 1821 between Iturbide and O'Donojú recognizing independence in practice, rejected by Spain.

The title of O'Donojú, the chief political authority of New Spain under the Cádiz constitutional system, not a viceroy.

A monarchy limited by a constitution and assembly, the form planned in 1821.
Abrazo de Acatempan
The traditional account of the embrace of Iturbide and Guerrero, whose details come from later accounts.
Liberal Triennium
The period of liberal government in Spain from 1820 to 1823.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Mexican independence was not a single event or movement. It was a sequence of struggles in which a mass insurgency, a constitutional experiment, years of guerrilla survival, and a final negotiated coalition each contributed something and each ended in a different way. The people who fought, from villagers and miners to priests, militia officers, and royalist commanders, were divided by interests, beliefs, and fears, and the war was as much a conflict within colonial society as a conflict with Spain.

It also shows how political outcomes can differ from the intentions of those who made them. Hidalgo and Morelos raised questions about slavery, caste, sovereignty, and land that the final settlement of 1821 addressed only partially. The coalition that achieved independence did so by protecting the Church, property, and peninsular Spaniards, and by postponing the republic. The result was an independent state whose founding compromise held together disparate groups and also contained the conflicts that would divide them.

The chapter connects Mexico to the wider Atlantic crisis. The Napoleonic invasion, the Cortes of Cádiz, Ferdinand VII's restoration, and the Spanish liberal revolution of 1820 were all decisive in shaping events in New Spain, and Mexican insurgents and royalists responded to them as participants in a shared Hispanic political world. At the same time, Mexico's war was distinctive in its popular character, its scale, and its negotiated ending.

Chapter 53 turns to northern South America, where Simón Bolívar and others pursued independence through a different combination of war, politics, and social conflict. Students should carry forward the lesson that independence took many forms across the continent, and that Chapter 59 will return to Mexico to examine how the new state, born of a compromise in 1821, confronted empire, republic, regional conflict, and war.
