Getúlio Vargas and the Transformation of Brazil

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: The Day the Electoral Calendar Stopped

On 10 November 1937, soldiers surrounded the Congress in Rio de Janeiro, the presidential election scheduled for January 1938 was cancelled, and the man who had governed Brazil since 1930 announced a new constitution that he had not submitted to any vote. The same government would, within six years, create the legal framework of Brazilian labor protection.

That day, the Estado Novo ("New State"), is the best entry point into this chapter because it concentrates its central problem. Getúlio Dornelles Vargas, a lawyer and politician from the far southern state of Rio Grande do Sul, had come to power in 1930 by overthrowing the Old Republic. Over the next fifteen years his government created a federal labor ministry, a system of state-recognized unions, labor courts, a minimum wage, a consolidated labor code, a national steel mill, a state mining company, and a propaganda apparatus that made him a daily presence in Brazilian life. In the same years it dissolved legislatures, appointed state governors, censored the press, imprisoned political opponents, and between 1937 and 1945 governed without elections. Vargas was then removed by the military in 1945, returned through a free presidential election in 1950, and died by his own hand in the presidential palace in August 1954. Few figures in Latin American history combined social reform and political repression so closely.

This chapter treats that combination as a historical problem to be explained, not as a paradox to be admired or condemned. The questions are institutional. How did a revolution made largely by dissident elites, regional rivals, and young army officers become a lasting reconstruction of the Brazilian state? Why did constitutional politics between 1934 and 1937 fail to hold? How did labor legislation work in practice, who was covered by it, and who was left outside? How did state-led industrialization depend on bargaining with foreign powers? Why did the dictatorship fall in 1945 while Vargas's political coalition survived it? And why, in 1954, did an elected president who commanded wide popular loyalty find himself facing an ultimatum from military leaders?

Brazil in 1930 was a large and uneven country. It was the only Portuguese-speaking state in the Americas, a former monarchy that had abolished slavery only in 1888 and become a republic in 1889. The census of 1940 would count about 41 million people, roughly two-thirds of them living in the countryside, and a majority of adults could not read. Political power rested on regional machines, landowners, and a very restricted electorate. Questions of race, land, and citizenship that Spanish-speaking neighbors addressed through revolutions or reforms were handled in Brazil through gradual negotiation among elites, and Chapter 56 and Chapter 61 have shown how the empire managed independence and slavery in its own way. The Vargas era is the point at which the central government began to claim a direct relationship with ordinary Brazilians, and at which the idea of a nation to be built from above became state policy.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually contaminated. Constitutions, decrees, and laws are available and precise, but they describe what the state intended, not what happened. Vargas left a diary, published decades later, whose entries are revealing and also selective. The Department of Press and Propaganda produced speeches, newsreels, posters, and radio programs designed to build a particular image of the president. Police files record opponents and show methods of surveillance, but they were written by the persecutors. Labor courts left case records through which workers' own grievances can be read, though filtered by legal procedure. Memoirs, the novels and prison writings of opponents, and the later work of historians in Brazil and abroad supply the rest. Each source has a purpose, and the chapter treats them accordingly.

The chapter is organized in five divisions and twenty sections. Division I follows the Old Republic's crisis, the Revolution of 1930, the provisional government, the Constitutionalist Revolution of 1932, and the Constitution of 1934. Division II covers constitutional politics, ideological polarization, the uprisings of 1935, the fabricated Cohen Plan, and the Estado Novo as an institutional system. Division III turns to labor, corporatism, propaganda, and the everyday experience of different Brazilians. Division IV examines economic intervention, industrial state-building, diplomacy between Germany and the United States, and Brazil's role in World War II. Division V follows the end of the dictatorship, Vargas's return in 1950, the second presidency, and the crisis of August 1954, and closes with the question of how he has been remembered.

The chapter's boundaries are firm. Chapter 75 explained the regional economics of the Great Depression and import-substitution industrialization, so economic material here is limited to Brazilian political decisions and institutions. Chapter 77 will take up Argentina and the distinct system of Peronism, so comparisons with Argentina and with Cárdenas's Mexico (Chapter 74) are brief. The United States enters this story at specific points where Brazilian and American interests intersected, notably during the 1930s trade competition with Germany, the Second World War, and the Cold War politics of the 1950s, but Brazilian actors remain the protagonists throughout.

Historical Context

Brazil became independent in 1822 under a Portuguese prince who ruled as emperor (Chapter 56), and the empire lasted until a military coup proclaimed the republic on 15 November 1889, a year after the Golden Law ended slavery (Chapter 66). The Constitution of 1891 created a federal republic modeled partly on the United States. It gave states extensive powers, including the right to tax exports and to maintain their own armed forces, and it limited the vote to literate adult men, which excluded the great majority of the population. Brazil had been the largest slaveholding society in the Americas, and the legacies of slavery shaped inequality in labor, land, and citizenship long after abolition.

Between 1889 and 1930 the republic was dominated by coffee. São Paulo, Minas Gerais, and a few other states produced and exported a commodity that supplied most of Brazil's foreign exchange, and the state governments of those regions, together with the largest landowners, held disproportionate political influence. The economy was also changing. European and Japanese immigration had expanded the working population of São Paulo, textile and food industries had developed, and cities such as Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo had acquired organized workers, anarchist and socialist currents, and a general strike in 1917. Chapter 68 and Chapter 69 treated these export-led changes. What matters here is that the political system was slow to absorb the new urban groups, and that the state offered them little beyond repression and occasional concessions.

The army was a political actor in its own right. Officers had helped found the republic, and in the 1920s junior officers, the tenentes or lieutenants, staged repeated revolts against oligarchic politics. The Catholic Church, still disestablished, was regaining influence among elites. New political currents, including a communist party founded in 1922, nationalism, Catholic social thought, and the corporatist doctrines then prominent in Italy and Portugal, circulated among intellectuals and officers. When the world economy collapsed in 1929 and coffee prices fell, these tensions found a political outlet.

The Great Depression is treated in Chapter 75, and the comparison with Cárdenas's Mexico in Chapter 74 is useful: both governments built state institutions through labor and national resources in the 1930s, but through different paths, with Cárdenas relying on a revolutionary party and agrarian reform, and Vargas relying on a centralized executive and labor law while leaving rural property largely intact. Students should keep that contrast in mind. Throughout this chapter, "Vargas era" means the years 1930–1945, and "second Vargas government" means 1951–1954; the chapter also refers to "Varguismo" and "trabalhismo" (labor-centered politics) as political currents that outlasted the man himself.

Division I — The Crisis of the Old Republic and the Revolution of 1930 Section 76.1

The Old Republic, Regional Oligarchies, and the Crisis of Brazilian Politics

The First Republic (1889–1930), usually called the República Velha or Old Republic, was a federal system in which the states held most of the effective power. The Constitution of 1891 allowed state governments to tax exports, borrow abroad, and maintain militias, and state governors became the principal political brokers. Under President Campos Sales (1898–1902), a pattern known as the "politics of the governors" was consolidated: the federal government supported the dominant political machine in each state, and in return the state delegations in Congress supported the president. Elections were held, but the electorate was small, voting was open to pressure and manipulation, and results were often settled by the Congressional commission that verified the returns. Local landowners, the so-called coronéis, delivered the votes of dependents in the countryside in exchange for patronage. In 1930, fewer than six percent of Brazilians voted in the presidential election.

The standard shorthand for this system is café com leite ("coffee with milk"), meaning an alternation in the presidency between coffee-rich São Paulo and dairy-and-mining Minas Gerais. The label captures something real, since these were the two largest and most influential states, and their leaders did negotiate presidential succession. But historians have shown that the arrangement was never as regular or as stable as the phrase suggests. Presidents also came from Rio Grande do Sul, Bahia, Pernambuco, and Paraíba; the army officer Hermes da Fonseca won in 1910 against a vigorous civilian campaign; and succession disputes in 1910, 1922, and 1930 involved open competition among state machines. The system was a loose and shifting bargain among oligarchies, and what ultimately undermined it was the breakdown of that bargain.

Opposition came from several directions. The urban middle classes of Rio and São Paulo, including professionals, small merchants, and civil servants, wanted honest elections and wider participation. Urban workers, many of them immigrants or children of immigrants, organized through anarchist, socialist, and later communist groups, and went on strike in 1917 and 1919. Intellectuals associated with the Modern Art Week of 1922 questioned the cultural premises of the republic. Most dramatically, junior army officers, the tenentes, rebelled against oligarchic politics: at the Copacabana fort in Rio in July 1922, in São Paulo in 1924, and in the long march of the Prestes Column, which between 1925 and 1927 traveled thousands of kilometers through the interior, avoiding defeat while denouncing the government. Tenentismo had no single program. Its adherents shared moral outrage at corruption, a belief that the army should guide national regeneration, and support for electoral reform, centralized administration, and some social measures.

The political crisis of 1929–1930 combined these long-term pressures with a shock. When Washington Luís, a paulista, chose another paulista, Júlio Prestes, as his successor instead of a candidate from Minas Gerais, he broke an informal expectation and alienated leaders in Minas and Rio Grande do Sul. At the same time, the world economic collapse of 1929 sharply reduced coffee prices and strained credit, as Chapter 75 explains. Coffee planters who had been the republic's strongest supporters now demanded government help, and the government's response was widely seen as inadequate. The republic had not been defeated by a rising class; it was weakened by division within its own governing elites, a restless officer corps, and economic crisis.

Section 76.2

Getúlio Vargas, the Liberal Alliance, and the Revolution of 1930

Getúlio Vargas was born in 1882 in São Borja, a ranching town on the Uruguayan frontier in Rio Grande do Sul. He studied law in Porto Alegre and rose through the Republican Party of his state, which was dominated for decades by Antônio Borges de Medeiros and shaped by a positivist tradition emphasizing order, administration, and the state's regulatory role. Vargas served in the state legislature and in the federal Chamber of Deputies, became Finance Minister under Washington Luís in 1926, and was elected governor of Rio Grande do Sul in 1928. He was known as a conciliator and tactician who preferred negotiation to confrontation. Nothing in his early career marked him as a revolutionary.

The Liberal Alliance (Aliança Liberal) formed in 1929 to contest the succession. It united the political machines of Rio Grande do Sul, Minas Gerais, and Paraíba, along with the Democratic Party of São Paulo and elements of the tenentes. Vargas headed its ticket, with João Pessoa, governor of Paraíba, as vice-presidential candidate. Its program emphasized electoral honesty, including the secret ballot and an independent electoral judiciary, an amnesty for rebellious officers, and modest social measures such as regulation of women's and minors' labor. This was a reformist platform, not a revolutionary one, and its authors presented it as correcting the republic rather than replacing it.

The election of 1 March 1930 was won officially by Júlio Prestes, who received roughly 1.1 million votes to Vargas's roughly 740,000. Fraud was common on both sides, and the Liberal Alliance disputed the result, but the margin was probably real in São Paulo and in much of the country. Vargas himself appeared ready to accept defeat. The impulse toward revolt came from younger conspirators, including Osvaldo Aranha, and from tenentes such as Juarez Távora and João Alberto, along with the army officer Pedro Aurélio de Góis Monteiro, who was chosen to lead the military plan. On 26 July 1930, João Pessoa was shot to death in a pastry shop in Recife by João Dantas, a political enemy whose personal quarrel with Pessoa had been inflamed by a police raid on his office. The killing had local motives, but the opposition made it a national symbol, and it strengthened the case for revolt.

The uprising began on 3 October 1930 in Rio Grande do Sul and spread to Minas Gerais and the Northeast. It was not a unified social revolution, and its victory was far from certain. Revolutionary forces advanced north from Rio Grande do Sul toward São Paulo, but major combat was limited; a much-discussed confrontation near Itararé, on the São Paulo border, ended without a battle. Loyalist units held in many places. The decisive moment came on 24 October, when senior army and navy officers in Rio de Janeiro deposed Washington Luís and formed a military junta, partly to prevent a revolutionary triumph imposed from outside and partly out of doubts about the government's ability to survive. Luís Carlos Prestes, the best-known tenente leader, had refused to join the revolt, having declared himself a socialist earlier in 1930. Vargas arrived in Rio on 31 October, and on 3 November 1930 the junta handed him power as head of a Provisional Government.

Historians therefore separate three things: the uprising begun on 3 October, the military deposition of 24 October, and the assumption of power on 3 November. The 1930 revolution ended the Old Republic's political arrangements, but it did not create a stable democracy, and it did not carry out the program of the Liberal Alliance in an orderly way. It was a reorganization of power in which regional elites from outside São Paulo, middle-class reformers, and military officers gained control of the federal state, and in which what followed depended on the relationships among them.

Section 76.3

The Provisional Government: Centralization, Federal Intervention, and State Reconstruction

The first act of the new government was to dissolve the Congress, the state legislatures, and the municipal councils. Decree No. 19,398 of 11 November 1930 gave the Provisional Government both executive and legislative powers, suspended many constitutional guarantees, and placed its own decisions beyond judicial review. State governors were replaced by federal appointees called interventores, who became the government's chief instruments of control. Almost all states received new interventors in 1930–1931; the principal exception was Minas Gerais, where the established leader Olegário Maciel remained, a reminder that the revolutionaries had to bargain with the regional elites whose cooperation they needed. Interventors reported to the president, could appoint municipal prefects, and exercised the powers of both governor and legislature.

Many early interventors were tenentes, who regarded the revolution as an opportunity to purge the old machines and to introduce administrative reform. The tenente Juarez Távora oversaw much of the North and Northeast in a post informally called the "viceroy of the North," and in several states military outsiders displaced established families. This produced friction. Local landowners resented outsiders, and the tenentes, who organized their own pressure group (the Clube 3 de Outubro, founded in 1931), pushed for faster reforms than Vargas wished to carry out. Over the next three years Vargas gradually replaced radical interventors with civilians or conservative politicians who could accommodate local elites, a process that curbed the tenentes' political ambitions while preserving their administrative legacy. In São Paulo, where Vargas appointed the tenente João Alberto, local resistance proved especially intense (see Section 76.4).

Alongside political centralization came the creation of national administrative institutions. In November 1930 the government created the Ministry of Labor, Industry and Commerce and the Ministry of Education and Public Health, giving the federal government for the first time a cabinet-level presence in labor relations, schooling, and health policy. Over the following years it established national councils to regulate coffee, sugar, and other commodities, a national statistics institute that later became the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), and new bodies for mining, water, and energy. The Mining and Water Codes of 1934 asserted federal authority over subsoil resources and water power. These institutions did not appear fully formed, and many existed on paper long before they functioned, but together they altered the relationship between the capital and the states. Matters that had been left to state governments or to private interests, such as labor conditions, coffee prices, and commodity quality, became subjects of federal regulation.

The process combined centralization with accommodation. The federal government could now override state authorities, but it continued to depend on regional political networks to deliver votes, maintain order, and implement policy. Landowners who had controlled their counties before 1930 largely continued to do so afterward, and the revolution did not alter rural property relations or the political power that came with them. This helps explain a feature of the Vargas era that recurs throughout the chapter: the state became more powerful and more directly engaged with urban workers and industrial interests, while rural social structure remained comparatively undisturbed.

Primary Source Window

Decree No. 19,398 of 11 November 1930: Instituting the Provisional Government

Creator: The Provisional Government of Brazil · Date: 11 November 1930, eight days after Vargas assumed power · Type: Executive decree with the force of a constitutional act · Verified documentary record: Original decree (Chamber of Deputies)

Context: Issued from Rio de Janeiro, the decree declared the Provisional Government's authority and defined its powers. Students can consult the Portuguese text through the legislation portal of the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies.

Paraphrase of the decree's main provisions: the Provisional Government assumes the functions and powers of both the executive and legislative branches; the Congress and state and municipal legislative bodies are dissolved until constitutional reorganization; the 1891 constitution is maintained only as far as compatible with the decree; state governors are subject to replacement by delegates of the federal government; and judicial review of the acts of the Provisional Government and its appointees is excluded.

Analysis: The decree is a short, precise legal instrument, and it shows how a revolution was converted into formal government. Its most consequential features are concentration of both executive and legislative authority in one person and the removal of courts from review. Its limitation as evidence is that it records what the government claimed, not what happened in the states, where interventors differed widely in practice. It also reveals what the government did not promise: it contains no timetable for elections or a new constitution.

  4. What can historians not establish from the decree alone about how power operated in the states?
Historical Vocabulary
Interventor and Tenentismo

An interventor was a federal appointee who governed a Brazilian state in place of an elected governor, first during the Provisional Government (1930–1934) and again under the Estado Novo (1937–1945). The term comes from the constitutional idea of federal "intervention" in a state, which in 1891 was an exceptional remedy and under Vargas became an ordinary instrument of rule. Interventors were not all alike: some were outsiders who weakened local machines, others were local elites who preserved them.

Tenentismo was the movement of junior army officers, "lieutenants," who rebelled against the Old Republic in the 1920s. It was a cluster of tendencies, from authoritarian nationalism to socialism, and not a political party. Students should not equate it with the army as an institution or with Vargas's own political style, though many tenentes helped the 1930 revolution and held posts under it.

Section 76.4

The Constitutionalist Revolution of 1932, Electoral Reform, and the Constitution of 1934

São Paulo was the state most affected by the revolution. It had lost its position in the federal government, it was governed by an outsider tenente, and its business and professional elites feared that the provisional regime would last indefinitely. Paulistas demanded a prompt return to constitutional government, and the demand united groups that had little else in common: coffee planters, industrialists, lawyers, students, and urban middle-class professionals. The state's two main parties, which had been rivals, merged in a Frente Única Paulista, and a military conspiracy formed with the support of dissident officers elsewhere. On 23 May 1932 four young men were killed in a clash with government supporters in São Paulo, and their initials, MMDC, became a rallying cry.

The armed uprising began on 9 July 1932. Its leaders expected support from Minas Gerais and Rio Grande do Sul and an early collapse of the federal government. That support did not materialize, and São Paulo found itself fighting alone against federal forces. The state mobilized its resources impressively, with factories converted to produce arms, and tens of thousands of volunteers, including women who worked in support roles, joined the effort. But the forces were outnumbered and outgunned, and fighting ended in early October with the capitulation of the paulista command. Estimates of the dead vary from several hundred to more than a thousand, and the precise number is uncertain. Militarily, the Constitutionalist Revolution was a defeat.

Politically, the outcome was more ambiguous. Vargas had already announced in 1932 that a constituent assembly would be elected, and the rebellion confirmed that the demand for constitutional order could not be ignored. After the war he treated São Paulo with a mixture of firmness and concession: he arrested and exiled some leaders, but assumed part of the state's war-related debts, and in 1933 appointed a civilian paulista, Armando de Salles Oliveira, as interventor. The revolt thus accelerated, rather than prevented, the constitutionalization of the regime, while strengthening the federal government's position over the states.

The most lasting innovation of the period came with the Electoral Code issued by Decree No. 21,076 on 24 February 1932, months before the São Paulo uprising. It established the secret ballot, fixed voting eligibility at twenty-one years of age, introduced a form of proportional representation, and created a system of electoral courts, the Justiça Eleitoral, to administer elections independently of local officials. These were central demands of the Liberal Alliance and of earlier reformers. The code also recognized registered citizens of either sex as electors, a result of years of activism by organizations such as the Brazilian Federation for the Advancement of Women led by Bertha Lutz. Some states, notably Rio Grande do Norte, had allowed women to vote in the late 1920s, but the 1932 code made women's suffrage national. Its enacted voting clause did not expressly require a husband's authorization; civil-status law, administrative practice, and contemporary interpretations nevertheless affected women's effective access. The 1934 Constitution reaffirmed the vote for literate adults of either sex and reduced the eligibility age to eighteen. It made registration and voting obligatory for men and for women employed in remunerated public functions, but not for all women. Illiterate adults, still a majority, remained excluded.

The assembly elected in May 1933 included the first woman elected to the national legislature, the physician Carlota Pereira de Queirós, and wrote the Constitution promulgated on 16 July 1934. It confirmed the secret ballot and women's suffrage, maintained federalism with a stronger central government, included a body of social rights, and provided for labor courts. It also included 40 delegates elected by professional and employer organizations, an unusual corporatist feature. On 17 July 1934 the assembly itself, not the electorate, elected Vargas president for a term to last until 1938. The distinction between 1932 and 1934 is essential: the Electoral Code of 1932 created women's suffrage and the electoral system, and the Constitution of 1934 gave them constitutional status. Together they were real institutional advances, even as the president who benefited from them was selected indirectly.

Division II — Constitutional Politics, Ideological Conflict, and the Estado Novo Section 76.5

Constitutional Government, Presidential Authority, and the Limits of Political Liberalization

Between July 1934 and November 1937 Brazil lived under a constitution, an elected Chamber of Deputies, state assemblies, competing parties, and a press that was, within limits, free. This period is sometimes overlooked because it was short and ended in dictatorship, but it matters for two reasons. It shows that Vargas could govern within constitutional limits when circumstances required, and it shows how quickly those limits eroded when conflict sharpened. Historians such as Robert Levine have described these years as a time in which democratic institutions existed but lacked the political consensus that would have sustained them.

The elections of October 1934 returned a Congress in which Vargas's supporters held a majority, though not a unified one, and state assemblies chose governors, several of whom were independent of the president. Governors such as Flores da Cunha in Rio Grande do Sul, Armando de Salles Oliveira in São Paulo, and Juracy Magalhães in Bahia commanded their own political and sometimes military resources. The army, which had grown in importance since 1930, was divided between officers who favored a strong centralized executive and those who wished to return to the barracks. The Church, increasingly influential, supported order and criticized communism. Regional elites, whose interests the constitution had partly protected, were cautious toward a president who had taken power by force.

Vargas's own ambitions were visible. The constitution barred immediate reelection, and he was to leave office in 1938, but he and his advisers sought ways to expand executive authority. In 1935 and 1936, as Section 76.6 describes, Congress approved the National Security Law and constitutional amendments that increased the executive's emergency powers, and a "state of war" suspended many guarantees for much of 1936 and 1937. Congress also voted in 1936 to strip immunity from several legislators with leftist ties, who were then arrested. Courts and a free press continued to function in some respects, but the pattern was clear: security measures adopted against one set of opponents created instruments that could later be used against others.

Democratization remained fragile for structural reasons. The electorate was small and the countryside remained under the control of landowners who could deliver votes. Political parties were mainly regional and personal, with no national party able to organize a stable majority. Ideological movements of the left and right, discussed next, rejected liberal politics. The constitution of 1934 was therefore a promising framework that rested on weak foundations, and it should be distinguished clearly from the 1937 constitution that replaced it: the first was written by an elected assembly and operated through elected institutions; the second was imposed by the president and the armed forces and supported an authoritarian regime.

Section 76.6

Integralism, the National Liberation Alliance, and the Communist Uprisings of 1935

The mid-1930s were years of ideological polarization in Brazil as elsewhere. On the right, the Brazilian Integralist Action (Ação Integralista Brasileira, AIB) was founded in 1932 by the writer Plínio Salgado. It adopted a mass-movement style: members wore green shirts, used a Greek letter sigma as emblem, saluted with raised arm, and held rallies under the slogan "God, Fatherland, and Family." Its doctrine combined nationalism, Catholic traditionalism, hostility to liberalism and communism, and the idea of an organic nation represented by corporations. It borrowed from European fascism but cannot be reduced to it: its rhetoric was less openly racist toward Brazilians of African descent, though some of its writers, such as Gustavo Barroso, were fiercely antisemitic. The movement claimed several hundred thousand members, a figure that was almost certainly inflated, but it undeniably drew substantial support among middle-class urban men, some military officers, and Catholic conservatives.

On the left, the Brazilian Communist Party (PCB), founded in 1922, was small but organized, and its position changed in 1934–1935 when the Communist International turned toward broad antifascist coalitions. Luís Carlos Prestes, the legendary leader of the Prestes Column, returned from exile in the Soviet Union and joined the party in 1934. In March 1935 communists, socialists, reformist tenentes, and liberals founded the National Liberation Alliance (Aliança Nacional Libertadora, ANL), whose program called for suspending payment of foreign debts, nationalizing foreign enterprises, land reform, and protection of small property. Prestes was named honorary president. The ANL grew quickly, claiming membership in the hundreds of thousands, again a figure that should be treated with caution, and it appealed to urban workers, junior officers, and professionals who opposed fascism and economic dependence. The government banned it in July 1935, using the new National Security Law, after Prestes issued a manifesto calling for the overthrow of the government.

In November 1935 military uprisings broke out in three cities. On 23 November, soldiers in Natal rebelled and held the city for several days, establishing a revolutionary committee. On 24 November a revolt began in Recife, and on 27 November rebels in Rio de Janeiro seized the Third Infantry Regiment and an aviation school. Each was crushed. The Rio uprising lasted only hours, in part because the government had advance warning. The rebellions were led largely by communist officers and sergeants, with Prestes and agents of the Communist International playing a role whose extent historians still discuss. They did not represent a mass movement, and they had little support among the broader population, but they allowed the government to portray itself as the defender of order.

Repression was severe. Thousands were arrested in the following months, the government extended the state of war, and in 1936 it created a National Security Tribunal, a special court for political crimes whose procedures offered limited protection to defendants. Prisoners suffered torture and abuse, and the novelist Graciliano Ramos, who was jailed without trial, later described the experience in a prison memoir. Prestes was imprisoned until 1945. His partner Olga Benário, a German communist of Jewish origin, and Elise Ewert were deported to Nazi Germany in 1936, where Benário gave birth in prison and was later killed in a concentration camp. The case remains one of the most notorious episodes of the period.

Students should distinguish carefully among categories. Not every opponent of the government was a communist, not every member of the ANL took part in an uprising, and not every uprising participant was a communist ideologue. Many ANL members were reformers who had no knowledge of the conspiracy, and many arrested in the repression were liberals, labor activists, or intellectuals. The government's tendency to label all opposition as communist was a political strategy, not a description, and it prepared the ground for the events of 1937.

Section 76.7

The Cohen Plan, the Coup of 1937, and the Establishment of the Estado Novo

By mid-1937 the presidential succession was the central political question. Elections were scheduled for January 1938, and Vargas could not constitutionally run again. Three main candidates emerged: Armando de Salles Oliveira, the former governor of São Paulo, who led a civilian reformist coalition; José Américo de Almeida, a writer and former minister from the Northeast, who was initially close to the government; and Plínio Salgado of the Integralists. Vargas's own plans were unclear, but several of his closest associates, including War Minister Eurico Gaspar Dutra and army chief of staff Góis Monteiro, favored an arrangement that would continue strong executive rule. The question was how to justify abandoning the elections.

The answer was the Cohen Plan. On 30 September 1937 the army high command announced that it had discovered a communist plan for an imminent insurrection involving assassinations, arson, and pillage, and the document supposedly describing it was broadcast and published. Congress responded on 1 October by approving a state of war. The document was a forgery. It had been drafted by Captain Olímpio Mourão Filho, head of the Integralist movement's intelligence section, as a hypothetical scenario for the Integralist leadership, and it was seized or passed along and presented by army officers as a genuine communist plan. Its name was chosen to suggest a foreign, Jewish-communist conspiracy. The army's leaders knew or had reason to know that the plan was not authentic; historians have emphasized that even if some did not, its use was deliberate. Mourão Filho himself later acknowledged his authorship, and, in 1964, he would be the general who set troops in motion against President João Goulart (Chapter 84).

The plan served several purposes. It reinforced the anticommunist alarm that had followed 1935, it justified the state of war, and it weakened the candidacies of the opposition. Meanwhile the federal government sought control of the state forces that might resist, including those of Rio Grande do Sul, whose governor Flores da Cunha, once a Vargas ally, was isolated and forced into exile. Military leaders, concerned about disorder and attracted by the prospect of stronger central authority, supported the president. Many civilians, frightened of communism or weary of political conflict, were willing to acquiesce.

On 10 November 1937 troops surrounded the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate, and Congress was dissolved. That night Vargas broadcast a speech announcing a new constitution, drafted by the jurist Francisco Campos, and the beginning of the Estado Novo. The speech cited the communist threat and the inadequacy of liberal institutions. The elections were cancelled. The new constitution was to be submitted to a national plebiscite, which was never held. In December 1937 all political parties were abolished, including the Integralists, who had hoped for a place in the new order. The coup was carried out with minimal violence, and the speed with which institutions accepted it shows how weakly constitutional government was rooted.

Section 76.8

The Estado Novo: Authoritarian Government, Political Repression, and Federal Control

The Constitution of 1937, called the "Polaca" by its critics because of perceived similarity with the authoritarian Polish constitution of 1935, concentrated authority in the president. It preserved a federal structure in form while allowing the president to appoint interventors and to override state laws. It provided for a legislature, but the Congress was never convened, and in its absence the president could issue decree-laws that had the force of statute. The constitution permitted the president to declare a state of emergency or war, which suspended many guarantees. It reintroduced the death penalty for certain crimes, established censorship of the press, and described strikes as anti-social. Its provision for a plebiscite was never carried out, so the regime governed for eight years without electoral legitimation. The text itself shows that the Estado Novo was not an improvisation, but a legal order built on constitutional language.

At the state level, interventors governed in place of governors, and they appointed prefects. In a ceremony in Rio in November 1937 the state flags were burned in a symbolic act of national unification. The constitution banned taxes between states, ending a major source of regional fiscal autonomy. As in the provisional period, however, interventors often were local elites, and the regime depended on them. It also created new institutions to bypass them. The Department of Public Administration (DASP), founded in 1938, introduced competitive examinations, standardized procedures, and a centralized personnel system, and it became a model for later Brazilian administrative reform. This created a technocratic corps that gave the regime much of its capacity, and many of its members, including economists and engineers, continued to shape policy long after 1945.

The repressive apparatus was extensive. The political police, led in Rio by the chief of police Filinto Müller, conducted surveillance and arrests. The National Security Tribunal tried political cases. Prisoners, including communists, Integralists, and liberals, were held in jails and penal colonies, and torture was widely reported. Press freedom ended. The Department of Press and Propaganda, created in 1939, issued guidelines and censored newspapers, radio, theater, and film. Political parties, trade unions outside the official structure, and student groups were suppressed or brought under government supervision. Immigrant communities, notably German, Italian, and Japanese, faced campaigns of "nationalization" that closed schools teaching in foreign languages and restricted foreign-language publications. In these years the government also adopted secret instructions restricting the entry of Jewish refugees, a policy examined by historians such as Jeffrey Lesser.

The Estado Novo also turned against groups that had at first supported parts of its agenda. The Integralists, who expected recognition, were banned in December 1937. In the early hours of 11 May 1938 a group of Integralists and dissident officers attacked the presidential palace, Guanabara, in Rio. Vargas, his family, and a few guards held out until loyal troops arrived. The attack failed, its leaders were arrested, and Salgado was exiled to Portugal in 1939. The episode shows that the regime suppressed its erstwhile allies, and that Vargas was not simply the instrument of the far right. It also illustrates a more general feature of the system: it rested on the loyalty of the army, the police, and an administrative corps, and not on a mass party.

The regime thus functioned through a combination of legal instruments, administrative modernization, police power, and persuasion, and not through a single ideology. It resembled European authoritarian regimes in some features, including corporatist rhetoric, a strong executive, and censorship, but it lacked a mass party and the totalizing ideology of Italian Fascism or German Nazism, and Vargas himself was a pragmatic leader who changed course when circumstances demanded. Students should avoid both extremes: describing the Estado Novo simply as Brazilian fascism obscures its differences, and describing it as a benign paternalism ignores its repression.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From the Revolution of 1930 to the Estado Novo

Long-term causes

The weakness of the Old Republic's electoral system, the political role of the army, regional rivalries, the rise of urban middle and working classes, and the global crisis of liberalism in the 1930s.

Short-term causes

The polarization after 1935, the emergency laws of 1935–1937, the approaching succession, the Cohen Plan, and the loyalty of military leaders to a stronger executive.

Event or transformation

The coup of 10 November 1937 and the establishment of the Estado Novo.

Immediate effects

Dissolution of Congress and parties, cancellation of elections, appointment of interventors, censorship, and the suppression of Integralists and communists alike.

Long-term consequences

Eight years of dictatorship, a modernized central bureaucracy, the labor system of the 1940s, and a lasting association between emergency powers and anticommunism in Brazilian politics.

Historical complexity

The path from 1930 to 1937 was not inevitable. Constitutional alternatives existed, and the outcome depended on decisions by Vargas, military leaders, and political rivals, as well as structural weakness. It was not a straight line from revolution to dictatorship.
Division III — Labor, Corporatism, Propaganda, and Brazilian Society Section 76.9

Organized Labor, State-Supervised Unions, and the Construction of Corporatism

The Ministry of Labor, Industry and Commerce, created in November 1930, was the institutional core of Vargas's labor policy. Before 1930 the Brazilian state had treated labor questions largely as matters of public order, to be handled by police, and what protective legislation existed, such as a limited vacation law of 1925, was poorly enforced. The ministry asserted a different principle: that the state should mediate between workers and employers and that labor relations were a legitimate subject of national policy. Its first minister, Lindolfo Collor, spoke of replacing class conflict with cooperation, a theme that remained central to the regime's language.

The Law of Syndicalization, Decree No. 19,770 of March 1931, set the framework. Unions could be legally recognized by the ministry, and only recognized unions could represent workers, sign agreements, and receive benefits. Recognition carried supervision: the ministry could review statutes, inspect finances, and intervene in union affairs, and ministry-approved unions were expected to serve as collaborators with the state. Employers were organized in parallel associations. Later decrees, notably in 1939–1940, required one union per occupational category in a given area and created the imposto sindical, a compulsory annual contribution equal to a day's pay, collected from all workers in a category and distributed among unions and federations under state supervision. This gave recognized unions a stable income and a stake in the system, and it denied financial independence to those that sought it.

Workers reacted in different ways. During the early 1930s, many communist, socialist, and anarchist-influenced unions refused to seek recognition and tried to remain autonomous, while others entered the official structure in order to obtain legal standing and benefits. As time passed, the legal advantages of recognition and the repression of autonomous groups after 1935 and 1937 drew most organizations into the state system. Historians such as Angela de Castro Gomes have argued that many workers, particularly those in industries with a tradition of grievance and organization, actively engaged with the system, using the language of rights offered by the state to press demands. Others, including John French, have stressed how legal procedure channeled and limited conflict, while still giving workers real leverage in some cases.

The pattern is usually described as corporatism, a system in which the state organizes groups by occupation and incorporates them into a hierarchical structure, in which organizations act as intermediaries between members and government. The Brazilian version drew on Italian and Portuguese models and on Catholic social doctrine, but it differed in key ways. There was no single state party, no fully developed corporatist legislature despite the 1937 constitution's promise, and a substantial gap between legal design and practice. Strikes were discouraged and, under the 1937 constitution, defined as anti-social, though they did occur. Labor leaders who cooperated with the ministry, sometimes called pelegos, were often accused by critics of putting their own positions above their members' interests. In 1942 the government created the National Industrial Apprenticeship Service (SENAI), financed by employers and managed by industrial associations, an example of the cooperative structure the regime favored.

Students should distinguish real advances from restrictions. Workers gained legal recognition, institutional channels for grievances, and eventually concrete protections. At the same time, unions lost independence, strikes and political activity were restricted, and opponents of the system were imprisoned. The two aspects were connected: the same legal framework that conferred rights also supervised their exercise. How to weigh them is a central question in the historiography, taken up in the Historical Debate below.

Historical Vocabulary
Corporatism and Pelego

Corporatism is a form of political organization in which interest groups, especially employers and workers organized by occupation, are recognized, regulated, and linked to the state, which arbitrates their relations. In Brazil, it meant state-recognized unions, employer associations, a compulsory union tax, and labor courts, within an authoritarian framework after 1937. It differs from pluralism, in which groups organize independently, and from fascism, which usually involves a single party and totalizing ideology.

A pelego (literally a sheepskin pad placed between saddle and horse) was a pejorative term for a union leader seen as cushioning the relationship between workers and the ministry. The word reflects critics' view that the system rewarded accommodation. Students should treat it as a contemporary accusation, not a neutral description, since leaders varied widely in their goals and methods.

Section 76.10

The Consolidation of Labor Laws, the Minimum Wage, and the Labor Courts

The legal architecture associated with Vargas was built over more than a decade. In 1932 the government introduced the carteira de trabalho, a work card recording a worker's employment history, wages, and vacations, which became the documentary basis of formal employment, and it regulated hours in industry and commerce, set rules for women's and minors' work, and created conciliation boards to resolve disputes. The Constitution of 1934 gave constitutional status to a minimum wage, limits on working hours, weekly rest, paid vacations, and labor courts. During these years the government also developed social insurance through retirement and pension institutes organized by occupational category, beginning with maritime workers in 1933 and extending to commercial, bank, and industrial workers in the following years. These institutes were funded by contributions from workers, employers, and the state, and they created a stratified system, since the benefits and solvency of each fund varied by sector.

Labor adjudication was institutionalized in 1939 and 1941. A decree-law of 1939 organized a Labor Court system, with regional bodies and a national tribunal, and it was installed on 1 May 1941. Labor courts heard individual and collective complaints, included representatives of workers and employers alongside judges, and could issue binding judgments. They became a central arena in which workers pursued claims over unpaid wages, wrongful dismissal, and working conditions. Researchers using their records have found that workers frequently used them, often with success, which means that the system offered more than symbolic protection. At the same time, it directed conflict into legal channels, rewarded the formal documentation of employment, and favored those who could navigate legal procedure.

The minimum wage was established by a law of 1936 and put into effect by a decree-law of 1 May 1940, which set regional minimums, with different amounts for different areas depending on the cost of living, to take effect in July 1940. The announcement was timed to May Day and made before a mass rally. The minimum wage was not indexed to inflation, and the nominal amount set in the early 1940s remained unadjusted between 1943 and 1951, so inflation steadily reduced its real value. Its restoration by Vargas in the early 1950s, and the controversy over its 1954 increase discussed in Section 76.19, kept it central to Brazilian politics.

The crowning moment came in 1943. A commission headed by the Labor Minister, Alexandre Marcondes Filho, compiled existing legislation into the Consolidação das Leis do Trabalho (CLT), approved by Decree-Law No. 5,452 on 1 May 1943 and entered into force on 10 November 1943. It contained more than nine hundred articles covering individual contracts, hours, vacations, safety, women's and minors' work, union organization, collective agreements, and labor justice. The date was chosen for symbolic reasons, with May Day for approval and the sixth anniversary of the Estado Novo for entry into force. Students should note what the CLT was and was not. It consolidated, revised, and systematized a body of legislation developed over a decade, and it did not create Brazilian labor rights from nothing; some had been won by earlier struggles and earlier laws. It was also a legal code for urban, formally employed workers.

That limit is critical. The CLT did not apply to agricultural workers, whose conditions were governed by landowners' power, and who remained without comparable protection until a rural labor statute of 1963 and later laws. It excluded domestic workers, whose rights were only recognized partially in 1972 and more fully in 2013. Informal and self-employed workers were outside its scope. Even for covered workers, enforcement depended on the small number of ministry inspectors and on workers' ability to make claims. Historians therefore distinguish four things: laws enacted, workers legally covered, enforcement in practice, and groups excluded. The CLT was a major achievement for a minority of the workforce, and it left the majority of working Brazilians, concentrated in the countryside and in informal work, with little formal protection.

Section 76.11

Propaganda, Radio, Education, Nationalism, and the Image of the Father of the Poor

The Estado Novo invested heavily in shaping public opinion. After earlier, smaller agencies, the Department of Press and Propaganda (DIP) was created in December 1939 under Lourival Fontes. It had two functions that were closely linked: censorship and promotion. It reviewed newspapers, books, theater, film, and radio, banned material deemed hostile, and subsidized or rewarded cooperative outlets. It also produced the regime's own messages through newsreels, pamphlets, posters, a monthly magazine, and the daily radio program A Hora do Brasil, which stations were required to broadcast. Radio, which had spread rapidly in cities by the 1930s, was the regime's most effective medium, and the state-controlled Rádio Nacional, brought under government control in 1940, became the most popular station in the country, combining entertainment with official messaging.

The image of Vargas that emerged was carefully constructed. He was presented as a calm, tireless leader who watched over the nation's welfare, a father to workers, and the protector of the humble, and a popular formula called him "father of the poor," to which his critics added "mother of the rich." The regime did not need to invent the benefits it cited: unions, a minimum wage, and the labor courts were real. The announcements, however, were staged as gifts from a benevolent leader, and the language of rights was joined to a language of gratitude. May Day became the central ritual. Vargas addressed workers in a Rio football stadium and often used the occasion to announce new measures, including the minimum wage in 1940, the labor courts in 1941, and the CLT in 1943. Official holidays, youth organizations, school pageants, and mass choral performances under the composer Heitor Villa-Lobos reinforced a message of national unity, discipline, and loyalty to the leader.

Education policy, directed by Minister Gustavo Capanema (1934–1945), combined real expansion with ideological goals. The ministry promoted public schooling, technical and vocational training, and a national curriculum emphasizing Portuguese language, history, civics, and moral education, and it sponsored one of the most celebrated modernist buildings in the Americas, the Ministry of Education and Health headquarters in Rio, designed by a team of Brazilian architects with consulting advice from Le Corbusier. At the same time, the nationalization campaign of 1938–1939 forbade foreign-language instruction for children in elementary schools, closed numerous schools in German, Italian, and Japanese immigrant communities in the South, and sought to assimilate those communities. Literacy remained low, and rural schooling, in particular, remained limited.

Cultural nationalism also drew on and reshaped ideas of race. The sociologist Gilberto Freyre's Casa-Grande & Senzala (1933) argued that Brazil's mixture of Portuguese, African, and Indigenous peoples was a source of national strength, an idea later popularized as "racial democracy." The Estado Novo embraced the cultural half of this vision, elevating samba, capoeira, and Afro-Brazilian cuisine as national symbols: capoeira was legalized in the 1930s, and samba schools were brought into official Carnival, under regulations that discouraged themes the government disliked. But the same government placed Afro-Brazilian religions under police supervision, banned the Black Brazilian Front, which had grown into a political party, along with all other parties in 1937, and applied immigration policy that favored European arrivals and restricted others. Historians such as Daryle Williams have shown that cultural inclusion coexisted with exclusionary social realities. The notion of racial democracy, in other words, allowed the state to claim that racism was not a Brazilian problem while social inequality along racial lines persisted. Later scholars and activists, in Brazil and elsewhere, would challenge the idea precisely on those grounds.

Section 76.12

Everyday Life under Vargas: Women, Afro-Brazilians, Rural Workers, and Urban Society

Beyond official claims, the social effects of government policy depended on who a person was and where they lived. In 1940 Brazil's census counted about 41 million inhabitants, of whom roughly 31 percent lived in cities. Rio de Janeiro, the capital, had about 1.8 million people, and São Paulo was growing rapidly through industry and migration, including migrants from the Northeast and from rural Minas Gerais as well as immigrants from abroad. For industrial workers in textiles, food processing, metalwork, and transport, the Vargas era brought tangible changes: a work card, a legal minimum wage, paid vacations, a place to take grievances, and eventually access to pensions. Employers and managers did not always comply, and factory discipline remained harsh, but the legal framework gave workers something to invoke. The state also sought to shape their daily life, creating for example a food service for workers (SAPS) in 1940 that provided inexpensive meals.

Women's experience was marked by both advance and constraint. The right to vote, secured in 1932, was real, but its practical value was limited under a regime that after 1937 held no elections. Women worked in factories, particularly textiles, as well as in domestic service, teaching, and the early professions. The labor legislation protected maternity and restricted night work and dangerous tasks for women, protections that also reflected a view of women primarily as mothers and that, in practice, could limit their access to some jobs and justify lower pay. Civil law left married women under the legal authority of their husbands. Women such as Bertha Lutz and Carlota Pereira de Queirós entered national politics, and women organized in feminist, labor, and communist groups, but official ideology emphasized family and motherhood.

Afro-Brazilians were disproportionately poor, and the abolition of slavery in 1888 had not been followed by land, education, or compensation. In the cities they worked in manual labor, port and construction work, small trade, and, for women, domestic service, the largest occupation for urban Black women. Many lived in the growing favelas of Rio, which authorities regarded as a problem; in the early 1940s the government built "proletarian parks" to rehouse some favela residents, an initiative that moved people from central locations and imposed discipline. Black activism existed, notably the Black Brazilian Front of the early 1930s, but was curtailed in 1937. Black cultural contributions were celebrated, while structural exclusion remained, so that Afro-Brazilians were often covered by labor law only if they worked in the formal urban economy, a sector from which racial discrimination in hiring tended to exclude many.

Rural workers, the majority of the population, lived largely outside the new system. On coffee plantations, sugar estates, and cattle ranches, sharecroppers, tenants, and day laborers depended on landowners, and local power remained in the hands of the coronéis. A decree of 1944 allowed rural unions in principle, but landowners' power and the regime's priorities prevented any real application. The "March to the West," announced in 1938, promoted colonization of interior lands and the creation of agricultural colonies, and it brought government expeditions into areas inhabited by Indigenous peoples. The Indian Protection Service (SPI), established in 1910, was transferred from the Ministry of Labor, Industry and Commerce to the Ministry of War by Decree No. 24,700 of 12 July 1934, and reassigned to the Ministry of Agriculture effective 1 January 1940 under Decree-Law No. 1,736 of 3 November 1939. Its policies of "pacification" and assimilation and expansion of settlement brought pressure on Indigenous lands. Rural Brazil, in short, saw state expansion in the form of colonization and administration more than of social rights.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Work Card, the Factory Floor, and the Fazenda

For a textile worker in São Paulo, the carteira de trabalho became more than a document: it recorded the beginning and end of each job, and it was the basis for claims about dismissal, vacation, and pension. Workers carried it as proof of status, and being without one meant being outside the system. For women and children who had long worked without records, formal registration was an advantage but also a form of state supervision. Families commonly relied on several incomes, and wages, even above the legal minimum, often did not cover housing, food, and clothing in the growing cities.

In the countryside, a coffee worker's life continued to follow the logic of the plantation, with housing, food plots, and credit tied to the landowner. Domestic workers, mostly women and often girls from poor or rural families, lived in employers' homes with no legal limit on hours. Migration from the drought-affected Northeast toward the South, and the movement of rural families to cities, accelerated, bringing new neighborhoods and new forms of insecurity. The silences in the official record about these experiences are themselves evidence of whose lives the state counted and whose it did not.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Vargas Democratize Brazil's Social Structure While Centralizing and Restricting Political Power?

The question: How should historians weigh the social protections introduced under Vargas against the political authoritarianism that accompanied them, and who deserves credit for the former?

Interpretation A — Social inclusion through state paternalism: Some historians stress that the state brought millions of urban workers into legal recognition for the first time, and that the labor system, whatever its limits, gave them durable rights and a language of citizenship that endured. Angela de Castro Gomes has argued that a kind of "pact" between state and workers developed, in which workers recognized real benefits and used them, and that the later strength of trabalhismo as a political identity shows its roots in lived experience.
Interpretation B — Control and co-optation: Others emphasize that rights were granted from above by an authoritarian state that simultaneously crushed independent unions, banned strikes, and imprisoned opponents. On this view, the system demobilized the labor movement, tied it to state funding, and presented as gifts what workers had earlier demanded. Scholars of the older "populism" school and later critics have stressed that the labor code excluded the rural majority and discouraged class-based politics.

Evidence: Both rely on laws and decrees, union records, labor court files, speeches, and oral histories. John French's study of labor law, which draws on court records, complicates both positions by showing that workers used the system actively while the system also restricted them. Many scholars therefore speak of overlapping interpretations and no longer regard "manipulated masses" and "active citizens" as exclusive alternatives.

Why it matters: The question affects how Brazilians understand the origins of their labor institutions, the long political attachment to Vargas's memory, and whether authoritarian politics and social reform are necessarily linked. It also shapes how historians treat comparable cases in Latin America, including Argentina (Chapter 77).

Student task: Write a short paragraph defending each interpretation using evidence from this chapter, then identify what additional evidence, such as labor court case files or union newspapers, would help decide between them.

Division IV — Economic Nationalism, Industrialization, and World War II Section 76.13

Coffee Policy, Economic Intervention, and the Reorientation of Brazilian Development

Chapter 75 explained how the Depression reshaped Latin American economies. This section concerns the Brazilian political decisions that responded to it. The coffee economy was the starting point. The collapse of prices in 1929 threatened the planters on whom the old republic had rested, and the Vargas government, which depended in part on their acquiescence, could not abandon them. In 1931 it created the National Coffee Council, replaced in 1933 by the National Coffee Department, which purchased surplus coffee, regulated exports, and arranged for the destruction of large quantities of stocks, which over the following years amounted to tens of millions of bags. The program sustained prices at a level that kept many planters in business and, by maintaining incomes in the coffee zone, helped sustain domestic demand. Similar institutes regulated sugar and alcohol, mate, and other products.

Intervention in agricultural markets had a political side. Before 1930, state governments, especially São Paulo's, had controlled coffee valorization schemes. Now the federal government took over that role, shifting economic authority from state to national institutions. A decree of 1933 reduced farmers' debts, easing pressure on planters. The Estado Novo constitution banned interstate taxes, and the government suspended payments on Brazil's foreign debt after November 1937 before renegotiating terms in 1940. These measures centralized control of exchange, trade, and credit in the hands of federal agencies and the Bank of Brazil.

The government's relationship with agricultural exporters was thus one of protection and dependence, not conflict. Coffee growers lost the political monopoly they had held, but they kept subsidies and influence, and landed interests remained powerful in Congress after 1945. At the same time, urban industrialists, represented by figures such as the São Paulo industrialist Roberto Simonsen and organized in business federations, pressed for tariffs, credit, and infrastructure and clashed with agrarian interests over exchange rates and priorities. Vargas balanced the two groups, protecting agriculture while gradually giving industry more support, a pragmatism that shaped Brazilian development: industrialization proceeded without any attack on the agrarian structure, which Mexico under Cárdenas had directly altered.

The consequence was a reorientation rather than a break. Brazil remained an agricultural exporter, with coffee still providing a large share of foreign exchange, but the state became the principal manager of the economy, with powers over prices, credit, exchange, and investment that had not existed before 1930. That shift, more than any single policy, defined the economic legacy of the period and prepared the ground for the industrial projects described next.

Section 76.14

Steel, Mining, Infrastructure, and the Industrial Transformation of Brazil

Industrial policy under Vargas was concrete: it created institutions and built plants. The most celebrated project was the national steelworks. Brazil had small private steel producers, but no large integrated mill, and the idea of building one had been debated since the 1920s. A federal commission was established in 1940 to plan a national steel program, and a decree-law of January 1941 authorized the creation of the Companhia Siderúrgica Nacional (CSN), which was incorporated in April 1941 with the government as the main shareholder. The site chosen was Volta Redonda, in the Paraíba valley between Rio and São Paulo. It was close to both markets and to supplies of iron ore from Minas Gerais and coal that had to be imported.

The history of the project shows how the dates and meanings of industrial achievement must be separated. The company was created in 1941, construction took place between 1941 and 1946, financing came from several sources, including a loan from the U.S. Export-Import Bank, and the plant began producing steel in 1946, under Vargas's successor. Brazil depended heavily on foreign engineers, machinery, and technology, and American engineering firms planned and supplied the equipment, while wartime shortages delayed deliveries. Volta Redonda was also built as a company town, with housing, schools, and services for workers, and it became a symbol of national industrialization. Its significance lies partly in output, which eventually made Brazil largely self-sufficient in basic steel, and partly in the principle that heavy industry could be created and controlled by the state.

A second major enterprise concerned minerals. The Itabira iron ore deposits in Minas Gerais had long been controlled by foreign interests. As part of agreements reached in Washington in March 1942 with the United States and Britain, which wanted Brazilian ore for the war effort, the government reorganized the mining and railway assets, and by decree-law of 1 June 1942 created the Companhia Vale do Rio Doce, a state enterprise that became one of the world's largest mining companies. Also in 1942 the government created the Fábrica Nacional de Motores, initially intended to produce aircraft engines under license, and later converted to trucks and automobiles after the war. Distinguishing creation from operation matters here: the company existed on paper in 1942, but its first vehicles appeared only later in the decade.

Other initiatives addressed energy, transport, and finance. A national petroleum council was established in 1938, and the first commercially significant oil discovery was made in Bahia in 1939, though supplies remained small for years. The government expanded railways, roads, and hydroelectric capacity, and the Bank of Brazil and other agencies expanded industrial credit. A 1939 plan of public works and defense equipment set priorities. Throughout, projects relied on foreign finance, machinery, and expertise, a dependence that limited autonomy even as policy promoted self-sufficiency. The state's role grew in the form of mixed enterprises, regulatory agencies, and public companies, and the relationship between public and private capital became a continuing feature of Brazilian development, which Chapter 75 treated in comparative terms.

Historical Vocabulary
Desenvolvimentismo (Developmental Nationalism)

Desenvolvimentismo refers to the policy approach, widespread in mid-twentieth-century Latin America, in which the state takes a leading role in promoting industrial growth and economic independence through planning, credit, protection, and public enterprises. In Brazil, it combined an economic argument, that industry was necessary to escape dependence on commodity exports, with a political one, that national sovereignty required control over key resources such as steel, iron ore, and oil.

The term should be distinguished from socialism, since private enterprise remained central, and from simple protectionism, since it involved direct state investment. Its supporters disagreed about the role of foreign capital: some favored cooperation, others strict national control. Those disagreements structured the petroleum debate described in Section 76.19.

Section 76.15

Brazil between Germany and the United States: Diplomacy and Strategic Bargaining

In the 1930s Brazil traded with several partners and sought to expand its options. The United States was its largest customer for coffee, and Washington sought to strengthen ties through a reciprocal trade agreement signed in 1935. Germany, however, offered something different. Under Nazi economic policy, Germany conducted much of its foreign trade through bilateral clearing arrangements using compensation marks, which allowed Brazil to sell cotton, coffee, and other goods to Germany in exchange for German manufactures when its dollar earnings were constrained. By the late 1930s Germany had become one of Brazil's two leading trading partners. German firms also sold arms and equipment, and German-Brazilian trade offered an outlet for goods that Depression markets could not absorb.

Brazilian decision makers were divided. Some army leaders, including Góis Monteiro and Dutra, admired German military efficiency and favored military modernization with German equipment; a major contract with the Krupp company in 1938 for artillery was later blocked by the British blockade. Others, notably Foreign Minister Osvaldo Aranha, favored closer ties with the United States and pressed for American credit and cooperation. The Navy tended to look toward Britain and the United States. Vargas himself moved cautiously. In 1938 the government banned Nazi Party activities in Brazil and the German ambassador, Karl Ritter, was declared persona non grata after a diplomatic quarrel. In 1939 Aranha negotiated in Washington for credits and gold. In June 1940, aboard a warship, Vargas gave a speech that appeared to welcome a new era of vigorous nations and was widely read as sympathetic to the Axis, a reading that Brazilian diplomats worked to soften. His aim, as historians such as Gerson Moura have argued, was a "pragmatic equidistance" that kept Brazil's options open while seeking material gains from each side.

The strategic stakes were high. Brazil's northeastern bulge, around Natal and Recife, is the closest point in the Americas to West Africa, and in the early 1940s it was the logical staging area for aircraft and ships crossing the South Atlantic. Brazil also held raw materials the Allies valued, including iron ore, manganese, quartz, and rubber. These assets gave Brazil leverage. Vargas bargained: the United States wanted air bases and raw materials, and Brazil wanted financing for the steelworks, arms, and diplomatic recognition of its status. Washington's willingness to fund Volta Redonda, after earlier reluctance, followed from this logic, and was in part a consequence of competition with Germany for Brazilian goodwill.

It would be wrong to reduce Brazilian foreign policy either to ideological sympathy or to foreign manipulation. Brazilian leaders acted on a set of national objectives: industrialization, military modernization, and security. Vargas used international competition to pursue them, accepting aid from the United States while maintaining trade with Germany until war made that impossible. The United States, for its part, pursued its own aims, including hemispheric security and access to resources, under the "Good Neighbor" approach. The outcome was a bargain between two unequal parties, in which Brazil gained resources and status, and the United States gained bases and materials.

Section 76.16

Brazil in World War II: The Allied War Effort and the Contradictions of Dictatorship

When war began in Europe in 1939, Brazil declared neutrality. The pivotal events came in 1941–1942. After the attack on Pearl Harbor, the American republics met in Rio de Janeiro in January 1942, and Brazil broke diplomatic relations with Germany, Italy, and Japan. Earlier that month, and in the following months, Brazil negotiated a set of agreements with the United States, including permission to use northeastern airfields, financing, military supplies under Lend-Lease, and arrangements to produce strategic raw materials, among them the rubber that Japan's advances in Southeast Asia had cut off. The "rubber battle" drew tens of thousands of migrants, mostly from the Northeast, to the Amazon, where many suffered disease and abandonment; estimates of the number and of deaths vary widely.

German submarines began sinking Brazilian merchant ships in early 1942, and in mid-August a series of attacks off the coasts of Bahia and Sergipe killed several hundred people, mostly civilians. Public outrage was intense, with demonstrations and attacks on property of Germans, Italians, and Japanese in several cities. On 22 August the government recognized belligerency against Germany and Italy. Decree No. 10,358, issued on 31 August 1942, separately established a domestic state of war throughout Brazilian territory and suspended specified constitutional guarantees. Its operative provisions did not name Germany or Italy; the decree should not be confused with the preceding foreign-policy decision. Brazil then cooperated closely with the United States, with Natal becoming a major air transit hub, the Brazilian navy escorting convoys, and air patrols guarding Atlantic routes. Wartime security measures also increased persecution of immigrants from Axis countries: thousands were relocated or interned, including thousands of Japanese and Germans expelled from coastal areas near Santos in 1943.

Brazil was the only Latin American country to send a ground force to fight in Europe. A popular saying that it was likelier for a snake to smoke than for Brazil to fight became the unit's emblem, a smoking snake. The Brazilian Expeditionary Force (FEB), created in 1943 and numbering about 25,000 men, began arriving in Italy in July 1944 and was integrated into the U.S. Fifth Army. It entered combat in September 1944, took part in the winter fighting in the northern Apennines, captured the strategic position of Monte Castello in February 1945, and participated in the final offensive that ended with the surrender of a German division and other forces at Fornovo in late April 1945. Roughly 450 Brazilian personnel died in the Italian campaign; published tallies differ by service and casualty category, so a single exact figure without specifying its scope would be misleading. A Brazilian fighter group also flew missions with the U.S. Air Force, and Brazilian nurses served with the force. The FEB was equipped and supplied largely by the United States, and it fought as part of an American-led command, which shaped both its experience and the memory of it.

The war created an acute contradiction. A dictatorship that censored the press and imprisoned its opponents sent soldiers abroad to fight regimes widely described as enemies of freedom. Returning soldiers and the broader public noticed. In 1943 a group of prominent Minas Gerais figures published a manifesto calling for democratization, in January 1945 a congress of writers demanded political freedom, and in February 1945 a newspaper interview with the politician José Américo de Almeida criticized the regime and ended informal censorship. Vargas responded in stages: in February 1945 he announced that elections would be held, and in April he granted an amnesty that freed political prisoners, including Prestes. Wartime pressures did not cause the regime's fall alone, but they made its authoritarian character harder to sustain.

Division V — The Fall, Return, and Historical Legacy of Getúlio Vargas Section 76.17

The Collapse of the Estado Novo, the 1945 Political Transition, and the Vargas Political Coalition

By early 1945 the Estado Novo faced pressure from several directions. The war was ending in an Allied victory, the Brazilian army had fought alongside democracies, and opposition in the press, in professional associations, and among politicians had become open. Military leaders who had supported the dictatorship, including Góis Monteiro and Dutra, increasingly doubted that it could survive the war. In February 1945 an additional constitutional act set a date for elections, and the government legalized parties. The question was no longer whether the regime would change, but who would control the transition.

Three parties took shape in 1945. The National Democratic Union (UDN), formed in April, brought together opponents of Vargas, including liberals, former oligarchs, and critics of the dictatorship, and nominated Air Force Brigadier Eduardo Gomes for president. The Social Democratic Party (PSD), created mainly by the interventors and state machines that had run the regime, nominated Dutra, the former war minister. The Brazilian Labor Party (PTB), created largely by figures linked to the Labor Ministry and the official unions, was designed to channel the support Vargas had built among workers. The Communist Party was legalized in April and recovered its leaders, including Prestes, who left prison under the amnesty. Thus the main political currents of the next two decades were born in the last months of the dictatorship.

Vargas's supporters launched a movement called Queremismo, from the slogan "We want Getúlio" (queremos Getúlio), which called for him to remain in office and for a constituent assembly before elections. It drew union members, PTB organizers, and some communists who accepted a postponed electoral timetable in exchange for a broader democratic opening. Its opponents read it as a maneuver to keep Vargas in power. The tension peaked in late October when Vargas appointed his brother, Benjamim Vargas, as chief of police in Rio, a move the military leaders interpreted as preparation for a coup. On 29 October 1945 army leaders, led by Góis Monteiro, surrounded the presidential palace, and Vargas resigned. The chief justice of the Supreme Federal Tribunal, José Linhares, took office as caretaker.

The election of 2 December 1945 was won by Dutra, who received roughly 55 percent of the vote against about 35 percent for Gomes and about 10 percent for the communist candidate. Dutra had received Vargas's endorsement shortly before the vote, and PSD and PTB voters carried him to victory. Vargas himself was elected senator from Rio Grande do Sul and deputy in several states, and then returned to his ranch in São Borja. The new constitution of 1946 restored elections, Congress, and civil liberties, though it kept the corporatist labor structure intact and continued to exclude illiterates. In 1947 the government banned the Communist Party.

The central question is why the Estado Novo ended while Vargas's influence survived. The regime fell because its justification, defense against communism and the maintenance of order, no longer held in a postwar world, because the military withdrew its support, and because the contradiction between fighting for democracy abroad and ruling as a dictator at home became untenable. But the regime had created durable assets. The unions, the labor ministry, the PTB, and the political machines of the PSD all derived from it. Workers who had gained legal rights associated those rights with Vargas, and many voted accordingly. The institutions of the dictatorship survived under a democratic constitution, and with them survived the political coalition that Vargas had built.

Section 76.18

Vargas's Electoral Return in 1950 and the Second Presidency, 1951–1954

Dutra's government (1946–1951) opened the economy and spent the foreign exchange reserves accumulated during the war on imports, then restricted imports as reserves fell, while leaving the minimum wage unadjusted amid rising prices. The political class was divided, and the PSD and UDN, which had been rivals in 1945, often cooperated in Congress. Vargas, in São Borja, remained active, maintaining ties to union leaders and PTB organizers, and in 1950 he agreed to run for president as the PTB candidate, with the backing of the small Social Progressive Party of Ademar de Barros. His campaign emphasized industrialization, economic nationalism, and the protection of workers' rights, and he spoke of the need to complete what had been started.

The election of 3 October 1950 was held under the 1946 constitution, with competing candidates and a free press. Vargas won roughly 49 percent of the vote, ahead of Eduardo Gomes of the UDN at about 30 percent and the PSD candidate, Cristiano Machado, at about 21 percent. No runoff was required, since the constitution then allowed a plurality to win. The electorate, though larger than in 1930, still excluded illiterates, and was a minority of the adult population. Vargas's victory reflected strong support in industrial cities and among workers, as well as the split in the PSD, whose leaders were divided in their loyalties. He was inaugurated on 31 January 1951.

The second presidency differed fundamentally from the Estado Novo. Vargas now governed under a constitution that he had not written, with an elected Congress in which he did not command a reliable majority, opposition parties led by the UDN, a vigorous and mostly hostile press, and an independent judiciary. He could not issue decree-laws, censor newspapers, or dissolve Congress. He relied on coalitions, formed with the PSD and with smaller parties, and his cabinets shifted as alliances changed. Many opponents regarded his election as legitimate, but others, especially in the UDN, believed that the former dictator should not have been allowed to return, and some looked toward military solutions.

The coexistence of electoral legitimacy and persistent opposition shaped the administration. Vargas had a mandate, but not an overwhelming one, and the same political class that had been marginalized in 1937 now had institutional platforms from which to attack him. The press, led by newspapers such as Tribuna da Imprensa and, in other ways, O Globo, expressed relentless criticism. At the same time, he drew on organized labor, the PTB, and an urban electorate that associated him with social protection. These conditions explain why his second government was both more open and more unstable than his first, and why the political conflicts of 1953–1954 took the form they did.

What Stayed the Same

  • The corporatist union structure, the labor ministry, and the CLT remained in place
  • State-led industrialization and economic nationalism remained central to his program
  • The military remained a political arbiter, as 1945 and 1954 both showed
  • Rural labor and land relations stayed largely outside reform

Who experienced change most directly: Politicians, journalists, and opponents, who could now compete openly. Greater continuity: Urban workers within the labor system and rural workers outside it. Why the pattern matters: It shows that Vargas's political influence did not depend on dictatorship alone, and that the institutions of the 1930s could function in a competitive setting, which also exposed them to new conflict.

Section 76.19

Petrobras, Developmental Nationalism, Labor Conflict, and the Crisis of the Second Government

The second government returned to state-led development, with new institutions. A joint Brazil–United States commission produced project proposals, and in June 1952 the government created the National Economic Development Bank (BNDE) to finance infrastructure and industry, a body that would become the main source of long-term industrial credit in Brazil. The government expanded investment in energy and transport and proposed a state electric holding company, though that was not created until later. Industrial output continued to grow, but the government faced rising inflation, balance-of-payments strain, and fiscal pressure. Prices rose by roughly 20 percent in 1953, and the 1953 decision to adopt a system of multiple exchange rates, designed by Finance Minister Osvaldo Aranha, drew criticism from importers and exporters alike. A strike of several hundred thousand workers in São Paulo in 1953 showed that organized labor would press for wage gains in the face of inflation.

Petroleum was the most emotional issue. A nationalist campaign under the slogan "The oil is ours" had developed since the late 1940s, drawing support from military officers, students, unions, and the left. Vargas's own proposal, sent to Congress in December 1951, envisioned a state company in which private capital could take part. Congress, under pressure from nationalists across parties, including UDN legislators, amended the proposal to create a state monopoly. Law No. 2,004 of 3 October 1953 created Petróleo Brasileiro S.A., Petrobras, and gave it a monopoly over the exploration, production, refining, and transport of crude oil and derivatives, while leaving distribution to private firms. Petrobras began operations in 1954. The law reflected a convergence of economic and nationalist arguments and, notably, was shaped in Congress as much as by the president, which shows how different the second government was from the first.

Labor policy was the second flashpoint. Vargas appointed João "Jango" Goulart, a young PTB leader from Rio Grande do Sul and a rancher with ties to unions, as Labor Minister in June 1953. Goulart cultivated union leaders, supported wage increases, and was accused by opponents of encouraging labor agitation. In February 1954, a memorandum signed by dozens of army colonels and lieutenant colonels, the "Manifesto of the Colonels," complained about low military pay and warned against the labor minister's proposals, and Goulart resigned. On 1 May 1954 Vargas, at the traditional rally, announced a one hundred percent increase in the minimum wage. Business groups and the opposition condemned it as inflationary and demagogic, and supporters regarded it as a restoration of the wage's lost value.

These conflicts intersected with Cold War politics, business opposition, military divisions, and competing definitions of nationalism. Nationalists in the military and the left supported state oil and economic independence, while a faction in the officer corps and the UDN stressed anticommunism and alignment with the United States and warned that labor mobilization was dangerous. The United States ended the joint economic commission's work in 1953 under the incoming Eisenhower administration and was reluctant to extend generous financing. Within Brazil, "nationalism" meant different things: for Vargas's supporters it meant state protection of resources and workers, and for many opponents it meant order and free enterprise within an anticommunist hemispheric alliance. By mid-1954 Vargas faced an organized opposition, a divided military, and inflation, with a Congress where his majority could not be assured.

Section 76.20

The Crisis of August 1954, Vargas's Death, and the Transformation of Brazilian Political Memory

The final crisis began with an attack. Carlos Lacerda, a journalist and politician whose newspaper, the Tribuna da Imprensa, had waged a relentless campaign against Vargas and his circle, had been receiving escorts from young Air Force officers. In the early hours of 5 August 1954, as Lacerda arrived at his apartment building on Rua Tonelero in Rio's Copacabana district, gunmen opened fire. Lacerda was wounded in the foot, and Major Rubens Florentino Vaz, an Air Force officer serving as his escort, was killed. The shooting became a national scandal. The gunman, Alcino João do Nascimento, was captured and linked the attack to members of the presidential guard, including its chief, Gregório Fortunato, a close associate of Vargas. The Air Force opened its own inquiry, conducted at its base in Galeão, and the investigation took on a political character, leading to demands for Vargas's resignation.

Students should distinguish verified developments from allegations. It is established that the attack took place, that Vaz was killed, that the gunman was arrested and implicated members of the guard, and that Fortunato and others were later tried and convicted. What has not been established by documentary evidence is that Vargas ordered or knew of the attack. Historians generally regard the president as having been politically damaged by the conduct of his entourage, and many doubt that he directed it, but the question remained contested at the time and in later accounts. The investigation also uncovered evidence of corruption and influence peddling around the palace, which fed the opposition's campaign.

Military pressure mounted in the second half of August. Army generals and other officers issued manifestos calling for the president to step aside, and opposition politicians demanded resignation or impeachment. Vargas considered a leave of absence, and cabinet meetings on 23 and 24 August discussed possible solutions. On the morning of 24 August 1954 he was informed that the military leaders insisted on his departure. Later that morning, in his bedroom in the Catete Palace, Vargas shot himself in the chest and died. A political testament known as the carta-testamento circulated in typewritten form and was broadcast over the radio after his death. The public text denounced foreign economic interests and domestic opponents, defended his record on labor and national resources, and described his death as a final act of sacrifice. Its celebrated conclusion portrayed his departure from life as an entry into history. The circulated typescript is not identical to earlier handwritten material preserved among his papers; the drafting and editorial role of speechwriter José Soares Maciel Filho has been debated. The famous public formulation should therefore be attributed to the circulated text, not treated as an exact transcription of an uncontested Vargas autograph.

The public reaction was immediate and intense. Crowds gathered in Rio and in other cities, and mourners lined the route of the funeral procession. Demonstrators attacked the offices of opposition newspapers and, in Porto Alegre, the American consulate, in expressions of grief and anger that surprised the opposition. The military leaders who had demanded his resignation suddenly found their position weakened. Vice President Café Filho assumed the presidency, and elections were held in October 1954 as scheduled. In the following year, the presidential election was won by Juscelino Kubitschek, in an alliance of the PSD and the PTB, with Goulart as vice president, showing that Vargas's coalition remained politically powerful. The attempt to prevent that outcome by military means in November 1955 failed (Chapter 84 returns to the longer sequence that ended in 1964).

Vargas's legacy has been interpreted in contrasting ways. To some he was a modernizing statesman who built a national state and industrial base, to others the architect of labor protections that gave workers lasting rights, a nationalist who defended Brazilian resources, an authoritarian ruler who dissolved democracy and suppressed opponents, or an enduring symbol, around whom political currents of different tendencies organized for decades. Each view finds support in evidence: the labor code and Volta Redonda, the censorship and torture of the Estado Novo, the electoral victory of 1950, and the funeral crowds of 1954. His death did not resolve the institutional contradictions he left. The labor system, the role of the military as arbiter, the relationship between state and industry, and the unresolved questions of rural land and racial inequality remained at the center of Brazilian politics, as later chapters will show.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Getúlio Vargas transformed Brazil by building a centralized state that expanded labor protections, national industry, and public institutions while concentrating political power and, between 1937 and 1945, abolishing electoral competition. The central contradiction is not resolved by choosing between admiration and condemnation; it is explained by examining how institutions, coalitions, and circumstances combined.

The Old Republic was a federal system dominated by regional machines, with a tiny electorate and recurring conflict among oligarchies. The "café com leite" label oversimplifies a shifting bargain. In 1929–1930 a succession dispute, junior-officer discontent, and the Depression broke that bargain. The uprising of 3 October 1930, the military deposition of 24 October, and Vargas's assumption of power on 3 November were distinct stages, and none produced a stable democracy.

The provisional government centralized authority through federal interventors, new ministries, and national commodity and administrative bodies, while bargaining with regional elites. São Paulo's armed Constitutionalist Revolution of 1932 was defeated, yet it accelerated constitutional reform. The Electoral Code of 1932 introduced the secret ballot, electoral courts, and women's suffrage, and the Constitution of 1934 confirmed them and added social rights.

Constitutional politics from 1934 to 1937 proved fragile. Integralism and the National Liberation Alliance polarized the country, the communist uprisings of November 1935 were crushed, and emergency laws and special courts expanded the security state. The fabricated Cohen Plan, exploited by military leaders and the government, provided the pretext for the coup of 10 November 1937 and the Estado Novo.

The Estado Novo functioned through a presidential constitution, interventors, a modernized bureaucracy, political police, censorship, and propaganda. It suppressed communists and Integralists alike. It was not a copy of European fascism, but it was an authoritarian regime with real repressive capacity.

Labor policy created state-recognized unions, a compulsory union tax, labor courts, the minimum wage, and, in 1943, the CLT, which consolidated a decade of legislation. These measures gave urban formal workers lasting rights and a language of citizenship, and they restricted union independence and strikes. Rural workers, domestic workers, and informal workers were largely excluded, and enforcement depended on workers' ability to use the system.

Propaganda and education built the image of the "father of the poor" and promoted a cultural nationalism that celebrated mixture while leaving racial inequality intact. Everyday experience varied sharply among industrial workers, women, Afro-Brazilians, Indigenous peoples, and rural families.

Economic policy shifted authority over coffee, credit, and trade to federal agencies and created state enterprises such as CSN and Vale, financed and equipped with foreign help. Vargas bargained between Germany and the United States, then aligned with the Allies after 1942. Brazil sent an expeditionary force to Italy, and the contradiction of a dictatorship fighting for democracy contributed to the regime's fall on 29 October 1945.

The regime ended, but its institutions and coalition survived in the PSD, the PTB, and the labor system. Vargas returned by election in 1950 and governed under constitutional constraints that produced the Petrobras monopoly and intense conflict over wages, inflation, and nationalism. The crisis of August 1954 ended in his suicide and a letter that reshaped political memory. Historians continue to weigh his achievements and his authoritarianism, and the institutions he built remained at the center of Brazilian politics.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a state can expand social rights and restrict political rights in the same years, and why that combination is hard to classify. Vargas's Brazil was not a revolutionary society like Mexico's nor a mass-party regime like Argentina's would be; it was built from decrees, ministries, courts, and a loyal military. Its institutions gave urban workers lasting legal recognition while leaving the rural majority outside it.

The chapter also shows how much depended on contingency and on the choices of many actors. The Revolution of 1930 was a coalition of rivals, the Estado Novo grew from a forged document and military calculation, and the regime fell because its own wartime alliances undermined it. Learning to read laws, speeches, and images as products of particular interests is a skill that recurs throughout Volume IV.
