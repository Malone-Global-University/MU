Columbus and the Caribbean Encounter, 1492–1504

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: When Encounter Became Colonization

On 12 October 1492 a small Castilian fleet met the people of a Bahamian island; by 1500 a royal investigator was arresting the fleet's admiral on Hispaniola, where Spanish settlements, gold demands, forced labor, and armed conflict were already reshaping island life.

This chapter follows the period from 1492 to 1504, in which the two worlds prepared in Chapters 21 and 25–26 collided in the Caribbean. It is not a biography of Christopher Columbus. He supplies a chronological spine, but the subject is the Caribbean encounter and the rapid transformation of a reconnaissance voyage into settlement, extraction, coercion, and violence.

The central argument is that Columbus's voyages did not simply reveal unknown lands to Europeans. They began a contested relationship between Iberian newcomers and established Caribbean societies. Exploration, exchange, and mutual uncertainty gave way to demands for gold and labor, captive-taking, tribute, armed resistance, and colonial rule on Hispaniola. Meanwhile Europeans struggled to understand what they had found, interpreting islands and peoples through expectations shaped by Asia, Christianity, commerce, and earlier Atlantic expansion.

Indigenous people are actors throughout: Lucayans who gave the first recorded welcome, caciques such as Guacanagaríx and Caonabo who tried to manage the newcomers, and communities who fed, resisted, fled, and negotiated. The evidence, however, is overwhelmingly European: Columbus's writings, Bartolomé de las Casas's abstract of his journal, royal correspondence, and later chronicles. Historians must read these against archaeology and Indigenous history, asking constantly what the authors wanted and what they could not see.

The chapter deliberately leaves three subjects to neighboring chapters: the Treaty of Tordesillas and Iberian rivalry (Chapter 28), epidemic disease and demographic collapse (Chapter 29), and Cortés and the Maya coast (Chapter 30). Here we note only that Indigenous communities suffered hunger, displacement, violence, and mortality, and that Spanish claims soon provoked Portuguese and papal response.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the four voyages of Columbus, giving the principal dates and places of each.
  2. Explain why the first voyage was exploratory and the second a colonizing expedition.
  3. Analyze the Guanahaní encounter, distinguishing Indigenous hospitality from political submission.
  4. Describe the political pluralism of Hispaniola and the strategies of caciques such as Guacanagaríx and Caonabo.
  5. Explain the failures of La Navidad and La Isabela without treating Spanish colonization as inevitable.
  6. Trace the progression from exchange to demand, tribute, captivity, and coerced labor.
  7. Assess Indigenous resistance and the escalation of Spanish violence.
  8. Distinguish Columbus's skill as a navigator from his record as governor.
  9. Evaluate European categories such as “Carib” and “cannibal” as sources requiring criticism.
  10. Interpret how European conceptions moved from “the Indies” toward suspicion of a separate geography.
  11. Evaluate the transmission problem of Columbus's journal as historical evidence.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

Chapter 21 examined the Caribbean before sustained European colonization: Lucayan communities in the Bahamas, Taíno chiefdoms in the Greater Antilles under leaders called caciques, farming based on cassava, canoe exchange among islands, and Kalinago peoples in the Lesser Antilles. Chapter 25 traced Castile's Reconquista, monarchy, Christian expansion, and Atlantic ventures in the Canaries; Chapter 26 traced Portuguese voyages along West Africa, enslavement, and early plantation economies. Columbus carried these experiences with him: the Canaries as a model of conquest and settlement, West Africa as a model of coastal trade in gold and captives.

He also carried a proposal. Persuaded that Asia lay within reach by sailing west, and supported by Ferdinand and Isabella after the fall of Granada in 1492, he expected trade with the lands described by Marco Polo. His estimate of the Atlantic's width was wrong; unknown to him, two continents stood between Europe and Asia.

On the Caribbean side, no polity was a single “Taíno government.” Hispaniola contained several cacicazgos with their own rivalries, and the Bahamas, Cuba, and the Lesser Antilles differed in language, politics, and relations. Treating “Taíno” as one label, or accepting Columbus's division of peoples into peaceful and “Carib,” obscures this.

Section 27.1

The Atlantic Crossing of 1492

The voyage of 1492 grew out of a proposal, a negotiation, and a wager on geography. Columbus, a Genoese mariner with experience in Portuguese and Castilian Atlantic waters, argued that Asia could be reached by sailing west. His case rested on an estimate of the Earth's circumference that was much smaller than the figures accepted by most informed contemporaries, and on a generous reading of the eastward extent of Asia drawn from Ptolemy, Marco Polo, and later commentators. Portuguese advisers, who had spent decades measuring Atlantic distances along the African coast, judged the distance to Asia far too great. Columbus was right that the Atlantic could be crossed, but wrong about what lay on the other side. The lands he reached stood between Europe and Asia, and neither he nor his critics knew it.

After the conquest of Granada in January 1492, negotiations with Columbus resumed at Santa Fe, culminating in royal agreement that spring. The Capitulations of Santa Fe, concluded in April, promised Columbus the titles of Admiral of the Ocean Sea, viceroy, and governor of any lands he found, along with a share of the proceeds. These terms mattered later: they made Columbus not merely a captain but a prospective governor, and they explain why his removal in 1500 was so contested. The Crown's motives combined commerce, rivalry with Portugal, and a religious vision in which the spread of Christianity and the acquisition of wealth were closely linked, themes examined in Chapter 25.

The expedition consisted of three ships, the larger Santa María and the smaller Niña and Pinta, with roughly ninety men, many of them from the ports of Palos and Moguer in Andalusia. Martín Alonso Pinzón and Vicente Yáñez Pinzón, experienced local mariners, captained the Pinta and the Niña respectively, and their local knowledge was essential to recruiting the crews. The fleet left Palos on 3 August 1492 and stopped at the Canary Islands, already a Castilian possession, for repairs and provisions. It sailed westward from La Gomera in early September.

The decisive technical fact was the Atlantic wind system. Columbus, drawing on earlier Portuguese and Castilian experience, sailed south to the latitude of the Canaries and picked up the steady trade winds that blow from east to west, then returned home by turning north into the belt of westerly winds. This knowledge, rather than any single act of daring, made repeated round trips possible. Chapter 25 has examined the navigational background; the point here is that the route was reproducible, which meant that once Columbus returned, further voyages could follow quickly.

The journal reports growing uneasiness among the crew as the fleet passed the distance Columbus had predicted. Popular accounts describe a near mutiny, but historians treat the details cautiously, since the surviving text is filtered through later summary and Columbus had reasons to present himself as steadfast. Land was sighted in the early hours of 12 October 1492, and the fleet anchored off an island the local people called Guanahaní. Columbus named it San Salvador and claimed it for the Crown before a notary.

Which island this was remains disputed. The island now called San Salvador was known as Watling's Island until 1925, when the Bahamian government officially transferred the name San Salvador to it because it was then widely identified as Columbus's first landfall. Other candidates, including Samana Cay, have also been proposed by scholars reconstructing the route from the journal's sailing directions. The disagreement stems from the ambiguity of the text and from uncertainty about Columbus's reckoning, and historians have not reached consensus.

The methodological point is fundamental. Historians know the first voyage largely through European documentation. The original journal Columbus presented to Ferdinand and Isabella is lost, and the main surviving text is an abstract, with some passages copied and others summarized, made by Bartolomé de las Casas, who later wrote a history of the Indies. Columbus's letter of 1493 announcing the voyage and the biography attributed to his son Ferdinand supplement it. All are European, all reflect Columbus's purposes or Las Casas's later ones, and none records what the Lucayans thought was happening. Anything said about Indigenous perceptions must be reconstructed with care from archaeology and from what is known of Lucayan society, introduced in Chapter 21.

Section 27.2

Guanahaní: The First Recorded Encounter

Guanahaní was inhabited by Lucayans, a people whose ancestors had moved into the Bahamas from the Greater Antilles around the ninth century CE and who shared language and culture with the Taíno-speaking communities of Hispaniola and Cuba. They lived in villages, farmed, fished, and traveled between islands by canoe. To them the arrival of three large ships was a remarkable event, but islanders were used to visitors and exchange, and the first recorded reaction was curiosity and generosity rather than alarm.

According to the journal, a large number of people came to the shore and then out to the ships, some swimming, others in canoes. They brought parrots, balls of cotton thread, and darts, and accepted small items such as glass beads and bells in return. Columbus described them as unarmed by European standards, noted that some had scars, and recorded that they explained by gestures that people from other islands came to capture them. Scholars interpret this as evidence of raiding among islands, but the description depends on gestures and on Columbus's interpretation, and cannot be fully verified.

From the beginning Columbus's attention turned to gold. He noticed small ornaments worn in the nose and asked where the metal came from, and he understood the answer, whether accurately or not, to mean that gold could be found to the south and that a king lived on a larger land. This reading shaped his next moves, as the following section shows. Gold mattered because it was the most portable measure of success for a mission that had cost the Crown and promised profit to its sponsors.

The journal also reveals how quickly the relationship turned to control. Columbus wrote that the people would make good servants, that they seemed to have little understanding of religion and could easily be made Christians, and that with a small force they could be subjected. He took several of the people from Guanahaní aboard the ships as guides and prospective interpreters, some apparently by force. This is the beginning of the practice of captive-taking that recurs throughout the chapter and that later became a large-scale system.

On landing, Columbus performed a formal ceremony of possession. With the royal banner and officials present to record the act, he claimed the island for Ferdinand and Isabella. The Lucayans neither understood nor consented to this ritual, which belonged to a Castilian legal and political practice for formally recording claims of possession. What looked like a friendly first meeting therefore contained radically different assumptions about land, sovereignty, commerce, and human status.

A major interpretive point follows. Indigenous hospitality should not be mistaken for political submission. Gift exchange and welcome were normal ways of managing strangers and creating obligations, and did not imply any acceptance of Castilian authority. Columbus, reading hospitality as weakness, and Spanish writers after him repeatedly misread Caribbean diplomacy in this way, with consequences that grow more serious in later sections. The Lucayans' own later fate, including enslavement and depopulation of the Bahamas, belongs largely to the story continued in Chapters 29 and 33 and beyond, and is not narrated here.

The evidence for all of this deserves a final caution. The journal's account of Lucayan gestures, scars, and words is Columbus's understanding of a conversation conducted without a shared language, and the captives who could later have explained events left no surviving record of their own. Archaeology of Lucayan settlements shows farming villages, shell and stone tools, and connections to the wider Caribbean, and this material evidence is a necessary check on the European text. It cannot, however, recover what individual people said or intended on 12 October, and students should hold the scene with the humility that its sources require.

Section 27.3

Cuba, Hispaniola, and the Search for Asia

After leaving Guanahaní the fleet moved through the Bahamas, visiting several islands that Columbus renamed. Throughout, he sought a large land and its ruler, and used the people he had taken aboard as guides. They pointed him toward what he understood as a mainland to the south and west. He reached Cuba on 28 October 1492 and named it Juana. Struck by the size of the land, he concluded that it was part of the Asian mainland and that he was near the domains of the Great Khan, whom Marco Polo had described.

Interpreting what he found meant fitting new observations into an inherited framework. That framework combined Ptolemy's geography, the travel narrative of Marco Polo, the imaginative account attributed to Mandeville, cosmographic writings such as those of Pierre d'Ailly, and correspondence with Florentine scholars about a western route to Asia. The names Cipangu (Japan) and Cathay (China) came from these texts. When Indigenous people spoke of places, Columbus tended to hear them as Asian names. A frequently repeated suggestion, though not certain, is that a Cuban name for the interior was heard as a reference to the Great Khan.

The search took practical form. Columbus sent two men inland, Luis de Torres, who spoke Hebrew and some Arabic, and Rodrigo de Jerez, to find a ruler and deliver royal letters. They found a large village, not a court, and reported unfamiliar customs including the smoking of rolled leaves. The absence of the cities and markets described by Polo did not shake Columbus's conviction. He instead explained it by supposing that the expedition had not yet reached the right place, an argument that helped him retain the Asian framework for the rest of his life.

In late November the Pinta, under Martín Alonso Pinzón, left the fleet, apparently in search of gold, and did not return until January. Columbus continued east and reached the northern coast of the island he called La Española, Hispaniola, in early December. The people there called the land by names later recorded as Ayti and Quisqueya. The name Cibao, given to a gold-bearing interior region, sounded to Columbus like Cipangu, and this apparent confirmation of Japan encouraged him.

Hispaniola was densely settled and politically organized. Its people, whom Columbus found more numerous and more elaborately governed than the Lucayans, used gold in ornaments and pointed to its interior sources. This was the fact that reoriented his voyage. Cuba had disappointed, but here gold was present and available for trade, and the island's size and population suggested a place worth returning to. The next sections examine how Hispaniola's political landscape shaped the encounter that followed.

The point is not simply that Columbus was wrong. It is that new information was interpreted through an inherited geographic framework, and such frameworks decide what observers see and report. Columbus described real places and peoples, but named them for Asian destinations, reported Indigenous speech through his own expectations, and saw commercial prospects in every rumor of gold. The resulting text is invaluable as evidence and dangerous as description. Modern readers must separate what Columbus saw from what he thought it meant, a distinction that becomes important again when the chapter examines how European ideas about these lands changed after 1498 and how the Asian framework eventually collapsed.

The geography also shows how limited Columbus's knowledge remained. He did not know the size of Cuba, the extent of Hispaniola, or the existence of a mainland to the west, and the Indigenous guides who described larger lands did so from a knowledge of regional sea routes that the Europeans could only partly follow. Their information, filtered through gesture and a small shared vocabulary, was decisive for the route of the voyage and is one of the clearest early examples of how Indigenous knowledge underlay European exploration. The loss of the Santa María at Christmas, described in Section 27.5, would make that dependence even more visible.

Section 27.4

Taíno Political Worlds and the Encounter on Hispaniola

Chapter 21 examined Taíno society in detail, so only what the encounter requires is recalled here. Taíno-speaking communities on Hispaniola lived in villages of farmers who grew cassava, maize, and other crops, and who fished, hunted, and traveled by canoe. Communities were organized under caciques, whose authority rested on lineage, ritual roles, the management of exchange, and the redistribution of food and goods. Political life also involved ceremony: the ball game played on plazas, communal song and dance, and rituals involving carved images called zemís. This was a society with its own institutions, not a blank canvas awaiting European order.

Hispaniola was politically plural. Later Spanish writers, especially Las Casas and Oviedo, described the island as divided among several principal cacicazgos, usually listed as Marién in the northwest, Maguá in the Vega Real, Maguana in the interior south of the Cibao, Jaragua in the west, and Higüey in the east. That scheme should be treated with care, since it was written down by outsiders after conquest had begun and may impose a neater map on fluid alliances and rivalries. But the essential point is well supported: the Spanish were not entering a single kingdom. They entered a landscape of leaders such as Guacanagaríx in the north, Guarionex in the Vega Real, Caonabo in Maguana, and Bohechío in Jaragua, whose sister Anacaona would later become important. These leaders competed, allied, and married across community lines.

Guacanagaríx received Columbus in December 1492 with gifts and hospitality. The journal describes exchanges of cloth, a mask, and gold ornaments. For Taíno elites, objects of gold and of guanín, an alloy prized for its color and spiritual associations, were valued as markers of rank and ritual power rather than as money. Exchange with strangers created relationships and obligations. It is reasonable to suppose, though the sources do not say so directly, that Guacanagaríx also saw advantages in a link with powerful newcomers whose ships and weapons might strengthen his position against rivals. The Spanish, for their part, tended to read caciques as petty kings and their gifts as evidence of accessible wealth.

Indigenous leaders tried to understand and manage the newcomers using the categories available to them. The Spaniards' origin, purposes, and status were unclear. Were they visitors, allies, or dangerous outsiders, and did their presence carry spiritual significance? We know little of how Taíno people answered such questions, because the fullest texts were written by Europeans. Later Spanish narratives claim that some Indigenous people thought the newcomers came from the sky. Such claims should be read skeptically, since they also flattered Spanish assumptions, and may reflect the way Europeans translated respect for guests into the language of awe.

Two features of this encounter are essential for what follows. First, the meeting was not between Europeans and a united Indigenous world. Guacanagaríx's decisions were shaped by his own position among competing leaders, and the friendship he offered was that of one cacique, not a whole island. Second, Indigenous leaders had power to shape events. Their food, guidance, labor, and diplomacy kept Spanish enterprises alive at critical moments, and their choices to cooperate, withdraw, or resist changed outcomes. This chapter therefore avoids the simple binary of Europeans against Indigenous people and treats the encounter as a contest among many parties whose interests diverged.

Source criticism matters here as well. Names, boundaries, and the number and rank of caciques come mainly from writers such as Las Casas, Oviedo, and Peter Martyr, who wrote for European audiences and sometimes decades later. Archaeology adds an independent check, showing village layouts, ritual sites, and trade goods, but cannot by itself identify a particular cacique's decisions. Historians therefore combine documents with material evidence, and they treat statements about Indigenous motives as inferences rather than established facts.

Section 27.5

La Navidad: From Hospitality to Foothold

On the night of 24–25 December 1492, the Santa María ran onto a reef off the northern coast of Hispaniola. The journal blames a helmsman who had been left in charge while Columbus slept, and the ship could not be freed. The disaster made the expedition dependent on local help. Guacanagaríx sent canoes and people who worked to salvage the ship's cargo, and according to the journal the goods were stored safely and nothing was taken. This aid, given by a community with no obligation to the strangers, was a diplomatic act with consequences. It committed Guacanagaríx to the newcomers, and made the Spanish his guests and debtors.

With only two ships remaining, Columbus decided to leave part of the company on the island. He used timbers from the wrecked ship to build a fort, which he named La Navidad, Christmas, and left thirty-nine men under Diego de Arana with provisions, trade goods, and weapons. The men were intended to gather gold and information until he returned. Columbus left in early January 1493 aboard the Niña, met the Pinta, which rejoined the fleet, and after a storm-tossed crossing reached Portugal and then Palos in March.

The decision matters more than the fort's small size suggests. La Navidad was not yet a functioning colony. But leaving armed Europeans in a foreign land turned a voyage of reconnaissance into a continuing foreign presence, dependent on Indigenous hospitality, food, and protection, and it created a situation in which Spaniards would interact with Hispaniolan communities with no captain to restrain them.

When Columbus returned in November 1493, the fort had been burned and the men were dead. Reports of what happened differ, and every account has its own interests. Guacanagaríx and his representatives blamed Caonabo and another neighboring ruler for the attack and said that Guacanagaríx had been wounded while defending the Spaniards. When Diego Álvarez Chanca and an unnamed fleet surgeon examined his bandaged leg, however, Chanca reported that they could detect no obvious injury, deepening Spanish suspicion about the account. Later narratives offer an explanation drawn from within the garrison: after Columbus left, the men quarreled among themselves, dispersed in search of gold and women, took goods and people from surrounding communities, and provoked retaliation. Diego Álvarez Chanca, the fleet's physician, and later writers such as Las Casas provide the main details.

The evidence supports careful inference rather than a single certain story. The sources agree that the men died and the fort was destroyed. They indicate that Spanish misconduct, including demands for gold, sexual violence, and conflict with the surrounding communities, formed part of the context. They also show that Guacanagaríx stood in a difficult position between the Spaniards and rivals such as Caonabo. The destruction should not be presented as an unexplained Taíno attack. It was a violent breakdown in a relationship that armed newcomers had strained, in a politically divided island, and it is best understood as a case of early colonial violence with several possible authors.

Archaeology has contributed to the picture. Work at En Bas Saline in northern Haiti, directed by Kathleen Deagan and colleagues, has been identified as a major Taíno town, probably Guacanagaríx's principal settlement, and the site has produced Spanish material from the period. The exact location of La Navidad itself has not been definitively established, and scholars remain cautious. What the excavations show is a substantial Indigenous community in which contact goods appear early, consistent with the documentary record of exchange and of Spanish presence.

The source problem deserves emphasis. Everything known about La Navidad's fate comes from Spaniards, chiefly Chanca, Columbus, and writers such as Las Casas who drew on participants, and their accounts do not agree on who acted, when, or why. Chanca's skepticism about Guacanagaríx's wound shows that even contemporaries doubted the version they heard, while Columbus himself, dependent on the cacique's continuing goodwill, chose not to act against him. No Indigenous account survives. Historians therefore cannot reconstruct one uncontested sequence. They can weigh possibilities: internal disorder among the garrison, an attack by rivals of Guacanagaríx, his complicity, or some combination. What can be said is that the foothold collapsed within a year of its founding.

Section 27.6

The Second Voyage and the Beginning of Colonization, 1493

The second voyage marks the major break in the chapter. The first voyage had been exploratory. The second was a colonizing expedition, planned as such by the Crown and organized in Andalusia. The fleet sailed from Cádiz on 25 September 1493 with seventeen ships and, by most estimates, between 1,200 and 1,500 people. They included soldiers, officials, craftsmen, farmers, priests, and gentlemen hoping for wealth, along with horses, other livestock, seeds, cuttings, tools, and supplies. The presence of clergy, among them Bernardo Boil, reflected a stated purpose of converting Indigenous people, and royal instructions combined that purpose with the search for gold and orders to treat local people well.

The Crown's expectations mattered. Ferdinand and Isabella, who had received Columbus's report of the first voyage and had sought papal support in 1493, now regarded these lands as a royal enterprise requiring administration. The papal bulls of that year and the Portuguese reaction, examined in Chapter 28, are noted here only to indicate that the expedition was sent into a diplomatic context of competing claims. The fleet's size and equipment show that its planners intended to remain.

The route took the fleet by way of the Canary Islands and then across the Atlantic on a more southerly course than the first voyage. Land was sighted in early November 1493 at an island Columbus named Dominica, in the Lesser Antilles. The expedition then passed through a chain of islands, including Marie-Galante and Guadeloupe, and encountered communities that Europeans identified as Carib or Kalinago. Chanca's letter and other accounts describe villages, canoes, and captives, including women held by the local community whom the Spaniards freed or took, and a skirmish at an island Columbus named Santa Cruz. The fleet then continued past the Virgin Islands to Puerto Rico and reached Hispaniola in late November 1493.

These encounters require source criticism. The label Carib, and the accusation of cannibalism attached to it, came to Columbus from Taíno and Lucayan informants and were amplified by European expectations. Scholars debate whether ritual cannibalism was practiced in particular communities. The label soon acquired legal as well as rhetorical consequences. Spaniards increasingly used classifications such as Carib, cannibal, and hostile to justify captive-taking, and in 1503 the Crown explicitly authorized enslavement, under specified circumstances, of people classified as Caribs or cannibals. The Spanish accounts thus tell us about European categories and interests as much as about Kalinago society, described in Chapter 21.

The qualitative change is clear. In 1492 Columbus had sailed to find a route, make contact, and gather information. In 1493 ships were arriving to occupy, provision, evangelize, extract, and remain. The expedition brought the animals and plants that would begin to transform the island's environment, a subject reserved for Chapter 29, and the people who would try to build Iberian institutions on Caribbean ground. That is why later historians treat the second voyage, more than the first, as the beginning of colonization.

When the fleet reached Hispaniola, it came upon the ruins of La Navidad, and the newcomers' first experience of the island was not welcome but loss. The men who had come to settle discovered that the territory they meant to occupy was inhabited by communities with their own leaders, strategies, and grievances, some of whom were already hostile. Their expectations of quick and easy wealth faced their first serious test.

Section 27.7

La Isabela and the Problems of the First Colony

Columbus decided not to rebuild at La Navidad. Instead, in early January 1494 he founded a new settlement on the northern coast to the east, which he called La Isabela in honor of the queen. The site offered a harbor and a route toward the Cibao, where Indigenous informants had said gold could be found. Choosing it also allowed Columbus to establish a base independent of the ruins and the politics attached to them, although it distanced the Spaniards from Guacanagaríx's people.

The settlement was ambitious in conception. Its planners laid out streets and plazas on an Iberian model and constructed a church, storehouses, and public buildings, including stone structures. It was intended to be a permanent Castilian town, the first in the Americas, with a governing council, a church, and warehouses for trade goods and gold. But conditions quickly overwhelmed the plan. The fortified core occupied a defensible rocky promontory close to the route toward the Cibao, but the open bay offered poor protection from severe weather and the administrative settlement itself was not conveniently located beside freshwater or good agricultural soils. Richer alluvial land and better access to water existed nearby, and archaeology has revealed a secondary settlement at Las Coles that exploited those more favorable conditions. The settlers, meanwhile, arrived to a period of hard labor and poor rations.

Illness spread through the colony. Chanca and others describe widespread sickness, with Columbus among the ill, and archaeological work confirms the toll: excavations of the cemetery at La Isabela found many burials, and skeletal evidence points to disease, overwork, and poor nutrition. European provisions spoiled or ran short, and efforts to reproduce an Iberian food system produced uneven results. Some imported plants germinated rapidly, but crops such as wheat could grow quickly and then fail, while other vegetables and livestock adapted more successfully. The deeper problem was not that the Caribbean environment was generally infertile, but that Spanish expectations about diet, labor, provisioning, and agriculture did not function as planned. Settlers who had expected quick wealth found instead heavy labor, hunger, and dependence.

That dependence was on Indigenous food and knowledge. The Spaniards needed cassava, maize, fish, and local guidance, and they obtained these through exchange, demands, and eventually force. Excavations at La Isabela have recovered a great deal of Taíno pottery in use at the settlement, suggesting that Indigenous goods, and perhaps Indigenous people, were present in daily life, and showing that the town was not a purely European enclave. The colony's survival hinged on the very communities its members increasingly pressured.

Meanwhile the search for gold began. Alonso de Ojeda led an expedition into the Cibao in early 1494, followed by Columbus himself, and the Spaniards built a fort, Santo Tomás, in the interior. They found gold, but in quantities far smaller than expected, mostly collected by Indigenous people in streams. Columbus sent Antonio de Torres back to Castile in February 1494 with a sample of gold, reports, and a request for more supplies. He then left on an exploring expedition toward Cuba and Jamaica, leaving the colony under the command of his brother Diego and a council.

Discontent grew. Settlers quarreled, some conspired to leave, and colonists complained that the enterprise was not producing the profits promised. Kathleen Deagan and José Cruxent, whose archaeological study is the foundation of modern understanding, have argued that La Isabela's failure reflected more than a poor location. Imported European expectations of a familiar economy, social order, and diet did not operate as planned in the Caribbean. Bartolomé Colón established Santo Domingo on the south coast in 1496, and La Isabela was progressively abandoned, effectively gone by about 1498.

The larger importance of La Isabela is that it prevents Spanish colonization from appearing inevitable or competent. The first settlement was fragile, and its fragility helps explain why settlers and officials increasingly attempted to transfer costs onto Indigenous communities through demands for food, gold, and labor. The following section examines that process.

Archaeology has shown that La Isabela consisted of more than one settlement. The fortified core, El Castillo, held the church, storehouse, and main buildings, most of them stone, while most inhabitants lived in wood and thatch. At the nearby Las Coles site archaeologists have identified a service settlement better placed for water and cultivation. This matters because Hispaniola was not an agriculturally poor island. Indigenous communities fed large populations from the same landscape, and the region's alluvial plains were productive. The settlers' recurring hunger therefore reflects a mismatch between what they expected to eat and how they expected to labor, and what the island and its people could readily supply under those conditions. Solving that mismatch, they increasingly concluded, required Indigenous labor.

Section 27.8

Gold, Tribute, Captivity, and Coercive Labor

The central expectation of the second voyage was gold. Columbus had promised the Crown wealth, and the settlers who crossed in 1493 had come largely to acquire it. But Hispaniola's gold was of a kind that did not match those hopes. Indigenous communities gathered it in small quantities from streams and used it mainly for ornaments valued for rank and ritual, not as money. There was no great stockpile to seize and no mining system to take over. The Spaniards' demands therefore exceeded what existing Indigenous economies could supply, and the mismatch between expectation and reality drove the coercion that followed.

The historical process is best understood as a progression: gift exchange, then demands, then tribute, then captive-taking, then coerced labor, and finally a broader colonial domination. The stages overlapped, and they did not unfold at the same speed everywhere, but each depended on the one before. In 1492 and early 1493, gold and goods were exchanged in ways that Indigenous leaders could interpret as diplomacy. By 1494, hungry and disappointed Spaniards were taking food from villages, demanding guides and porters, and punishing what they called theft. Las Casas reports harsh punishments inflicted on Indigenous people by Spanish soldiers in the Cibao, including mutilation, and although Las Casas wrote as a critic and decades later, the pattern of escalating punishment is consistent with other sources.

The tribute system took shape after the campaigns of 1495. According to Las Casas and to Ferdinand Columbus's biography of his father, Columbus required Indigenous adults in the gold-bearing districts to deliver a fixed measure of gold, described as a hawk's bell full, every three months, while communities elsewhere were to supply a quantity of spun cotton. Payments were recorded by tokens that people wore around their necks as proof. The details come from later Spanish writers, and scholars differ over their precise figures, but the system's outlines are widely accepted. It was, in effect, an attempt to convert existing Indigenous tribute practices, described in Section 27.4, into a demand for a commodity that most communities did not possess in such amounts.

The results can be inferred from the sources. Communities that could not find enough gold were pushed toward mining, flight, or resistance, and time spent searching for gold was time taken from cultivation. Spanish accounts themselves suggest that many people fled to the mountains rather than comply. The system therefore produced hunger, dislocation, and further conflict, and by the late 1490s Columbus was allowing some communities to substitute labor service for gold. This substitution pointed toward compulsory labor as the eventual form of extraction.

Captive-taking ran alongside tribute. After the fighting of early 1495, Spaniards rounded up large numbers of Indigenous people, and in February of that year Antonio de Torres carried a shipment of captives to Castile. Michele da Cuneo, who returned with the fleet, reported that 550 captives were loaded for Spain; Las Casas later wrote of more than 500. Cuneo reported that roughly 200 died during the Atlantic crossing. When the survivors reached Castile, an initial royal order of 12 April 1495 permitted their sale, but a second order four days later suspended it while jurists and theologians considered whether the captives could lawfully be sold. The reversal exposed an unresolved tension between royal claims that Indigenous people were subjects and colonial demands for captive labor.

Forced labor developed as tribute proved unworkable. Spaniards obliged Indigenous people to carry supplies, build fortifications and houses, plant fields, and work the gold streams, and the Roldán settlement of 1498–1499, discussed in Section 27.10, granted rebel settlers land and the labor of Indigenous communities. These grants are the first documented allocations of Indigenous labor to individual Spaniards on Hispaniola. Historians therefore see them as precedents for the encomienda and repartimiento systems, but this chapter does not treat them as fully formed institutions. Those systems, examined in Chapter 33 and later, were shaped in part by Ovando after 1502 and by later royal legislation.

Women bore particular burdens. Spanish sources mention the seizure of women, coerced unions, and sexual violence, which were among the grievances that fed hostility, and captured women appear among those shipped to Castile. Indigenous women also performed agricultural and household work on which the Spaniards depended. The sources rarely describe these experiences in women's own voices, and historians must infer them from Spanish reports written from a position of power.

Evidence for this whole process is uneven. Columbus's letters and the reports of Chanca and Cuneo were contemporary, but written by participants with interests to defend. Las Casas and Oviedo wrote later with different agendas. No Indigenous account of the first tribute demands survives. Historians combine these texts, cross-check them, and use archaeology of settlements such as La Isabela to test their claims. What they agree on is the direction of change: by 1500 coercion had become the organizing principle of Spanish rule.

Section 27.9

Indigenous Resistance and Spanish Violence

Indigenous people did not passively absorb these pressures. Their responses ranged across a spectrum from accommodation to open war, and often changed as circumstances did. Individual communities gave food and labor, negotiated with Spaniards, withdrew from contact, and attacked. Leaders weighed the costs of each course and compared them with what their neighbors were doing. This variety is important, because it means that resistance was a set of choices by political actors rather than a single reflex.

One frequently reported form of resistance was flight. Communities moved away from Spanish settlements and routes, especially in the Cibao, and withdrew to mountain and forest areas beyond easy reach. Spanish writers also claimed that Indigenous people withheld food or planted less in order to starve the settlers. That claim appears in Oviedo and other writers, and its reliability is uncertain, but the settlers' repeated hunger suggests that food supply could be a weapon as well as a burden. Refusal, concealment, and slow compliance were everyday forms of resistance that left few traces in the sources.

Armed resistance also occurred, and in 1494 it took a serious form. Caonabo emerged as one of the principal opponents of the Spanish presence. Spanish sources report that his forces besieged the Spanish fort of Santo Tomás in the Cibao until relieved. Caonabo was afterward captured by Alonso de Ojeda in a celebrated stratagem, generally dated to late 1494, although the details survive through later Spanish accounts and require caution. In 1496 Caonabo was placed aboard a vessel bound for Castile but never reached Spain; later accounts disagree over the precise circumstances of his death. Whatever the exact sequence, his capture removed a principal leader from the interior and did not end resistance.

In March 1495, Spanish forces accompanied by Indigenous allies under Guacanagaríx confronted a substantial Indigenous force in the Vega Real. Spanish narratives disagree over the resistance's leadership and greatly exaggerate its numbers, but they consistently describe the decisive use of horses, steel weapons, projectile weapons, and war dogs. The small Spanish contingent, reported as a few hundred men, prevailed. Afterward, many caciques submitted, captives were taken, and the tribute regime described in Section 27.8 was imposed.

The Vega Real should not be treated as evidence of automatic Spanish superiority. The Spaniards were few, often ill and hungry, and dependent on Indigenous allies, guides, and provisions. Indigenous communities knew the terrain and outnumbered them. Horses, dogs, and steel were unfamiliar and effective in open ground, and cohesion and leadership mattered as well. The outcome depended on politics as much as on weapons, on Guacanagaríx's alliance with the Spaniards and on the divisions among Indigenous leaders that they could exploit.

Resistance did not end with the defeat of 1495. In 1497 a group of subordinate caciques in the Vega Real, under intense pressure from tribute demands, became involved in a renewed conspiracy associated with Guarionex. The following year Guarionex fled into the mountainous Ciguayo country of the northeast, where the leader Mayobanex refused Spanish demands to surrender him. Bartolomé Colón eventually campaigned into the region and captured both leaders. These episodes show that armed resistance continued after Vega Real and increasingly overlapped with the Spaniards' own internal political crisis during the Roldán rebellion.

Violence escalated through a struggle over land, labor, food, gold, and sovereignty. Each Spanish demand provoked evasion or attack, each attack produced punishment, and punishment made further cooperation more difficult. This is why the chapter avoids describing the violence simply as the result of European brutality or of Indigenous hostility. It was a dynamic process in which the Spaniards' aims and weaknesses, and Indigenous leaders' strategies and rivalries, interacted.

The consequences were severe and lasting. Villages were disrupted, fields neglected, and communities dispersed. Indigenous people experienced hunger, displacement, violence, and mortality. The systematic demographic analysis of population collapse, including epidemic disease, belongs to Chapter 29, and this chapter does not attribute the decline to any single cause. What it establishes is that by the end of the 1490s the island's political and social order had already been profoundly altered by Spanish demands and the wars that resisted them.

Section 27.10

Columbus as Governor: Disorder, Opposition, and Removal

Columbus had been granted the right to govern the lands he found, and after 1493 he did so, with his brothers Bartolomé and Diego. The office required different skills from navigation. He had to manage settlers who arrived with their own expectations, allocate scarce food and labor, administer justice, and negotiate with Indigenous leaders and with the Crown. Historians widely agree that his record as a governor was troubled. Success as a transatlantic navigator and success as a colonial administrator are different things.

Opposition appeared early. Some colonists and clergy complained of hunger, illness, and of a government that favored Columbus's family and denied settlers a share of wealth and labor. Complaints reached Castile in 1494 with returning settlers, and in 1495 the Crown sent Juan de Aguado as a royal investigator. Columbus returned to Spain in 1496 to defend himself. His decision to appoint Bartolomé as adelantado, a senior office, was resented by many Spaniards, who saw a foreign family monopolizing power. Punishments were severe, and Columbus ordered executions.

The most serious challenge was Francisco Roldán's rebellion. Roldán, whom Columbus had made chief magistrate, withdrew with followers to the west in 1497 and challenged the admiral's authority. Columbus, weakened, reached a settlement in 1498–1499 that restored Roldán's position, gave the rebels land, and allowed them to claim the labor of Indigenous communities. This agreement extended coercion by rewarding settlers with control over Indigenous people, a result at odds with the Crown's stated concern for their treatment.

By 1499, complaints had convinced the Crown to act. It sent Francisco de Bobadilla, who arrived at Santo Domingo in August 1500 with wide powers as a royal investigator. According to the sources, he found Spaniards who had been executed on Columbus's orders, took testimony from aggrieved settlers, arrested Columbus and his brothers, and sent them to Spain in chains in the autumn of 1500. The image of the admiral in irons is famous, but it should be understood as the result of a political and legal process, not merely of Bobadilla's hostility.

In Spain, Ferdinand and Isabella ordered Columbus released and received him, and they did not reinstate him as governor. His economic and honorific privileges were partly preserved, but the government of Hispaniola passed to others. Bobadilla himself proved a controversial ruler, favoring settlers' access to Indigenous labor and gold, and he drowned in 1502 when a fleet carrying him home was lost in a hurricane. The episode shows that the Crown was concerned with control of a colony that had become valuable, and not primarily with Indigenous welfare.

Coercion did not depend on Columbus personally. Nicolás de Ovando arrived as governor in 1502 and consolidated Spanish control. His original royal instructions emphasized that Indigenous inhabitants were to remain free vassals rather than people held in servitude. After Ovando reported that the colony could not obtain sufficient labor, however, a royal provision of 20 December 1503 authorized him to compel Indigenous people to work in mines, agriculture, and construction while requiring that they receive wages and maintenance and continue to be treated legally as free persons, not serfs. In practice, this measure gave a firmer legal framework to the coercive labor allocations developing on Hispaniola and helped institutionalize the repartimiento/encomienda regime examined later in Chapter 33. Ovando also founded new Spanish towns and enlarged the mining economy.

In 1503 Ovando launched a campaign against the western province of Jaragua. Spanish accounts describe the killing of numerous Indigenous leaders and the capture of Anacaona, Caonabo's widow and the ruler of Jaragua, who was subsequently executed by hanging. The precise circumstances remain difficult to reconstruct from the interested Spanish sources. Her fate shows that Indigenous leaders remained political actors, that Spanish violence continued after Columbus's removal, and that the Crown's officials, not just Columbus, built the colonial regime. By 1504 Hispaniola was governed through Spanish towns, forced labor, and armed control.

Section 27.11

The Third and Fourth Voyages, 1498–1504

The third and fourth voyages matter chiefly for the expansion of geographic knowledge and for Columbus's continuing search for Asia. They do not dominate this chapter, because the center of colonial change lay on Hispaniola, but they show how far Columbus's interpretation of the world diverged from the evidence he gathered. In April 1495 the Crown briefly opened discovery and trading voyages to private subjects, cutting across the monopoly Columbus claimed under the Capitulations. Columbus protested, and the general authorization was revoked in 1497. By 1499, however, individual captains were again receiving licenses for voyages beyond Hispaniola. Columbus was no longer the only Spanish explorer gathering information about the western Atlantic.

Columbus left Sanlúcar in May 1498 with six ships, sending three directly to Hispaniola with supplies and taking a southern course with the others. On 31 July 1498 he sighted an island he named Trinidad, and in early August entered the Gulf of Paria, between the island and the coast of what is now Venezuela. There he encountered communities on the Paria peninsula who wore pearls and gold ornaments and traded with him, and he saw pearl-bearing waters near the island of Margarita. The gulf, and the strait through which he left it, put him in contact with peoples whose own exchange networks stretched along the northern coast of South America and inland.

The decisive observation was water. The gulf received an enormous flow of fresh water from the Orinoco River, and Columbus reasoned that only a very large landmass could produce such a river. In his letters he wrote that he had reached a great land, and he worked to reconcile it with existing cosmography. He proposed that the earth was not a perfect sphere but had a swelling, pear-like form, and he speculated that the region he had reached lay near the earthly paradise described in Christian tradition. These interpretations combined observation with inherited belief. He still did not conclude that he had found a continent separate from Asia. He thought rather that he had reached an unknown portion of the Asian mainland.

His arrival at Santo Domingo in August 1498 plunged him into the crisis described in Section 27.10, which dominated the rest of his governorship. Other mariners meanwhile moved along the same coasts. Alonso de Ojeda, accompanied by the cartographer Juan de la Cosa and by Amerigo Vespucci, sailed along the northern South American coast in 1499, and Vicente Yáñez Pinzón explored the coast farther south and east in 1499–1500. Their voyages, made with Crown licenses, meant that geographic knowledge was no longer controlled by Columbus and supplied additional evidence that European navigators, mapmakers, and writers would interpret in competing ways.

The fourth voyage began in May 1502 with four ships, and its aims were to find a passage to the Indian Ocean and its markets. Columbus was refused entry to Santo Domingo by the new governor, Ovando. Tradition says that he warned of an approaching storm and was ignored, but the detail comes from later narratives. He sailed west and reached the Bay Islands off Honduras in the summer of 1502, where he met a large trading canoe. That encounter, and the Maya-related world beyond it, belong properly to Chapter 30, and this chapter does not narrate them further.

From Honduras Columbus followed the coast eastward and southward through what are now Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama, searching for a strait. He heard of gold at Veragua and in early 1503 attempted to found a settlement on the Belén river. Resistance from the local leader Quibían, and the difficulty of maintaining the post, forced the Spaniards to abandon it in April 1503. The expedition's ships were worm-eaten and failing, one was abandoned, and the survivors struggled northward toward Hispaniola. Columbus wrote of the coast as rich in gold and as a place where he was told of another sea beyond, but no passage was found.

In June 1503 the two remaining ships were beached on the northern coast of Jamaica, where the crews were stranded for about a year. They survived through trade with Indigenous communities and through the efforts of men such as Diego Méndez, who paddled a canoe across to Hispaniola to seek help. Part of the crew mutinied under the Porras brothers. A well-known account, given by Columbus's son Ferdinand, holds that Columbus used his knowledge of a lunar eclipse in February 1504 to pressure the local Taíno into renewing food supplies. The story is widely repeated and should be treated as a reported episode. Relief reached the castaways in mid-1504, and Columbus reached Spain in November of that year, worn out and in poor health. Queen Isabella died later that month.

Section 27.12

From “The Indies” toward a New Geography

The second half of the chapter's purpose is to explain how European conceptions changed. The change was gradual, and it is crucial to see that it did not occur within Columbus's own mind. He appears to have retained an Asian geographical framework throughout his life, and to have died in 1506 still interpreting the lands he had reached within it. What changed was the accumulation of evidence, and the gradual recognition by other Europeans that it did not fit.

The conceptual sequence runs from expected Asia, to the term the Indies, to accumulating anomalies, to growing suspicion of a separate geography. In 1492 Columbus expected to reach the Asian islands and mainland described by Polo. The name the Indies, las Indias, was the label he and the Crown used for the lands he had reached, and it persisted in Spanish law and administration for centuries after the belief behind it had faded, which is one reason historians avoid reading the word as proof of continuing confusion. The Casa de Contratación, created at Seville in 1503 to regulate trade and navigation with these lands, reflected their growing administrative importance.

Anomalies accumulated steadily. Coastlines ran farther than any Asian island chain described by Polo. Hispaniola and Cuba were large, but the cities, courts, and spice markets of Cathay were nowhere found. The Orinoco discharge implied a huge landmass. The peoples encountered spoke languages unlike any known in the Old World, lived in societies unfamiliar from texts, and lacked the commodities Europeans sought. Columbus explained these mismatches by supposing that he had not yet reached the right place, and each new voyage tested that supposition.

Other mariners pushed the interpretation further. Ojeda's and Pinzón's voyages of 1499–1500 extended knowledge along the South American coast. Maps drawn in the early years of the century, such as the Cantino map of 1502, placed the new islands and coasts alongside the lands discovered by Portuguese sailors to the east. Reports associated with Amerigo Vespucci, circulating in 1503–1504, explicitly advanced the idea of a new world, although European geographical understanding remained unsettled. The clearest cartographic statement of a separate western continent came only with Waldseemüller's map of 1507, after this chapter ends.

The Indigenous contribution to this knowledge deserves attention. Taíno, Lucayan, and other guides supplied information about islands, currents, coasts, and trade routes, and their geographic knowledge made much of Columbus's exploration possible. European geographers recorded it in their own terms. The changing European picture therefore rested in part on Indigenous knowledge, acknowledged only rarely and imperfectly in the sources.

The chapter can therefore end in 1504 with Europeans still debating what these lands were. By that year, sustained Iberian presence in the Caribbean was no longer hypothetical. Spanish settlements, coercive labor demands, Indigenous resistance, and royal intervention had already begun transforming Hispaniola, while Columbus's voyages had vastly enlarged European knowledge of the western Atlantic without resolving what these lands were. At the same time, Spain's claims had raised another question: who had the right to claim and colonize the Atlantic world? Papal bulls issued in 1493 and the treaty negotiated with Portugal in 1494 had begun to answer it. Chapter 28 takes up the contest among Iberian powers, Brazil, and the practical limits of their claims.

Historical Vocabulary
Tribute

Tribute is a regular payment in goods or labor from dependent communities to a ruler. Hispaniolan caciques received food, cotton, and other goods from dependents, so Taíno society knew tribute. The Spanish demand of 1495 differed in scale, in its insistence on gold, which most communities did not hold in such quantities, in its enforcement by armed outsiders, and in its use of tokens to record compliance. Its importance lies in showing how exchange became obligation, and obligation became labor. Modern analogies to taxation can mislead: the Spanish tribute was imposed by armed outsiders without Indigenous political consent and operated very differently from the reciprocal obligations embedded in Indigenous political relationships.

Historical Vocabulary
Cacique

Cacique is a Taíno-derived word, adopted by the Spanish, for a leader of a community or group of communities. Caciques, both men and women, held authority through lineage, ritual roles, and the management of exchange and redistribution. Spaniards often translated the word as king or lord, which suggested more centralized power than a cacique necessarily held. The word matters because Columbus's successors negotiated, fought, and allied with individual caciques such as Guacanagaríx, Caonabo, Guarionex, and Anacaona, and later made the title part of colonial rule.

Historical Vocabulary
The Indies

The Indies (las Indias) originally meant the lands of Asia that Columbus expected to reach. Spanish officials kept using the term for the Caribbean and the American mainland long after Europeans suspected those lands were not Asia, and it became a legal and administrative label. Its continued use therefore does not prove continuing confusion. Modern readers should also distinguish it from Indian as applied to Indigenous Americans, a name that preserved Columbus's error.

Author and date: Christopher Columbus, 1492. Transmitter: Bartolomé de las Casas, whose abstract and partial copy, made decades later (probably the mid-sixteenth century), is the only surviving text; the original is lost. Guacanagaríx and his people: A cacique who had aided the Spaniards faces returning newcomers, rival caciques such as Caonabo, and accusations of complicity. His account blamed others, and his position depended on staying useful to the Spaniards.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Hispaniola in 1492 and 1500

What changed? In 1492 Spanish ships were dependent on Indigenous hospitality, guidance, and food. By 1500 there were permanent Spanish settlements, armed enforcement, tribute and labor demands, recurrent warfare, and a royal investigator sent to examine colonial government. The Crown's agent arrested the admiral himself.

What remained continuous? Hispaniola's political plurality, its farming communities and exchange networks, the leadership of caciques, and Indigenous capacity to choose among cooperation, withdrawal, and resistance persisted. Columbus's belief in an Asian geography also persisted, as did Spanish dependence on Indigenous food.

Who experienced change most directly? Communities in the Cibao and the Vega Real, who faced gold demands and warfare, and Indigenous women and captives. Who experienced more continuity? Communities in regions the Spaniards had not yet reached, such as Jaragua until 1503, and Spanish officials in Castile, who saw the colony largely through reports. Why does the pattern matter? It shows how quickly a relationship could be transformed within a single decade.

Cause and Effect

The Escalation from Exchange to Coercion on Hispaniola

Long-term causes: Iberian experience of conquest, settlement, gold trade, and enslavement in the Canaries and West Africa; royal commercial and religious aims; Columbus's commitments to the Crown for wealth. Short-term causes: Fragile settlements dependent on local food; settlers who expected quick riches; limited gold; Indigenous political divisions and resistance; Columbus's weak control over settlers.

The transformation: Gift exchange gave way to demands, tribute, captive-taking, and coerced labor. Immediate effects: Warfare, flight, hunger, displacement, the Roldán settlement, and the Crown's intervention. Long-term consequences: Precedents for the repartimiento and encomienda systems, examined in Chapter 33, and precedents for Caribbean labor and captive-taking that Spanish colonizers later adapted elsewhere in the Americas. Historical complexity: No single cause explains the process. Columbus's personal decisions mattered, but structural pressures, including fragile colonies and a Crown's need for revenue, drove it, and Ovando continued it after Columbus was removed.

Everyday Life and Social History

For Hispaniolan villagers, the arrival of Spanish demands meant that time once spent in cassava fields, fishing, and ceremony had to be diverted to carrying, building, searching streams for gold, or hiding. Women carried much of the food production and were exposed to seizure, coerced unions, and sexual violence. Children and elders were affected by hunger when fields were neglected or abandoned. Ordinary Indigenous life continued in ceremony, exchange, and kinship, but under heavy pressure.

For the Spanish, daily life at La Isabela meant illness, hunger, hard labor on buildings, and dependence on Indigenous food and pottery. Few Spanish women were present in the earliest settlements, and relations with Indigenous women were often coerced. Sailors and settlers came from Andalusia's ports and towns, and their expectations of wealth shaped how they treated the people around them. The sources describe these lives mainly from the settlers' side, and archaeology at La Isabela and En Bas Saline adds material evidence of both worlds.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the twelve years in which a Castilian voyage of reconnaissance became the first sustained Spanish colonial enterprise in the Caribbean. Its central argument is that Columbus's voyages began a contested relationship between Iberian newcomers and established Caribbean societies. Exploration and exchange gave way to settlement, demands for gold and labor, captive-taking, tribute, armed resistance, and colonial rule on Hispaniola, while Europeans struggled to understand what they had found.

The first voyage, reconstructed from a lost journal known through Las Casas and from Columbus's letters, brought Europeans to the Lucayans of Guanahaní in October 1492. The encounter contained radically different assumptions. Lucayan gifts and hospitality belonged to established diplomacy, while Columbus performed a ceremony of possession and noted that the people could be made servants. Indigenous hospitality should not be mistaken for political submission.

Columbus interpreted what he found through an inherited geographic framework that combined Ptolemy, Marco Polo, and contemporary cosmography. He identified Cuba with the Asian mainland and Hispaniola's Cibao with Cipangu, and he read Indigenous guidance as confirming these expectations. The framework decided what he saw and what he reported, which makes his texts indispensable evidence and unreliable description.

On Hispaniola the Spanish entered a politically plural landscape of cacicazgos led by figures such as Guacanagaríx, Guarionex, Caonabo, and Bohechío. Guacanagaríx's aid after the loss of the Santa María allowed the construction of La Navidad, but leaving armed men on the island turned reconnaissance into continuing presence. The fort's destruction by 1493 is best understood as a violent breakdown in a relationship the newcomers had strained, explained differently by every interested source.

The second voyage of 1493 was a colonizing expedition of roughly seventeen ships and well over a thousand people. La Isabela, founded in 1494, was fragile. Illness, hunger, and the mismatch between Spanish expectations and Caribbean conditions, rather than simple infertility of the environment, left it dependent on Indigenous food and knowledge. That dependence, and the disappointing gold, pushed settlers to transfer costs onto Indigenous communities.

The resulting progression ran from exchange to demands, tribute, captivity, and coerced labor. The tribute of 1495, the shipment of captives to Castile, and the Crown's hesitation over their sale exposed a tension between royal claims that Indigenous people were subjects and colonial demands for their labor. The Roldán settlement granted settlers Indigenous labor and set precedents for the repartimiento and encomienda, although no mature system existed yet.

Indigenous people resisted through flight, withheld provisions, negotiation, and armed opposition. Caonabo, captured by Ojeda, and the Vega Real fighting of 1495 show the violence of the conflict, although the sources disagree on leadership and numbers. Spanish victory was not automatic. It depended on Indigenous allies, on divisions among Indigenous leaders, and on Spanish horses, dogs, and steel. Resistance continued under Guarionex and Mayobanex.

Columbus proved a skilled navigator and a troubled governor. Opposition from settlers led to the Aguado inquiry, the Roldán rebellion, and Bobadilla's arrest of the admiral in 1500. Ovando's arrival in 1502 and the royal provision of December 1503 gave the regime a firmer legal form, and his Jaragua campaign ended with Anacaona's capture and execution. Coercion was the work of a colonial system, not of a single man.

The third and fourth voyages enlarged European knowledge, from the Orinoco and the Gulf of Paria to the coast of Central America and Jamaica, without converting Columbus from his Asian interpretation. Other mariners, the Crown's own institutions, and emerging maps increasingly strained that interpretation, and by 1504 Europeans were still debating what these lands were. The Spanish claim to them raised a question that Chapter 28 takes up: who had the right to claim and colonize the Atlantic world.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how quickly an exploratory voyage became a colonial regime, and how much depended on Indigenous food, knowledge, and politics. The transformation was not inevitable or smooth. It came through fragile settlements, disappointed expectations, disputes among Spaniards, and the choices of Indigenous leaders who weighed cooperation and resistance.

It also shows the limits of European understanding. Columbus described real places with real peoples, but through an Asian framework that decided what he noticed. His texts are invaluable and misleading, and the chapter has tried to teach students to read them critically, to ask whose voices are missing, and to use archaeology to test them.

The patterns first visible on Hispaniola, tribute, captive-taking, the allocation of Indigenous labor, and royal attempts to regulate what settlers demanded, shaped later Spanish conquest and colonial rule, especially in Mexico and the Andes, while related but distinct systems developed elsewhere in the Atlantic world, including Portuguese Brazil. Indigenous political plurality, leadership, and resistance likewise did not end in 1504; the Indigenous societies introduced in Chapter 21 did not simply disappear in 1504; their people, communities, knowledge, cultural practices, and forms of resistance continued under profoundly transformed colonial conditions.

Chapter 28 turns to the contest that Spanish claims provoked, the papal bulls, the Treaty of Tordesillas, Portuguese expansion in Brazil, and the practical limits of Iberian claims. Chapter 29 then examines the biological and demographic transformation that accompanied these events. Together the two chapters complete the account of how an encounter became an Atlantic empire.
