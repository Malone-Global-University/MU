Civil War in El Salvador and Guatemala

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Recovering the Evidence

In 1992, as El Salvador's war was formally ending, forensic specialists began to dig in a small building beside the village church at El Mozote. What they recovered did not settle every question about the war, but it changed what could responsibly be said about one of its most disputed events.

The excavation at El Mozote, in Morazán department, was carried out by the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team (EAAF), a group that had learned its methods investigating the disappeared of Argentina's dictatorship. The team recovered the remains of well over a hundred people from the building, a large majority of them children, together with cartridge cases that specialists could examine. Eleven years earlier, in December 1981, soldiers of the Salvadoran army had entered El Mozote and nearby hamlets. In January 1982 two foreign newspapers reported that civilians had been killed in large numbers. Salvadoran and United States officials denied or discounted the reports, and for a decade the dead were, in official terms, an allegation. The excavation, survivor testimony, and later investigations turned an allegation into a documented atrocity, and the recovery of names became a project of the survivors themselves.

Guatemala's equivalents appear in the same decade: forensic teams opening clandestine graves in the western highlands and Petén, church investigators collecting thousands of testimonies, and, much later, the digitization of police and military archives. Together these efforts supply the evidence on which this chapter rests. They also frame its central problem. Between roughly 1960 and 1996 Guatemala, and between 1980 and 1992 El Salvador, experienced prolonged internal wars in which armed forces, paramilitary groups, and revolutionary organizations fought, and in which most of the dead were civilians. Understanding those wars requires reconstructing not only the decisions of generals, commanders, and guerrilla leaders, but the lives of rural families, Maya communities, refugees, and the people who organized to find the missing.

This chapter asks why two neighboring countries experienced such wars, and why they ended in different ways. Chapters 89 and 90 followed revolution and counterrevolution in Nicaragua, and Chapter 79 traced the 1954 overthrow of Guatemalan president Jacobo Árbenz. Here the account returns to the Guatemalan aftermath of that coup and places it beside El Salvador, where coffee wealth, military rule, and political exclusion produced a different but related crisis. The two cases share features: concentrated land ownership, armies that dominated politics, revolutionary movements influenced by the Cuban Revolution and by local conditions, and Cold War assistance from outside powers. They also differ sharply. El Salvador's war was a contest between a professionalizing army and a unified insurgent coalition, concentrated in about a dozen years. Guatemala's was longer, began earlier, and in its most destructive phase, from 1981 to 1983, took the form of mass killing of Maya communities that a United Nations-backed commission later described, in specific regions and periods, as acts of genocide.

The chapter's argument has several parts. First, structural inequalities and political exclusion created conditions for conflict, but did not make war inevitable; decisions by officers, politicians, revolutionary leaders, and foreign governments shaped escalation. Second, neither war can be reduced to a proxy struggle between the United States and communism, though both were profoundly shaped by Cold War intervention. Third, civilians were not only victims; they organized unions, cooperatives, churches, refugee communities, and human-rights groups, and many survived by negotiating between armed actors. Fourth, the peace settlements of 1992 and 1996 ended organized armed conflict through different processes, and neither resolved the inequalities and institutional weaknesses that had underlain the wars.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. Military communiqués, guerrilla proclamations, embassy cables, and newspaper dispatches were all produced by parties with interests. Truth-commission reports, forensic records, and archival collections were produced afterward, under mandates and political constraints that shaped what they could say. Quantitative claims, especially, measure different things: complaints received, victims documented, estimates of total deaths. This chapter introduces each type of source, explains its limits, and asks students to hold apart what a source documents from what it cannot.

The chapter's boundaries also matter. It covers El Salvador chiefly from the 1970s to 1992 and Guatemala from the 1960s to 1996, with the Guatemalan background from 1954 and a limited documentary epilogue extending to the truth-commission reports of 1993 and 1999. The wider regional history of human-rights activism belongs to Chapter 88, the Nicaraguan war to Chapters 89 and 90, Panama's crisis to Chapter 92, and the comparative history of democratization and transitional justice to Chapter 94.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how land concentration, coffee agriculture, military rule, and political exclusion shaped El Salvador before 1979, and why those conditions did not make civil war inevitable.
  2. Trace the escalation from disputed elections and repression to the 1979 coup, the formation of the FMLN in 1980, and the opening of full-scale war.
  3. Reconstruct the El Mozote massacre and distinguish it from the Sumpul River and El Calabozo killings by evidence, perpetrators, and circumstances.
  4. Analyze the 1989 offensive, the murder of the Jesuits, and the road to the Chapultepec Peace Agreement of 16 January 1992.
  5. Explain Guatemala's trajectory from the 1954 coup through the early guerrilla movements, military governments, and Maya and peasant mobilization of the 1970s.
  6. Evaluate the counterinsurgency campaigns of 1981–1983, including Plan Victoria 82, and the evidentiary basis for the Commission for Historical Clarification's finding of acts of genocide.
  7. Compare Río Negro, Plan de Sánchez, and Dos Erres as distinct events with different local and military contexts.
  8. Describe civil patrols, model villages, refugee flight, and Communities of Population in Resistance as different civilian experiences of militarization.
  9. Assess the roles of the United States, Cuba, Nicaragua, Mexico, Israel, and regional diplomacy without reducing either war to external direction.
  10. Distinguish documented cases, complaints, and estimated totals in the Salvadoran and Guatemalan truth-commission reports, and between historical findings and criminal judgments.
  11. Compare the 1992 and 1996 settlements and identify the legacies each left unresolved.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did land, labor, and political power reinforce one another in El Salvador and Guatemala, and why did conventional political institutions fail to resolve agrarian conflict?
  2. At what points were alternatives to escalation available in each country, and who made the decisions that closed them?
  3. How did revolutionary organizations differ in strategy, composition, and relationship to the mass organizations around them?
  4. Why did counterinsurgency in both countries repeatedly target civilian communities, and what distinguishes lawful military operations from massacre?
  5. In what ways did Guatemala's ethnic and Indigenous dimensions require a different interpretation from El Salvador's war?
  6. How did Cold War intervention shape each conflict, and what limits does the evidence place on arguments that it caused them?
  7. How should historians use truth-commission reports, forensic work, and declassified documents together, and what can each not establish?
  8. Why did a negotiated settlement end El Salvador's war in 1992 and Guatemala's in 1996, and what did each leave unresolved?

Historical Context

Both countries entered the twentieth century as agrarian export economies in which a small landowning elite dominated land, credit, and, in different ways, the state. Coffee became the engine of both after the mid-nineteenth century. Liberal governments encouraged coffee by privatizing communal and village lands, creating labor regimes that pushed rural people toward estates, and building the infrastructure of export. In El Salvador, a small and densely settled country without a large unsettled frontier, this process absorbed most arable land into private estates. In Guatemala, with a much larger Maya population concentrated in the western and northern highlands, it produced a system of forced and debt labor on coffee plantations that persisted into the 1940s and shaped relations between Ladinos (people identified as non-Indigenous, culturally Hispanicized) and Maya communities.

Armies were central in both. Salvadoran officers, often from middle-class backgrounds, built an institution that became arbiter of politics from the 1930s, working with and sometimes against the landowning families. Guatemala's army, shaped by dictatorships and by its role in the 1944 revolution, became after 1954 an increasingly autonomous institution with its own economic interests and intelligence structures. In neither country was the army simply the instrument of landowners, though its interests often aligned with theirs.

The Cold War entered through several channels. The Cuban Revolution of 1959 inspired revolutionary organizations and alarmed governments, as Chapters 80 through 82 explained. The United States, after the 1954 coup in Guatemala, treated left-wing movements in the region as threats, trained and equipped security forces through programs described in earlier chapters, and later, under Presidents Carter, Reagan, and George H. W. Bush, developed policies toward El Salvador and Guatemala that differed in important ways. But outside influence operated on societies whose divisions were domestic, and the chapter treats those domestic origins as an independent subject.

Religious change also shaped the 1960s and 1970s. After the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965) and the Latin American bishops' conference at Medellín in 1968, parts of the Catholic Church committed to pastoral work with the poor. Catechists, lay delegates, and Christian base communities emerged in rural areas of both countries. Their relationship to politics was contested. Chapter 88 examines liberation theology and human-rights activism across the region; this chapter focuses on how religious organizations and their members were drawn into, and sometimes targeted by, civil war.

Finally, terminology requires care. This chapter distinguishes civil war (organized armed conflict within a state), counterinsurgency (military and political efforts to defeat an insurgency), massacre (the killing of a large number of unarmed or defenseless people), crimes against humanity and genocide (legal categories with specific definitions), and truth commission (an investigative body whose findings are historical and moral, not criminal judgments). These terms are not interchangeable.

El Salvador: Land, Military Government, and the Origins of Civil War

Primary chronological scope: 1932–1980
Section 91.1

Land Concentration, the Coffee Economy, La Matanza, and the Origins of Political Exclusion

El Salvador is the smallest mainland country of Central America, with a Pacific coast, a volcanic highland spine, and a northern frontier with Honduras marked by mountains and river valleys. Before the late nineteenth century much of its farmland was held by villages, including Indigenous communities of the west, whose members grew maize, beans, and subsistence crops on communal and village lands, alongside private estates producing indigo. When indigo declined, coffee on the volcanic slopes became the country's principal export, and coffee reshaped land tenure. Liberal governments in the 1880s abolished communal landholding, declaring that land should be private property. Villages lost lands to coffee growers, rural people became wage workers, seasonal laborers, tenants, or migrants, and a small group of families came to own the best land and control processing and export.

Héctor Lindo-Fuentes and Aldo Lauria-Santiago show that the shift from communal land to coffee estates was uneven: some rural households retained tenancy or limited plots. Nevertheless, land, wealth, and power became concentrated. Coffee exporters influenced government and relied on military institutions to sustain labor discipline. Officers pursued their own institutional interests rather than merely obeying landowners, but the two groups maintained a durable political alliance.

The partnership was tested in 1932. The world depression collapsed coffee prices and wages. President Arturo Araujo, elected in 1931 on a reformist platform, was overthrown in December of that year by a coup that brought his minister of war, General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez, to power. In January 1932 a rebellion broke out in the western departments, centered on Sonsonate, Ahuachapán, and the Izalco area, where Indigenous (Nahua-Pipil) and other rural communities had suffered land loss and depressed wages, and where communist organizers were active. The rebellion was poorly coordinated, and the government had information about it in advance. Rebels attacked towns and killed a small number of officials and civilians; they were quickly defeated by the army.

What followed is known as La Matanza, "the massacre." Soldiers, National Guardsmen, and civilian militias killed thousands of people, many of them not involved in the uprising, and identified targets partly by appearance and dress, which in the west meant that Indigenous people were singled out. Estimates of the dead vary widely, from several thousand to figures commonly cited in the range of ten to thirty thousand; no reliable count exists. Farabundo Martí, a Salvadoran who had worked with the Communist movement and had been arrested before the uprising began, was executed in early February. His name later became the identity of the FMLN, which claimed his legacy; historians caution that the relationship between the 1932 events and the 1980s movement is one of memory and symbolism more than direct continuity.

The consequences were long-lasting. Hernández Martínez ruled until 1944. In the western highlands Indigenous communities suppressed public markers of identity, including language and dress, and the repression became a silenced subject for decades, until historians and survivors brought it back into public discussion. For the political system the lesson drawn by ruling groups was that rural organization carried the risk of mass violence and that the army was the guarantor of order. After 1944 the military governed through parties and elections that it controlled, notably the Party of National Conciliation (PCN) from the early 1960s, while the state expanded schools, roads, and some industry without altering land tenure. Rural conditions deteriorated as population grew and as cotton and cattle expansion displaced tenants; by the 1970s a large share of rural families had little or no land.

These were structural origins of the civil war, and they should be distinguished from its immediate causes in 1979 and 1980. Inequality and exclusion created grievances and limited peaceful channels. They did not determine that war would occur, or take the form it did. Other societies with comparable inequality did not undergo comparable wars. What mattered were choices: by military leaders who rejected reform, opposition organizations that turned to arms, and outside governments that supplied money and training. The following sections trace those choices.

Section 91.2

Military Governments, Electoral Fraud, Rural Organization, and the Growth of Revolutionary Movements

From the early 1960s Salvadoran politics offered limited openings. The PCN, the military's party, won presidential elections, while opposition parties, including the Christian Democrats, led by José Napoleón Duarte, a civil engineer who became mayor of San Salvador in 1964, competed in legislative and municipal contests and sometimes won. The 1969 war with Honduras, which followed the expulsion of tens of thousands of Salvadoran migrants from Honduras, strengthened nationalism briefly, but it also closed a safety valve: Salvadoran peasants who had settled in Honduran territory returned to a country with no land to offer them.

The presidential election of February 1972 was a turning point. A coalition of opposition parties, the National Opposition Union (UNO), nominated Duarte for president and Guillermo Manuel Ungo, a social-democratic leader, for vice president. Early returns indicated that they were ahead; the government then halted the count, and when it resumed the official winner was the PCN candidate, Colonel Arturo Armando Molina. An attempted coup by dissident officers failed; Duarte was arrested, beaten, and sent into exile. The election of 1977 repeated the pattern: General Carlos Humberto Romero was declared president over the opposition candidate, Colonel Ernesto Claramount, amid allegations of fraud, and security forces killed protesters in San Salvador's Plaza Libertad in late February. Many Salvadorans, including moderates, drew the conclusion that elections would not allow change.

Meanwhile, rural and urban organization grew. The Federation of Christian Peasants of El Salvador (FECCAS), created in the 1960s with Catholic Church support, and the Union of Rural Workers (UTC) organized agricultural laborers and small farmers around wages, rents, and land. Catechists and lay delegates created Christian base communities that combined Bible study with discussion of local problems, and some priests, including Rutilio Grande, a Jesuit in the parish of Aguilares, supported peasant organizing; Grande was shot dead on 12 March 1977. Students and teachers organized in the university and in teachers' associations. By the mid-1970s several "popular organizations" had formed, among them the Popular Revolutionary Bloc (BPR), linked to FECCAS and UTC, and the United Popular Action Front (FAPU). They organized strikes, marches, and occupations of churches and ministries.

Mass organizations overlapped with, but were not identical to, armed groups. Salvador Cayetano Carpio's Popular Liberation Forces (FPL), founded in 1970, favored protracted rural war; the People's Revolutionary Army (ERP), founded in 1972, favored more immediate armed action. After ERP members killed poet Roque Dalton, a 1975 split produced National Resistance (RN). The Central American Workers' Revolutionary Party (PRTC) developed by 1976, while the Communist Party later established armed structures. These groups differed in ideology, strategy, and relationships with unions and popular organizations.

The state responded with force. Security institutions included the National Guard, the National Police, the Treasury Police, and army intelligence. In the countryside the government relied on ORDEN (Organización Democrática Nacionalista), a rural paramilitary and informant network created in the 1960s, whose members, often peasants, received privileges and acted as local enforcers and informers. Opponents were arrested, tortured, disappeared, or killed. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, which investigated in 1978 at the government's invitation, documented arbitrary detention, torture, and killings involving security forces and ORDEN before the war reached its intensive phase.

By the late 1970s, therefore, two developments coincided. Closed elections and repression strengthened arguments within the opposition for armed struggle. And mass organizations developed political capacities of their own, with membership, leadership, and strategies that were not controlled by the guerrilla groups. Treating all popular organizing as guerrilla activity, as security forces tended to do, erased that difference, and it contributed to escalation.

Section 91.3

The October 1979 Coup, Reformist Failure, Óscar Romero, and Escalating Political Violence

The overthrow of President Romero on 15 October 1979 was carried out by a group of younger officers, who were influenced by reformist ideas and by the fall of Anastasio Somoza in Nicaragua three months earlier (Chapter 89). They argued that the army should end repression, hold fair elections, and carry out reforms to forestall revolution. The coup was not a purely military project: the officers invited civilians, including Guillermo Ungo, the social democrat, and Román Mayorga, rector of the Central American University, to join a Revolutionary Government Junta. The United States, concerned by the Nicaraguan example, signaled support for a reformist government.

The experiment failed within three months. The junta's civilian members found they could not control the security forces, which continued arrests, killings, and attacks on popular organizations. The reformist officers were themselves divided, and more conservative commanders, including Colonel Jaime Abdul Gutiérrez and the defense minister, Colonel José Guillermo García, held the practical power of the army. Civilian ministers demanded that the military subordinate itself to the government, investigate disappearances, and open dialogue with popular organizations. When the military refused, many resigned in early January 1980, including Ungo and most of the cabinet. The Christian Democrats then chose to join a reconstituted junta, a decision that divided the party, and Duarte entered the government in March.

The reformist government then adopted the measures that its supporters hoped would remove grounds for revolution. In March 1980 it nationalized the banks and the coffee export trade and announced an agrarian reform in stages: Phase I expropriated the largest estates and converted them into cooperatives; Phase III, the "land to the tiller" decree, transferred land to tenants and sharecroppers. The reform was designed with advice from American specialists and received American backing. Its implementation, however, took place amid escalating violence. The state of siege declared at the same time suspended civil liberties, and in the countryside army units and paramilitary groups killed peasants, including cooperative leaders and technicians. Critics on the left saw the reforms as counterinsurgency; critics on the right saw them as confiscation; many beneficiaries received land, though the implementation of the program was uneven and partial, and violence against participants was common.

Archbishop Óscar Romero, appointed in 1977, increasingly denounced repression after the murder of Father Rutilio Grande. His widely broadcast homilies recorded killings and disappearances. In February 1980 he urged President Carter to halt military assistance; on 23 March he appealed to soldiers to refuse unlawful killing orders. He was assassinated while celebrating Mass on 24 March. The 1993 Truth Commission concluded that former major Roberto D'Aubuisson ordered the killing through a civilian-military network; he denied involvement. Violence disrupted Romero's 30 March funeral, leaving dozens dead, though responsibility for that attack remains disputed.

Killing extended to political leaders. In 1979 and 1980 many labor, church, and political organizers died, and in November 1980 six leaders of the Democratic Revolutionary Front, including Enrique Álvarez Córdova, a former landowner who had become a reformist minister, were seized at a Jesuit high school in San Salvador and killed. The government could not, or would not, control the armed institutions committing such acts. In the Sumpul River region in May 1980, as discussed in Section 91.5, military and paramilitary forces killed hundreds of fleeing civilians.

The 1979 coup thus did not produce a stable reformist transition. Competing political goals, military repression, elite resistance, and an increasingly organized revolutionary opposition drove the country toward prolonged war. The moderate center was killed, exiled, or marginalized in this process; later negotiations would depend on actors who had been shaped by it.

Section 91.4

Death Squads, the Formation of the FMLN, and the Beginning of Civil War

The term "death squad" in El Salvador referred to armed groups, often composed of or protected by members of security forces and acting clandestinely, that abducted and killed suspected opponents and left bodies in public places. Names changed, among them the White Warriors Union and the Maximiliano Hernández Martínez Anticommunist Brigade, and the groups' relationships to state institutions varied. The Truth Commission and later investigations found that many killings attributed to death squads involved active or retired military and security personnel and civilian financiers, and that networks linked security institutions and civilian supporters. Not every killing can be attributed to one organization or commander, however, and historians identify responsibility by incident and by evidence.

Roberto D'Aubuisson, a former army major and intelligence officer, became the most prominent public figure of the Salvadoran far right. In 1980 and 1981 he gave television presentations accusing named people of being subversives, and he was arrested in May 1980 with other officers in connection with an alleged coup plot, and soon released. In September 1981 he and associates founded the Nationalist Republican Alliance (ARENA), a political party that drew support from landowners, businessmen, and military allies, and that would later become an electoral force. The Truth Commission linked D'Aubuisson to the Romero assassination and to death-squad activity, while recognizing that ARENA as a party later changed, and that many people who voted for it did so for reasons unrelated to violence.

The FPL, ERP, RN, PRTC, and Communist Party's armed wing joined the Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front (FMLN) in October 1980, encouraged by Cuba and shared experience of repression. The coalition developed a joint command but retained organizational divisions; a deadly FPL leadership struggle in 1983 underscored them. The FMLN was distinct from the Democratic Revolutionary Front (FDR), created in April 1980 to bring together civilian political parties, unions, professional associations, and popular organizations. The FDR became an important diplomatic partner rather than an armed component of the front.

On 2 December 1980 four American churchwomen, the Maryknoll sisters Ita Ford and Maura Clarke, Ursuline sister Dorothy Kazel, and lay missionary Jean Donovan, were abducted, raped, and killed by Salvadoran National Guardsmen near the international airport. The killings caused a political storm in the United States. The Carter administration suspended aid temporarily, though it resumed it in January 1981 in response to the guerrillas' coming offensive. In 1984 five guardsmen were convicted in Salvadoran courts; later investigations raised questions about the chain of command that remain a subject of historical and legal attention.

On 10 January 1981, the FMLN launched what it called a "final offensive," timed to the end of the Carter administration. Its leaders expected urban insurrection and defections from the army. Neither occurred on the scale hoped. The offensive was repelled, though guerrilla forces held positions in the north and east, and the army, supplied by the United States, expanded. The result was the transition from a short insurrectional strategy to a protracted war in which the FMLN would control or contest rural zones while the army sought to deny it civilian support.

Historians have noted that the FMLN's resort to arms was, from the insurgents' perspective, a response to repression and electoral closure; from the government's, a threat; and from many civilians' perspective, a danger that arrived from both sides. El Salvador's civil war emerged from both state repression and the consolidation of an organized insurgency, each pursuing political objectives that increasingly depended on military force.

Historical Vocabulary Study I
Revolution, Insurgency, and Counterinsurgency

Guerrilla warfare refers to irregular warfare by small, mobile forces that rely on civilian support, terrain, and surprise. Insurgency is the broader political and military effort to overthrow or transform a government; counterinsurgency is the state's response, which may combine military operations, intelligence, civic programs, and, in these cases, attacks on the civilian populations presumed to sustain insurgents.

A death squad is a clandestine armed group that kills opponents outside legal process, usually with some connection to or protection from state agents. A military junta is a ruling committee of officers, or of officers and civilians. Political exclusion describes institutional barriers, such as fraud or repression, that prevent groups from competing for power. Agrarian reform means redistribution or restructuring of land tenure. A popular front is an alliance of diverse organizations around shared goals.

Why it matters: These terms are used by participants as weapons. "Subversive" and "communist" were applied by security forces to unionists and priests, while "popular army" and "liberation" carried their own claims. Historians use the terms analytically and test each claim with evidence about who did what.

El Salvador: Civil War, Human Rights, and the 1992 Peace Settlement

Primary chronological scope: 1980–1992
Section 91.5

Military Counterinsurgency, the El Mozote Massacre, and the War against Rural Communities

The Salvadoran army expanded greatly during the 1980s with U.S. equipment, advisers, rapid-response battalions, and air power. Early counterinsurgency operations combined attacks on guerrilla positions with attempts to isolate insurgents from rural support networks. Commanders sometimes treated communities in contested areas as presumed guerrilla supporters, a practice that exposed civilians to indiscriminate violence.

Distinguishing lawful military operations from unlawful attacks on civilians is basic to understanding the war. The laws of war permit attacks on armed combatants and military objectives; they prohibit deliberate attacks on civilians, the killing of persons who have surrendered or are defenseless, and indiscriminate violence. The documented record shows that Salvadoran forces repeatedly killed unarmed people, including women, children, and the elderly, and that the victims were not combatants. Evidence in each case must still be assembled separately.

In May 1980, before the FMLN's formation, the Sumpul River massacre occurred along the border with Honduras. Thousands of civilians, many from Chalatenango department and organized in peasant groups, were fleeing a military operation and tried to cross the river into Honduras. According to survivors, journalists, and later the Truth Commission, Salvadoran soldiers and members of ORDEN attacked them, while Honduran troops blocked the crossing on the far bank. Estimates of the dead range from several hundred to more than six hundred. At El Calabozo, in San Vicente department, in August 1982, troops including units of the Atlacatl Battalion surrounded civilians along a river and killed them; the Truth Commission described the evidence and sufficient evidence that troops deliberately killed more than 200 detained civilians, without presenting that count as a definitive census. These cases share patterns but differ in location, units, and circumstances, and students should not merge them.

El Mozote is the most thoroughly documented. In December 1981 the army launched an operation in northern Morazán department, a guerrilla zone, using the Atlacatl Battalion, a rapid-response unit created with American training in 1980–1981. On 10 December, soldiers reached the hamlet of El Mozote. The village's population included evangelical Protestants as well as Catholics, and some residents had stayed in the belief that neutrality would protect them. According to testimony, soldiers gathered men and women separately, interrogated them, and over the following days killed them, then children; they then moved through nearby hamlets, including La Joya, Los Toriles, Ranchería, and Cerro Pando, and killed residents there too. One woman, Rufina Amaya, escaped by hiding, and her testimony became central to the investigation. In January 1982, Raymond Bonner of the New York Times and Alma Guillermoprieto of the Washington Post reached the area and reported what they found and what survivors told them. Salvadoran officials and the Reagan administration, which had to certify that El Salvador was making progress on human rights to receive aid, denied or minimized the reports; the Times and the Post came under public criticism.

Forensic work in 1992 by the EAAF, supported by the Salvadoran courts and by a group of survivors and human-rights lawyers, recorded 117 separately identified skeletons in the field at the convent site near the church; subsequent laboratory analysis established a minimum of 143 individuals, including 136 children and adolescents and seven adults, alongside ballistic evidence. The Truth Commission, drawing on the exhumations, witness accounts, and military documents, concluded that the Atlacatl Battalion had killed civilians at El Mozote and nearby hamlets and that senior officers had been aware of it. Compilations by human-rights organizations, based on lists of names, give higher totals than the documented remains from a single excavation, and sources vary; the documented minimum and the compiled estimates should be distinguished, and the exact figure is not fixed. A 1993 amnesty law prevented prosecution in El Salvador for many years.

The massacres were not the inevitable results of combat. They involved decisions: to enter a zone with civilians in it, to separate and detain them, and to kill them over days, in a context in which military leaders believed the population supported guerrillas. Counterinsurgency operations frequently targeted or endangered rural populations on the assumption that civilian communities were the social foundation of the guerrilla movement, and the cases of the early 1980s show how that assumption produced atrocity.

Section 91.6

The FMLN's Protracted War, American Assistance, Elections, and the Changing Salvadoran State

From 1982 to 1988 the war evolved into a conflict of attrition. The FMLN controlled or contested zones in Chalatenango, Morazán, Cabañas, San Vicente, and Usulután, where it operated schools, clinics, and local administrations in some areas, and drew on peasant supporters, many of them organized before the war. It sabotaged power lines and bridges, attacked army posts and, in October 1984, destroyed the Puente de Oro over the Lempa River, a key crossing. Its leadership gradually moved from large offensives toward small-unit operations, and its supply depended on captured weapons, imports across the Honduran and Nicaraguan routes discussed in Section 91.17, and clandestine networks.

The army grew and modernized. American military assistance rose from small sums in 1980 to hundreds of millions of dollars annually by the mid-1980s; advisers trained Salvadoran officers and rapid-response battalions, and the air force, using helicopters and A-37 aircraft, bombed areas of FMLN influence. Congress imposed conditions requiring the executive to certify human-rights progress, which created recurring disputes. Some American officials pressed Salvadoran commanders to restrain abuses and to reduce death-squad activity, with partial results; others emphasized the insurgent threat. The government's strategy shifted at times toward civic and development programs in contested areas, and Salvadoran officers later said the army changed its tactics after the heavy casualties of the early 1980s.

The 1982 constituent assembly, elected without FMLN-FDR participation, drafted the 1983 Constitution. José Napoleón Duarte defeated Roberto D'Aubuisson in the 1984 presidential election and sought talks at La Palma, which failed. His administration struggled to restrain military abuses, revive a war-damaged economy, and implement agrarian reforms whose cooperative and tenant provisions faced serious obstacles. ARENA, later led by businessman Alfredo Cristiani, won the 1988 legislative and 1989 presidential elections.

The FMLN also committed abuses, and historians apply the same evidentiary standards to them. The Truth Commission attributed a minority of the complaints it received to the FMLN, about five percent, including executions of civilians accused of collaboration, killings of mayors and local officials, and attacks that endangered civilians. In June 1985 gunmen attributed to FMLN elements killed thirteen people, including four United States Marines, at cafés in San Salvador's Zona Rosa. Insurgent forces laid mines that killed and maimed civilians. These acts are documented and should not be minimized, nor does their documentation make the pattern and scale of violence by state agents and allied groups equivalent; the Commission found the greater share of violence attributable to the latter.

Conducting elections during war posed particular problems. Voters in contested areas faced threats from both sides, and many left rural zones to live in cities or in camps for displaced people, which changed the electorate. The FMLN and FDR argued the elections were unfair and sometimes urged boycotts or disrupted voting; the government and its allies pointed to turnout as evidence of legitimacy, and observers disagreed about how far conditions allowed free choice. Political institutions continued to develop, but electoral procedures alone could not resolve the relationship among armed forces, civilian government, and organized insurgency. That required negotiation of the army's role, and the war's trajectory in the late 1980s pointed toward a stalemate that made negotiation conceivable.

Section 91.7

The November 1989 Offensive, the Murder of the Jesuits, and the Military Stalemate

By 1989 neither side seemed able to win. The FMLN had survived nine years of war and remained able to strike, but it had not provoked an insurrection. The army, though larger and better equipped, had not destroyed the insurgents. In March 1989 Cristiani won the presidency for ARENA; the FMLN had proposed to take part if the vote were postponed, and the government had rejected the proposal. Meanwhile peace initiatives in the region, described in Section 91.17, and the end of Soviet support for the region's revolutions raised the question of what outside backing each side could expect.

On 11 November 1989 the FMLN launched its largest offensive, called "Hasta el Tope" ("To the Limit"), bringing fighting into San Salvador, including its wealthy neighborhoods and its poor districts, and into other cities. Its objectives included demonstrating that the army could not protect the capital and forcing negotiations on better terms; it did not expect to take power outright, and it did not produce a general uprising. The army responded with air attacks, including bombing of populated districts, and with ground operations; civilians suffered heavily on both sides, and the fighting lasted for about two weeks.

On 16 November, in the early morning, soldiers entered the campus of the Central American University (UCA), a Jesuit institution, and killed six Jesuit priests: Ignacio Ellacuría, the rector; Ignacio Martín-Baró, a social psychologist; Segundo Montes, a sociologist; Juan Ramón Moreno; Joaquín López y López; and Armando López. They also killed the housekeeper Elba Ramos and her sixteen-year-old daughter Celina. Ellacuría had argued publicly for a negotiated settlement and had acted as an intermediary; the Jesuits' public analyses were critical of both the government and the guerrillas, though their work was labeled subversive by military propaganda.

A U.S. Embassy investigator's February 28, 1990 memorandum reconstructed the Jesuit killings from witness statements and identified evidence implicating Salvadoran military personnel, while recognizing unresolved questions about senior command. A 1991 trial convicted Colonel Guillermo Benavides and another officer but acquitted others. The 1993 Truth Commission identified greater responsibility within the high command; an amnesty soon freed those convicted. These proceedings show why establishing who ordered a massacre requires testimony, records, and access to military institutions.

The massacre transformed international attention. Members of the United States Congress, who had supported conditional assistance, increased pressure to cut aid, and European governments and churches intensified criticism. The murders also affected the FMLN, which understood that the offensive had shown its strength while also exposing its limits. The offensive and the murders demonstrated that neither side had achieved a sustainable military victory and that the costs of continued war were becoming politically untenable. In December 1989, with the end of the Cold War in Europe, Salvadoran leaders on both sides turned toward the United Nations.

Section 91.8

United Nations Negotiations, the Chapultepec Peace Accords, and the End of War in 1992

Negotiations began earlier but took on seriousness after 1989. Earlier talks at La Palma (1984) and later initiatives, including Central American presidents' declarations at Esquipulas, had failed to produce agreements. In 1990 President Cristiani, an ARENA leader who needed to show he could manage the army and the business community, and the FMLN, whose leaders recognized the limits of the offensive and the changed international setting, asked the United Nations Secretary-General for mediation. The UN Secretary-General, Javier Pérez de Cuéllar, and his representative, Álvaro de Soto, conducted the talks.

Negotiations advanced through successive agreements. The April 4, 1990 Geneva accord set objectives of ending armed conflict and protecting democracy and human rights. A July San José agreement created a basis for UN human-rights verification; ONUSAL began work in 1991 before the ceasefire. Mexico City agreements in April 1991 addressed constitutional reform. September's New York accord established COPAZ, the multiparty peace commission, and specified further military and police reforms. The principal commitments were completed on December 31, 1991, and signed at Chapultepec Castle, Mexico City, on January 16, 1992.

The Chapultepec agreement made far-reaching changes. It reduced the size of the armed forces and limited their role to defense of the country's sovereignty, dissolved the intelligence and paramilitary structures, including the National Guard and the Treasury Police, and established an Ad Hoc Commission to review the records of officers, which resulted in the removal of a number of senior officers. It created a new National Civil Police (PNC) to be recruited from civilians, former police, and former FMLN members, under an academy and civilian control, replacing the old security forces. It provided for reform of the judiciary, a human-rights ombudsman, and electoral institutions. The FMLN would demobilize in phases under UN supervision and become a legal political party. On land, the agreement provided for the transfer of land to former combatants and others in conflict zones, a program that was partial and slow. A Truth Commission was established to examine the gravest acts of violence.

The signing date should be distinguished from the ceasefire and the implementation period. The formal ceasefire began on 1 February 1992, and demobilization took place in stages through 1992 under ONUSAL's supervision, including a disputed delay in 1992 when some commitments on land transfer and police reform were not met on schedule, leading to a renegotiated timetable. The armed conflict was declared formally ended on 15 December 1992, and the FMLN was legalized as a party. ONUSAL monitored the ceasefire, the human-rights commitments, and, later, the 1994 elections, and left in 1995.

The FMLN's transformation from a guerrilla coalition to a party was one of the settlement's most important outcomes. It contested elections in 1994 as the second force in the legislature, and later won municipal and national offices. The army remained an institution but lost its role in internal security and its autonomy.

El Salvador's civil war ended through a negotiated institutional settlement that altered the state's security and political arrangements. A peace agreement did not automatically eliminate violence, inequality, or the consequences of the conflict: the land question remained largely unresolved, crime and violence rose after the war, and the 1993 amnesty law, passed days after the Truth Commission report, closed the path to prosecutions, as Section 91.19 and Chapter 94 discuss.

Guatemala: Land, Revolution, Military Rule, and the Expansion of Insurgency

Primary chronological scope: 1954–1982
Section 91.9

Guatemala after the 1954 Coup: Land Inequality, Military Government, and the Origins of Insurgency

Guatemala's modern history is inseparable from the decade that preceded the 1954 coup. The revolution of 1944–1954 ended the dictatorship of Jorge Ubico, abolished forced labor laws, and under Presidents Juan José Arévalo and Jacobo Árbenz introduced labor rights and, in 1952, Decree 900, an agrarian reform that expropriated uncultivated portions of large estates, including land of the United Fruit Company, and distributed it to hundreds of thousands of rural people, many of them Maya. In June 1954 Árbenz was overthrown by an army-led movement under Colonel Carlos Castillo Armas, organized and supported by the Central Intelligence Agency. Chapter 79 narrates that episode; here it matters as the beginning of the political order in which the later war occurred.

The new government reversed the agrarian reform, returning most land to former owners, dissolved peasant leagues, banned the Communist Party and many unions, and detained and exiled thousands of activists. Castillo Armas was assassinated in 1957, and a series of governments followed, some elected under conditions managed by the army, others imposed by coup. The army became arbiter. Its officers participated in the economy, held land, and developed intelligence services; and the alliance among the military, the large landowners, the export sector, and, in different ways, the United States set the limits of reform. Civilian parties existed, but contests for the presidency took place within boundaries the army policed.

The political crisis of the late 1950s culminated on 13 November 1960 in a revolt by junior officers at military bases in Zacapa and Puerto Barrios, who protested corruption, the use of Guatemalan territory for training of Cuban exiles for the operation that became the Bay of Pigs (Chapter 81), and the army's condition. The rebellion failed within days, but several of its leaders, including Marco Antonio Yon Sosa and Luis Turcios Lima, fled into the eastern mountains and Mexico and later founded guerrilla groups. The rebellion's origins in the officer corps, not in rural communities, is important: the first insurgency was a split within the army and the political class, joined by radical students and Communist activists, and its early actors were mostly Ladino.

Maya communities had varied histories and land arrangements. In the western highlands, K'iche', Mam, Kaqchikel, and other households often combined small maize plots with seasonal plantation labor. Q'eqchi' communities in Alta Verapaz faced pressure from coffee estates and cattle ranching; the Ixil and Ixcán regions also developed settlement and cooperative movements. Traditional municipal institutions and Catholic Action networks both supplied community leadership. Conflicts over land titles, labor, language, and political authority preceded the guerrilla war; they were not evidence that Maya villagers were uniformly insurgents.

It is essential to distinguish Indigenous land struggles and demands for political rights from guerrilla membership. Communities organized for land, schools, cooperatives, and recognition without taking up arms, and many Maya people were suspicious of both the army and the guerrillas. At the same time, the racism embedded in Guatemalan society, which treated Maya as inferior and as a labor reserve, shaped how the army and landowners interpreted any organization among them. Guatemala's armed conflict developed within a political order shaped by the 1954 coup, concentrated landownership, military power, and exclusion, but the motivations and experiences of its Indigenous communities cannot be reduced to class conflict alone, as Rigoberta Menchú and others later emphasized and as historians such as Beatriz Manz and Ricardo Falla documented.

Section 91.10

Guatemala's Early Guerrilla Movements, Counterinsurgency, and the Militarization of Politics

The first guerrilla organizations took shape in the early 1960s. The Revolutionary Movement of 13 November (MR-13), led by Yon Sosa, and the Rebel Armed Forces (Fuerzas Armadas Rebeldes, FAR, sometimes translated as Revolutionary Armed Forces), formed in 1962 and associated with Turcios Lima and the Guatemalan Labour Party (PGT, the Communist Party), operated in the eastern departments of Izabal and Zacapa and in the Sierra de las Minas. They were influenced by the Cuban Revolution, which provided inspiration and, in some cases, training, and by the foco theory associated with Che Guevara (Chapter 82), which held that a small armed group could create conditions for revolution. Their membership included former officers, students, and Communist activists; Ladino peasants in the east provided some support, but the movement did not draw on the Maya majority.

In the late 1960s army campaigns in Zacapa and Izabal, associated with Colonel Carlos Arana Osorio, used intelligence, local allies, and extensive violence against suspected rural supporters. American training and equipment contributed to Guatemala's counterinsurgency capacity. Civilian killings and disappearances spread beyond active guerrilla areas. Contemporary sources disagree about exact totals, so the chapter differentiates broad estimates from documented individual events.

Counterinsurgency changed the army and politics. The army gained experience, intelligence organizations, and a doctrine that treated political opposition as part of a communist subversive threat, and its dominance over civilian government increased. The elected civilian government of Julio César Méndez Montenegro (1966–1970) took office under conditions that left the army in control of internal security; the state of siege and the army's autonomy continued. Arana Osorio, who became president in 1970 on the ticket of the army's political arm, governed under a state of siege that targeted the university, unions, and political leaders. Violence became routine in Guatemala City and in some rural areas, and it extended beyond guerrillas to people of the democratic left, including Christian Democrats, social democrats, and trade unionists.

In the early 1970s new organizations emerged with different geographic and strategic emphases. The Guerrilla Army of the Poor (EGP) entered the Ixcán region in the northern forests in January 1972, led by Ladino activists, and aimed at building a base among rural communities through long-term organization in the Ixil area, Quiché, and Huehuetenango, which had large Maya populations. The Organization of the People in Arms (ORPA), formed in 1971 and announced publicly in 1979, operated on the Pacific piedmont and the volcanic highlands of San Marcos, Quetzaltenango, and Sololá, and gave explicit attention to the participation of Indigenous people and to the ethnic question, though its leadership remained mostly Ladino. The PGT, which retained a more political approach, decided to adopt armed struggle only in the late 1970s. Their differences in geography, ideology, and relations with communities made them distinct actors.

Guatemala's insurgency and counterinsurgency evolved through several phases. The later rural violence against Maya communities cannot be explained adequately by simply extending the account of the 1960s guerrilla war: it involved different organizations, a much larger population, a different army doctrine, and, as Section 91.13 shows, a different scale of killing.

Section 91.11

Indigenous Mobilization, Peasant Organizations, Military Governments, and the Crisis of the Late 1970s

Guatemala from 1970 to 1982 was governed by three generals, Carlos Arana Osorio (1970–1974), Kjell Laugerud García (1974–1978), and Romeo Lucas García (1978–1982), each elected in contests in which the army controlled the outcome. In 1974 the official victor, Laugerud, was seen by many as having defeated General Efraín Ríos Montt, the candidate of an opposition coalition, through fraud. The generals were also economic actors: officers acquired land in the Northern Transversal Strip, a region of Alta Verapaz, Huehuetenango, Quiché, and Petén where oil, nickel, and cattle development was promoted, and where peasants and cooperatives had settled. Land concentration and conflict increased in the areas that would become centers of massacre.

Maya communities were not passive. Cooperatives, Catholic Action, peasant leagues, literacy projects, and bilingual education programs created organizations with capacities for collective action. In the western highlands communities organized around land, wages on the coast, education, and recognition of their language and cultural practices; in the municipal governments, Maya candidates challenged Ladino control. In Ixcán and the Ixil area, cooperatives were supported by missionary priests. The Catholic Church, divided in itself, included bishops who supported the military and priests and catechists who worked with peasant organizations. Such activity was not necessarily revolutionary, but it challenged the patterns that had held since the colonial period.

The earthquake of 4 February 1976, which killed some twenty-three thousand people and left more than a million homeless, exposed inequality: the poor, living in adobe houses in the highlands and in urban slums, suffered most, while reconstruction aid was often diverted. It also strengthened community organization, as survivors rebuilt through committees, and increased awareness among urban and rural poor of the state's failures.

Two events in 1978 marked escalating repression. On May 29, soldiers killed demonstrators in Panzós, Alta Verapaz, during a conflict over land and local authority; estimates vary, and Maya Q'eqchi' survivors identified armed-state responsibility. The Committee for Peasant Unity (CUC), founded that year, organized Indigenous and Ladino laborers around wages and land. These movements pursued distinct aims and cannot simply be equated with guerrilla recruitment.

Distinguishing Indigenous and peasant political agency from the armed objectives of guerrilla movements is a central historical task. CUC was not a guerrilla organization, though it had contacts with the EGP, and some members joined armed groups; many did not. Villagers who organized cooperatives, attended catechism classes, or demanded wages and land titles were not necessarily revolutionaries. Yet the military increasingly interpreted every form of organization as subversion, and landowners, officers, and civilian elites supported that interpretation. Guatemala's political crisis deepened as the military increasingly treated peasant organization and Indigenous demands for justice as potential manifestations of revolutionary insurgency. That interpretation guided the repression that followed.

Section 91.12

The Spanish Embassy Fire, Urban Repression, the URNG, and the Road to Mass Counterinsurgency

On 31 January 1980, a group of K'iche' peasants from Quiché, accompanied by students and activists, occupied the Spanish Embassy in Guatemala City to draw attention to killings and abuses by the army in their region. The occupiers included relatives of victims and community leaders; among them was Vicente Menchú, father of Rigoberta Menchú. The Spanish ambassador, Máximo Cajal, was present, and he asked the Guatemalan government not to intervene. Security forces, however, surrounded and stormed the building. A fire broke out, and about three dozen people inside died, among them Menchú, embassy staff, and visitors, while the ambassador survived. One surviving protester, taken to a hospital, was abducted and killed.

The government's account blamed the occupiers, asserting that they had set the fire; the ambassador and survivors disputed it, and Spain broke diplomatic relations. Later investigations, including those of the Commission for Historical Clarification and, much later, a Guatemalan court that in 2015 convicted a former police commander of responsibility, attributed the deaths to the actions of security forces. The incident is therefore a case in which government claims made at the time were contradicted by later evidence, and in which the sources must be compared: official statements, the testimony of the ambassador and survivors, press reports, and judicial findings.

Repression intensified under President Romeo Lucas García. Students, unionists, politicians, priests, and catechists were murdered or disappeared; organized opposition faced clandestine threats and public killings. The bishop of Quiché closed the diocese in 1980 in response to threats. Army operations broadened from attacks on selected leaders toward the destruction of entire communities in some areas. The CEH later found a sharp rise in violations after 1978, with the most destructive phase in 1981–1983.

The revolutionary organizations, meanwhile, grew and sought coordination. The EGP expanded in Ixil, Ixcán, and Huehuetenango; ORPA in the south-west highlands; the FAR in Petén and the Verapaces; and the PGT's armed wing. Numbers of fighters remained modest relative to the army, estimated in the low thousands, but they had a large base of civilian supporters in some areas, and in some communities support was extensive. In February 1982, under pressure from the Cuban and Nicaraguan governments, which urged unity (Section 91.17), and from the need to coordinate, they formed the Guatemalan National Revolutionary Unity (Unidad Revolucionaria Nacional Guatemalteca, URNG). As with the FMLN, unity did not erase differences in strategy and organization among the member groups.

The guerrillas also committed abuses, including killings of civilians accused of collaboration with the army, attacks on military commissioners and landowners, and forced recruitment in some areas; the CEH attributed a small proportion of documented violations, about three percent, to the guerrillas. These acts must be documented and not excluded, but they do not alter the finding that the great majority of documented violence came from state forces and associated groups.

By 1981–1982 Guatemala had entered a phase of systematic military violence directed against civilian populations and suspected insurgent support networks, while revolutionary groups sought greater organizational unity. The CEH's chronology treats the Spanish Embassy fire, the repression of labor and popular organizations, the expansion of military operations, and the formation of the URNG as interlocking developments of this period. What followed, under a new government, was not a continuation but an escalation.

Primary chronological scope: 1982–1996
Section 91.13

Efraín Ríos Montt, Plan Victoria 82, and the Military Campaigns against Maya Communities

On 23 March 1982, a group of junior officers overthrew Lucas García in a coup that followed a fraudulent presidential election the previous week. The officers installed a junta headed by General Efraín Ríos Montt, a retired general who had been the opposition candidate in 1974 and who had become an evangelical Protestant associated with a California-based mission church. In June he dissolved the junta and assumed the presidency himself. The officers justified their coup by pointing to corruption, electoral fraud, and the army's failure to defeat the insurgency, and Ríos Montt promised moral renewal; weekly televised sermons became a feature of his government. The coup did not end the violence of the preceding years. It reorganized it.

The central military document of the period is the Campaign Plan Victoria 82, issued by the army general staff in June 1982. The National Security Archive preserves a copy; the catalog metadata for one copy gives an incorrect year of 1962, but the document itself and its context place it in 1982. The plan set out a campaign to destroy the guerrillas' support among the population, combining military operations with measures to control civilians; it was followed by successor plans, including Firmeza 83 and others. The plan and related documents, including American intelligence reporting, show that the campaign was centrally planned, issued through the military command structure, and carried out by regional commands. Its language, as scholars of the document have observed, treats the civilian population in guerrilla zones as the enemy's base and aims to separate the guerrillas from it, in some passages explicitly through control or elimination of the support network. Students should read the original text rather than rely on summaries.

The campaign was carried out in 1982 and 1983 mainly in the western highlands, in Quiché (including the Ixil area), Huehuetenango, Chimaltenango, Baja Verapaz, and Alta Verapaz, and in Petén. Army units entered villages, killed residents, burned houses and crops, destroyed food stocks and livestock, and drove survivors into the mountains. Survivors and later investigators described patterns: killing of community leaders, catechists, and members of cooperatives; separation of men from women and children; rape of women and girls; killing of children; destruction of churches and ritual sites; and the targeting of whole villages. Others who survived fled, were gathered into army-controlled settlements, or were conscripted into civil patrols (Section 91.15). Government officials described these as counterinsurgency operations against subversives. Historical investigators documented that many victims were unarmed civilians who were not legitimate military targets.

The Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) recorded 626 massacres and concluded that state agents committed acts of genocide against groups of Maya people in specified regions during 1981–1983. It assessed mass killings, destruction of communities, and evidence of intent against the definition in the 1948 Genocide Convention. Its report did not criminally convict individuals; that role belonged to courts. In May 2013 a Guatemalan court convicted Ríos Montt of genocide, but the Constitutional Court annulled the judgment on procedural grounds and he died in 2018 before a completed retrial. Chapter 94 addresses the subsequent legal history.

Historians have emphasized several distinctions. The violence was not equal in every region: the army's behavior in the Ixil area differed from its behavior elsewhere in degree and form. Not all Maya communities were targeted: some, for reasons that included local politics and army alliances, were spared or incorporated. And the army's justification should not be accepted uncritically, nor should the finding of genocide be generalized to every operation. The Guatemalan military's counterinsurgency campaigns became a system of extreme violence against civilian communities, including acts identified by the truth commission as genocide. That conclusion requires careful documentary analysis rather than unsupported generalization.

Historical Vocabulary Study II
Indigenous Communities and State Violence

Scorched-earth campaign refers to destruction of crops, homes, and livestock to deny resources to an enemy and, in these cases, to civilians. Forced displacement is the compulsion of people to leave their homes. A civil defense patrol (patrulla de autodefensa civil, PAC) was a compulsory local armed group organized under army command. A model village was a planned settlement built under army supervision to concentrate and control displaced populations. Communal land is land held by a community under customary or collective rules.

Genocide is a legal term, defined in the 1948 Convention as specified acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group in whole or in part. Command responsibility is the legal principle that superiors may be held responsible for crimes by subordinates they ordered, or knew of and failed to prevent. Crimes against humanity are widespread or systematic attacks on civilians, defined separately.

Why it matters: Genocide, crimes against humanity, counterinsurgency, and civil war are not interchangeable labels. A historian may describe an event as part of a counterinsurgency campaign and also evaluate whether it meets a legal definition; a truth commission may make a finding that a court has not yet adjudicated; and a court may rule on individual criminal responsibility while historians continue to debate scale and motive.

Section 91.14

Río Negro, Plan de Sánchez, Dos Erres, and the Geography of Massacres

Guatemala's massacres were numerous, and comparing three of them shows their variety. Each took place in a different setting, involved different units and local actors, and has been documented through different evidence.

Río Negro was an Achí Maya community on the Chixoy River, near Rabinal, threatened with displacement by the Chixoy hydroelectric dam. Residents challenged resettlement terms that endangered fields, homes, and burial grounds. From 1980 through 1982, army personnel and associated patrols killed community leaders and massacred groups of residents, including women and children, in successive operations. Hundreds died across these events, with totals varying by which incidents are counted. The case connects a contested development project, local land claims, and military counterinsurgency.

Plan de Sánchez. On 18 July 1982, soldiers and civil patrol members entered the hamlet of Plan de Sánchez in Rabinal municipality, also in Baja Verapaz and also Achí Maya. According to survivors and later investigations, they gathered villagers, raped women and girls, and killed those present, then burned the site. The Inter-American Court record identifies approximately 268 victims; its figure is not represented as a complete census. Survivors later returned, exhumed remains, held memorial ceremonies, and brought a case to the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, which in 2004 issued a judgment in which Guatemala accepted responsibility. The case shows how community testimony, forensic exhumation, and international legal proceedings combined to recover a past that official channels had denied.

Dos Erres. Dos Erres was a settlement of colonists from varied backgrounds in La Libertad municipality in Petén, a frontier region of the northern lowlands. In December 1982, soldiers including members of the Kaibil special forces, an elite counterinsurgency unit, entered the settlement, apparently in search of weapons that had been taken from a patrol, and over several days killed residents, including children. Forensic exhumations in the 1990s recovered the remains of at least 162 people. Contemporary United States Embassy reporting, preserved by the National Security Archive, includes a cable of 28 December 1982 and a follow-up of 31 December; these show how reports of a mass killing reached American officials and how they evaluated competing explanations before the full circumstances were known. Dos Erres matters analytically because its victims were not Maya villagers in the highlands, which reminds readers that the army's violence extended beyond Indigenous communities, even though the CEH's data show that most victims were Maya.

Students must distinguish the number of victims documented through particular investigations from retrospective estimates. A forensic exhumation recovers remains from a particular site and cannot count those killed elsewhere, whose bodies were not recovered or were burned. Testimony may include names that cannot be matched to remains. Estimates compiled afterward draw on multiple sources and have ranges. The CEH, REMHI, and judicial proceedings each applied different standards and covered different cases, so figures drawn from them should not be combined without qualification.

The reconstruction of these crimes depended on several kinds of evidence. Surviving testimony, collected by church workers, human-rights organizations, and the CEH, was crucial; forensic archaeology, carried out after 1992 by the Guatemalan Forensic Anthropology Foundation (FAFG), created by Guatemalan and Argentine specialists, provided physical evidence; military records, including operational plans and, from 1999, the leaked intelligence log known as the Diario Militar, and the police archive found in 2005, added documentary evidence; and later judicial proceedings tested the claims. Each kind of evidence has limits, and historians use them together.

Guatemala's massacres were not interchangeable incidents. Their individual histories reveal the interaction of military doctrine, local economic and political conflicts, civilian vulnerability, and command responsibility.

Section 91.15

Civil Defense Patrols, Model Villages, Refugees, and Everyday Life under Military Control

The army followed massacres with efforts to control survivors and the countryside. Its most extensive instrument was the Civil Self-Defense Patrol (PAC). Patrols had been organized locally from 1981 and were expanded and institutionalized from 1982; by the mid-1980s, hundreds of thousands of men, with some estimates approaching a million, were enrolled. Participation was in practice compulsory in many communities, and refusal could be treated as subversion. Patrollers, unpaid and mostly armed with old rifles or clubs, stood guard, searched for guerrillas, and in some cases participated in killings and abuses. The system turned communities against themselves: neighbors informed on one another, local disputes were settled by denunciation, and patrol commanders gained power. Participation could also be a means of survival, and some communities negotiated with the army for protection. Historians and the CEH have therefore emphasized that the PACs involved both coercion of civilians and responsibility for abuses by some of their members.

The army also created "model villages" and "development poles," where displaced survivors were concentrated in planned settlements under military supervision, particularly in the Ixil area, the Ixcán, and Alta Verapaz. The slogan of "beans and rifles" (frijoles y fusiles) associated with Ríos Montt's period captured the idea that food and development would accompany military control. Officials described the settlements as providing housing, food, and services; many residents described them as places of surveillance, forced labor, hunger, and loss of land, where families were required to live in rows of houses, to patrol, and to attend the army's political education. The stated development and security purposes should be distinguished from the coercive experiences documented by many affected communities.

Large numbers of Guatemalans fled into Mexico, especially Chiapas, where community networks, churches, Mexican authorities, and UNHCR eventually offered assistance. Following cross-border army incursions in 1984, some refugees were transferred to Campeche and Quintana Roo. Many more people were displaced internally, hiding in the mountains, moving to cities, or seeking seasonal employment. Communities of Population in Resistance (CPRs) in Ixcán, the Ixil region, and Petén established isolated settlements and basic services while avoiding army patrols. Military authorities alleged insurgent ties; residents asserted their civilian status and need for safety.

Women bore a particular share of the burden. Many were widowed, and they became heads of households. Women carried water and firewood, raised children, farmed the remaining land, and traveled to find food. Women were also targets of sexual violence, which the CEH documented as a pattern in the army's campaigns. In refugee camps and displaced communities, women organized food distribution, schooling, health care, and weaving cooperatives, and later formed organizations, such as CONAVIGUA, the national coordinating committee of widows, founded in 1988. Children were separated from families, orphaned, or raised in military or displaced settings, and some were adopted abroad; the loss of land and of communal institutions endangered the transmission of languages and practices, which many communities worked to preserve through displacement.

The experiences differed. Those who fled to Mexico had access to international aid but lived in exile; those displaced internally had little protection and often lived in hiding; those who remained under military surveillance faced patrol duty, informants, and the memory of killing. Military violence transformed the geography and social organization of rural Guatemala, and civilian communities developed different strategies for survival, accommodation, flight, and resistance under conditions of extreme coercion.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household in War: Reconstructing Survival from Testimony

The documented testimonies collected by the CEH, by church investigators, and by scholars such as Ricardo Falla in Ixcán and Beatriz Manz in the highlands allow historians to reconstruct the outlines of household life during war without inventing individual stories. Highland Maya households before the war combined farming small plots, seasonal work on the coast, weaving, and participation in religious and civic duties. When army units approached, families fled with what they could carry, often leaving behind crops, animals, and tools; they hid for weeks or months, ate wild plants, and sometimes lost children to hunger and disease. Men were conscripted into patrols, or killed; women assumed household leadership and, in refugee camps in Chiapas, learned Spanish and organized collective kitchens.

In El Salvador, rural households in Chalatenango and Morazán experienced a comparable cycle of flight and return, with refugees crossing to camps in Honduras or moving to San Salvador, and in repopulated communities, from the mid-1980s, returning to rebuild under the protection of churches and international observers. The sources record these experiences unevenly: testimonies depend on who survived to speak, who was asked, and under what conditions. Silence, in many communities, was itself a strategy of survival, and the recovery of these histories is partial.

Section 91.16

The 1985 Constitutional Transition, Civilian Government, Indigenous Rights, and the 1996 Peace Accords

On 8 August 1983 General Óscar Humberto Mejía Víctores overthrew Ríos Montt, who had lost support among officers and among some of the religious and business groups that had initially backed him. Mejía Víctores continued counterinsurgency but began the army's plan for a controlled return to civilian rule. A constituent assembly was elected in 1984, a new constitution was adopted in 1985, and in the elections of that year the Christian Democrat Vinicio Cerezo won the presidency, taking office on 14 January 1986. The 1985 Constitution, among other provisions, established a Constitutional Court, a human-rights ombudsman, and recognized aspects of Indigenous identity.

The restoration of constitutional civilian government should be distinguished from the end of the civil war. Armed conflict and military influence continued. Cerezo, as he later acknowledged, governed within limits set by the army; the army maintained its intelligence apparatus and its control of the countryside, and attempted coups occurred in 1988 and 1989. Violence continued in lesser forms, and human-rights groups, such as the Mutual Support Group (GAM), founded in 1984 by relatives of the disappeared, worked under threat. Cerezo's government nevertheless took part in the regional Esquipulas peace process (Section 91.17) and opened the way to the first contacts between the government and the URNG in 1987, in Madrid.

Peace negotiations developed unevenly under Cerezo's successors. Jorge Serrano (1991–1993) began direct negotiations with the URNG, but in May 1993 he attempted to dissolve the Congress and courts in a "self-coup," which collapsed under domestic and international pressure; Ramiro de León Carpio, the human-rights ombudsman, was chosen to complete the term. In 1994 the United Nations assumed a larger role as moderator, with a group of friendly countries, the Assembly of Civil Society, representing organizations including Maya, women's, religious, and labor groups, participating in consultation. Álvaro Arzú, who won the presidency in a January 1996 runoff and took office that month, carried the negotiations through to conclusion.

The March 1994 human-rights agreement established commitments monitored by MINUGUA. The June Oslo accord created the Commission for Historical Clarification. The March 31, 1995 Indigenous Rights Agreement recognized Guatemala as multiethnic, multicultural, and multilingual and addressed language, spirituality, participation, and land. Further agreements in 1996 concerned socioeconomic reform, civilian authority, and military restructuring. After a December 4 definitive ceasefire agreement, the parties signed the Agreement on a Firm and Lasting Peace in Guatemala City on December 29, 1996.

The commitments extended beyond demobilization: the URNG would disarm and become a political party, and the state committed to reforms in the justice system, the army, police, land policy, taxation, and Indigenous rights. Implementation problems followed. A national referendum in May 1999 on constitutional reforms needed to implement the Indigenous-rights commitments was rejected by voters, in a low turnout; fiscal commitments, such as increasing tax revenue to fund social spending, were not met; land reform was limited; and the impunity of perpetrators was largely unaddressed, with the 1996 National Reconciliation Law excluding genocide, torture, and forced disappearance from amnesty. Guatemala's war ended through a prolonged process of negotiation that went beyond disarmament and included commitments concerning Indigenous identity, civilian political authority, human rights, social inequality, and military reform.

Section 91.17

The United States, Cuba, Regional Governments, and International Dimensions of the Civil Wars

The United States was the Salvadoran government's principal foreign sponsor. President Carter backed the reformist junta and briefly suspended aid after the four American churchwomen were murdered in 1980; assistance resumed in January 1981. The Reagan administration substantially increased military and economic support, though congressional human-rights certifications provoked controversy as military abuses continued. Following the Jesuit killings in 1989, Congress restricted some aid under President George H. W. Bush, whose administration also supported UN-mediated negotiations.

American policy toward Guatemala followed a different course. The Carter administration suspended direct military aid in 1977 amid concern over security-force abuses. Later attempts to restore some sales and training met congressional restrictions; limited training resumed in 1983, but direct aid, approved commercial arms sales, intelligence contacts, and military education were distinct categories. U.S. officials received intelligence about army operations against Maya communities during 1982–1983. On December 21, 1990, the State Department suspended materiel assistance, including cash arms sales, after failures to investigate the killing of American citizen Michael DeVine and other abuses. Military education was not stopped at that exact moment, and later restrictions differed by program. These distinctions matter when assessing American responsibility and leverage.

Cuba supported revolutionary movements in different ways. It trained fighters, offered advice, hosted leaders, and, in 1979–1980, urged the Salvadoran and Guatemalan groups to unify, as it had urged the Nicaraguan groups. Nicaragua's Sandinista government hosted the FMLN's leadership in Managua and was a transit route for supplies to Salvadoran guerrillas, especially in 1980–1981; a United States "White Paper" of February 1981 argued that the Salvadoran insurgency was directed from abroad, a claim that critics challenged as overstated, while later scholarship found that outside supply was real but that the insurgency drew mainly on domestic recruitment and captured weapons. Confirmed material assistance should be distinguished from political sympathy, and from allegations lacking documentation. The Soviet Union provided limited direct support, mainly through Cuba and other allies.

Israel developed military relationships with several Central American governments, including Guatemala's, from the late 1970s, as the United States restricted its sales. Reports and later research describe Israeli sales of small arms and equipment, such as Galil rifles, and of aircraft and communications and surveillance equipment, and advisory contacts; the exact type, scale, and duration are difficult to document, and claims about operational involvement should be treated with caution unless supported by reliable sources.

Mexico took diplomatic positions that differed from Washington's. In August 1981 Mexico and France issued a declaration recognizing the FMLN-FDR alliance as a representative political force in El Salvador, a step that the United States condemned. Mexico received Guatemalan refugees and hosted talks, and Mexico City was the site of the Chapultepec signing. Regional diplomacy developed in stages. The Contadora Group, formed in 1983 by Colombia, Mexico, Panama, and Venezuela, proposed a framework for regional settlement and was later supported by Argentina, Brazil, Peru, and Uruguay; it did not reach a final treaty, but it legitimized a diplomatic approach. The Esquipulas process, which led to the August 1987 agreement known as Esquipulas II, associated with Costa Rican president Óscar Arias, committed the Central American presidents to national reconciliation, ceasefires, democratization, and an end to aid to irregular forces. And United Nations diplomacy, through the Secretary-General's good offices, the ONUCA observer mission in 1989, ONUSAL, and MINUGUA, became the framework for both settlements.

The Salvadoran and Guatemalan wars were shaped by Cold War intervention but cannot be explained as conflicts entirely directed by external powers. Their principal social and political divisions had substantial domestic origins, as Sections 91.1 through 91.12 showed, and their outcomes depended on decisions by Salvadoran and Guatemalan actors, including their officers, insurgent leaders, civilian politicians, church figures, and organized communities. External assistance, however, affected the scale and duration of the wars, and the end of the Cold War altered the incentives for settlement.

Section 91.18

Refugees, Displacement, Churches, Women, Indigenous Leadership, and the Humanitarian Consequences of War

The wars displaced hundreds of thousands of Salvadorans internally and abroad. Refugees sought shelter in Honduras, Mexico, Nicaragua, the United States, and Canada. In Honduran camps such as Mesa Grande and Colomoncagua, families organized schools and health programs under intense security scrutiny. Organized repopulations later returned to parts of Chalatenango, Cabañas, and Morazán. Salvadoran asylum applicants faced restrictive U.S. procedures, inspiring a church sanctuary movement; Congress created temporary protected status for eligible Salvadorans in 1990. These migrations continued to shape later regional history.

Guatemalan Maya refugees in southern Mexico experienced a different set of conditions. In Chiapas they lived first in border settlements, then in camps, where UNHCR and the Mexican refugee commission supported them, and organized committees, schools in Spanish and Maya languages, and women's groups. After the 1984 relocation to Campeche and Quintana Roo, refugee communities developed distinct settlements, and in 1992, after negotiations between refugee representatives and the Guatemalan government, they began organized returns, with the first collective return in January 1993. Returnees often faced land disputes, army suspicion, and slow reintegration.

Women organized in many ways. In El Salvador, mothers and relatives of prisoners and disappeared persons founded committees, notably COMADRES in 1977, that documented cases, publicized abductions, and confronted authorities, and their members were themselves detained and killed. In Guatemala, GAM, CONAVIGUA, and later groups did similar work for the disappeared and widowed. In refugee camps and displaced communities, women organized food distribution, schooling, health care, and documentation of abuses; in repopulated communities in El Salvador and in CPRs in Guatemala, they were central to maintaining households and community institutions. Their roles expanded their political participation, though the conditions that forced it were traumatic, and the extent to which it changed gender relations after the wars varied by community.

Religious institutions played divergent roles that should not be generalized. Catholic bishops, priests, and lay workers included people who denounced abuses, accompanied refugees, and documented violence, among them Archbishop Romero, his successor Arturo Rivera y Damas, whose archdiocesan legal aid office, Tutela Legal, recorded abuses, and, in Guatemala, Bishop Juan Gerardi, who directed the archdiocese's human-rights office and the Recovery of Historical Memory project (REMHI), whose report, Guatemala: Never Again, was presented in April 1998; Gerardi was murdered two days later. Other Catholic leaders supported military governments or kept silent. Protestant churches, including evangelical and Pentecostal congregations that had grown rapidly in the region, were also divided: some pastors and congregants were killed or fled, while some church leaders supported Ríos Montt and the army, and many people converted to evangelical churches during the war for reasons that included a search for protection and community. Historians of the period caution against portraying either Catholicism or Protestantism as uniformly one thing. The relationships between pastoral protection, political advocacy, and institutional support for military governments differed by place, period, and individual.

Indigenous communities, particularly Maya, merit independent attention. Maya leaders, authorities, spiritual guides, and organizations continued to act under repression, and their mobilization shaped the war and the peace. Rigoberta Menchú, a K'iche' woman whose father and brother were killed and whose testimony I, Rigoberta Menchú (1983, English edition 1984) drew wide attention, won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1992; in 1999 the anthropologist David Stoll challenged some details of her account, an argument that provoked debate about the nature of testimony, and the CEH's conclusions rest on a far larger body of evidence. Maya organizations, such as CUC, the Academy of Mayan Languages (founded 1986), and the Coordinating Body of Maya Peoples (COPMAGUA) formed in 1994, linked the defense of language, spirituality, and land with the peace negotiations. Communities preserved cultural practices through displacement, including weaving, language, and ritual, often at the cost of public visibility.

Survivors also sought missing relatives. In El Salvador the association Pro-Búsqueda, founded in 1994 with the Jesuit priest Jon Cortina, searched for children separated from their families during counterinsurgency operations. In Guatemala, family associations pressed for exhumations, and communities preserved names and testimony in memorial ceremonies. Civilians were not merely passive victims. They developed networks of survival and political organization, although their capacity to act was constrained by displacement, poverty, violence, and fear.

Section 91.19

Truth Commissions, Forensic Investigations, Documentary Evidence, and the Question of Historical Responsibility

The Salvadoran Commission on the Truth and Guatemala's Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) are the two most important investigative sources for the wars, and they differ in design. The Salvadoran commission was created by the 1991 Mexico City agreement, composed of three non-Salvadoran members appointed by the UN Secretary-General (Belisario Betancur, Reinaldo Figueredo, and Thomas Buergenthal), given about six months, and empowered to investigate "serious acts of violence" whose impact on society demanded public knowledge. It received testimony from thousands of witnesses and from the parties, conducted exhumations, and issued its report, From Madness to Hope, on 15 March 1993. It named individuals it held responsible in specific cases, including military officers and, in the Romero case, D'Aubuisson, a feature that provoked strong reaction from the armed forces and the government, which passed a general amnesty five days later.

The CEH was created by the June 1994 Oslo agreement, with a mandate that excluded attribution of individual responsibility and could not be used for judicial purposes. It consisted of Christian Tomuschat, a German jurist who served as coordinator, Otilia Lux de Cotí, a Maya K'iche' educator, and Alfredo Balsells Tojo, a Guatemalan lawyer. It began work in 1997, and its report, Guatemala: Memory of Silence, was presented on 25 February 1999 in twelve volumes. It relied on testimony, a database of cases, archives, expert studies, and previous investigations, including REMHI. The differences in mandate, with one commission empowered to name names and one prohibited from doing so, and in time and resources, shaped the findings.

Numbers must be read carefully. The Salvadoran commission recorded more than 22,000 complaints of serious violence, and witnesses attributed nearly 85 percent of the cases reported to it to state agents, allied paramilitary groups, and death squads, and a small percentage, about 5 percent, to the FMLN. These figures describe the commission's collected complaints, not a census of all crimes or deaths during the war. The CEH documented 42,275 victims in its case database, and, using its findings alongside other studies, estimated that more than 200,000 people had been killed or disappeared. It identified Maya people as 83 percent of the documented victims and attributed 93 percent of the documented violations to state forces and associated groups, with about 3 percent to guerrillas. These percentages refer to the documented cases and cannot be extended automatically to every death in the war. Historical findings should also be distinguished from criminal judgments: neither commission was a court.

Truth commissions do not stand alone. Forensic anthropologists, such as the EAAF in El Salvador and the FAFG in Guatemala, provided physical evidence of individual massacres; witnesses and church records provided names and narratives; civil-society investigators compiled case files; and declassified government documents, from the United States and, as they came to light, from Guatemalan military and police archives, provided evidence of planning and knowledge. The National Security Archive and others obtained and published thousands of American documents. Each source has limits: forensic work covers specific sites; testimony depends on survivors; documents record what institutions chose to write.

The CEH found that state agents committed acts of genocide against groups of Maya people in specified regions during the 1981–1983 campaigns. Its conclusion was grounded in patterns of killing, destruction of communal life, and evidence of intent under the Genocide Convention, not a claim that every death in Guatemala's war constituted genocide. Scholarly and legal debate concerns the evidentiary assessment of intent and responsibility, but the commission's finding is documented. Truth commissions, survivor testimony, forensics, and archival records illuminate different dimensions of these wars; none alone supplies an exhaustive account.

Historical Vocabulary Study III
Peace Negotiation and Historical Accountability

A ceasefire is a suspension of hostilities, which may precede or follow a comprehensive peace agreement. Demobilization is the disarming and discharge of combatants, usually followed by reintegration into civilian life. A truth commission is a temporary, officially sanctioned body that investigates past abuses and reports its findings, normally without judicial power. A forensic exhumation is the scientific excavation and analysis of graves. Human-rights verification means monitoring compliance with human-rights commitments, as ONUSAL and MINUGUA did. Transitional justice refers to measures, including trials, truth-telling, reparations, and institutional reform, for addressing past abuses. Historical memory refers to the ways societies remember and contest the past.

Why it matters: Each term describes a different tool with different powers and limits. A ceasefire ends fighting but not conflict; a truth commission reveals patterns but does not convict; an amnesty may enable peace while foreclosing prosecution. Students should distinguish among them and avoid treating any one as equivalent to justice or to peace.

Section 91.20

Comparing El Salvador and Guatemala: Peace Settlements, Historical Legacies, and the End of the Central American Wars

The two wars share important features. Both occurred in agrarian societies where land was concentrated and rural people had little political voice; in both, armies were political actors with autonomy and economic interests; in both, revolutionary organizations emerged after periods of political closure, influenced by Cuba and by local conditions; in both, counterinsurgency treated civilians as the base of the enemy; and in both, foreign assistance shaped the scale of fighting. In both, civilians were displaced in the hundreds of thousands, and the documented violations by state forces and allied groups far exceeded those by insurgents, though insurgents also committed abuses.

El Salvador's war centered on a sustained military contest between state forces and the FMLN. After the 1989 offensive reached the capital, stalemate and changing international conditions favored a settlement that reformed security institutions and legalized the FMLN. Guatemala's much longer conflict included devastating operations against Maya communities, while insurgent forces were comparatively weak by the final negotiations. Its accords consequently gave particular prominence to Indigenous rights and civilian control of the military. Neither country's settlement removed the deeper inequalities that had helped sustain war.

The agreements themselves differed. Chapultepec was a single comprehensive document built on earlier accords, with a compact timetable, a UN verification mission with substantial powers, and a Truth Commission established as part of the process. The Guatemalan accords were a series of thematic agreements negotiated over two years and signed in 1996, with a UN mission, MINUGUA, that verified human rights from 1994 and the peace commitments afterward, a historical clarification commission that could not name individuals, and a commitments agenda, including constitutional reform, that was broader in scope and harder to implement. The Salvadoran former combatants were reintegrated through programs that had mixed results, notably on land and employment; the Guatemalan ex-combatants and the URNG, as a smaller organization, had less leverage after demobilization.

Both countries have faced continuing disputes. Landownership remained highly unequal, and post-war reform programs reached only limited numbers. Poverty and migration persisted, with emigration to the United States forming transnational communities and linking war to later developments discussed in Chapter 111. Memory has been contested: in El Salvador the 1993 amnesty law blocked prosecutions until the Supreme Court's Constitutional Chamber declared it unconstitutional in 2016, and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights examined the El Mozote massacres in a 2012 judgment; in Guatemala, exhumations, memorials, and judicial cases, including the genocide proceedings against Ríos Montt, have produced both landmark rulings and reversals. Wartime institutions retained political influence, in different forms. These subjects are treated more fully in Chapter 94.

A brief comparison with Nicaragua is useful but must be limited. Nicaragua's revolution of 1979 took power, and the counterrevolutionary war that followed (Chapters 89 and 90) was fought against a revolutionary government, whereas in El Salvador and Guatemala the insurgents did not take power and the states they fought remained in place. That difference helps explain why Nicaragua's war was framed internationally as a conflict over a government's survival, and why the Salvadoran and Guatemalan settlements concentrated on reforming existing state institutions.

The end of Guatemala's war in December 1996 marked the termination of the region's last major Cold War-era civil conflict, while leaving unresolved social and institutional questions. Peace settlements ended organized armed conflict but did not erase the inequalities, institutional weaknesses, communal losses, and political disagreements that had shaped decades of violence.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Domestic Inequality or Cold War Intervention?

The question: The documentary record establishes extensive human-rights violations in both countries, so the debate is not whether atrocities occurred. It concerns the relative roles of domestic inequality, military institutions, revolutionary strategies, foreign support, and Cold War ideology in the emergence and course of the wars, and whether a single explanation fits both.

Interpretation A — Domestic structures as the primary driver: Scholars such as Elisabeth Wood and Tommie Sue Montgomery, writing on El Salvador, and Jim Handy and Beatriz Manz on Guatemala, emphasize land concentration, labor coercion, and political closure. On this view, mobilization arose from rural grievances and local organization, and external powers amplified and prolonged conflicts whose origins were domestic. In Guatemala, the argument adds the long history of racial hierarchy.
Interpretation B — Cold War ideology and intervention as decisive: Other scholars, and many participants in governments, emphasize the effect of Cold War doctrines, the 1954 intervention, counterinsurgency training, and aid in shaping security forces' views and capacities, and the effect of Cuban influence on insurgents. On this view, the doctrine of the internal enemy turned social conflict into war, and the scale and duration depended on external resources.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same documentary base: military plans, American and other embassy cables, guerrilla documents, truth-commission reports, forensic evidence, and testimony. Most historians combine elements of both, and the weight differs by case: Guatemala's ethnic dimension, which neither account alone explains, led scholars such as Greg Grandin, Victoria Sanford, and Virginia Garrard-Burnett to emphasize the history of racism and state-building, and the specific doctrines of Ríos Montt's army.

Why it matters: The answer affects how responsibility is assigned and how peace is judged. If domestic structures were primary, peace requires land, justice, and institutional reform; if external intervention was primary, the end of the Cold War might be expected to resolve conflicts. The aftermath suggests that the structural questions outlasted the Cold War.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation using evidence from this chapter, then evaluate whether the same explanation adequately accounts for both countries or whether Guatemala's Indigenous dimension requires a substantially different interpretation.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Agrarian Inequality to Negotiated Peace

Long-term causes

Land concentration from coffee expansion, exclusion of rural and Indigenous populations, army-oligarchy partnerships, racial hierarchy (especially in Guatemala), and memories of 1932 and 1954.

Short-term causes

Fraudulent elections and repression in the 1970s, popular organization, the Cuban and Nicaraguan examples, the failure of the 1979 reform coup, and the army's treatment of organization as subversion.

Event or transformation

Insurgent unification (FMLN 1980, URNG 1982) and escalation of counterinsurgency into mass violence against rural communities.

Immediate effects

Massacres, displacement into Mexico, Honduras, and cities, civil patrols and model villages, expanded U.S. involvement in El Salvador, and international human-rights attention.

Long-term consequences

Negotiated settlements in 1992 and 1996; truth commissions; security-sector reform in El Salvador; Indigenous-rights commitments in Guatemala; persistent inequality and impunity; migration.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains either war. Domestic grievances, military institutions, insurgent decisions, foreign support, and international change interacted, and alternatives existed at several points.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that El Salvador and Guatemala experienced prolonged civil wars because long-standing land inequality, military political power, and political exclusion combined with specific decisions in the 1970s and 1980s, and that those wars ended through negotiated settlements, in 1992 and 1996, that did not resolve their underlying causes. Neither war was inevitable, and neither can be reduced to a proxy conflict.

In El Salvador, coffee expansion from the 1880s concentrated land, dispossessed communal landholders, and bound landowners and officers in a political partnership. The 1932 uprising and the repression known as La Matanza established military rule and a lasting memory of mass killing, especially in the Indigenous west. From the 1960s the military's party governed through controlled elections; the fraud of 1972 and 1977 closed the electoral route, while peasant federations, base communities, unions, and students built popular organizations. The armed groups that emerged, the FPL, ERP, RN, PRTC, and the Communist Party's armed wing, differed from the mass organizations around them, though security forces and ORDEN treated them as one.

The October 1979 coup raised hopes for reform but failed to restrain the security forces. Civilian members resigned, the agrarian reform and bank nationalization proceeded amid violence, and death squads and military units killed organizers, political leaders, and, on 24 March 1980, Archbishop Romero. The FMLN, formed in October 1980 and distinct from the broader FDR, launched an offensive in January 1981 that failed to produce insurrection and led to protracted war.

The Salvadoran army's counterinsurgency, expanded with American assistance, repeatedly targeted rural communities. The Sumpul River, El Mozote, and El Calabozo cases differ in circumstance and evidence, but together they show the assumption that civilians were the guerrillas' base. El Mozote, documented by journalists, survivors, forensic work in 1992, and the Truth Commission, shows how official denial was overcome by evidence. The FMLN also committed documented abuses, though the commission attributed most complaints to state agents and allied groups.

The war's trajectory, from the 1984 election of Duarte to ARENA's rise, showed that electoral procedures alone could not settle the army's role. The November 1989 offensive and the murder of the Jesuits revealed stalemate and raised the political cost of the war, and UN-mediated negotiations produced the 1992 Chapultepec agreement, which reformed the armed forces, created a civilian police, and turned the FMLN into a party, while leaving land and violence unresolved.

Guatemala's war grew from the 1954 coup, which reversed agrarian reform and entrenched an army-landowner order. The 1960 officers' revolt and early guerrilla groups were largely Ladino and eastern; the army's Zacapa campaign, and its doctrine and networks, set precedents. In the 1970s Maya communities organized through cooperatives, Catholic Action, and the CUC, and EGP and ORPA built support in the highlands. Panzós, the earthquake, and the Spanish Embassy fire marked escalating state violence, and the army increasingly equated organization with subversion.

Under Ríos Montt from 1982, and in the campaigns guided by Plan Victoria 82, the army killed civilians on a vast scale in the western highlands. Río Negro, Plan de Sánchez, and Dos Erres illustrate different local contexts. The CEH documented 626 massacres attributed to state forces and concluded that acts of genocide had been committed against Maya groups in specific regions in 1981–1983, a finding that is historical and not a criminal judgment. Civil patrols, model villages, refugee flight, and CPRs reshaped rural life.

The return to civilian government in 1986 did not end the war, which continued until the accords negotiated under UN moderation in 1994–1996, culminating on 29 December 1996. The 1995 Indigenous-rights agreement acknowledged Guatemala as a multiethnic, multicultural, and multilingual nation, but implementation was partial.

External actors mattered but did not direct either war. The United States supported the Salvadoran state massively and had a changing relationship with Guatemala's army; Cuba and Nicaragua supported insurgents in limited and documented ways; Mexico, Israel, and others had distinct roles; and regional diplomacy, from Contadora to Esquipulas to the UN, created the framework for settlement.

Civilians organized for survival and justice, through refugee camps, women's associations, churches, Maya organizations, and searches for the missing. The truth commissions and forensic investigations created a documentary record, but their statistics measure different things and must be read within their mandates. Peace ended organized armed conflict; it did not erase inequality, impunity, or loss. This is the chapter's central lesson, and the one Chapter 94 carries forward.

MGU Library Research Project

Reconstructing a Civilian Massacre through Contemporary Records, Forensic Evidence, and Truth-Commission Findings

This project asks you to reconstruct one massacre from several kinds of evidence and to assess how knowledge of it changed over time.

  1. Select a case. Choose El Mozote, Río Negro, Plan de Sánchez, or Dos Erres.
  2. Find a contemporary record. Locate a contemporary report or government record, such as a press report, embassy cable, or military document, and summarize what it said and who wrote it.
  3. Find witness testimony. Summarize testimony from survivors or investigators, noting when and for whom it was given.
  4. Find forensic and commission findings. Summarize the forensic findings and the analysis by a truth commission or court.
  5. Write your findings. In a 5–7 page essay, identify what was known at the time, what was disputed, how later evidence changed understanding, and what uncertainties remain.

Library resources to use: The National Security Archive collections on El Salvador and Guatemala, the two truth-commission reports, forensic reports, and the scholarly works in the Scholarly Register below.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how societies with deep inequalities and armed institutions can move from exclusion to war through a sequence of choices, and how those choices fell hardest on rural families and Indigenous communities who were neither the architects of the conflict nor its principal beneficiaries. It also shows that the evidence for these events was created by many hands: survivors, journalists, church workers, forensic teams, diplomats, and commissioners, often at great risk and against official denial.

The wars connect regions and periods. They link the 1954 Guatemalan coup to the highland campaigns, the Cuban and Nicaraguan revolutions to insurgent strategy, the American policy of the Cold War to the scale of fighting, and the refugee movements to migration histories that continue in Chapter 111. They also show that Indigenous history did not end with conquest: Maya communities were targeted, organized, and negotiated within the modern state.

The settlements of 1992 and 1996 ended organized war, and their reforms were real. Yet land inequality, impunity, and the influence of wartime networks endured, and the later chapters of Volume V show how those unresolved conditions interacted with democratization, gangs, and organized crime.

Chapter 92 turns to Panama and the invasion of 1989, the last major episode of Part XX, where the end of the Cold War order in the hemisphere took a different form. Students should carry forward the habit this chapter models: distinguishing what a source documents, what it estimates, and what it cannot establish.
