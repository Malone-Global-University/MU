New Organized-Crime Frontiers: Tren de Aragua, Brazil, Ecuador, and Transnational Networks

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Prison, a Name, and a Transnational Problem

The organizations examined in this chapter were born in prisons, ports, and neighborhoods that states had stopped governing, and their histories show how criminal power can travel faster than the evidence about it.

On 20 September 2023, roughly eleven thousand Venezuelan police and soldiers, according to government statements, entered the Tocorón prison in Aragua State. They found a facility that had long been described as the headquarters of an organization called Tren de Aragua, and they reportedly found its leaders already gone. Within three years that organization's name would appear in indictments in a dozen American states, in Chilean courtrooms, and in a United States terrorism designation, and its most famous leader would be reported killed in the gold-mining country of southeastern Venezuela. The story of how a prison-born group became a transnational name, and of how little the name sometimes explained, opens this chapter.

Chapter 112 asks how Latin American organized crime developed beyond the Colombian and Mexican cartel systems of Chapters 97 through 104. The answer is not a single organization or model. In Venezuela, an inmate-led prison organization expanded along migration routes. In Brazil, two prison-born factions, the Primeiro Comando da Capital and the Comando Vermelho, developed over decades into very different organizations. In Ecuador, a country that had been known as an island of peace, factions linked to Mexican organizations fought over ports and prisons and drove homicide rates to the highest in the region. Around them, Caribbean gangs, illegal gold mining, and money laundering connected local violence to international markets.

The central historical question is how prison governance, migration, international markets, territorial competition, corruption, and state responses produced these new criminal networks, and why those in Venezuela, Brazil, and Ecuador took different forms. The chapter's argument is that no single cartel model explains the period. Some organizations developed centralized discipline; others operated through loose cells or shifting coalitions. Their growth depended on institutional conditions, opportunities for profit, control over territory or logistics, and relationships with state officials.

The chapter also takes responsibility for a vocabulary of care. A United States designation of an organization as a Foreign Terrorist Organization is a legal act of the executive branch, not a court verdict and not a scholarly classification. An indictment is an allegation, a conviction is a judicial finding, and a government's count of people charged is not a measure of an organization's size. Venezuelan migrants, the great majority of them ordinary families, must not be confused with the criminals who exploited them. The chapter treats prisoners, migrants, merchants, port workers, women, children, and residents under armed control as historical subjects, not as statistics.

Evidence for this subject is uneven. Historians rely on court records and indictments, government statements and designations, intelligence assessments, reports by international organizations such as the UN Office on Drugs and Crime and INTERPOL, human-rights investigations, academic studies of prisons, and investigative journalism. Many of these sources were created by police, prosecutors, or governments with political interests, and the voices of victims and of people in controlled neighborhoods are often indirect. Contemporary sources are used with their dates, and the chapter's cutoff is 16 November 2026, so that events still unfolding are marked as such.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first two follow Tren de Aragua from the prison at Tocorón to its expansion across the Americas, its prosecution in the United States, and the June 2026 announcement of Niño Guerrero's death. The third examines Brazil's PCC and Comando Vermelho, the October 2025 Rio de Janeiro police operation, and the 2026 United States designations. The fourth traces Ecuador's security crisis under President Daniel Noboa. The fifth turns to Caribbean and Haitian armed groups, illegal mining, and transnational finance, and then compares the cases.

In the larger textbook, this chapter belongs to Part XXV, which carries Latin America through 2026. It builds on the cocaine and cartel chapters of Part XXII and Part XXIII, the Venezuelan crisis of Chapter 109, and the Central American gang history of Chapter 111, and it prepares for the discussion of political realignment and security politics in Chapter 113 and of resource geopolitics in Chapter 114. Chapter 115 returns to Venezuela's wider crisis and the 2026 events that frame the June operation in Bolívar State.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the geography and organization of Latin American organized crime changed beyond the Colombian and Mexican cartel systems.
  2. Distinguish hierarchical trafficking organizations, prison gangs, territorial gangs, criminal federations, and decentralized networks.
  3. Trace the origins of Tren de Aragua in the Venezuelan prison system and the role of the Tocorón prison.
  4. Evaluate evidence for Tren de Aragua's structure, leadership, and membership, distinguishing estimates from established facts.
  5. Analyze how migration, local criminal markets, and corruption shaped the organization's expansion without equating migrants with criminals.
  6. Distinguish allegations, indictments, convictions, and legal designations in the cases of Tren de Aragua, the PCC, the Comando Vermelho, and Ecuadorian factions.
  7. Compare the institutional histories of the PCC and Comando Vermelho and their relationship to Brazilian prisons and territory.
  8. Assess the October 2025 Rio de Janeiro operation using official and independent sources.
  9. Explain why Ecuador became a major trafficking corridor and how its factions fragmented.
  10. Evaluate the legal and political controversies of Ecuador's internal armed conflict and the limits of homicide statistics.
  11. Interpret Caribbean, mining, and financial criminal markets as part of a transnational system without treating the Caribbean as a single criminal system.
  12. Connect the chapter's cases to debates about militarized suppression and to the political questions of Chapter 113.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did prison governance produce organizations that could expand beyond prison walls, and why did the results differ in Venezuela, Brazil, and Ecuador?
  2. What evidence supports and what limits the claim that Tren de Aragua is a centralized organization?
  3. How can historians discuss criminal organizations that exploited migrants without implying that migrants were criminals?
  4. What does a Foreign Terrorist Organization designation establish, and what does it leave unproven?
  5. Why did the PCC and the Comando Vermelho develop different structures?
  6. How should the Rio operation of October 2025 be evaluated when official and independent accounts differ?
  7. Why did a country with low homicide rates become a center of criminal violence within a few years?
  8. What can homicide statistics reveal about the success of emergency policies, and what can they conceal?
  9. Why do operations against leaders often produce fragmentation rather than the end of criminal markets?
  10. How do illegal mining, arms flows, and financial laundering connect local violence to international markets?

Historical Context

Chapters 97 through 104 described the Colombian cocaine economy and the Mexican cartel wars, and Chapter 100 followed the Colombian peace process. Those processes changed the supply side of the market: Colombian organizations fragmented, the FARC demobilized in 2016, and dissident groups and new intermediaries filled gaps. Chapter 109 described Venezuela's economic collapse and emigration, and Chapter 111 showed how prison, deportation, and gang politics reshaped Central America. This chapter takes those developments as background and does not retell them.

Three structural conditions recur. The first is the prison. Latin American prisons are among the most overcrowded in the world, and in several countries the state delegated order to inmate organizations. The second is trade infrastructure: ports, rivers, and highways that move legitimate commerce also move illicit goods, and corruption of inspectors and workers is essential. The third is demand, especially the growth of European cocaine consumption alongside continued demand in North America. Against that background, new organizations adapted to local conditions.

The United States enters this story through demand, firearms, finance, and policy, but it is not the protagonist. In 2025 and 2026, Washington used terrorism designations, sanctions, and prosecutions at an unprecedented scale, and it cooperated with governments in Venezuela and Ecuador. Latin American governments responded in different ways: Brazil's government rejected the terrorism label for the PCC and CV, while Ecuador's government welcomed foreign designations and cooperation. These choices reflected domestic politics as much as security analysis.

Terminology requires discipline. An allegation is a claim; an indictment is a formal accusation; a conviction is a court finding; a designation is an executive determination. Membership estimates are estimates. Finally, the chapter distinguishes events established by multiple independent sources from events reported by one government or still unfolding as of 16 November 2026.

Division I

Tren de Aragua: Origins, Prison Power, and Expansion

Sections 112.1-112.4

Section 112.1

The Changing Geography of Latin American Organized Crime

Chapters 97 through 104 followed the Colombian and Mexican trafficking systems from the rise of the Medellín and Cali organizations to the cartel wars and the synthetic-drug markets of the 2020s. Those chapters trace the organizations most often meant when English-language writers use the word "cartel." This chapter asks what happened to organized crime in the regions and countries those narratives leave at the edges. Its answer is that the traditional picture, in which a handful of Colombian and Mexican hierarchies supply a consuming North, no longer describes how illicit markets in the Americas are organized. By the 2010s and 2020s, prison-born brotherhoods, territorial gangs, shifting coalitions, and loosely linked local cells were building businesses of their own in Venezuela, Brazil, Ecuador, Haiti, and the Guiana Shield, and some of them were operating on several continents.

Several developments created the opening. The fragmentation of the large Colombian organizations in the 1990s, followed by the demobilization of the FARC after 2016 (Chapter 100), changed who controlled coca and cocaine supply, and coca cultivation and cocaine production reached record levels in the early 2020s according to UN Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) estimates. Demand in Europe grew during the same period, so that Atlantic routes through Brazil, Ecuador, the Caribbean, and West Africa became commercially important alongside the older corridors to Mexico and the United States. Containerized shipping, the expansion of ports, and the sheer volume of legitimate trade offered cover for illicit cargo. At the same time, weak or captured institutions, above all prisons, gave criminal leaders organizational spaces that the state itself had created and could no longer govern.

The chapter therefore uses a comparative vocabulary rather than a single model. A hierarchical trafficking organization centers on a leader or small leadership group who coordinate purchase, transport, and sale; the Medellín organization is the best-known example. A prison gang forms inside the penal system, where membership, discipline, and finance are organized among incarcerated people and then extended outward. A territorial gang draws its power from controlling a neighborhood, a road, a river crossing, or a mining camp, and profits from taxing the people and businesses within it. A criminal federation links semi-autonomous groups under common rules or a shared brand. A decentralized network consists of cells and brokers whose cooperation is transactional. Real organizations combine these forms, and they change over time. The categories are tools for comparison and not labels to be assigned once.

The word "cartel" itself deserves caution. Economists use it for firms that collude to fix prices, and the Colombian and Mexican organizations rarely behaved that way. Applying it to Tren de Aragua, the Primeiro Comando da Capital, or Ecuador's factions would add little clarity and would import assumptions about structure that the evidence does not always support. Governments, too, choose labels for political and legal reasons. A government that calls a gang a terrorist organization, a criminal faction, or an enemy combatant is invoking different legal powers, and Section 112.7 and Section 112.12 return to this point. Historians must keep the label separate from the analysis.

Finally, the chapter treats organized crime as a form of political economy in which states are participants as well as opponents. Corrupt officials sold protection, prison authorities delegated control to inmate leaders, politicians relied on gang influence in elections, and security forces committed their own violence against civilians. At the same time, ordinary people living under armed control, migrants crossing borders, merchants paying extortion, and incarcerated people held in overcrowded cells are the main historical subjects of this story, even though the surviving records often come from police, prosecutors, and journalists who saw them as victims, informants, or suspects. Evidence for this chapter is uneven and often contested, and each section says whose account it relies on.

The transition was not a simple replacement of large organizations by small ones. Colombian suppliers, Mexican transport groups, Brazilian intermediaries and local armed actors continued to cooperate when cooperation served them, even as they competed in particular territories. A port-based shipment may therefore involve a producer, an inland transporter, a corrupt official, a financing broker and a destination distributor who belong to different organizations. Police investigations often identify one portion of this chain and call it a network; the absence of evidence about its other participants should not be mistaken for evidence that there were none. Historians must reconstruct these relationships through dated cases and distinguish a recurring commercial partnership from membership in a common command.

Section 112.2

The Origins of Tren de Aragua and the Tocorón Prison System

To understand Tren de Aragua one must begin inside Venezuelan prisons rather than at borders. By the 2000s, Venezuela's penal system was badly overcrowded, with facilities designed for far fewer inmates holding many times their capacity, and state control inside many prisons had eroded. Inmate leaders known as pranes assumed authority over cell blocks, regulated who lived where, settled disputes, and collected payments from other prisoners for security, sleeping space, visits, and protection. Researchers and journalists describe this arrangement, sometimes called the pranato, as an informal governance system in which officials tolerated or negotiated with inmate leaders because they could not or would not impose order. The government created a Ministry of Penitentiary Services in 2011 after a long standoff at the El Rodeo prisons, but violent confrontations and inmate rule continued in many facilities.

The prison at Tocorón, in Aragua State in north-central Venezuela, became the best-known case. According to a 2025 assessment by the U.S. National Intelligence Council (NIC), Tren de Aragua formed around 2007 and used Tocorón as its first operational base. The name, as InSight Crime and other observers explain, may derive from a labor union connected to a never-completed railway project in the state, though the origin is not firmly documented. Three men are regularly identified as the group's founding leaders: Héctor Rusthenford Guerrero Flores, known as "Niño Guerrero"; Yohan José Romero, known as "Johan Petrica"; and Larry Amaury Álvarez Núñez, known as "Larry Changa." InSight Crime dates Guerrero's command of the group to around 2015. These attributions come from police sources, investigative journalism, and later U.S. charging documents, and they have not been tested in a Venezuelan or international trial.

Inside Tocorón, reporting describes a prison that functioned as a headquarters. Inmate leaders reportedly controlled access and movement, taxed other prisoners and merchants, ran communications, and managed money flowing from outside businesses. Accounts of recreational facilities, including a swimming pool and nightclub, circulated widely in the press and in the government's own later statements, and they became a symbol of how far control of the prison had shifted. Such accounts need care. They come from journalists, former inmates, officials with an interest in showing the prison had been lost, and visitors, and they cannot be audited. What they show reliably is that a prison served as an organizational center, a place from which orders could be given, revenue collected, and loyalty enforced.

The origins of Tren de Aragua should be distinguished from the broader Venezuelan crisis examined in Chapter 109. The organization took shape during the 2000s, before the hyperinflation, shortages, and mass emigration of the later 2010s. Those later conditions did not create the group, but they altered its opportunities. When millions of Venezuelans left the country after 2015, a prison-born organization with contacts, money, and a reputation for violence found new markets in the routes migrants used. Likewise, the government's decision to cede practical control of some prisons to inmate leaders was a matter of state policy and capacity, and it should not be confused with a claim that the government directed the group's later foreign operations, a distinction the NIC assessment makes central (Section 112.7).

Tren de Aragua also grew by absorbing and displacing rivals. InSight Crime reports that after the killing of the leader of a competing Venezuelan gang, Tren del Llano, in 2016, Tren de Aragua took over parts of its territory in Aragua and neighboring Guárico. Control of territory outside the prison, including neighborhoods, transport routes, and later mining areas, supplied revenue streams that did not depend on inmate payments. By the mid-2010s the organization combined a prison base, street-level extortion, and links to drug sales, a combination that would travel well.

A prison's importance extends beyond the presence of an imprisoned leader. Confinement brings together people from different neighborhoods, permits reputations to circulate and creates a population dependent on scarce goods, protection and access to visitors. The imprisoned leader's authority may rest on the ability to settle conflicts and arrange resources, even when those activities are enforced through coercion. Outsiders who supply families, collect payments or connect prisoners to other local markets can become part of the organization without themselves being incarcerated. This arrangement illustrates an institutional paradox: when authorities make the prison less accountable, incarceration can become a setting in which criminal organizations are consolidated rather than dissolved.

Section 112.3

Niño Guerrero, Leadership, Membership, and Criminal Organization

Niño Guerrero is the figure around whom most accounts of Tren de Aragua are organized, and the evidence for his role is of several kinds. Venezuelan court records, as reported by journalists, show that he faced charges in Maracay over killings, robberies, weapons offenses, and drug trafficking. The U.S. Treasury Department sanctioned him in 2025, together with other alleged leaders including Romero. A federal indictment in New York, reported in December 2025, charged him with racketeering, drug, and firearms offenses, and the State Department offered a reward of up to five million dollars for information leading to his arrest or conviction. None of these documents is a conviction. They record what Venezuelan and American authorities alleged, and they must be read as claims backed by varying amounts of evidence that the public has not yet seen tested.

Other alleged leaders have different profiles. Romero is associated in reporting with the Las Claritas gold-mining district in Bolívar State, where InSight Crime says he built a base that taxed miners, merchants, and service workers. Álvarez Núñez was reported detained in Colombia and remained in custody there as of mid-2026, according to InSight Crime. U.S. prosecutors in Colorado allege that two other men, Giovanni Vicente Mosquera Serrano and Brawnis Dominique Suárez Villegas, managed Tren de Aragua operations in the United States, Colombia, and the rest of the Americas, and Mosquera Serrano was placed on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted list. Those are allegations in an indictment, and the case illustrates how U.S. prosecutors describe a hierarchy of regional managers beneath the founding leaders.

Structure is the central analytic problem. Some accounts portray a centralized organization with a command in Tocorón issuing orders to cells abroad. Others describe a brand or franchise: individual cells, often led by Venezuelans who had passed through Aragua prisons or had personal ties to its leaders, operate in their own cities, borrow the name for credibility, and may send money or pay deference to leaders without receiving instructions. The EU Agency for Asylum and U.S. intelligence analysts both describe flexible external networks whose connections to the leadership vary. Judicial records in Chile, summarized by InSight Crime, show lieutenants dispatched to cells in several countries and sometimes rotating among them, which suggests real coordination, while the same body of evidence shows cells acting with considerable independence. Historical Debate 1, later in this chapter, takes up the problem.

Membership estimates deserve particular skepticism. Figures cited in government statements and press reports range from the low thousands upward, with an estimate near 4,000 appearing in some assessments, and U.S. officials have at times used larger numbers. None of them is a census. A membership count depends on definitions: whether it includes core leadership, paid associates, members of allied gangs, people who use the name, or those merely charged. Arrests complicate the picture further. The number of people charged in a country says as much about police priorities and charging practices as about the organization's size, and the appearance of the name in a police report does not prove authentic membership. Tattoos, nationality, and residence in a particular neighborhood are not evidence of membership, and the sources reviewed for this chapter warn explicitly against such inferences.

The organization's boundaries also shifted through splintering. A federal case in New York, for example, involves a faction called "Anti-Tren," described in the indictment as a splinter tied to Tren de Aragua, and prosecutors charged 27 alleged members in a 38-count superseding indictment with racketeering, kidnapping, sex trafficking, and murder-related offenses. The existence of such a faction illustrates that the label could cover groups that competed with one another as well as groups that cooperated. Students should keep this fluidity in mind when reading any single total.

Membership figures in this setting cannot be read like the head count of a registered association. Analysts may count the men identified as leaders, people seen acting for a cell, persons arrested by police or individuals who claim a collective identity. Each approach has different risks of overcounting or omitting participants. Investigators may also rely on communications, financial relationships and testimony from cooperating defendants; none by itself proves command over activity in another country. A leader's name can function as a brand for intimidation even after his practical control has diminished. The death announced in 2026 consequently raised two separate questions: who could claim succession within the original organization, and whether distant cells would acknowledge such a successor at all.

Section 112.4

The 2023 Tocorón Operation and the Loss of the Prison Base

On 20 September 2023, Venezuelan authorities launched a large security operation at the Tocorón prison. Government statements, reported widely, put the force at roughly eleven thousand police and military personnel, and the operation was presented as the restoration of state control over a prison that had long served as a criminal headquarters. When forces entered, accounts report, the facility was largely emptied of its leaders. Guerrero and other senior figures were gone, and officials said they had left before the operation began. Inmates were transferred to other prisons, and the government publicized the dismantling of the facilities that had symbolized inmate rule.

The operation's meaning is contested. Venezuelan officials portrayed it as a victory over a gang and as evidence that the state controlled its prisons. Critics and analysts noted that the departure of the leadership suggested advance warning, and they raised the possibility that officials had assisted or tolerated the escape. The sources reviewed here do not establish who warned the leaders or whether any official was prosecuted for doing so, and allegations of collusion remain allegations. What can be said is that the leaders were not captured at Tocorón, that the prison ceased to function as their base, and that Venezuelan authorities have not made public a detailed investigation of how they left. Official reporting in Venezuela during this period was limited by restrictions on independent press and by the government's own political interest in controlling the narrative, as discussed in Chapter 109.

The loss of the prison had organizational consequences. Analysts argue that Tocorón had been the node through which payments, instructions, and prestige flowed, and that its fall weakened the centralized command that had defined the group. InSight Crime's reporting concludes that after 2023 Tren de Aragua's international cells continued operating without coordinating through a central leadership, and the Latin Times summary of analyst views makes the same point. At the same time, networks already established in Colombia, Peru, Chile, and elsewhere did not depend on the prison to survive. Their revenue came from local extortion, smuggling, and sales, and their members had ties of kinship and acquaintance that outlasted the prison. Dismantling a headquarters, in other words, removed a symbol and a coordinating hub without ending the businesses it had helped launch.

The aftermath also shows how the group adapted. Leaders dispersed, and reporting places Guerrero later in Bolívar's mining region, though the sequence of his movements is not publicly documented. The mining enclaves of southeastern Venezuela, where state control was thin and gold offered immediate cash, became a refuge and a source of revenue, a connection Section 112.8 and Section 112.18 develop. Venezuelan prison policy shifted toward more state control and transfers of prisoners, but the underlying conditions of overcrowding, corruption, and delegated authority that allowed inmate governance to develop were not resolved in a single operation.

The Tocorón raid also reveals the limits of using the number of deployed personnel as a measure of success. Eleven thousand officers and soldiers may demonstrate state capacity to surround and enter a facility, but the relevant institutional outcomes are different: whether prisoners receive lawful treatment, whether communications and corruption are interrupted, whether senior suspects are lawfully apprehended and whether later prison governance is subject to scrutiny. Public announcements and photographs of seized property are evidence of an operation, not a complete accounting of its results. In the absence of publicly accessible inspection records, researchers should leave questions about conditions after the transfer of prisoners open rather than infer that durable reform followed automatically.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Pran and Pranato

A pran is a Venezuelan prison term for an inmate leader who exercises authority over a cell block or an entire facility. The system of inmate rule, often called the pranato, grew in overcrowded prisons where the state could not guarantee order.

The term matters because Tren de Aragua's origin was a pran-led prison organization. It is misleading to translate pran as "warden" or "gang boss" alone: a pran combined enforcement, taxation, dispute resolution, and negotiation with guards and officials, and his authority depended on arrangements with the state as well as on violence.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life in a Pran-Governed Prison

For incarcerated Venezuelans, prison life under inmate governance depended on money and status. Payments to inmate leaders could determine where a person slept, whether he received visits, and whether he was protected from violence. Families brought food, medicine, and cash, and women, who made up most visitors, often bore the burden of paying and of enduring searches and uncertainty. Children grew up with a parent's incarceration shaping household budgets and travel.

The record is thin because most surviving accounts come from officials, journalists, and a few former inmates. Researchers must therefore be careful to avoid portraying prisoners as a single group: some were serious offenders, others had been held for years awaiting trial, and many were simply poor.

Section 112.5

Expansion across Colombia, Peru, Chile, and the Americas

Venezuela's emigration crisis, treated in Chapter 109, ultimately sent more than seven million people abroad, most of them to other Latin American countries, according to figures compiled by United Nations agencies. The overwhelming majority were ordinary families and workers seeking safety, food, and income. Criminal organizations followed the same routes, but it would be a serious error to treat the migrant population as the criminal organization's population. Authorities in several countries and researchers have emphasized that Venezuelan migrants were far more often victims of armed groups than members of them, and a person's nationality, asylum claim, or border crossing says nothing about gang affiliation.

The first major foreign foothold, according to InSight Crime, was in Colombian border areas, notably the town of La Parada near Cúcuta, where migrants entered Colombia through irregular crossings. Armed groups, including Colombian paramilitary successor groups and guerrillas, already taxed those crossings, and Tren de Aragua's members reportedly found a niche there by charging for passage, controlling informal routes, and lending money to arriving migrants. From Colombia the network is described as extending to Bogotá, Medellín, and other cities, working through arrangements with local criminal groups rather than conquering them.

Peru and Chile became the best-documented cases. In Peru, authorities identified Venezuelan groups involved in extortion, robbery, and sex trafficking in Lima and northern cities, some using the Tren de Aragua name and others, according to investigators, acting independently or under other names. Chile offers the clearest judicial record. Chilean prosecutors tied Tren de Aragua cells to kidnappings and killings, including the 2024 abduction and murder of former Venezuelan military officer Ronald Ojeda in Santiago, a case Chilean prosecutors attributed to a network with links to Tocorón. Venezuelan authorities denied responsibility. In July 2026, InSight Crime reported that eleven alleged members had been sentenced in Chile, a reminder that some prosecutions have produced convictions even as the larger structure remains disputed.

Ecuador, Bolivia, Brazil, Argentina, and other countries reported smaller or less consistently documented presences, often involving individuals arrested for crimes who were said to belong to Tren de Aragua. European agencies, including the EU Agency for Asylum, have noted the organization's limited and uncertain presence in Europe. The evidence base varies sharply from country to country, and Quantitative Study 1 below organizes it in a table to emphasize that reported presence is not the same as control.

Why did this expansion occur? Several conditions mattered: large populations of vulnerable, often undocumented migrants who had little access to formal credit, labor markets, or police protection; local criminal economies that could be tapped through partnership; weak border control and corruption at crossings; and the group's reputation for violence, which made threats credible even when the people making them had little organization behind them. Expansion also reflected a pattern common to prison-based organizations, in which members released or fleeing to other countries recreate relationships there. The result was a patchwork of local cells whose links to Aragua could be real, nominal, or fabricated.

Expansion also depended on the legal position of those most exposed to crime. A Venezuelan migrant arriving without documents or without access to local banking might have no lawful means of borrowing money, renting a room or obtaining secure employment. Criminal lenders and traffickers could then present themselves as providers of a needed service before using threats to demand payment or labor. These vulnerabilities differ between cities: a functioning migrant-support office, a trusted police complaints mechanism and community organizations can change an offender's opportunities without changing the nationality of the people living there. Regional migration figures therefore belong in the chapter as a measure of displacement, not as a proxy for membership in a gang. Most displaced Venezuelans were pursuing ordinary household goals, and their legal status must never be treated as a criminal classification.

A second problem is attribution. An investigation may find the same symbols, slang or name in different countries, but repeated naming does not itself establish a common treasury, leadership hierarchy or decision-making chain. Chilean judicial outcomes are stronger evidence about specific people and acts than anonymous claims of affiliation, yet even a conviction of one local cell does not establish the organization of every alleged cell elsewhere. Comparative history should classify jurisdictions according to what was actually demonstrated: claims of presence, arrested suspects, prosecuted defendants and adjudicated convictions.

Section 112.6

Extortion, Human Trafficking, Kidnapping, and Criminal Revenue

Authorities attribute a wide range of crimes to Tren de Aragua networks, and the evidence for each differs. Extortion is the most consistently documented. In Colombia, Peru, and Chile, cells are described as charging fees to merchants and residents under threat of violence, and as operating gota a gota lending, a form of informal credit with daily collections at very high effective interest rates, which became associated with Venezuelan migrant populations in several countries. Extortion works through intimidation and the credible threat of retaliation, and many victims are other migrants with little recourse to authorities.

Smuggling and trafficking must be distinguished. Migrant smuggling involves facilitating illegal border crossing for payment and, in principle, ends when the person arrives, although it can involve abuse and death. Human trafficking, as defined in the 2000 United Nations Palermo Protocol, involves recruitment or movement of a person by force, fraud, or coercion for exploitation, and it need not involve crossing a border at all. Investigations in several countries and in the United States describe cases in which women and girls were moved by Tren de Aragua-linked groups and forced into commercial sex, and U.S. prosecutors have charged members with sex trafficking, including of minors. Victims often faced debts claimed by their exploiters, threats against relatives, confiscation of documents, and fear of deportation, which discouraged reporting. Because the crime depends on concealment, court cases are the main source and represent only the cases that reached prosecution.

Kidnapping and violent enforcement appear in both Latin American and American records. Prosecutors allege that cells kidnapped rival criminals, debtors, and migrants, and carried out killings to punish disloyalty. On 1 July 2026, the U.S. Justice Department announced charges in the Northern Districts of Texas and Illinois against eight alleged members of Tren de Aragua for kidnappings resulting in death and racketeering involving murder, with the defendants accused of kidnapping three people on or about 24 August 2024. These are charges, and the department's own press release describes them as such.

Other alleged revenue sources include narcotics distribution, robbery, and financial crimes. In January 2026, the Justice Department announced an additional indictment in an investigation of "ATM jackpotting," a scheme to cause cash machines to dispense money, bringing the total to 87 charged defendants, many of them Venezuelan and Colombian nationals, and alleged that the proceeds funded Tren de Aragua. The government's characterization of the scheme as a funding mechanism for the organization is part of its case, and the degree to which proceeds reached a centralized leadership has not been independently established. In Venezuela itself, illegal gold mining and the taxation of mining communities became a significant source of income, a theme taken up again in Section 112.18.

It is useful to compare what the evidence can and cannot support. Court records establish that individuals who identified with or were prosecuted as members of the group committed specific crimes. They do not establish that every crime attributed to the name was ordered by a single leadership or that crimes of this type are unique to the group. They also say little about the experience of victims, whose voices appear mainly through prosecutors' summaries and a few investigative accounts. Everyday Life Study 2 later in this chapter tries to restore that perspective.

A close account of exploitation requires attention to the path from an initial promise to coercion. A person may accept a loan, a journey or a job on terms represented as voluntary, and only later encounter confinement, threats or an asserted debt that cannot realistically be repaid. If investigators describe the entire process simply as illegal migration, the coercive episode and its victims disappear from view. Conversely, not every unauthorized border crossing is trafficking. The Palermo framework requires particular conduct and, for adults, specified means of coercion or deception for an exploitative purpose; the treatment of children differs. This legal distinction also explains why prosecutions from different countries cannot simply be combined into a universal TdA trafficking total.

Victim protection is also an evidentiary issue. Witnesses may fear both the alleged traffickers and the authorities from whom they seek assistance, particularly when immigration enforcement and criminal investigation occur together. Those pressures affect the number of reported crimes and the willingness of witnesses to testify. Governments that count only identified victims may report a low figure in areas where access to protection is weakest. A responsible textbook therefore reads official trafficking totals as observed cases within a reporting system, not a complete census of coercion.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Smuggling and Trafficking

Migrant smuggling is facilitating illegal entry across a border for payment; human trafficking, under the 2000 UN Palermo Protocol, is recruiting, moving, or holding a person through force, fraud, or coercion for exploitation, whether or not a border is crossed.

The distinction matters because the two crimes have different victims, laws, and remedies. A smuggled migrant may consent to travel, while a trafficked person is exploited; yet smuggling debts can turn into trafficking, and criminal groups may do both.

Section 112.7

Tren de Aragua in the United States: Designation and Prosecution

Alleged members of Tren de Aragua appeared in American criminal cases from the early 2020s, and the issue became a major subject of national politics in 2024. On 20 February 2025, the U.S. State Department designated Tren de Aragua, together with several Mexican and Central American organizations, as a Foreign Terrorist Organization (FTO) and Specially Designated Global Terrorist. An FTO designation is a legal instrument under U.S. immigration law. It makes it a federal crime to knowingly provide material support to the organization, allows the freezing of assets, and makes non-citizens associated with the group inadmissible. It is a decision by the executive branch, and it is not a verdict of any court and not a classification used by scholars of organized crime. Critics note that the legal definition of terrorism requires political or ideological aims that do not obviously describe profit-seeking criminal groups, while supporters argue that the groups' violence and transnational reach warrant the legal tools.

The designation was soon connected to immigration enforcement. In March 2025 the White House invoked the Alien Enemies Act of 1798, a wartime statute, claiming that Tren de Aragua was invading the United States at the direction of the Venezuelan government, and the administration removed Venezuelan men to a prison in El Salvador. That action produced extensive litigation over due process and over whether the statute applied, and the legal record is long and contested. Students should consult court records rather than summaries, and should note that deportation proceedings and criminal prosecutions are separate processes with different standards of proof.

The government's central factual claim, that the Venezuelan state directed Tren de Aragua to operate in the United States, was examined in an April 2025 assessment by the National Intelligence Council. According to coverage of its declassified portions, the NIC found insufficient evidence of a centrally directed campaign by the Venezuelan government and assessed that the group was decentralized, while recording a dissenting view by some analysts that individual Venezuelan officials may have cooperated with members. That disagreement, between analysts who saw no policy and those who suspected facilitation by individual officials, is a useful example of how intelligence agencies express uncertainty. Elsewhere, the FBI was reported to have assessed that Venezuelan officials "likely facilitate" migration of members, a lower-level claim than a directed campaign.

Criminal prosecutions have grown. The Justice Department reported charges against more than 70 individuals in a set of indictments unsealed in December 2025, including the New Mexico case in which eleven alleged members and leaders were indicted for racketeering related to a kidnapping and killing in Albuquerque, and the Colorado case against alleged leaders. Its public totals have risen over time: "more than 290" in January 2026 and "nearly 350" by July 2026, counts that include people charged and not necessarily convicted. Some defendants have pleaded guilty or been convicted, and the record varies by district. Students should therefore distinguish an administrative designation, an indictment, an arrest, a prosecution, a guilty plea, and a conviction and sentence, and should treat government totals as claims by prosecutors until compared with docket records.

The United States case illustrates how several different legal systems intersect without being identical. Criminal indictments describe conduct that prosecutors must prove against named individuals. Immigration proceedings determine whether a particular noncitizen may remain in the country. Foreign Terrorist Organization designation identifies a group under the criteria in federal law and has consequences for material support and immigration enforcement. These determinations are related in public discussion but do not legally establish one another. A person cannot be treated as convicted merely because a press release describes the individual as an affiliate of a designated organization.

The April 2025 National Intelligence Council assessment adds another distinction: government misconduct by particular Venezuelan officials would not, by itself, prove that the Maduro government centrally directed criminal migration to the United States. The document's majority assessment and the Federal Bureau of Investigation's separate view of some officials' relationships with TdA must both be represented. Intelligence analysis is not a court judgment, and disagreement within the intelligence community is itself evidence of the uncertainties attached to identifying relationships between a state, corrupt intermediaries and a decentralized network.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Foreign Terrorist Organization

A Foreign Terrorist Organization (FTO) is a designation by the U.S. Secretary of State under Section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act. It triggers a ban on material support, asset freezing, and immigration consequences. It is distinct from the Specially Designated Global Terrorist (SDGT) authority under Executive Order 13224.

It is a U.S. legal designation, not a court judgment or a scholarly classification. Using it as a descriptive term for an organization's nature, as opposed to its legal status in the United States, blurs a distinction this chapter maintains.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate 1: Is Tren de Aragua a Centralized Transnational Organization or a Network of Cells?

The question: Did Tren de Aragua operate as a hierarchy directed from a core leadership, or as a brand shared by semi-independent cells?

Interpretation A — Coordinated organization: U.S. indictments describe leaders directing cells, including photographs of a victim's body sent to leaders to confirm a killing, and Chilean judicial records show lieutenants dispatched to cells in several countries. On this view, the group exercised real command, especially while Tocorón functioned.
Interpretation B — Decentralized network: The NIC memorandum, the EU Agency for Asylum, and analysts at InSight Crime stress flexible external networks, local autonomy, and use of the name by independent criminals, especially after the loss of Tocorón in 2023.

Evidence: Both views draw on indictments, intelligence assessments, and journalism. The views may be partly compatible: coordination may have been stronger early and weaker later, or stronger in some countries than others.

Why it matters: If the group is centralized, targeting leaders is plausible strategy; if decentralized, leadership killings may fragment without eliminating it, and legal attributions of group responsibility become harder.

Section 112.8

The June 2026 Death of Niño Guerrero and the Future of Tren de Aragua

In 2026, the political situation in Venezuela changed in ways examined in Chapter 115, including the January capture of Nicolás Maduro by U.S. forces and the emergence of an interim government under Delcy Rodríguez. Against that background, the Venezuelan armed forces launched an operation on 10 June 2026 in the Las Claritas mining area of Bolívar State, near the borders with Brazil and Guyana, an area linked to Tren de Aragua. On the evening of Friday, 12 June, President Donald Trump announced that U.S. Southern Command had killed Héctor Rusthenford Guerrero Flores in a strike. Venezuela's information ministry confirmed his death hours later in a statement describing a combined operation between Venezuelan and U.S. security bodies in southeastern Bolívar in which, after clashes, Guerrero was "neutralized."

The two accounts differ in emphasis. The American president described a U.S. military strike, and the Venezuelan statement described a joint operation with clashes, and the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime later described an air strike on a compound in the Venezuelan Amazon. Reuters, the Anadolu Agency, Al Jazeera, and other outlets reported both announcements. The sources reviewed for this chapter do not include independent forensic documentation of the death, and students should treat the details of the operation as reported by the two governments and press. That does not mean the death is in serious doubt. Both governments announced it, no contrary claim has emerged, and the U.S. had long offered a reward for him.

The consequences are less clear than the event. Analysts quoted in several outlets argued that the killing was a symbolic and operational blow but not a decisive one, because Guerrero's day-to-day control had weakened after he fled Tocorón in 2023, regional cells had acted with growing autonomy, and the organization's revenue did not depend on him. InSight Crime identifies Yohan José Romero, "Johan Petrica," as the last original founder at large and a likely successor, while Chilean commentary has named Mosquera Serrano as another possible senior figure. Authorities had not, in the reports reviewed, announced Petrica's death or capture. The Global Initiative argued that Tren de Aragua is one of many Venezuelan criminal organizations and that the key brokers of Venezuela's criminal economy are state-embedded actors who may be less affected by operations against any single gang.

The operation also raises questions about mining governance. InSight Crime suggests that the deployment at Las Claritas likely broke arrangements between the government and the mining gang that had allowed it to operate with impunity. Whether the new Venezuelan authorities will sustain pressure on illegal mining, and whether other armed groups will fill the space, was unresolved.

As of 16 November 2026, the following were established: Guerrero's death was announced by both governments on 12 June, and U.S. federal charges against him had been filed in New York in December 2025. Several matters remained uncertain: who leads the organization, whether its foreign cells still acknowledge any central authority, how much territory and revenue the successor groups control in Bolívar, and whether U.S. and Venezuelan cooperation will continue. A student writing about this event should avoid either declaring the organization finished or assuming it remains a single command, since the evidence supports neither.

Historical interpretation after a leader's death must separate immediate disruption from longer-term outcomes. A death can eliminate a broker who maintained relationships among otherwise independent groups, trigger competition over revenue, or have little operational effect on actors who already managed local businesses autonomously. The evidence needed to judge these possibilities includes subsequent convictions, reliable reports of leadership selection, changes in victimization and documented changes in financial networks. None was comprehensively available by the chapter cutoff. The reported cooperation between the United States and Venezuelan officials is also significant as a diplomatic development, but it must not be transformed into proof that their governments agreed on the organization's full structure or on the circumstances of every alleged offense.

Sections 112.9-112.12

Section 112.9

Primeiro Comando da Capital: From Prison Brotherhood to Criminal Enterprise

The Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC, "First Command of the Capital") was founded in 1993 in the São Paulo state prison system, and its origin cannot be separated from the brutality of Brazilian incarceration. On 2 October 1992, military police stormed the Carandiru detention house in São Paulo and killed 111 prisoners, an event that became a lasting symbol of state violence against incarcerated people. Within a year, a group of inmates at a prison in Taubaté formed a brotherhood that presented itself as a defense of prisoners' rights against abuse and as a means of ending violence among inmates. The group's early statute, circulated among prisoners, set rules of loyalty, forbade killings of fellow members without approval, and required contributions. Whatever its founders intended, the rules gave the organization a durable method of discipline in a system where official order was weak.

Several features distinguish the PCC from a Mexican-style cartel. It grew through membership and rules rather than through a family or a single trafficking enterprise. Members are "baptized" into the organization, pay monthly dues, and are subject to internal discipline administered through a hierarchy of cells and regional coordinators. The organization developed procedures for judging disputes among members, which reduced violence among them and, in effect, supplied a private justice system in prisons and in many peripheral neighborhoods. Researchers who have studied the group, including Brazilian sociologists and the International Institute for Strategic Studies in its analyses, emphasize that this governance function, and not only trafficking, explains its legitimacy among many prisoners.

The PCC demonstrated its capacity in May 2006, when coordinated attacks on police and public buildings across São Paulo state followed the transfer of leaders to isolation, and in the violent reprisals by police, hundreds of people died in a few days. The episode showed both the organization's ability to coordinate from within prisons, using smuggled mobile phones, and the willingness of state forces to respond with lethal force. Afterward, the state expanded isolation and surveillance measures, while the organization continued to grow, often shifting from open confrontation to quieter business activity.

Expansion followed prison transfers and commercial opportunity. As São Paulo's government moved leaders to other states and as the organization recruited in prisons across Brazil, the PCC established a presence in most Brazilian states. Its leaders also built relationships along the border with Paraguay and Bolivia. In 2016, the killing of the Paraguayan trafficker Jorge Rafaat near the Brazilian border, widely attributed to PCC-linked actors, marked a shift toward direct control of supply chains for cocaine and marijuana crossing into Brazil. Estimates cited by U.S. officials in 2026 put the PCC and Comando Vermelho at more than 50,000 members combined, and press reports say the PCC operates in nearly thirty countries through members, affiliates, and collaborators. These are estimates by officials and analysts, with the same limitations as other membership figures.

Over time the PCC became increasingly a business organization, engaging in cocaine export, but also in the legitimate economy through fuel distribution, financial services, and other firms. Section 112.12 discusses the August 2025 operation that targeted that activity. The organization's trajectory shows how a prison brotherhood can develop sophisticated criminal governance over decades, but also how the state's own punitive policies, including mass incarceration, supplied the organization's recruits and rationale. Brazil's incarcerated population grew to be among the largest in the world, and research suggests that in many prisons membership in a faction offered protection that the state did not.

The memory of the 1992 Carandiru massacre mattered to the PCC's founding story because it presented state violence as a shared grievance. That does not make the organization a human-rights movement or justify its later criminal activities. Rather, it helps explain how inmate claims of mutual protection and dignity could become a language of recruitment in a system marked by overcrowding, unequal discipline and high levels of violence. Researchers have described both the internal rules that reduced some disputes among members and the punishment used against people who violated those rules. The same organization could offer predictability to insiders and impose fear on outsiders.

The PCC's expansion into trafficking also depended on institutional geography. São Paulo is connected by road and commerce to Brazil's interior, the Port of Santos and borders with Paraguay and Bolivia. Local prison relationships could support cooperation among brokers at these separate locations, but a shipment through the port did not necessarily require orders from a single central leader. The distinction between common rules and direct command is therefore essential when comparing PCC with Tren de Aragua and Comando Vermelho.

Section 112.10

Comando Vermelho: Rio de Janeiro, Favelas, and Territorial Competition

The Comando Vermelho (CV, "Red Command") is Brazil's oldest major criminal faction. It emerged in the late 1970s in Rio de Janeiro's prison system, in a setting shaped by the military dictatorship examined in Chapter 84. At the Cândido Mendes prison on Ilha Grande, common criminals were held in proximity to political prisoners convicted under the national security law, and accounts describe the common prisoners adopting organizational methods and a rhetoric of collective solidarity. The group first took the name Falange Vermelha and later Comando Vermelho. How much political ideology it absorbed is debated; many historians argue that organizational technique, rather than political commitment, was what the prisoners took from the encounter.

In the 1980s, Rio became a major cocaine transit and consumer market, and the CV moved from prison solidarity to control of retail drug sales in hillside communities (favelas, or morros). Control meant armed presence at the entrances, taxation of local commerce, and the settlement of disputes. In many neighborhoods where the state provided little else, armed groups became the local authority, at times offering services or aid while also imposing curfews and punishing perceived informants. Scholars describe a relationship with residents that included coercion, dependence, and in some cases consent, and they caution against reducing favela residents to either victims or collaborators.

The CV's history is one of fragmentation. Rival factions emerged from splits, including Amigos dos Amigos and Terceiro Comando Puro, and from the start of the 2000s armed competition among factions defined Rio's violence. The CV also differs from the PCC in structure: it is usually described as a looser federation of territorial groups with considerable local autonomy, and it relies more on heavily armed control of neighborhoods. That difference shaped how each organization responded to police operations and to rivals. The CV's rivalry with the PCC became a national war after a truce collapsed in 2016, and in early 2017 prison massacres in Manaus and Roraima, in which dozens of inmates died, were widely attributed to the breakdown of the alliance between the PCC and its northern allies and the CV.

The CV must also be distinguished from milícias, the militias that control other parts of Rio's metropolitan region. Militias, organized in large part by current and former police officers, firefighters, and soldiers, present themselves as protectors against drug gangs but charge residents for security and for services such as gas, transport, and internet, and they have engaged in killings and land seizures. They are a different form of armed territorial control with a different relationship to the state, and sometimes they cooperate or compete with drug factions. Reducing Rio's violence to a war between "gangs" and police misses this third element.

The CV has expanded beyond Rio, especially into the Amazon region, the Brazilian North, and border areas, where it competes with the PCC and local groups over river routes and towns. Its growth reflects the logic of Brazil's drug economy: as cocaine moves from Andean production zones to export ports, control of corridors matters as much as control of Rio's hills. Section 112.11 turns to those routes.

Rio's armed territorial politics involve more actors than one criminal faction. Milícias, many with members or leaders drawn from serving or former security personnel, developed systems for charging residents and businesses for services and protection in some neighborhoods. Their rivalry with drug factions sometimes caused violent changes of control; elsewhere different groups coexisted or negotiated. Residents might encounter a supposed security provider, an extortion collector and a political intermediary in the same local space. It would be misleading to describe every community under criminal control as a voluntary constituency of the controlling group.

The public-security debate also turns on the relationship between tactical operations and everyday government. Residents want protection from armed actors, but raids that interrupt schools, clinics and transport or produce civilian deaths can deepen distrust of authorities. The existence of dangerous armed groups does not remove the state's obligation to investigate every death and protect people who are neither suspects nor participants. These distinctions are particularly important in interpreting the October 2025 operation and the subsequent investigation.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Facção and Milícia

A facção (faction) is the Brazilian term for a prison-born criminal organization such as the PCC or CV, with membership rules and territorial or market interests. A milícia is a paramilitary-style group, typically led by current or former security-force members, that controls territory and charges residents for protection and services.

The two should not be conflated. Militias claim to protect communities from drug factions, while factions arise from prison networks; both use violence, and both may collaborate or fight depending on local interests.

Section 112.11

Cocaine Routes, Ports, and Brazil's International Criminal Networks

Brazil is both a consumer of cocaine and a transit country for exports to Europe and Africa. Cocaine produced in Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia reaches Brazil through land crossings, airstrips, and the Amazon river system, including the Solimões route into Manaus and the Paraguay–Paraná waterway that links Bolivia, Paraguay, and Brazil to Atlantic ports. Brazil's long borders, its large export economy, and its dense network of ports make it a convenient platform. The main Brazilian port, Santos, one of the largest in the Southern Hemisphere, handles an immense volume of containers, and seizures there and at other ports show that cocaine is hidden among legitimate cargo such as sugar, coffee, and frozen goods.

The international market adds relationships beyond Brazil's borders. Cocaine shipped from Brazil reaches European ports, often through major hubs in Belgium and the Netherlands, and through West African countries and the Iberian Peninsula. Investigators and researchers also report connections between Brazilian factions and Italian and other European criminal groups, though the strength and nature of such ties differ across cases. The sources reviewed for this chapter support these as patterns reported by law enforcement and think tanks but do not provide a complete map of who controls what, and the secrecy of illicit trade makes any map provisional.

Corruption is an essential part of the system. Port workers, customs and police officials, and the owners of logistics businesses can be recruited or coerced to place and remove cargo. Money laundering draws on both informal systems and formal companies. The next section describes how Brazilian investigators identified fuel companies and financial technology firms used for laundering, and the PCC's 2024 designation of an operative by the U.S. Treasury for laundering roughly $240 million shows the international dimension of such schemes.

The relationship between the PCC and the CV shaped these routes. For years the two organizations cooperated in some places and competed in others. After the 2016 split, they fought for border towns, river corridors, and prisons in the north, and their rivalry spread violence into places such as the Amazon states where cocaine routes converge. Border communities suffer the consequences: extortion, displacement, killings, and the recruitment of young people into armed groups. Indigenous territories and riverside communities, which often lack a strong state presence, have been drawn into these conflicts, a theme that overlaps with the illegal mining discussed in Section 112.18.

Brazil's export infrastructure creates opportunities precisely because it serves an enormous legitimate economy. Most agricultural, industrial and container movements have no connection to crime; the historical problem is that illicit actors seek access to selected points in this volume of lawful trade. A detected shipment may reveal a single compromised link rather than control of an entire port, and a large seizure may reflect improved inspections as well as increased trafficking. Researchers should therefore use recurring court cases, customs records and port-security assessments to infer the persistence of routes rather than extrapolate from one spectacular interdiction.

The geography also shapes who suffers. Border communities along Amazonian rivers and overland routes may face armed pressure and corrupt enforcement, while residents of wealthy export cities encounter the proceeds mainly through financial crime investigations. Trafficking networks bridge these places, but their members do not necessarily occupy the same organizational rank or share the same political connections. This is why a national criminal-faction label is less useful than a grounded account of local partnerships, transport corridors and markets.

Section 112.12

Brazil's Security Crisis, the 2025 Rio Operation, and 2026 Designations

Brazil's police are among the most lethal in the world in absolute terms, and police killings are a major element in the country's security crisis alongside criminal homicides. The Brazilian Forum on Public Security, a nongovernmental research institution, reports annual figures for total intentional violent deaths and for deaths caused by police, and these numbers must be kept separate because definitions differ. In Rio de Janeiro, police operations in favelas have repeatedly produced mass casualties, and in 2020 and again in later years the Supreme Federal Court restricted such operations during the pandemic and then set rules that the court finalized in April 2025, including requirements for body cameras and the preservation of crime scenes.

On 28 October 2025, Rio de Janeiro's state government launched "Operation Containment" against the Comando Vermelho in the Penha and Alemão complexes, deploying around 2,500 police officers with armored vehicles and helicopters. It became the deadliest police operation in the city's history. The state government's figures at one point counted 117 suspects and four police officers dead, and other counts, including those of the Public Defender's Office and Human Rights Watch, reached 122 victims. Officials said the operation followed a year of investigation, and the state governor defended it as a necessary blow against a violent faction. Residents recovered bodies from a forested hillside after police withdrew, according to Human Rights Watch.

The principal independent investigation is Human Rights Watch's October 2026 report, "A Strategy of Death," based on interviews with 41 witnesses, relatives, and officials, 2,800 pages of autopsy files, and about 4,400 hours of police body-camera footage. HRW concluded that officers and commanders violated the Supreme Court's orders and police protocols, that body cameras were obstructed or failed, and that none of the footage it reviewed shows the killings. HRW also reported that the Comando Vermelho continued to control the complexes after the police withdrew. Its conclusions are those of a human rights organization and rest on documents and interviews, and the state government's account of the operation differs. UN experts and Brazilian public defenders also called for independent investigation. Students should compare the sources and note that an official count of "suspects" and a victim count can describe different groupings.

International law enforcement turned the issue into a diplomatic one. On 28 May 2026 the U.S. State Department designated the PCC and the CV as Specially Designated Global Terrorists, with Foreign Terrorist Organization status effective 5 June 2026, making them the first Brazilian entities on the FTO list. The Lula government rejected the decision, stating that these groups are profit-driven criminal enterprises and not terrorist organizations under Brazilian law, and it feared foreign interference. Political allies of the Bolsonaro family supported the designation, and reporting says Senator Flávio Bolsonaro had urged the step. The designation can freeze U.S. assets and criminalize material support, and law firms advised financial institutions and businesses with Brazilian operations about compliance.

Between these positions lies a legal question. In international and Brazilian law, "terrorism" and "organized crime" are separate categories, and the choice to apply one or the other determines the powers available to governments. A designation also does not by itself describe a group's structure. In August 2025, Brazil had already carried out "Operation Hidden Carbon" (Carbono Oculto), its largest operation against the PCC, targeting a scheme that allegedly used fuel companies and financial firms to launder money. That operation used the tools of financial investigation and prosecution that Section 112.19 discusses, and it is a reminder that the Brazilian state has more than one response available.

Interpreting the Rio death toll requires a precise denominator. Human Rights Watch's October 2026 investigation reported 122 deaths in all, consisting of 117 people who were not police officers and five police officers; four officers died immediately and another later. Official accounts characterized many of the non-police dead as suspects, but that description is not an independent judicial finding about each individual. The investigation's review of thousands of hours of camera footage did not locate footage showing a killing, raising questions about both recording compliance and the investigation's ability to reconstruct lethal encounters. An honest assessment should acknowledge serious armed threats faced by officers while requiring individual scrutiny of the uses of lethal force.

At the national level the Brazilian Forum on Public Security's 2026 yearbook offers a different perspective from reports about a single raid. It counted 40,775 intentional violent deaths in 2025, a rate of 19.1 per 100,000, alongside 6,602 deaths resulting from police intervention. Because the former classification includes police-caused deaths, the latter cannot be added to it as though they were separate categories. Declining overall lethal violence can therefore coexist with a serious problem of police lethality. Students should ask whether an intervention improves neighborhood safety over time rather than assessing its value only from the number of people killed or weapons seized.

Sections 112.13-112.16

Section 112.13

Ecuador's Transformation into a Major Trafficking Corridor

Ecuador was long described as an "island of peace" between Colombia and Peru, the world's two largest coca producers for much of the period examined in this volume. Official homicide rates were below ten per 100,000 residents as late as 2019, and the country was known for banana exports, oil, shrimp, and tourism. Within a few years, Ecuador's homicide rate rose to among the highest in Latin America, and ACAPS, a humanitarian analysis organization, reports about 8,248 homicides in 2023, 7,063 in 2024, and 9,216 in 2025, corresponding to rates of about 46.2, 39.3, and 50.9 per 100,000 inhabitants. These series require matching definitions and publication dates, as Quantitative Study 2 explains.

The explanation begins with geography and economy. Ecuador sits between Colombian and Peruvian production zones and has Pacific ports, notably Guayaquil, through which huge quantities of legitimate goods, especially bananas, pass on the way to Europe and North America. Containers of fruit offer cover for contamination of cargo by traffickers, with the cooperation or negligence of port, shipping, and customs workers. The Colombian peace process of 2016, which demobilized the FARC but left dissident groups and a power vacuum in border regions, shifted the incentives of armed actors along the northern frontier. Meanwhile, rising cocaine demand in Europe and the declining reliance on Caribbean routes made Pacific-to-Europe shipments more attractive.

Ecuador's dollarized economy, adopted in 2000 after a severe financial crisis, also matters. It is easier to launder and move dollars through a dollarized system, and analysts have pointed to the vulnerability of its financial sector, though the extent of laundering is hard to measure. Lawful export industries such as bananas and shrimp must be distinguished from the illicit operations that exploit their infrastructure. The overwhelming majority of Ecuadorian exporters, port workers, and fishers are not criminals, and many have suffered extortion and violence.

Ecuador also became linked to powerful foreign organizations. Research and official statements describe relationships between Ecuadorian factions and Mexican organizations, with Los Choneros associated with the Sinaloa organization and Los Lobos with the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, as well as reports of European criminal partners. Such ties placed Ecuadorian groups within the international systems described in Chapters 102 and 104. Prisons, in turn, supplied the organizational structure, as the next section shows.

Ecuador's position on the Pacific coast made commercial infrastructure as important as geography between two coca-producing countries. Containers carrying legitimate agricultural and seafood products travel through ports with large flows of private trucks, warehousing services and maritime connections. Criminal organizations did not have to replace that export economy; they needed access to selected logistical links and protection from enforcement. Investigations into contaminated shipments are therefore evidence of compromised segments of a complex supply chain, not proof that ordinary exporters or port workers were broadly criminal.

The timeline of Ecuador's transformation matters. A relatively low homicide rate in the early 2010s was not evidence that trafficking had been absent, but the later sharp rise in killings demonstrated that competition and institutional weakness had intensified. Changes in Colombian criminal markets, growth in European demand, corruption and local political violence interacted rather than unfolding as one master plan. Port cities such as Guayaquil and communities near strategic roads faced particularly acute pressures. For residents the result was not only fear of murder: transport costs, schools, business opening hours and the willingness to report extortion were altered as armed groups competed for control.

The country's dollarized currency simplified some lawful international commerce, but dollarization alone cannot explain trafficking or laundering. Criminal revenue also depends on coercion, trusted intermediaries, legal and illicit businesses, and decisions made by enforcement agencies. Comparative work should resist attributing a national security crisis to one economic characteristic when the same legal currency is used for millions of ordinary transactions.

Section 112.14

Los Choneros, Los Lobos, Prison Violence, and Gang Fragmentation

Los Choneros originated in Manabí province, on the coast, and its leader José Adolfo Macías Villamar, "Fito," directed activities while imprisoned for years. The organization's founding-era leader, Jorge Luis Zambrano, was killed in December 2020, and the aftermath of that killing, according to analysts, began a first wave of fragmentation. Los Lobos emerged from the Choneros' split, and other groups, including the Tiguerones, the Chone Killers, and various smaller factions, formed through alliances, betrayals, and territorial disputes. The result was a changing landscape of competing factions rather than a single mafia.

Prisons were the arena. Ecuador's prisons were overcrowded and poorly controlled, and gang leaders governed cell blocks. A series of massacres beginning in 2021 killed hundreds of prisoners, including episodes in which more than a hundred people died in a single event at a Guayaquil prison, and many victims were killed with firearms, explosives, and knives. The violence reflected conflict over control of prison populations and the drug trade, and the government's inability to prevent it exposed a collapse of state authority.

The conflict escalated outside the prisons. In August 2023 the presidential candidate Fernando Villavicencio, an anticorruption journalist, was assassinated after a campaign event in Quito. In January 2024, Fito escaped from a prison in Guayaquil, and on 9 January armed men entered the studios of the television station TC Televisión during a live broadcast, taking staff hostage on camera. Violence in several cities followed, with attacks on police and prisons. The sequence presented a direct challenge to the state and prompted the government's declaration of an internal armed conflict.

Fragmentation continued after 2024. Fito was recaptured in June 2025 and extradited to the United States in July 2025, an event that analysts say altered the balance of power among criminal groups, and the Choneros struggled to remain united. In September 2025 the United States designated Los Choneros and Los Lobos as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, and InSight Crime reports that it later added other Ecuadorian groups, including the Tiguerones and the Chone Killers. Such designations can affect the groups' external finances and extradition possibilities, but analysts also observe that the arrest or killing of leaders has fueled infighting rather than reducing violence.

The fragmentation of Los Choneros illustrates the dangers of imagining criminal organizations as permanent entities. When a dominant figure dies or is imprisoned, local commanders may bargain over territory and revenue and seek protection from external trafficking partners. They may retain old symbols while exercising independent authority, or adopt new organizational names to recruit and intimidate. Prison violence can then spill onto the streets as families, transport businesses and neighborhoods become targets of retaliation. The historical question is not only which faction won a confrontation but what institutions failed to prevent imprisoned leaders from directing conflict outside prison walls.

For Los Lobos and other factions, the label of alliance with a foreign cartel must be assessed with care. A recurring purchase or transport relationship with an organization abroad does not necessarily mean direct command by that organization, and police intelligence, journalistic reporting and criminal trials may give different pictures. Researchers should distinguish a reported working relationship from a documented financial or hierarchical tie. This same method applies to the comparisons with PCC, CV and Tren de Aragua.

The January 2024 crisis made prisons and public institutions simultaneous theaters of violence. The escape of the Choneros leader known as Fito, episodes of prison disorder and the on-air seizure of a television studio produced an atmosphere of emergency that reached far beyond the immediate victims. For journalists the broadcast attack was an assault on public communication; for families it reinforced a perception that no space was secure; and for the executive it provided a justification for sweeping measures. Each perspective matters, but the sequence does not establish that every subsequent security operation was effective or lawful.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Medellín to Dispersed Criminal Networks

What changed? Hierarchical Colombian and Mexican trafficking organizations gave way, in several countries, to prison-born federations, territorial gangs, and loose networks; new routes to Europe and Africa grew; terrorism designations became a major legal tool.

What remained continuous? Demand abroad, corruption, weak prisons, and the use of legitimate trade as cover persisted, as did the focus of state responses on force.

Who experienced change most directly? Residents of controlled neighborhoods, migrants, prisoners, port workers, and merchants paying extortion.

Who experienced greater continuity? Consumers and financial institutions that benefit from illicit flows, and elites able to avoid scrutiny.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that changing organizational form does not eliminate criminal economies.

Section 112.15

Daniel Noboa, the Internal Armed Conflict, and Emergency Security Policies

President Daniel Noboa responded to the January 2024 crisis with Executive Decree 111, which declared an "internal armed conflict" and identified more than twenty criminal groups as terrorist organizations and "belligerent actors." The decree authorized the armed forces to operate against them, and the military took over security in prisons. The government cast the move as a war, in a language Noboa has used repeatedly, and combined it with states of emergency, curfews, mass arrests, new legislation, and cooperation with foreign governments.

The legal basis was controversial. Ecuador's Constitutional Court reviewed the decree and related emergency measures, and legal scholars and human rights organizations debated whether calling domestic crime an armed conflict was consistent with constitutional limits and international humanitarian law. Human Rights Watch has documented both the severity of criminal violence and allegations of serious abuses by security forces. One widely reported case involved four boys from the Las Malvinas neighborhood of Guayaquil, detained by soldiers in December 2024 and later found dead; prosecutors charged soldiers in the case. Such cases show why critics argue that emergency powers carry risks for civilians, especially poor and Black and Indigenous communities, while supporters argue that the state must respond forcefully to groups that had overwhelmed it.

Policy evolved over time. The government moved large numbers of prisoners to a new maximum-security facility, El Encuentro, in November 2025. Voters in a November 2025 referendum rejected proposals that included allowing foreign military bases, indicating limits on public support for some elements of the security agenda. In March 2026 Noboa joined other leaders at a security summit hosted by Donald Trump in Florida, reflecting growing alignment with the Trump administration's approach, and Ecuador collaborated with the United States on military operations. In July 2026 the government adopted a new security plan that kept the militarized approach but promised measures to reduce unemployment and school dropout, according to InSight Crime.

Judging effectiveness requires care. In 2026 the government repeatedly announced declines in homicides, including a reported 28 percent decrease in March compared with March 2025 during a curfew in four provinces, and a reported 39 percent decrease in May after a curfew in nine provinces. The Interior Ministry reported a 27.7 percent decline in intentional homicides over the first five months of 2026. Analyses by independent organizations, however, found more modest declines or sustained violence: one reported 4,882 homicides between January and July 2026 compared with 5,295 in the same period of 2025, a drop of about eight percent, and a Dialogo report noted that 2026 remained on pace to be among the deadliest years on record. Police later described a violent second half of the year as Los Lobos, Los Choneros, and a dissident faction fought over routes. Differences in periods, definitions, and sources explain part of the gap, and the numbers should not be treated as evidence for or against the policy without them.

The declaration of an internal armed conflict represented more than a change in rhetoric. It involved a government effort to frame armed organizations as enemies to be confronted by military as well as civilian authorities. International humanitarian law sets specific legal criteria for the existence of a non-international armed conflict, including the organization of armed groups and the intensity of hostilities. The government's classification cannot alone establish that those criteria were met in every locality or for every listed organization. Conversely, authorities retain duties to protect civilians and investigate abuses regardless of the legal vocabulary selected.

Emergency powers also changed the everyday operation of the state. Curfews and checkpoints could temporarily limit armed movement, but they also complicated commuting, informal commerce and access to medical care. In areas where residents already feared criminals, a second fear arose when officers treated broad demographic characteristics or presence in a neighborhood as evidence of guilt. Human-rights monitors argued for individualized grounds for detention and accessible complaint mechanisms. Public opinion about tough security measures must be interpreted alongside these legal questions and actual crime trends, rather than assumed to establish the success or failure of every operation.

The November 2025 referendum provided an institutional test of the government's broader agenda: voters rejected all four questions, including the proposal about foreign military bases. That outcome was not a referendum on every police or military action then in force. The opening of the high-security prison El Encuentro in the same month was a separate administrative development, and its effects on prison order could not be settled immediately. By 2026, judicial decisions, legislative debates and subsequent security indicators all remained necessary to evaluate the state response.

Section 112.16

Ecuador's Violence through 2026: Criminal Rivalries and Civilian Consequences

The human costs of the crisis extend beyond homicide. Extortion, locally called vacunas (literally "vaccines"), affects shops, transport operators, farms, and schools, and when payment is refused, owners and workers may be threatened or attacked. Kidnapping for ransom, including of business owners and their families, rose, and the fear of both has changed daily routines, including school attendance, business hours, and travel. Some communities were displaced by fighting among factions, and local officials, prosecutors, judges, and journalists have been threatened or killed.

Violence is geographically concentrated. The provinces of Guayas, Los Ríos, El Oro, Manabí, and Esmeraldas, together with cities such as Guayaquil, Durán, and Machala, bear much of the burden. Press accounts report 319 homicides and 11 massacres in Los Ríos in the first four months of 2026 despite states of emergency, and an analyst cited by Dialogo reported that Durán reached roughly 140 homicides per 100,000 inhabitants, while homicides in Quito rose 22 percent in 2026 according to official data. Such concentration means that a national average can mask extremely high local rates, and that declines in one area may reflect displacement of violence to another.

The economy is also affected. Ports, shipping, tourism, and investment are sensitive to insecurity, and analysts have warned that continued disorder around ports could threaten export industries. In August 2026 the U.S. Treasury sanctioned an Ecuador-based network associated with Los Choneros and Los Lobos that allegedly used fishing businesses and vessels around Manta to move cocaine toward Mexico and the United States, according to the GIS Reports analysis. That action illustrates how the same coastal economy that sustains legitimate fishing households is used by illicit networks.

As of November 2026, the security situation remained unsettled. Government operations had captured or killed numerous leaders, Fito was in U.S. custody, and officials claimed reductions in homicide. At the same time, fragmentation, infighting, and the persistence of high violence in several areas suggest that these developments did not yet amount to durable security. Government announcements, foreign designations, and the arrests of leaders are not, by themselves, evidence of long-term success, and the 2026 totals were not final when this chapter was completed.

The distinction between a complete calendar year and a partial-year comparison is decisive. Reports available by the historical cutoff stated that Ecuador recorded 9,216 violent deaths in 2025, while police figures published on November 12 counted 7,026 from January 1 through November 9, 2026, compared with 7,941 in the same period of 2025. That is a reported decline of about 11.5 percent on a like-period basis; it does not make the 2026 year-end figure known. Government announcements of much larger percentage declines during a particular curfew month answer narrower questions and cannot be substituted for a national annual trend.

A decline in killings also does not establish the disappearance of criminal control. Extortion can continue when rival groups negotiate an uneasy territorial division, and displacement may increase where residents no longer believe the police can protect witnesses. Citywide totals conceal differences among neighborhoods, as do national comparisons between a dangerous coastal province and less violent areas. The prosecution of alleged gang leaders and the operation of new high-security prisons should be assessed alongside case clearance, legal process, the protection of witnesses and the safety of ordinary families.

Women and adolescents frequently bear consequences that do not appear directly in homicide tables. Families may change routes to school, close small shops earlier, withdraw from civic events or avoid reporting threats. People harmed by forced recruitment and sexual exploitation may remain invisible when institutions count only killings and major seizures. A mature historical account therefore pairs quantitative indicators with documented testimony, while avoiding fictitious representative anecdotes. The security crisis should be described as a change in the relationship between communities and state institutions, not simply a sequence of spikes and declines in reported deaths.

Prison figures also change with both the census date and definition of custody. A June 2026 Ecuadorian census counted 38,179 inmates and reported substantial overcrowding; those people should not be equated with convicted offenders, since detention before trial forms part of prison populations. The figure is not comparable without adjustment to a Brazilian end-of-2025 prison stock or to annual admission totals. Quantitative Study 2 uses both national examples to teach the difference between prison population and the flow of arrests or convictions.

Section 112.17

Caribbean Trafficking Networks, Haiti, and Maritime Crime

The Caribbean is a zone of movement connecting South American production areas, Central American and Mexican routes, and North American and European markets. Go-fast boats, fishing vessels, small aircraft, and containers carry drugs, firearms, and people, and island and coastal states with limited resources struggle to police their waters. Historically, Caribbean ports had also been centers of contraband and smuggling in the colonial period (Chapter 43), but present-day criminal flows should not be treated as a simple continuation. The region is not a single criminal system. Trafficking in Jamaica, the Dominican Republic, Trinidad and Tobago, Haiti, and the Guianas differs in actors, markets, and state responses.

Haiti is the most severe case of armed-group control. In 2023, major coalitions of gangs formed an alliance called Viv Ansanm ("Living Together"), associated with the leader Jimmy "Barbecue" Chérizier, and United Nations reports describe gangs controlling much of the capital, Port-au-Prince, and key roads. Gangs engage in kidnapping for ransom, extortion at checkpoints and ports, sexual violence, and killing, and they have displaced hundreds of thousands of people. The country's long political crisis, which this volume treats from the Haitian Revolution (Chapter 50) forward, left weak institutions, and the 2021 assassination of President Jovenel Moïse deepened the collapse. In 2025 the United States designated Viv Ansanm and another Haitian group as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, and international security missions were authorized, the details of which students should check in UN Security Council records.

Haitian gangs are not mainland-style trafficking organizations. Reports by the UN Office on Drugs and Crime and the UN Security Council emphasize that arms and ammunition enter Haiti largely from outside, including from the United States, while the gangs' revenue relies heavily on kidnapping, extortion, and control of territory more than on exporting drugs. This distinction matters for policy: interdiction of firearms, financial sanctions, and security support address different parts of the problem. Haitian civilians, including women and children, are the principal victims, and their testimonies appear in UN human rights reports.

Haiti's case requires a separate institutional history from the transnational trafficking corridors of the wider Caribbean. The coalition Viv Ansanm brought together previously rival armed groups and sought to control strategic road connections and access to parts of Port-au-Prince. Gran Grif, a distinct organization associated with violence in the Artibonite region, was also designated by the United States in May 2025. These designations are executive legal determinations; they do not replace investigations of particular attacks or establish identical structures for the two groups. Haiti's armed actors often extracted revenue through control of neighborhoods, transport corridors, markets and access to essential services, rather than through the export-oriented cocaine model familiar from earlier chapters.

State breakdown has serious humanitarian consequences even where criminal profit is difficult to quantify. Movement to hospitals, schools or food markets can become dangerous or prohibitively expensive. International security operations face the competing requirements of confronting heavily armed groups and avoiding further harm to residents; a temporary reopening of a road cannot itself demonstrate durable civilian protection. UN reports on forced displacement, sexual violence, attacks on children and restrictions on humanitarian operations should therefore stand beside enforcement statistics. Migrant and displaced people require protection irrespective of which side controls the road they must cross.

The Caribbean's lawful maritime trade also matters. Islanders depend on shipping, fishing, tourism and movement between ports; criminal activity exploits pieces of these systems rather than constituting the system as a whole. Arms entering Haiti, narcotics passing through selected locations and money moving through international intermediaries involve different crimes and different investigative authorities. Combining them into one story without identifying the actors would obscure the highly localized character of coercion and the political diversity of Caribbean states.

Section 112.18

Illegal Mining, Amazonian Frontiers, and Environmental Crime

Gold prices rose sharply over the past decade, and INTERPOL's Secretary General said in 2026 that illegal gold mining had become the fastest-growing revenue stream for organized crime groups in the region. Illegal mining in the Amazon and the Guiana Shield involves many actors: small-scale miners who work in hazardous conditions, owners of dredges and equipment, suppliers of fuel and mercury, traders who buy and export gold, armed groups that tax or protect operations, and, at the end of the chain, refiners and buyers abroad. The criminal organizations in this chapter engage with the sector in different ways. In Venezuela's Bolívar State, mining camps such as Las Claritas have been controlled by armed groups linked to Tren de Aragua, and in Brazil the Yanomami emergency, which the Brazilian government declared in 2023, drew attention to illegal mining on Indigenous land.

Mining crime does not operate separately from legitimate business. Gold laundered through legal export companies reaches international markets, and officials, security forces, and local politicians have been implicated in protection and corruption. Labor exploitation and human trafficking, including of women and children in mining camps, are documented in regional reporting. Environmental consequences include deforestation and mercury contamination of rivers and food chains, which harms Indigenous and riverside communities. These harms are a matter of environmental history that Chapter 114 treats in the context of resource geopolitics, and this section stays focused on criminal markets.

INTERPOL's January 2026 announcement of "Operation Guyana Shield" provides a recent example of international response. Carried out in December 2025, the operation brought together police and prosecutors from Brazil, French Guiana, Guyana, and Suriname for the first time in a joint cross-border operation. It involved more than 24,500 checks of vehicles and people and resulted in 198 arrests, seizures of gold, cash, mercury, firearms, and drugs, and the interception of a bus carrying undocumented migrants, including minors, whom officers suspected had been exploited. Officials said suspects might link to a major gold exporting company in Guyana. As with any enforcement operation, the statistics show activity and not the size of the illicit economy, and the arrests are not convictions.

Illegal gold mining becomes more dangerous when armed control is layered onto longstanding questions of land tenure, Indigenous rights and weak inspection capacity. Some miners work independently or enter the sector because they lack alternatives; others labor under threats or debt. Armed groups may charge for access, extort transporters or use violence to control routes. These different roles should not be collapsed into the claim that every informal miner belongs to organized crime. Distinguishing informal work, environmental violations and forced labor is necessary for both ethical analysis and effective policy.

The environmental damage is similarly distributed. Mercury can enter waterways and accumulate in food chains, while excavated riverbeds and deforestation alter fisheries and traditional livelihoods. Indigenous and riverine residents may suffer these effects without receiving the financial benefits of gold exports. When authorities focus only on armed miners, they may miss the legal firms, purchasing networks and corrupt officials through which unlawfully extracted material reaches international markets. Financial tracing and due-diligence rules are thus not separate from conservation; they address a different stage of the same historical problem.

Operation Guyana Shield provides an unusually well-dated example. The December 2025 operation, announced by INTERPOL on January 22, 2026, involved 198 arrests and more than 24,500 checks. Those numbers document a coordinated intervention involving four jurisdictions, not the number of people ultimately convicted or an estimate of the gold market's value. The more demanding research question is what happened after the operation: whether trafficking routes changed, whether labor exploitation declined and whether institutions maintained oversight of legal mineral purchases. The material available by November 2026 could not settle all of these outcomes.

Section 112.19

Money Laundering, Corruption, Arms, and Transnational Criminal Finance

Criminal revenue is only useful if it can be spent, invested, and protected. Historically, organized crime in the Americas has used cash-intensive businesses, trade invoicing, real estate, informal money transfer systems, and corruption to place proceeds into the economy, and more recently financial technology firms and fuel distribution companies. This section describes the institutional landscape and not technical methods.

Brazil's Operation Hidden Carbon, launched in August 2025, is a case in which investigators alleged that the PCC used fuel companies and financial firms in a large laundering scheme. A 2024 U.S. Treasury designation of an alleged PCC operative for laundering about $240 million, and the 2021 sanctions on the PCC under a different executive order, show the American financial tools in use. Ecuador's August 2026 sanctions on a network using fishing businesses illustrate the same approach. Such actions rely on financial intelligence units, banking regulators, prosecutors, and international cooperation, and they can freeze assets and cut access to the dollar system. Their impact is difficult to measure, and a seizure figure does not measure the total size of an illicit market, a point Quantitative Study 3 develops.

Arms are another linkage. Haiti's gangs and many Latin American groups obtain firearms illegally from abroad, including from the United States, while other weapons come from stolen military stocks and local diversion. Corruption, including payments to police and military officers, prison staff, customs officials, and politicians, ties these systems to the state. The relation between criminal revenue and government capacity runs in both directions: weak, underfunded institutions are easier to corrupt, while corruption reduces institutional capacity further. Prison systems, in particular, show how a state function can be delegated to criminal actors.

The designation of groups as terrorist organizations adds a layer of compliance risk for banks and companies, since material-support rules can apply to dealings with designated groups. Law firms advising on the Brazilian designations warned businesses about exposure in sectors such as fuel and agribusiness. This development also raises questions about proportionality and enforcement: ordinary people forced to pay extortion could in principle face legal complications, and debates continue about how to protect victims while enforcing sanctions.

Financial enforcement is often less visible than the seizure of drugs or weapons, but it is central to the durability of organized crime. Criminal groups need to pay associates, reward compliance, compensate corrupt intermediaries and preserve assets against rivalry and enforcement. Prosecutors and regulators investigate allegations that illicit proceeds move through ordinary corporate and financial structures, while businesses and banks face obligations to identify suspicious activity. Describing those institutions is different from explaining how to evade them. Students should understand the historical relationship between criminal revenue and legitimate markets without treating an allegation against one enterprise as proof that its entire sector was compromised.

A documentary distinction is particularly important here. A sanctions designation may prevent access to regulated financial services; an indictment may allege money laundering; an asset seizure may temporarily restrain funds; and a conviction is a finding of guilt. None of these figures measures total criminal income. The publicized value in one investigation may reflect the amount prosecutors allege passed through an identified group of companies, rather than the net profit retained by a criminal organization. Anti-money-laundering policy therefore needs outcome measures such as convictions, asset disposition and independently assessed disruption, not only headline amounts.

Transnational cooperation also has limits. Banks, customs agencies and prosecutors operate under different legal standards, and exchange of evidence depends on treaties, data protections and institutional trust. When agencies act together they may identify links between environmental offenses, arms trafficking, corruption and illegal payments that a local police investigation could not see. But cooperation without judicial safeguards can expose innocent parties to sweeping restrictions. The historian's task is to evaluate law-enforcement capacity alongside due process, not to assume that maximal state power produces maximal security.

Section 112.20

Historical Synthesis: The Changing Organization of Crime through 2026

Comparison across the cases shows both shared conditions and real differences. Tren de Aragua arose in a prison where the state had delegated authority, expanded along migration routes, and by 2026 was a dispersed network with uncertain central leadership. The PCC developed over three decades from a prison brotherhood with internal rules into a national and transnational organization with legitimate-economy investments. The Comando Vermelho, older but looser, remained rooted in territorial control of neighborhoods and expanded into border regions. Ecuador's factions grew rapidly in a few years around ports, prisons, and international demand, and fragmented as leaders were arrested or killed. Haitian gangs built power through control of territory in a collapsed state, and mining-related crime links armed groups, licit businesses, and international buyers across the Guiana Shield.

Shared conditions include overcrowded and poorly governed prisons, corruption, proximity to cocaine supply and to export infrastructure, populations with little access to protection from the state, and demand in Europe and North America. Differences include organizational form, degree of centralization, relationship with the state, and the type of violence. Governments responded differently as well: Venezuela through prison raids and cooperation with the United States, Brazil through police operations and financial investigations, Ecuador through emergency powers and military deployment, and the United States through designations, sanctions, and prosecutions. These approaches cannot be graded by a single measure, since homicide, territorial control, and financial disruption are separate outcomes.

The evidence about fragmentation, consolidation, and expansion is mixed. Leadership losses at Tren de Aragua and the Ecuadorian Choneros were followed by fragmentation and infighting, not by the disappearance of the markets. The PCC appears to have become more institutionalized and the subject of long-term financial investigation. Incarceration has repeatedly served as a recruiting and organizing mechanism, so that mass imprisonment can strengthen organizations that it was meant to weaken. Historical Debate 2 asks students to weigh these claims.

The central lesson is that dismantling leaders, capturing territory, or increasing incarceration does not necessarily resolve the economic and institutional conditions that sustain organized crime. Those conditions include prison governance, port and financial vulnerabilities, corruption, informal economies, migration without legal protection, and persistent international demand. They also include states' own use of force, as the Rio operation and Ecuadorian emergency show. Chapter 113 turns from the criminal organizations to the political systems that must respond to them, examining how insecurity shaped electoral politics, including the appeal of security-centered leaders and the debates over how far democracies should go in using militarized methods.

The most productive comparison concerns the organizational mechanisms that survive leadership turnover. TdA's changing degree of central coordination, the PCC's durable prison rules, CV's local territorial affiliates and Ecuador's rapidly shifting factions show that the word 'organization' can describe very different arrangements. A leader's removal may matter greatly where revenue and dispute resolution depend on that person; it may matter less where local actors already control their own income and alliances. Prison reform likewise has no uniform effect unless it changes the conditions that allowed incarcerated leaders to exercise power outside their cells.

Policy evaluation must also keep time horizons separate. A successful raid may reduce immediate armed presence, a financial investigation may constrain a particular revenue channel, and a durable prevention strategy may require trustworthy policing, access to justice, legal employment and protection of vulnerable communities. The measurable effects of these interventions may arise on different schedules. By November 16, 2026, many of the central cases were still moving through courts or institutional reform processes. Treating provisional outcomes as settled would distort the historical record this chapter aims to preserve.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that contemporary Latin American organized crime cannot be explained through a single cartel model. After the era of the Medellín, Cali, and Mexican organizations, criminal power developed in prison-born brotherhoods, territorial gangs, loose networks, and coalitions whose forms depended on institutional conditions and opportunities.

Tren de Aragua formed around 2007 in the Tocorón prison in Aragua State, in a Venezuelan penal system where inmate leaders governed under arrangements with officials. Its founders, including Niño Guerrero, Johan Petrica, and Larry Changa, built a prison base that taxed inmates and extended into neighborhoods and, later, mining areas.

The organization expanded after 2015 along the routes of Venezuelan emigration. In Colombia, Peru, Chile, and other countries, cells engaged in extortion, smuggling, trafficking, kidnapping, and drug sales, often alongside local groups. Most migrants were victims or ordinary families, and nationality or residence is not evidence of membership.

Evidence about structure is contested. U.S. indictments describe coordination and leadership, while the National Intelligence Council, the EU Agency for Asylum, and analysts emphasize decentralization and local autonomy, especially after Venezuela's September 2023 operation at Tocorón. Membership estimates, near 4,000 in some assessments, are not censuses.

In the United States, the February 2025 FTO designation and the March 2025 invocation of the Alien Enemies Act placed Tren de Aragua at the center of immigration and security politics. Prosecutors reported charging nearly 350 people by July 2026. These are charges, not convictions, and the designation is a legal decision by the executive branch. The 2025 intelligence assessment found no evidence of a centrally directed Venezuelan government campaign, while recording a dissent about individual officials.

In June 2026, the U.S. and Venezuelan governments announced the death of Niño Guerrero in Bolívar State. Analysts expected succession disputes and fragmentation and warned that the organization's revenue, including mining, did not depend on one man. As of 16 November 2026, leadership and cohesion remained uncertain.

Brazil's PCC and Comando Vermelho illustrate how prison organizations can develop differently. The PCC, founded in 1993 after the Carandiru massacre, built a disciplined, increasingly business-oriented organization with transnational links; the CV, from Rio's prisons in the late 1970s, remained a looser, territorial federation. Militias are a distinct form of armed control.

Brazil's security crisis includes police lethality. The 28 October 2025 Rio operation killed at least 117 and, by Human Rights Watch's count, 122 people; HRW's October 2026 investigation found body-camera failures and violations of court orders. In 2026 the United States designated the PCC and CV as terrorist organizations, which Brazil's government rejected, while Brazilian prosecutors pursued financial cases such as Operation Hidden Carbon.

Ecuador shows how quickly a security environment can deteriorate. Its ports, dollarized economy, and prisons, together with European demand and ties to Mexican organizations, enabled Los Choneros, Los Lobos, and others to compete violently. Reported homicides rose from about 7,063 in 2024 to 9,216 in 2025, and the Noboa government's internal armed conflict, curfews, and cooperation with the United States produced announced declines in 2026 that independent analyses found more modest or uncertain, alongside allegations of abuses.

Caribbean and Amazonian cases broaden the picture. Haitian gangs, organized in Viv Ansanm, control territory in a collapsed state, while illegal gold mining links armed groups, businesses, and buyers across the Guiana Shield, as INTERPOL's Operation Guyana Shield showed. Money laundering and arms flows tie these systems to formal finance and to the United States.

The chapter's central lesson is that removing leaders, taking territory, or increasing incarceration does not necessarily resolve the economic and institutional conditions that sustain organized crime, which include prison governance, port and financial vulnerabilities, corruption, informal economies, migration without protection, and demand. Chapter 113 turns to the political consequences.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that organized crime in twenty-first-century Latin America cannot be understood through the single image of a cartel kingpin. Prison-born brotherhoods, territorial gangs, shifting coalitions, and loose cells arose where states had delegated or lost control of prisons, ports, and borders, and where international demand rewarded those who could move goods across them.

It also shows how much depends on evidence and on the words used to describe it. Designations, indictments, convictions, and statistics have different meanings, and governments sometimes use them for political purposes. Reading them carefully is part of understanding the period, as is remembering that most migrants, prisoners, merchants, and residents in these stories were not criminals but people trying to live under pressure.

The cases connect regions. Venezuelan migration linked Aragua to Santiago, cocaine linked the Andes to Santos and Guayaquil and then to Europe, gold linked Bolívar to Guyana and international buyers, and finance and firearms linked all of them to the United States. The chapter also shows that the response to crime, whether prison raids, emergency decrees, police operations, or terrorism designations, has itself shaped violence and politics.

Chapter 113 turns from criminal organizations to the political systems that must answer them, examining how insecurity helped redefine electoral politics, from security-centered leaders to debates over democratic limits. Chapter 114 will take up the resource geopolitics that overlap with illegal mining, and Chapter 115 returns to Venezuela in 2026. Students should carry forward the lesson that capturing leaders or territory is not the same as resolving the conditions that produce organized crime.
