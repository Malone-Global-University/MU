Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Market That Silver Could Not Feed

A mining city of the seventeenth century could produce silver, but it could not feed, clothe, house, or transport itself. Every ounce of silver minted at Potosí stood on top of fields, herds, looms, mule trains, credit contracts, and market stalls that were often hundreds of kilometers away.

Consider the supply of a large mining town in the Andes during the 1600s. Historians reconstructing the commerce of Potosí describe a flow of goods that reached the city from many directions: wheat and maize from valley estates and Indigenous villages, wine and brandy from coastal and southern valleys, coca leaf from the eastern slopes, cattle and mules from the grasslands to the south, woolen cloth from Andean workshops, and European and Asian manufactures that arrived through Atlantic and Pacific ports. Merchants, muleteers, estate managers, market women, and officials all depended on that traffic. The silver that gave the city its fame was only the most visible part of an economy that reached across several climatic zones and through Atlantic and Pacific routes connected to wider Eurasian commerce.

This chapter studies that wider economy. Chapter 42 examined mining as an institution and technology; Chapter 43 asks how land, agriculture, labor, transportation, credit, ports, and cities were organized to supply miners, feed urban populations, and send commodities overseas. The central question is how the Spanish and Portuguese empires transformed American land and labor into agricultural wealth, commercial networks, and colonial cities, and how Indigenous, African, European, and mixed-descent people shaped those systems rather than merely serving them.

The chapter begins with land because every other part of the colonial economy rested on it. Indigenous communities held and worked land under diverse systems before 1519 and 1532, and colonial law did not simply erase those arrangements. Instead, grants, purchases, inheritance, litigation, forced resettlement, and epidemic mortality produced new patterns of ownership in which large estates and Indigenous communities coexisted, competed, and depended on one another. The first division of the chapter explains that landscape of estates, villages, smallholdings, and herds.

The second division turns to export agriculture. Sugar, cacao, tobacco, indigo, and cochineal linked American producers to European and global consumers. Sugar in particular required heavy capital investment, industrial processing, and a coerced labor force, and its expansion in the Caribbean and northeastern Brazil helped bring the Atlantic slave trade to the center of the colonial economy. This chapter explains the economic role of enslaved labor in plantation production but reserves the full social, legal, and cultural history of slavery and Afro-Latin communities for Chapter 44.

The third and fourth divisions follow goods through markets, workshops, merchant houses, customs offices, convoys, galleons, and smuggling networks. They show that imperial regulation was real but incompletely enforced, that merchants and credit often mattered more than formal law in determining who prospered, and that colonial Latin America participated in Pacific as well as Atlantic commerce. The fifth division examines the cities where these systems converged, including the vendors, artisans, apprentices, servants, and household managers whose labor is often missing from accounts of merchant elites.

Evidence for these topics is uneven. Notarial records, wills, land titles, litigation files, account books, customs registers, and city council minutes survive in large numbers, especially for Mexico and Peru. Indigenous-language documents, such as Nahuatl testaments and pictorial tribute records, preserve community perspectives. Travel accounts and technical treatises, such as Antonil's study of Brazilian sugar, describe production in detail but reflect the viewpoint of observers who were not themselves workers. Throughout, the chapter asks who produced each source, for whom, and whose experience it leaves unrecorded.

The chapter concentrates on roughly 1500 to 1800. Late-colonial reforms, especially the commercial regulation of 1778, appear as transitions that set up the independence era covered in Volume III, not as the chapter's main subject.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how colonial landownership was created through conquest, grants, purchase, litigation, and negotiation rather than the transfer of empty territory.
  2. Distinguish the hacienda, estancia, fazenda, and plantation by crop, labor, scale, and market orientation.
  3. Analyze the roles of Indigenous communities, smallholders, tenants, and wage workers in agricultural production, and distinguish encomienda, repartimiento, mita, tenancy, and debt-based labor.
  4. Trace the Atlantic sugar revolution from the Atlantic islands and Caribbean to northeastern Brazil, and explain the engenho as an agricultural-industrial complex.
  5. Assess why plantation profitability depended on coerced labor while recognizing that comprehensive treatment of slavery belongs to Chapter 44.
  6. Compare cacao, tobacco, indigo, and cochineal as export commodities and identify who produced each.
  7. Explain how mining districts stimulated regional food, textile, transport, and credit economies.
  8. Evaluate the roles of merchants, consulados, guilds, taxation, and regulation, including the limits of imperial control.
  9. Describe the Spanish fleet system, the Manila-Acapulco galleon trade, and the Portuguese Atlantic system, including Dutch competition in Brazil.
  10. Interpret contraband as a structural feature of colonial commerce rather than only as lawbreaking.
  11. Analyze colonial cities as political, commercial, and social spaces, including women's and workers' economic lives.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of colonial economies as extractive dependencies or as systems with substantial internal commercial autonomy.

Historical Context

Earlier chapters described the agricultural foundations of Indigenous America: maize, beans, and squash in Mesoamerica; potatoes, quinoa, maize, and camelid herding in the Andes; manioc and managed forests in the lowlands. Chapters 33 through 39 traced conquest and early colonial formation, and Chapters 40 and 41 explained how the Spanish and Portuguese crowns governed. Chapter 42 showed how silver mining at Potosí, Zacatecas, and Guanajuato drew labor, mercury, and capital into a few districts. This chapter assumes that background and asks what economic life surrounded and sustained those institutions.

Two imperial systems framed the story. The Spanish Crown claimed American territories as part of Castile, supervised them through viceroys, audiencias, and the Council of the Indies, and tried to channel trade through licensed ports and fleets. The Portuguese Crown granted captaincies to donatários along the Brazilian coast, later asserted stronger royal government, and tied Brazilian sugar to Lisbon, Atlantic islands, and African ports. From 1580 to 1640 the two crowns shared a ruler, which blurred some commercial boundaries without merging the empires. Their institutions, settlement patterns, and commercial policies differed in ways the chapter keeps visible.

Demography shaped everything. Epidemic disease reduced Indigenous populations drastically in the sixteenth century, as Chapter 29 explained, and recovery in many regions began only in the seventeenth century. Population loss left land vacant or lightly used in some areas, strengthened the bargaining position of surviving workers in others, and drove colonial employers toward coercive mechanisms or toward enslaved African labor. These pressures varied enormously: densely populated central Mexico and the Andean highlands differed sharply from the thinly settled Río de la Plata or the Caribbean islands, where Indigenous populations had collapsed very early.

Finally, the chapter treats Indigenous people as economic actors. Indigenous farmers fed cities, Indigenous porters and muleteers moved goods, Indigenous artisans and market women sold in colonial markets, and Indigenous communities went to court to defend land. Using sources such as Nahuatl testaments, tribute records, and litigation, historians have revised older accounts that treated the colonial economy as a European enterprise operating on a passive Indigenous base.

Section 43.1

The Transformation of Land: Indigenous Territories, Colonial Grants, and Property Rights

Before the Spanish and Portuguese arrived, Indigenous land systems differed widely. In central Mexico, altepetl held territory that included lands assigned to rulers, nobles, temples, and commoner households, and the chinampa system of Lake Xochimilco and Chalco produced intensive harvests on reclaimed lakeshore plots. In the Andes, ayllus held and rotated fields across altitude zones, using terraces and irrigation, and the Inca state had assigned some lands to the state and to religious institutions. In Brazil, Tupí-speaking communities practiced shifting cultivation of manioc along the coast, with land use organized by village and kin rather than by transferable title. Treating these as a single Indigenous land system hides the differences colonial newcomers confronted.

The Spanish Crown asserted that conquered land belonged ultimately to the king, who could grant it. Royal and municipal authorities issued mercedes de tierras, grants of land to settlers, and city councils allocated house lots and farm plots to residents. The Portuguese system rested on sesmarias, a medieval Portuguese institution for distributing uncultivated land on condition that grantees cultivate it. In Brazil, captaincy holders and later royal officials issued sesmarias, though conditions requiring cultivation were often weakly enforced. In both empires the grant created a legal claim, but the boundaries, the labor to work the land, and the rights of people already living there had still to be settled in practice.

A grant's language could suggest a clean transfer, but effective possession often depended on surveying and recognition by neighbors. Boundaries might be described by streams, trees, hills, roads, or named settlements rather than by measurements that later litigants would consider precise. Indigenous witnesses, translators, local governors, and notaries therefore mattered greatly. A town defending pasture or a water source did not necessarily reject Spanish law; its representatives might instead invoke royal protection and present testimony that a neighboring rancher had exceeded his original concession. Maps painted by Indigenous specialists could serve as arguments about jurisdiction and memory as well as topographical guides. A judgment in favor of a community did not always guarantee enforcement, particularly when the opposing landholder possessed officials' favor or sufficient capital to prolong proceedings.

Population collapse changed the meaning of land. Indigenous communities that lost large shares of their members in the sixteenth century could no longer cultivate all their fields. Spanish officials responded in part through congregación, the concentration of dispersed Indigenous populations into planned towns, which opened vacated lands to Spanish petitioners. Colonists also acquired land through purchase from Indigenous nobles or communities, sometimes legitimately and sometimes under pressure. Later, the Crown tried to regularize defective titles through composición de tierras, in which holders paid a fee to confirm possession, a policy driven by royal fiscal needs from the late sixteenth century.

Land also acquired a monetary value that differed from its older political, ritual, and subsistence meanings. Owners mortgaged parcels, endowed chaplaincies, and used estates as collateral for loans from merchants and religious foundations. These arrangements drew distant lenders into village disputes and helped transform some local conflicts into long lawsuits over titles. Yet possession was not a simple binary between Spaniards and Indigenous inhabitants: members of Indigenous elites held individual estates, Indigenous communities sometimes leased parcels to outsiders, and families of mixed descent acquired land by purchase or inheritance. The history of colonial property is consequently a history of changing institutions and unequal bargaining power, rather than a single moment of dispossession.

Nevertheless, conquest did not erase Indigenous property rights. Spanish law recognized Indigenous communities as corporate landholders and, in many regions, protected a village core and commons. Communities defended these rights through petitions, painted and written titles, and lawsuits, sometimes producing documents (the títulos primordiales of central Mexico) that asserted ancient possession. The result was a landscape of contested overlapping claims. Colonial landownership was created through conquest, negotiation, legal process, and conflict, not by the settlement of empty territory.

Section 43.2

Haciendas, Estancias, Fazendas, and the Formation of Rural Estates

Large rural estates emerged across Spanish and Portuguese America, but the vocabulary can mislead. The Spanish American hacienda was a landed estate that combined agricultural or pastoral production with resident or seasonal labor and sold primarily to regional markets. The estancia originally meant a grant for livestock or a ranch, and in the Río de la Plata it remained the usual term for a cattle estate. In Brazil the fazenda was a general term for a farm or estate, applied to cattle, cotton, and other colonial enterprises (and, increasingly, coffee in later centuries), while sugar properties were engenhos. A plantation, in this book, means a large unit producing a commercial crop for distant markets with a concentrated coerced labor force, as in sugar. Haciendas and plantations overlapped in some regions but were not the same institution.

Estates were assembled in several ways. Original grants provided cores that owners extended by purchase, by marriage, by inheritance, and by acquiring neighboring lands through debt. Estate-building was often a family strategy over generations. Owners included conquerors' descendants, officials, merchants who reinvested commercial profits in land, and religious institutions. The Jesuit order, before its expulsion from the Spanish empire in 1767 and from Portuguese territories in 1759, operated some of the largest and best-managed estates, using them to finance colleges and missions, and other orders and convents also held property and made loans secured by land.

Estate organization depended as much on labor and water as on acreage. A landowner in a dry Mexican valley might possess extensive grazing land but cultivate only the limited area supported by irrigation; a wealthy owner near a city could earn more from smaller, intensively managed fields. In the Andean highlands, the distance between an estate and an Indigenous village influenced access to seasonal labor and the terms under which workers or tenants cultivated plots. Livestock operations required herders, branding, seasonal movement, and negotiated access to pasture. Properties closer to reliable roads could send grain or animals to urban markets more cheaply than larger but isolated estates. For this reason, historians assess estate power by debts, workers, water rights, markets, and political connections as well as by recorded boundaries.

Regional comparison shows how varied estates were. In central Mexico, haciendas in the Puebla and Bajío districts grew wheat, maize, and livestock for cities and mining towns, and some produced pulque for urban consumers. In the Río de la Plata, estancias raised cattle and horses, with hides and tallow as major products, on open grasslands where fences were rare. In Andean valleys, estates grew wheat, maize, grapes, and sugar on irrigated land, often alongside Indigenous communities that retained part of the valley. In Brazil, fazendas in the interior sertão raised cattle that supplied meat, hides, and draft animals to coastal sugar districts.

The records of estate owners also reveal economic vulnerability. Bad weather, livestock disease, delayed transport, fluctuating prices, and interest obligations could consume the returns of a successful harvest. Land was prestigious but not automatically profitable. Religious institutions and merchant creditors sometimes became influential because they could extend longer-term loans that kept cultivation going, while smaller cultivators might be forced to borrow at unfavorable terms. When plantations expanded on Brazil's coast, their processing equipment required a different scale of capital from many inland cattle fazendas. The shared language of landed elites should not obscure those differences in production and financial risk.

Historians once portrayed the hacienda as a self-sufficient feudal unit. Later research emphasized its commercial links: estates sold to markets, borrowed from merchants and church funds, and responded to prices. Estate size also varied from modest properties to vast domains, and many owners were absentee, relying on managers. The hacienda had no single size, crop, labor system, or purpose, and the chapter avoids treating it as an institution with a fixed form.

Section 43.3

Indigenous Communities, Small Farmers, Rural Labor, and Agricultural Markets

Estates did not occupy the countryside alone. Indigenous villages, mestizo and Spanish smallholders, tenants, sharecroppers, and independent farmers produced much of the food that fed colonial populations. In central Mexico and the Andes, Indigenous communities continued to farm communal and household plots, growing crops for their own consumption and for sale in local markets, and often dominated production of maize, beans, and vegetables. Their output reached towns through weekly markets and through Indigenous merchants and carriers.

Rural labor took many forms, and the terms are not interchangeable. The encomienda granted a Spaniard the right to receive tribute, and initially labor, from an Indigenous community; labor service was restricted after the New Laws of 1542 and the grant became mainly a claim on tribute. The repartimiento (called coatequitl in Nahua central Mexico) was a state-supervised rotation of Indigenous workers to Spanish employers for wages, widely used in the sixteenth century. The mita in the Andes adapted an Inca labor-rotation system into a colonial draft, used chiefly in mining but also for other work. Tenancy let a family farm part of an estate in exchange for rent or labor, and wage workers were hired seasonally for planting and harvest. Debt-based labor grew as employers advanced cash or goods to workers and expected repayment in work.

The bargaining position of rural workers depended partly on the availability of land outside estates. In regions where an Indigenous community retained functioning commons, a worker could combine household cultivation with temporary employment; where access to land was severely restricted, estate food plots and advances were more consequential. The timing of agricultural work produced recurring negotiations: planting, irrigation repair, herding, harvest, and transport each demanded people at different moments. Villages organized collective labor for their own purposes even while authorities demanded workers for colonial enterprises. Such overlapping obligations could cause conflict when officials treated community members as a ready labor pool without considering the seasonal requirements of their own fields.

Historians dispute how coercive debt-based labor was. Earlier scholars described "debt peonage" as a system that bound workers to estates for life; later research found that indebted workers could often move, that advances were also a means of attracting scarce labor, and that conditions varied by region and period. Indebtedness could be a tool of control in some places and a negotiated advantage for workers in others.

Market participation also gave rural people room for initiative. Households sold vegetables, poultry, woven goods, charcoal, and grain, sometimes carrying products to town themselves and sometimes using brokers. Women could control portions of this commerce even when legal documents were written in the names of husbands or male relatives. Indigenous councils negotiated access to market squares and defended customary water use because both were essential to community reproduction. In a poor harvest year, rural households might prioritize food over cash sales, contributing to shortages for merchants and cities. Attention to these decisions helps explain why an estate owner's plan could fail without local cooperation: economic hierarchy was powerful, but producers retained their own strategies for survival.

Communities negotiated constantly with estates over water, pasture, forests, roads, and market access. Disputes over irrigation rights, grazing, and boundaries filled colonial courts, and Indigenous cabildos and caciques acted as legal representatives. The coexistence of estates and communities was therefore neither harmonious nor purely exploitative. Agricultural production depended on many producers and several labor arrangements, not on large plantations alone.

Section 43.4

Food Production, Livestock, Regional Specialization, and the Supply of Colonial Society

The foods of colonial society combined Indigenous and imported crops. Maize remained the staple of Mesoamerica and much of the Andes; potatoes and quinoa sustained the highlands; cassava (manioc) fed the Caribbean, Brazil, and enslaved plantation workforces; beans and chili peppers were widespread; cacao served as both drink and, in Mesoamerica, a medium of exchange. Spaniards introduced wheat, which they considered essential for bread and for the Eucharist, together with grapes, olives, sugarcane, citrus, and other crops. John Super's study of food and colonization shows that Spanish settlers worked to reproduce familiar foods but adapted to local supplies.

Livestock transformed the American landscape. Cattle, horses, sheep, pigs, goats, and mules arrived with Europeans and multiplied quickly on open lands. Sheep supplied wool for cloth, cattle supplied meat, hides, and tallow, and mules, bred in large numbers, became the principal means of overland transport. Livestock also competed with Indigenous cultivation, since herds trampled unfenced fields, a cause of frequent complaints and litigation.

The Columbian exchange did more than add plants and animals to existing diets. New livestock supplied manure and traction in some regions, but expanding herds could damage planted terraces or disrupt Indigenous grazing and water arrangements. Wheat farming often required new mills and irrigation channels, giving mill owners leverage over cultivators who needed grain ground before sale. Conversely, maize remained attractive to urban consumers because it suited local ecologies and food preparation, while cassava's durability and adaptability made it valuable where transport and storage were difficult. Colonial consumption therefore represented selective adaptation rather than a wholesale replacement of Indigenous foods by European ones.

Climate, altitude, soil, and transportation costs produced regional specialization. Cold highland valleys grew potatoes and barley, temperate valleys wheat and maize, hot lowlands sugar and cacao, and grasslands cattle. Because overland transport was slow and expensive, bulky goods such as grain were usually supplied from nearby districts, while high-value goods such as cacao, cloth, or wine traveled farther.

Food markets also reflected political priorities. Municipal councils supervised weights, measures, milling, slaughterhouses, and prices when shortages threatened urban order. Church establishments, hospitals, monasteries, military units, and fleets were institutional purchasers with predictable needs. Transport losses and tolls raised the cost of provisions as they moved from farm to city, and a merchant who owned mules or maintained credit at several stopping points enjoyed an advantage over small producers carrying one load. In the Andes, ecological complementarity could connect highland potatoes and animal products to crops from lower valleys; in New Spain, cultivated zones around the Basin of Mexico and the Bajío supported very different urban and mining markets. The resulting networks made colonial economies more integrated but also exposed consumers to drought, war, and disruption far beyond their own localities.

Students should be cautious with any neat map of colonial agricultural specialization. Farming zones changed with epidemic recovery, the discovery of mineral deposits, new roads, and shifting demand. What appeared to one Spanish official as vacant pasture might have supported Indigenous seasonal use or forest gathering. The environmental history of livestock, forests, water, and agricultural frontiers is inseparable from the economic history of exports and cities.

The demand of population centers organized these supplies. Mexico City and Lima required enormous quantities of maize, wheat, meat, and firewood; Potosí and mining camps imported food and fodder on a vast scale; ports needed provisions for ships and garrisons. The result was a set of interdependent regional economies linking rural production, colonial cities, and mineral-producing districts, the subject developed in Section 43.9.

Creator: Indigenous Nahua painters and scribes of Tepetlaoztoc · Date: mid-sixteenth century, often dated c. 1554–1556 · Document: pictorial and glossed manuscript petition · Repository: British Museum, Am2006,Drg.13964.

Context: The community documented tribute and labor demands imposed by its encomenderos, placing Indigenous pictorial accounting into a colonial petitionary setting. The extant manuscript is a concrete object, not a hypothetical example of an Indigenous land title.

Identified editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): In an advocacy account of obligations, the manuscript enumerates forms of produce, textiles, and labor that the community claimed to have supplied. Its economic evidence is strongest for demands and contributions, rather than for a surveyed boundary or a final judgment on land title.

Documentary limitation: The manuscript and museum identity are independently catalogued. This study does not claim to transcribe or collate individual manuscript folios; students should consult a facsimile and published specialist interpretation for image-by-image analysis.

Analysis: The record illustrates how Indigenous producers converted local accounting traditions into testimony before colonial authorities. Because it is a petition, its claims must be compared with decisions, opposing arguments, and later collection records. It cannot establish a precise estate boundary or a full balance sheet merely from its inventory of complaints.

Historical Vocabulary
Hacienda and Plantation

A hacienda was a Spanish American landed estate, typically producing grain, livestock, or other goods for regional markets and relying on resident, tenant, seasonal, or draft labor. A plantation was a large agricultural-industrial unit producing a single commercial crop, chiefly sugar, for distant markets with a concentrated coerced workforce.

The terms are often confused because both refer to large estates. Hacienda labor was frequently mixed (wage, tenancy, debt), and haciendas sold mainly within the colonial economy; plantations, especially in the Caribbean and Brazil, relied heavily on enslaved labor and exported. Using the words carefully prevents students from imagining one uniform rural system.

Section 43.5

The Atlantic Sugar Revolution and the Expansion of Plantation Agriculture

Sugarcane originated in Asia, spread through the Mediterranean, and was cultivated by Portuguese and Spanish producers on Atlantic islands such as Madeira and São Tomé before reaching the Americas. Columbus carried cane to the Caribbean, and sugar enterprises were established on Hispaniola in the early sixteenth century, followed by Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and Cuba. Early Caribbean production was modest by later standards, and some islands declined as Indigenous labor collapsed and as attention shifted to the mainland silver economies.

The decisive expansion occurred in Portuguese Brazil. After captaincies were granted in the 1530s, sugar enterprises appeared in Pernambuco and São Vicente, and the 1530s and 1540s saw significant expansion in the northeast. Pernambuco and Bahia became the principal centers. Their coastal plains had suitable soils, rainfall, and access to ports, and Portuguese merchants had existing commercial ties with Atlantic islands and European markets, including Antwerp and later Amsterdam. By the later sixteenth century Brazil was the world's largest sugar exporter, a position it held until Caribbean competition rose in the seventeenth century.

Atlantic precedents mattered because Madeira and São Tomé had already combined cane growing, mills, commercial credit, and coerced labor before large American plantations emerged. Transfer to the Americas did not reproduce these arrangements unchanged. In Hispaniola, initial sugar projects competed with mining and encountered labor crises, disease, and uneven royal patronage. In Brazil the plantation frontier expanded through grants and private investment but depended on ports, watercourses, accessible coastal land, and commercial relationships with Portugal and northern Europe. Pernambuco's prominence grew out of this conjunction, not from soil or climate alone. Sugar producers also needed barrels, pottery, metal tools, draft animals, and skilled mill workers, stimulating enterprises beyond the cane fields.

Sugar required heavy capital. Land, a mill, boiling equipment, draft animals or water power, a large labor force, and ships all had to be financed before profit appeared, and cane had to be processed quickly after cutting. The harvest imposed intense seasonal work, and investors depended on European demand and credit. Stuart Schwartz's study of Bahia shows how sugar shaped class relations, family strategies, and institutions in Brazilian society.

A plantation's apparent prosperity could mask fragility. A drought might reduce cane yields while an epidemic damaged the workforce; delayed shipping could leave sugar exposed to humidity and spoilage. Credit tied planting decisions to merchants who advanced supplies months before an export cargo could be sold. European warfare and competition among Atlantic islands, Brazil, and Caribbean territories changed both costs and expected prices. By the seventeenth century, the emergence of new Caribbean sugar centers increased competitive pressure, while plantation owners in Brazil still relied on local food suppliers, livestock estates, coastal shippers, and Atlantic merchants. The sugar revolution was therefore a system of linked environments and institutions, not merely the opening of more fields for cane.

The plantation was thus an agricultural, industrial, commercial, and coercive institution at once. It cultivated fields, processed a crop in a mill, sold to merchants in distant markets, and relied on coercion to command labor. Plantations were not identical: some were large and well capitalized, others small; some relied on tenant cane growers, others on their own fields; labor arrangements and production capacities varied. Early Caribbean sugar was smaller in scale than the Brazilian complexes of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Section 43.6

Engenhos, Mills, Processing Technology, and the Organization of Sugar Production

Sugar became an export commodity only after cane had been crushed, boiled, purified, and crystallized. The Brazilian engenho was the complex in which these operations took place. It included cane fields, a mill house with rollers to press the cane, boiling houses with kettles and furnaces, a purging house in which sugar was drained in clay molds, storehouses, a chapel, the residence of the owner or manager, and quarters for workers. The word engenho referred both to the mill and to the whole estate.

An engenho required power, fuel, and skill. Some mills were driven by water (engenhos reais) and others by oxen or horses (trapiches). Furnaces consumed large quantities of firewood, and the demand for fuel contributed to deforestation of the Atlantic coastal forest. Skilled workers, including enslaved and free specialists who boiled and clarified the juice, were critical, because errors could ruin a batch. Maintenance of equipment, shipping schedules, and credit also shaped the enterprise.

The technical sequence placed unusually strict demands on coordination. Cut cane lost sweetness if it sat too long before crushing, so fields and transport animals had to be organized around the mill's operating hours. Pressed juice entered a succession of heated vessels where workers monitored boiling and clarification; subsequent draining, drying, and packing required different spaces and skills. An interruption in fuel supply or the breakdown of mill rollers could ruin a crop even when cultivation had succeeded. Such constraints explain why skilled mill personnel held knowledge that could not simply be replaced by additional laborers. Accounts by Antonil and other observers are valuable precisely because they describe machinery, work rhythms, and the commercial calculations of an operating complex.

The economic relationships around the mill were layered. The senhor de engenho owned the mill, but many owned only part of the cane they ground. Independent cane growers (lavradores de cana), some renting land from the mill owner and others owning their own, supplied cane in exchange for a share of the sugar. Merchants advanced goods and credit, exporters shipped the product, and lenders financed investment. Debt often tied mill owners to merchants and could lead to foreclosure.

Investment decisions were social as well as technical. A mill owner might process cane grown by other farmers, receiving a negotiated portion of output rather than owning every cultivated field. This allowed cane growers to enter export production without purchasing expensive crushing equipment, while giving the mill a regional monopoly over processing. However, disagreements could arise over waiting times, the quality of cane, deductions for the mill, and access to wagons. Plantation records also reveal a distinction between nominal estate value and the money available to pay suppliers. Buildings and machinery were expensive assets, but credit had to be renewed every season. The physical organization of an engenho thus linked labor discipline, capital concentration, and the dependence of neighboring cultivators on a few processing centers.

The landscape bore these economic choices. Mills consumed wood and water, roads crossed fields and forests, and demand for cattle feed tied coastal sugar districts to inland grazing regions. These connections help explain why the history of one sugar estate cannot be separated from its ecological and regional setting.

Because the investments were large, sugar encouraged concentration of economic power in the hands of mill owners and the merchants who financed them, and it created a hierarchical society in which status depended on ownership of an engenho. Research on Portuguese America identifies the engenho as an agricultural-industrial complex central to the organization of northeastern Brazilian society.

Historical Vocabulary
Engenho

Engenho (Portuguese, literally "engine" or "device") denoted both the sugar mill and the entire estate that grew and processed cane. The senhor de engenho was a social and political figure whose status rested on the mill.

The term shows that Brazilian sugar production was industrial as well as agricultural, and that mills, rather than fields alone, organized property, credit, labor, and power. Students should avoid translating it simply as "plantation," which obscures the processing complex.

Section 43.7

Plantation Labor, Coercion, Profitability, and the Atlantic Economy

Plantation production depended on large and disciplined labor forces. Planting, cutting, hauling, milling, and boiling required the work of many people, and the harvest was concentrated in a few months. Profitability depended on controlling workers, land, processing equipment, transportation, and commercial credit together, so a planter who lost any one of these could fail. This section explains the economic role of plantation labor; Chapter 44 develops the history of African enslavement, family, law, freedom, and resistance.

Several labor arrangements existed and should be distinguished. In the early Caribbean and in sixteenth-century Brazil, Indigenous people were coerced into plantation work through enslavement, raiding, and negotiated or forced village labor, despite royal restrictions that were weakly enforced. Enslaved Africans, imported through the Atlantic slave trade, became the main workforce on major sugar regions, first in northeastern Brazil and later in the Caribbean. Wage laborers, tenants, and free skilled workers also appeared, particularly in cane-growing and milling specialties.

Plantation owners faced the economic problem of assembling an intensive workforce whose tasks ranged from heavy field labor to highly skilled operations at boilers and mills. Different tasks carried different dangers, authority, and opportunities for negotiation. Workers who understood the processing machinery could exercise practical influence even within an overwhelmingly coercive system; enslaved workers could also preserve knowledge, community ties, and cultural practices that owners could not control completely. Nevertheless, the violent power to compel work and to punish resistance fundamentally shaped the institution. Treating enslaved people simply as a cost item in plantation accounts obscures the human relationships and force on which the accounts depended.

Maintaining a large workforce posed costs and consequences. Planters had to provide food, which could mean growing provision crops such as manioc or buying supplies; housing; and minimal medical care. Mortality on sugar estates was high, and planters often relied on new imports of enslaved people rather than on natural increase, which meant that the plantation economy reproduced itself by continuing the slave trade. The reproduction of labor, in short, was a cost that planters met through continued purchase of people.

Provisioning was another central calculation. Some estates set aside plots for food; others purchased manioc flour, meat, or vegetables from local growers and merchants. When owners favored export cane over food cultivation, they increased vulnerability to shortages and price spikes. Slavery also implicated property law, credit, and risk: merchants offered loans secured against enslaved persons or future sugar shipments, making human beings a form of collateral in commercial contracts. This chapter identifies these mechanisms because they affected sugar's pricing and profitability, while Chapter 44 will examine in full the lives, families, legal struggles, resistance, and cultures of the people subjected to the system.

Nor should students suppose that commercial profitability guaranteed stable political control. Plantation districts witnessed flight, sabotage, litigation, negotiations over work routines, and collective resistance. Maintaining an estate meant confronting those acts and the costs of surveillance, punishment, and replacement labor. The historical accounts of investors reveal what they sought to profit from; they cannot by themselves establish the experience of those compelled to make the profit possible.

Plantation wealth thus rested on violence and insecurity for workers, a contradiction at the heart of the Atlantic economy. Profits flowed to planters, merchants, shippers, and Crown tax collectors, while workers faced coercion and early death. The demand of the major plantation regions was the foundation for the expansion of the Atlantic slave trade, and the next chapter takes up that trade, the societies it created, and the people who endured it.

Section 43.8

Cacao, Tobacco, Indigo, Cochineal, and the Diversification of Colonial Exports

Sugar was not the only commodity linking colonial producers to global markets. Cacao, cultivated in Mesoamerica long before the conquest, remained a Mesoamerican drink and currency, and in the colonial period production expanded in Soconusco, Guayaquil, and Venezuela. Venezuelan cacao grew rapidly in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, supplying Mexico and Spain, and the Caracas (Guipuzcoana) Company, chartered in 1728, shaped its trade. Tobacco, of Caribbean and American origin, was grown in Cuba, Brazil, and Venezuela; in Cuba the Crown established a monopoly purchase system in the early eighteenth century, and in Bahia tobacco was also used in the Atlantic slave trade.

Indigo, a blue dye plant, was produced in Central America, especially in the regions around Santiago de Guatemala and San Salvador, and sold to European textile manufacturers. Cochineal, a red dye derived from insects raised on nopal cactus, was produced largely by Indigenous communities in southern Mexico, particularly Oaxaca, and became, after silver, one of New Spain's most valuable exports. Cochineal production was typically organized through Indigenous households and communities, with Spanish officials and merchants advancing credit and goods, a system that historians have studied for its coercive features.

Each commodity followed a distinct path from cultivator to overseas buyer. Cochineal dye required the cultivation and harvesting of insects that lived on prickly-pear cacti; Indigenous producers in Oaxaca retained specialized expertise that merchants could not readily transplant to another region. Indigo depended on particular plants and processing facilities, including fermentation vats, which made capital, water, and labor arrangements important in Central America. Cacao was grown in tropical areas such as coastal Ecuador and Venezuela as well as parts of Mesoamerica, while its markets included American cities as well as Europe. These contrasts challenge the image of colonial export production as always a single-crop plantation using identical labor systems.

These commodities entered European and global markets through merchants and ports, and prices depended on distant demand, shipping, and imperial regulation. Cultivation differed by region. Some crops (sugar, much indigo) were produced on estates, while cochineal and much cacao were cultivated or processed by Indigenous communities and smallholders. Merchants and officials influenced production through credit, purchase monopolies, and trade rules.

Tobacco showed the power of policy as well as the importance of markets. Small farmers and larger producers could participate, but the Crown's later monopoly arrangements in some territories sought to regulate purchase, processing, and sale. Smuggling and informal distribution arose partly because producers and consumers could earn more outside official channels. For cacao and cochineal, merchant advances could bind producers to a particular buyer, yet intermediaries also carried specialized goods long distances and made wider markets possible. Taxes, crop disease, and changes in European taste altered the fortunes of producing districts. An apparent boom in exports might therefore enrich merchants while leaving cultivators vulnerable to debt or declining prices.

The importance of these commodities was cultural as well as commercial. Tobacco smoking and snuff, chocolate consumption, and the use of American dyes circulated through multiple societies. Demand in Europe did not mean that Americans stopped using the products themselves. The movement of a commodity across the Atlantic transformed the scale of its market but did not erase the people and knowledge systems that produced it.

Export specialization had consequences. Regions that concentrated on commodities could reduce local food production, shift land use, and tie livelihoods to volatile prices, while also generating regional wealth. Colonial commodity histories demonstrate that American products entered increasingly global markets while retaining distinct local production and labor systems.

Section 43.9

Mining Hinterlands, Food Markets, Mule Trains, and Regional Trade

Mining cities, whose technical and institutional history appears in Chapter 42, created demand on a scale unmatched in the colonial world. Potosí, Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and smaller camps concentrated thousands of workers, merchants, and officials who needed food, fodder, tools, candles, leather, clothing, timber, and fuel, and the animals that powered mills and carried ore. Most of these goods were produced elsewhere, so mining stimulated production and exchange across large areas.

Supply districts developed around each center. Potosí drew maize, wheat, and potatoes from highland and valley producers, wine and brandy from southern Peruvian and Chilean valleys, coca from the eastern slopes, livestock and mules from the southern grasslands, and cloth from Andean workshops. Zacatecas and Guanajuato drew on the northern and Bajío estates for grain, cattle, hides, and tallow, and on central Mexican regions for other goods. Cattle ranches in the north, wheat haciendas in the Bajío, and Indigenous farming villages all benefited, and their owners included producers who never entered a mine.

Supplying Potosí required moving goods through a challenging Andean landscape. Producers in nearby valleys grew maize and wheat, eastern districts sent coca leaf, and southern grasslands supplied pack animals. Routes crossed high-altitude passes and dry plateaus where mules consumed fodder and travelers depended on inns, market towns, and seasonal knowledge. Merchants organized caravans and offered credit to suppliers before delivery, so an exchange recorded in Potosí was often the end of a long chain of transactions. The availability of animals, not merely the existence of roads, could determine whether crops reached the market before spoiling. Transport itself became an occupation supporting breeders, blacksmiths, saddlers, muleteers, and innkeepers.

Transport structured the trade. Mule trains were the principal carriers over mountains and difficult terrain, often organized by professional muleteers (arrieros, many of them Indigenous or mixed-descent), while wagons used better roads in flatter regions such as northern Mexico. Roads were frequently poor, mountain crossings dangerous, and rivers uncrossable in rainy seasons, so freight rates were high. Intermediate trading towns developed at junctions where goods were exchanged, stored, and financed.

New Spanish mining districts developed comparably extensive but not identical networks. Zacatecas and later Guanajuato were connected to farming estates in the Bajío and to routes leading toward Mexico City and Veracruz. A mining boom could encourage the cultivation of grain, the expansion of ranching, and investment in roads; at the same time it could raise food prices and intensify competition for water and land. Small merchants and Indigenous producers entered these markets, even though the largest creditors and wholesalers often captured disproportionate profits. Silver circulated internationally, but a large share of the surrounding exchange involved ordinary goods bought and sold locally in American markets.

An economic historian must therefore distinguish a mine's output from the value of the network it stimulated. A fall in silver production might reduce demand for mules or wheat long before it closed a particular workshop. Conversely, market towns could develop lasting commercial functions after local mining declined. Looking outward from Potosí and Zacatecas exposes the colonial economy's interdependence instead of treating the mine as a self-contained engine of wealth.

Commercial routes linked agricultural districts with mining regions, ports, and administrative capitals, and the circulation of silver reinforced them by providing coin and credit. The central analytical point is that silver production stimulated whole regional economies, enriching estate owners, muleteers, merchants, and market vendors, and creating labor demand in agriculture and transport. At the same time, regions tied to mining were exposed to its fluctuations, a vulnerability that appeared when output declined.

Section 43.10

Obrajes, Artisan Workshops, Textile Production, and Urban Manufacturing

Colonial cities and towns were also manufacturing centers. Textile production was the largest sector, carried out in large workshops called obrajes, in smaller shops, and in households. Other trades included leatherworking, saddlery, carpentry, metalworking, silverworking, ceramics, soap making, and candle making. Much manufacturing served the local and regional market, supplying clothing, tools, and household goods to populations who could not rely on imports.

The obraje produced woolen cloth using Spanish-style looms and processes. It brought spinning, weaving, fulling, and dyeing under one roof, and it often held its workers in conditions of coercion, including workers confined by debt or by criminal sentence. Specialist scholarship documents the expansion of Puebla's textile obrajes during the later sixteenth century and the coercive labor practices found in some establishments. Other centers included Querétaro in Mexico and Quito, whose highland obrajes supplied cloth across the Andes. Not all obrajes were alike: some were large and heavily coercive, others small and relying largely on wage work, and household weaving by Indigenous women remained widespread.

The term obraje itself could refer to establishments with different levels of equipment and labor discipline. Some workshops processed wool or cotton for large regional markets; others depended heavily on nearby customers or contracts with estates and institutions. Owners needed looms, wool, dye materials, storage space, and access to merchants willing to finance production before cloth was sold. Domestic spinning and weaving could compete with or supply the larger establishments, and Indigenous communities continued to produce textiles outside the formal workshop system. Guild regulation in some cities shaped apprenticeship and the right to sell certain goods, but it did not eliminate workshop labor undertaken beyond guild oversight.

Craft production was organized by guilds (gremios) in many Spanish American cities. Guilds regulated quality, prices, apprenticeship, and entry to trades, and their ordinances sometimes restricted participation by Indigenous and African-descended people, although enforcement varied and many such artisans worked outside guild rules. Guilds differed from obrajes in scale: a master with a few apprentices and journeymen ran a guild shop, while an obraje might hold dozens of workers under an owner-entrepreneur.

Quito provides a well-documented example of the difficulty of treating official law as a reliable description of actual labor conditions. In 1743 the president of the Audiencia, José de Araujo, conducted an inspection of urban obrajes, examined in a study by Luis Javier Ramos Gómez. The inspection addressed recruitment and confinement as well as conditions within workshops. Because a visit was an inquiry into alleged abuses, its record is evidence about the questions authorities asked and what witnesses reported; it should not be taken automatically as a neutral statistical survey of all workshops. The case nevertheless demonstrates that forced recruitment, restrictive conditions, and regulation of labor were contested matters within colonial government itself.

Textiles moved through merchant networks connecting urban consumers, miners, rural households, and regional fairs. Competition from imported fabric changed over time, but transport costs and demand for inexpensive local cloth supported production across colonial America. The social history of manufacture therefore includes artisans seeking status, workshop owners seeking returns on capital, and workers negotiating or resisting constraints on their movement.

The workforce was not uniform. Indigenous workers, enslaved and free people of African descent, mestizos, and Spaniards worked in different capacities and under different conditions. Some artisans accumulated property and status; some obraje workers were trapped by debt or confinement. Urban manufacturing cannot be reduced to a single workforce or labor system, and Section 43.19 returns to apprenticeship and household production.

Section 43.11

Merchant Families, Commercial Credit, Partnerships, and Distribution Networks

Merchants did far more than exchange goods. Wholesale merchants imported European and Asian goods, bought agricultural and manufactured products for resale, advanced goods and cash to producers, financed mining and estate operations, and sometimes held land and offices. Retail merchants sold in shops and stalls. Both depended on credit, since coin was often scarce and payment was delayed by distance and the arrival of fleets.

Credit instruments included written promissory notes, advances against future harvests or production, and partnership contracts in which investors supplied capital and traveling partners handled sales. Merchants extended credit to estate owners, mine operators, artisans, and muleteers, and used notaries to record contracts. Insurance, though limited, existed for some overseas shipments, and church institutions also provided long-term loans secured by property.

A merchant's advantage often lay in coordinating transactions over time rather than possessing a single commodity. A wholesaler might provide an estate owner with imported cloth, tools, or cash at planting season, accept agricultural produce or silver as repayment, and use another account to finance transport. Notarial credit contracts, partnerships, bonds, and powers of attorney linked people who might never meet. The account book therefore recorded not only exchange but also obligations: who owed whom, when goods were expected, and which assets stood as security. A delayed convoy or failed harvest could set off a chain of defaults among parties whose fortunes had become intertwined.

Commercial relationships linked estate owners, mine operators, artisans, transporters, and consumers into networks of mutual dependence. A merchant might supply goods to a mine, receive silver in payment, buy wheat from a hacienda, and extend credit to a muleteer. Credit could create dependence, binding producers to powerful trading houses that set prices and demanded repayment in kind or in labor.

Mexico City's merchant elite illustrates the political weight of those networks. As Louisa Schell Hoberman shows for 1590–1660, leading merchants managed relationships with miners, crown officials, and transatlantic traders while turning access to credit into influence. Family partnerships were valuable because kinship could reduce the costs of policing distant business, but commercial trust also crossed family, regional, and ethnic boundaries. Merchants trained agents, used correspondence to track prices, and sometimes acquired estates or mining interests when debts went unpaid. Smaller retailers and traveling traders operated within the same economy at a very different scale and often remained exposed to powerful wholesalers' terms.

The historian's evidence is necessarily selective: a notarial deed preserves a formally recorded obligation, whereas many market transactions left no comparable paper. Reconstructing the world of merchants thus requires reading account books against court disputes, municipal records, and evidence of the ordinary producers whose goods passed through elite hands.

Louisa Schell Hoberman's research on seventeenth-century Mexico City demonstrates that merchant networks were central to both economic organization and elite political influence. The city's merchant elite, closely connected through kinship, partnership, and the Consulado, controlled much of the import trade and lent to the Crown, and its members invested in land, mines, and office. Lima's merchants, similarly, dominated Pacific and Andean commerce, and other colonial cities had smaller merchant communities. Merchants therefore combined economic and political power, a feature that shaped colonial society.

Section 43.12

Colonial Taxation, Commercial Regulation, Guilds, and Economic Privilege

Imperial governments tried to regulate and tax colonial commerce. The alcabala, a sales tax inherited from Castile, was extended to New Spain in 1574 at a low rate and later raised, and it fell on many transactions. Customs duties, including the almojarifazgo, taxed goods entering and leaving ports, and the avería funded convoy protection. The Crown also created royal monopolies (estancos) on certain goods, including mercury (Chapter 42), and in some regions tobacco, salt, and playing cards.

Merchants organized themselves in consulados, corporate bodies of merchants with legal jurisdiction over commercial disputes and, often, responsibility for collecting taxes and maintaining roads or ports. The Consulado of Seville served as the model; Mexico City received one in 1592 and Lima in 1613, and others followed in the late eighteenth century. Consulados are distinct from artisan guilds: the former represented wholesale merchants and judged commercial cases, while the latter regulated craft production.

The alcabala could be charged on sales as goods moved through regional markets, creating incentives to shift where transactions were declared or to seek exemptions. Customs duties operated at ports, while royal monopolies on selected goods or services generated revenue through privileged production and distribution. The Crown also relied on farming or contracting out some fiscal collection, which introduced private intermediaries into what appeared to be a state function. For producers and retailers, taxes were part of a broader cost structure that included transport, storage, credit, tolls, and the need to negotiate with local officials.

Regulation created privileges and exemptions. Licenses, monopolies, tax exemptions, and favored status gave advantages to some merchants and officials, and discretion in enforcement created openings for bribery, patronage, and negotiation. Conflicts arose between royal officials who wanted revenue, municipal governments that regulated local markets, merchants who sought privilege, and small producers who resisted taxes and restrictions.

A merchant consulado was neither simply a town council nor the same thing as an artisan guild. It represented major commercial interests and exercised jurisdiction over mercantile disputes, while a craft guild attempted to regulate training, quality, and occupational entry within a particular trade. Their privileges could lower uncertainty for members but exclude outsiders and smaller competitors. The Mexico City consulado's authority and the interests of Lima's merchants differed from those of port officials who depended on customs revenue. Tensions emerged whenever imperial decrees disrupted established regional routes or threatened a city's commercial position.

Imperial economic policy therefore involved bargaining as well as command. A regulation issued in Madrid might be delayed, adapted, or contested in an American court; merchants could seek exemptions while officials weighed both revenue and political order. Such negotiations do not mean the empire lacked coercive power. They mean that enforcement relied on people who had their own interests. The distinction becomes especially clear in the overseas trade system, where a legal monopoly existed alongside flourishing commerce that the Crown had not authorized.

Colonial economic regulation was therefore not synonymous with complete state control. Rules existed and were sometimes enforced, but evasion, negotiation, exemptions, and local interests shaped how they operated. The regulation of trade between Spain and the colonies, treated in the next division, shows the same pattern at imperial scale.

Historical Vocabulary
Consulado

A consulado was a corporate body of merchants, established by royal license, with authority to judge commercial disputes and to represent the interests of the merchant community. The consulados of Seville, Mexico City, and Lima were the most influential in the Spanish empire; others were founded in the late eighteenth century at ports such as Buenos Aires, Havana, and Cartagena.

The consulado differed from an artisan guild, which regulated craft production, and from a municipal cabildo, which governed a city. Its significance is that it gave merchants institutional power, including legal jurisdiction, financial influence, and a voice in policy, and often made the merchant elite a political actor.

Section 43.13

Veracruz, Cartagena, Portobelo, Havana, and the Spanish Imperial Fleet System

The Spanish Crown tried to channel American trade through a regulated system known as the Carrera de Indias. The Casa de Contratación, established in Seville in 1503, licensed ships and goods and recorded cargoes. From the 1560s, the Crown formalized a convoy system in which merchant ships sailed together under naval escort to protect against pirates and privateers. Seville held the monopoly of the trade until the Casa de Contratación was moved to Cádiz in 1717, though Cádiz had already become the effective port of departure.

Two main routes served the Americas. The fleet (flota) sailed to New Spain, calling at Veracruz, where goods were exchanged for silver and other products, which moved overland from Mexico City. The galleons (galeones) sailed to Tierra Firme, calling at Cartagena de Indias and Portobelo on the Isthmus of Panama. At Portobelo, merchants from Peru met arriving European goods at trade fairs, and silver and goods moved across the isthmus between the Pacific and the Caribbean, with Callao as the Pacific port for Lima. Havana served as the gathering point where the fleets assembled for the return voyage.

The fleet system joined different routes rather than creating one uninterrupted line of ships. Vessels bound for New Spain could reach Veracruz through Caribbean waters, while the Tierra Firme connection supported commerce through Cartagena, Portobelo, and the Isthmus of Panama toward Peru. Havana offered a strategically important point where homeward vessels gathered and prepared to cross the Atlantic. Ports became cities of pilots, shipwrights, sailors, stevedores, customs employees, warehouse owners, food vendors, and merchants, many of whom earned their living regardless of whether they personally owned transatlantic cargo. A convoy's arrival could abruptly increase demand for lodging, provisions, pack animals, and credit.

Ports functioned as commercial complexes. Warehouses, customs houses, shipping services, repair yards, merchant agents, and urban markets all developed around them, and ports had to be provisioned with food and labor. Veracruz, Cartagena, and Havana were fortified because of their strategic and commercial importance, and the fortifications themselves required labor and expenditure that circulated through local economies.

Spain's attempt to protect shipping from privateers and enemy fleets made sense during European wars, but convoy protection came at an economic cost. Convoys did not always depart when merchants wished; ship availability, storms, war, and royal priorities introduced delays. Merchants tied up capital while awaiting an authorized sailing and might compensate through higher prices. Markets in the Caribbean and American interiors, however, could not simply stop consuming goods whenever a fleet was postponed. Unlicensed traders and informal exchanges met some of that demand. Colonial officials sometimes prosecuted these dealings, while others tolerated them or participated in their profits.

The official trade corridor changed over time. The Portobelo fairs declined, wartime disruptions repeatedly forced exceptions, and eighteenth-century reforms widened authorized routes. Even in periods of strict regulation, intercolonial trade and coastal movement supplied people who lived far from the large ports. The shipping system is best understood as the regulated skeleton of a more complicated commercial world, not as an exhaustive map of every transaction undertaken by imperial subjects.

The system had limits. Piracy and privateering by English, French, and Dutch ships threatened shipping, and wars disrupted sailings. The convoy system was formalized in the 1560s, but its voyages were not maintained on a perfectly annual schedule throughout the colonial period; schedules slipped, fairs were delayed, and the Portobelo fairs declined in the eighteenth century. High prices created by limited sailings encouraged contraband, discussed in Section 43.16, and the Crown eventually adjusted the system through reforms.

Section 43.14

The Manila Galleons, Acapulco, and Latin America's Connections to Asia

In 1565 the Spanish expedition led by Miguel López de Legazpi, with the navigator and friar Andrés de Urdaneta, established a return route across the Pacific from the Philippines to Acapulco. The Manila–Acapulco galleon trade that followed linked New Spain to Asia and endured until the early nineteenth century. Manila, founded as a Spanish settlement in 1571, served as the Asian terminus, and Chinese merchants who traded at Manila supplied much of the cargo.

The trade exchanged American silver for Asian goods. Silver minted in Mexico and Peru traveled to Manila, where it paid for Chinese silk, porcelain, lacquerware, spices, and other merchandise, and the galleons returned to Acapulco with these goods. The Crown restricted the trade to protect Seville merchants, and a regulation commonly dated to 1593 limited the number of ships and the value of cargo permitted each year, though evasion was frequent.

The Pacific crossing worked because American and Asian traders occupied complementary positions. Spanish ships carried silver from New Spain toward Manila, where Chinese, Filipino, and other Asian merchants supplied textiles, ceramics, lacquerware, and luxury goods. The return voyage, aided by knowledge of Pacific winds and currents, could take months and required substantial food and water. Acapulco's fair attracted carriers, brokers, retailers, and representatives of Mexico City merchant houses. Merchandise that began in Asian workshops might later appear in a New Spanish household, church, or shop, bearing witness to long-distance commercial connections that older Atlantic-centered histories overlooked.

Goods reached consumers through a chain of commerce. At Acapulco, an annual fair brought merchants from Mexico City and elsewhere, who purchased Asian goods and sent them overland to Mexico City, where some were redistributed to other parts of Spanish America and some forwarded to Veracruz and across the Atlantic. Merchant financing, transcontinental transport by mule train, and imperial regulation all shaped the trade. Merchants in Lima also sought Asian goods and silver flowed in the other direction, although the Crown tried to restrict direct Peru–New Spain trade.

The Crown struggled to restrain a profitable trade. A royal decree of January 1593 sought to limit the number and size of galleons and impose value ceilings, including nominal allowances of 250,000 pesos of merchandise departing Manila and 500,000 pesos in return silver; later rules and revisions altered the limits. Formal records capture the permitted cargo, not necessarily every good actually carried, since merchants had incentives to understate quantities or arrange transactions outside authorized channels. Cargo rights themselves became valuable. A merchant with capital but no allotted space sought access through intermediaries, while a person holding an entitlement could gain from allowing another to use it.

The impact extended beyond luxury consumption. Chinese and Filipino artisans, American muleteers, port workers, merchants, translators, and buyers each participated in the route's economy. Religious art and domestic furnishings in New Spain sometimes adapted Asian materials or forms, complicating a simple division between European colonial culture and American society. The Manila galleons show how the circulation of silver linked multiple regional economies, but the wealth generated was distributed unevenly among those who made the route function.

Asian commodities influenced American material culture. Silk and cotton textiles, porcelain, folding screens, and other goods entered homes and churches, and Asian styles influenced local artistic production, from ceramics in Puebla to furniture and painting. The Metropolitan Museum of Art documents the Manila–Acapulco trade and its influence on material culture in New Spain. The galleon trade shows that colonial Latin America participated in a global economy extending beyond Spain, Portugal, and the Atlantic.

Section 43.15

Portuguese Brazil, Atlantic Commerce, and Dutch Competition

Brazil's economy was tied to Portugal, the Atlantic islands, African ports, and European markets. Sugar was shipped from Salvador, Olinda, and Recife to Lisbon, where it was refined or reshipped to northern Europe, and merchants financed planters, shipped goods, and supplied enslaved workers from West and Central African ports. Shipping, refining, merchant credit, and plantation production connected northeastern Brazil with the wider Atlantic economy, and Dutch merchants had long participated in the carrying and refining of Brazilian sugar.

During the Iberian Union (1580–1640), Dutch hostility toward Spain exposed Brazil to attack. The Dutch West India Company occupied Salvador da Bahia in 1624 and lost it in 1625 to a Spanish-Portuguese fleet. In 1630 it invaded Pernambuco, seized Olinda and Recife, and gradually extended control over much of the northeastern sugar coast. Recife became the center of Dutch Brazil.

Brazilian coastal commerce was not limited to a simple bilateral movement between a plantation and Lisbon. Merchants connected sugar districts to ports supplying tools, textiles, food, enslaved laborers, and credit. Ships linked Portuguese America to Atlantic islands, West and Central Africa, and northern European markets, though legal arrangements shifted with war and political change. Cattle from the sertão supplied food and hides, while coastal towns handled storage and export. Commercial relationships were vulnerable to exchange-rate changes, shipping losses, and competition among metropolitan and foreign merchants; plantation owners therefore often borrowed against anticipated harvests and remained dependent on creditors even when they held substantial land.

Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen governed Dutch Brazil from 1637 to 1644. He promoted rebuilding of damaged mills, extended credit to planters, supported scientific and artistic work in Recife, and tolerated Catholic and Jewish worship, though his policies were controversial within the Company. Conflicts arose over planter debts, as the Company and its merchants demanded repayment, and over Dutch control of the slave trade, which the Company extended to West Africa.

The Dutch occupation of northeastern Brazil introduced a distinctive set of tensions. Under Johan Maurits, Dutch administrators sought revenues from a sugar economy that required cooperation with Portuguese-speaking planters. Credit arrangements, religious divisions, urban development around Recife, and military demands complicated that effort. The occupation could not be sustained solely by holding coastal fortresses: sugar plantations lay inland, landowners had alternative loyalties, and warfare damaged the infrastructure from which revenue was expected. Resistance and changing Dutch imperial priorities helped bring about the final Portuguese recovery in 1654.

Dutch participation in Atlantic sugar trading and investment did not vanish with the military withdrawal. Competition in Caribbean markets intensified, and Brazilian producers faced a new commercial environment rather than a return to a stable earlier order. The episode reveals that the profits of agricultural export depended on naval power, credit, international prices, and political alliances as much as on the productivity of a given plantation.

Planters resisted. An uprising beginning in 1645 combined Luso-Brazilian planters, Afro-Brazilian and Indigenous soldiers, and royal support, and Dutch forces were expelled in 1654. Warfare had damaged mills and disrupted production, and Dutch competition moved to the Caribbean, where Dutch knowledge of sugar technology contributed to the rise of English and French sugar colonies, which competed with Brazil from the mid-seventeenth century. The political history of Dutch rule is a relatively brief episode compared with the longer economic history of Brazilian sugar, but it was a major moment in the competition for Atlantic commercial wealth.

Section 43.16

Contraband, Smuggling, Privateering, and Trade beyond Imperial Monopolies

Commercial exchange frequently moved outside official channels. Restrictive port systems, high prices for legal goods, shortages, wars, and long distances all encouraged unofficial trade, and settlers wanted cheaper and more abundant goods than the fleet system supplied. Foreign merchants wanted access to American markets and silver. Local officials, ship captains, settlers, and consumers all participated, often with the connivance of authorities who benefited from bribes or lower prices.

Contraband flourished in particular regions. In the Caribbean, English, Dutch, and French merchants traded with Spanish settlements, especially after Jamaica, Curaçao, and other bases were established. In northern South America, the coast of Venezuela and New Granada had active illicit trade. In the Río de la Plata, Buenos Aires, refounded in 1580, was officially isolated but traded unofficially with Brazil, with Portuguese merchants, and with European smugglers, and the Portuguese settlement of Colonia do Sacramento, founded opposite Buenos Aires in 1680, became a center of contraband. Frontier regions were similarly difficult to police.

Smuggling was not always a dramatic nighttime landing; it could involve ordinary merchants exploiting the gap between formal rules and local needs. In frontier ports, ships might exchange provisions, cloth, hides, or enslaved laborers with buyers lacking regular access to authorized commerce. Local officials confronted a dilemma: strict enforcement could deprive settlements of needed supplies and customs revenue, while tolerance undermined the royal monopoly. Some sought bribes or shared profits, others reported violations, and still others asked the Crown to change trade rules that were impossible to enforce consistently.

It is useful to distinguish related terms. Smuggling meant trade that evaded duties or prohibitions. Privateering was attack on enemy shipping under a government commission, and piracy was attack without such authority, though the line could blur. Legal commerce passed through licensed ports and paid duties. Contraband sometimes accompanied violence but often consisted of ordinary exchange in which both parties benefited.

Geography encouraged these exchanges. The Río de la Plata was distant from the main Spanish Atlantic fleet routes but linked by river to inland settlements; Buenos Aires and Colonia do Sacramento became important points of legal and clandestine commerce under changing regimes. In the Caribbean, islands and straits facilitated exchange with merchants from several European empires, whose possessions were close together even when their governments were at war. In northern South America, long coastlines and dispersed communities limited surveillance. Merchants often combined licit and illicit ventures, making it difficult for historians to separate an entire commercial career into two tidy categories.

Privateering should be distinguished from piracy and from contraband. A privateer operated under a government's authorization to seize enemy shipping in wartime; a pirate lacked such legal sanction; a smuggler traded in defiance of commercial rules. These distinctions mattered in law even if their victims experienced similar insecurity. The broader lesson is that the economic map produced by actual ships and transactions was larger than the map permitted by imperial decrees.

The growth of Buenos Aires illustrates the distinction between informal commercial growth and formal liberalization. Unofficial trade built its commercial community in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, and later reforms, including the creation of the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in 1776 and the commercial regulation of 1778, authorized much of what had been informal. The persistence of contraband reveals the tension between imperial mercantilist ambitions, which sought to preserve trade for the metropolis, and the economic interdependence that colonial communities developed across political boundaries.

Section 43.17

Colonial Cities, Urban Planning, Cabildos, and the Built Environment

Colonial cities were administrative, commercial, religious, and economic centers. Mexico City, Lima, Salvador da Bahia, Recife, Havana, Cartagena, and Puebla differed in origin and function but shared features: concentrations of officials, merchants, clergy, artisans, and servants, and links to agricultural hinterlands. Some were founded on or beside Indigenous centers (Mexico City on the ruins of Tenochtitlan; Cuzco on the Inca capital), while others were new foundations (Lima, 1535; Puebla, 1531; Cartagena, 1533; Salvador, 1549 as the Portuguese capital of Brazil).

Spanish planning drew on European ideals and on practical experience. The ordinances of 1573 on discovery, settlement, and pacification gave detailed guidance on siting towns, laying out a central plaza, and arranging streets in a grid, though many cities had already been laid out before 1573 and local topography often altered the plan. The plaza mayor typically held the cathedral or main church, the cabildo (city council) hall, and the residence of officials, and surrounding streets housed markets, shops, and the homes of elites. Portuguese towns more often followed irregular plans, with upper and lower towns, as in Salvador.

Urban planning codes helped shape colonial spaces but did not impose a uniform design everywhere. Royal ordinances favored central plazas and orderly streets, yet officials adapted town plans to terrain, defensive needs, river access, and preexisting Indigenous settlements. Mexico City rose over Tenochtitlan, retaining canals, causeways, neighborhood institutions, and many Indigenous residents while erecting major Spanish institutions around the central square. Lima, by contrast, developed as a planned administrative center near a crucial Pacific port. Salvador da Bahia's steep topography created divisions between upper administrative areas and lower port districts. Comparing these cities shows how economic geography modified legal prescriptions.

The built environment reflected commercial life. Warehouses and customs houses stood near ports, markets occupied squares and streets, water systems supplied fountains and aqueducts, and defenses protected port cities. Housing varied from the large houses of merchants and officials, with shops on the ground floor, to modest dwellings of artisans and the rented rooms of poor residents. Indigenous neighborhoods often lay on the margins of Spanish cities, sometimes retaining their own institutions.

A city required expensive daily maintenance. Aqueducts and fountains supplied water unevenly; markets and slaughterhouses needed supervision; roads, bridges, and docks demanded labor and taxation. Floods, earthquakes, epidemics, and fires could alter urban plans and impose heavy costs. Officials might prioritize ceremonial squares and public buildings while marginal neighborhoods received fewer services. Artisans repaired houses and ships, market vendors supplied food, and rural households delivered the produce that kept residents alive. Municipal records therefore preserve disputes about sanitation, water access, street space, and selling rights as well as grand projects sponsored by governors or bishops.

Urban residence also changed the reach of authority. Courts, parishes, guilds, merchant institutions, and religious houses offered channels for securing contracts or lodging petitions, but they also enforced hierarchies of status and privilege. Indigenous neighborhoods maintained separate councils or corporate institutions in some cities; elsewhere populations mixed across boundaries that were more permeable in practice than in official maps. The colonial city was both an instrument of empire and a place where ordinary people pursued their own economic claims.

The cabildo regulated urban life, distributing urban lots, setting prices of bread and meat, inspecting weights and measures, supervising markets, and maintaining streets and water. Cities depended on hinterlands for food, firewood, and labor, and urban demand shaped rural production. Urban spaces concentrated political authority and commercial activity while creating new forms of social interaction and inequality, as people of different origins and status lived and worked in close proximity.

Section 43.18

Marketplaces, Guilds, Neighborhoods, and the Social Organization of Urban Commerce

Urban commerce was organized through public markets, street trading, shops, workshops, warehouses, and taverns. Markets were often large and crowded, supplying staples such as maize, bread, meat, and vegetables, and in many cities Indigenous vendors dominated the supply of produce and prepared food. In Mexico City, the Tlatelolco market tradition survived in colonial markets, and in the Andes, markets such as those of Cuzco and Potosí brought together producers from many districts.

Municipal regulation and informal activity coexisted. Cabildos tried to confine sales to designated places, set prices of staples, license vendors, and inspect weights, but street vendors, itinerant sellers, and unlicensed traders were pervasive. Food distribution involved a chain from rural producers through carriers and wholesalers to retail vendors, and local transportation by porters, carts, and canoes mattered, especially in lake-based Mexico City.

A city market connected many levels of exchange. Large merchants arranged wholesale imports and supplied shops on credit; retailers purchased smaller quantities for resale; women and men carrying produce from rural villages sold food at stalls or in streets. Some trades worked from permanent premises, while others moved with customers and festival calendars. Perishable goods demanded frequent delivery, so access to market space, water, and safe transportation was crucial. Colonial authorities regulated weights, prices, and locations, especially in times of scarcity, but informal sellers often occupied areas beyond the approved stalls. A fine or confiscation could therefore threaten a household's livelihood without changing the demand that drew buyers to the street.

Indigenous towns and urban neighborhoods participated in municipal and regional commerce. Scholarship on urban Indigenous populations demonstrates the importance of artisans, traders, neighborhoods, and Indigenous municipal institutions within colonial Mexican cities. Indigenous cabildos governed neighborhoods and negotiated with Spanish officials over market access, taxes, and labor.

Guilds promoted apprenticeship and product standards but could also restrict entry to recognized masters, excluding workers by status, gender, or ancestry. Their influence differed by city and craft; not every artisan belonged to a formally organized corporation. Indigenous craftsmen continued working through community networks, and family workshops trained relatives in trades that mixed labor with domestic responsibility. Markets associated with churches, plazas, and neighborhoods created points of interaction across legal categories of population. Yet equality in a transaction did not mean equality before tax collectors, courts, or guild officers.

The sources themselves need careful reading. An ordinance describing where sellers ought to stand reveals the authorities' preferred market order, while a dispute about illegal vending shows that actual exchange escaped the official plan. A notarial apprenticeship contract may record a master's obligations but not whether the learner received fair treatment. Historians combine such records with probate inventories, court testimony, and Indigenous-language sources to reconstruct economic lives that the leading merchants' account books barely mention.

Urban commerce was socially layered. Merchants operating through long-distance credit networks differed sharply from small vendors whose livelihoods depended on daily sales, and both differed from artisans in guild shops. Conflicts arose over market locations, prices, taxes, privileges, and the right to sell particular goods, as guilds, merchants, and officials defended their positions against vendors and smaller producers, and the outcomes shaped who could earn a living in the city.

Section 43.19

Women, Families, Apprentices, Vendors, and Everyday Urban Economic Life

Economic activity below the level of the merchant elite was the daily substance of colonial urban life. Women worked as market vendors, shopkeepers, textile producers, food sellers, tavern keepers, household managers, and, in some cases, owners of property and participants in commercial credit. Spanish law gave women rights to hold property and, in many circumstances, to make wills and contracts, and married women retained rights to their dowries. These rights created economic opportunities, though they were limited by gender expectations, marital authority, and, for many women, poverty.

Households were units of production, consumption, inheritance, and cooperation. A craft household might include a master, his wife, children, apprentices, and servants, and the wife's work in the shop or market was often essential. Apprentices lived with masters and learned a trade over several years under contract; domestic workers, port laborers, servants, and children also contributed labor. Households received and passed on property through inheritance, and women often managed property as widows.

Women's legal opportunities varied with civil status, ancestry, marriage, and jurisdiction. Some widows managed businesses or estates because they controlled inherited property; married women could participate in trade through household arrangements even when formal contracts named husbands. In cities, women prepared food, sold textiles or produce, made clothing, extended small loans, and negotiated rents. Domestic service was also part of the urban economy, often involving young Indigenous and African-descended people whose bargaining power was limited. Family economies crossed boundaries between household and shop: children delivered goods, relatives spun or sewed, and profits from a stall could help finance rent, religious obligations, or an apprentice's training.

Opportunities and restrictions varied by status. Indigenous women often dominated market vending in central Mexico and the Andes and held property in their own right in some communities; African-descended women, both free and enslaved, worked as vendors, laundresses, cooks, and domestic servants, and some earned money to purchase freedom; Spanish women could inherit and manage property but faced stronger social expectations about seclusion and honor, especially for elites; and women of mixed ancestry occupied diverse niches. Chapter 45 examines gender and family more fully.

The case of Angelina Martina sharpens the picture. Margarita Vargas-Betancourt's study, using a sixteenth-century Indigenous testament, bill of sale, and the Florentine Codex, identifies her as a pochtecatl associated with the trade in textiles and featherwork in Mexico City's luxury artistic economy. The significance is not that she was representative of all women: her transactions illuminate a kind of specialized merchandising easily obscured by sources focused on male artisans or Spanish wholesale houses. The published study identifies the nature of the documents, but the chapter does not claim to reproduce their uncollated original wording or assign unsupported amounts or dates to her individual transactions.

Apprentices also deserve attention as economic actors. A contract might promise instruction, food, or lodging in exchange for years of service, while a master's supervision could restrict the learner's freedom. Training opened routes toward skilled work for some and reinforced dependency for others. By studying women's trade alongside apprenticeship, markets, and property, students can recognize the urban economy as a system of households and small enterprises, not merely a network of distant merchants and royal officials.

Historical research documents these roles through specific cases. A study of the Indigenous trader Angelina Martina, based on a sixteenth-century testament and bill of sale, offers an especially valuable documented case for teaching women's independent commercial agency. Such cases matter because they allow students to see individual economic choices, rather than relying on invented representative biographies.

Section 43.20

Markets, Inequality, Imperial Reform, and the Historical Legacy of Colonial Economic Development

Taken together, the chapter's divisions show that colonial Latin America developed complex regional and global economies rather than remaining a collection of isolated mines and plantations. Large estates, Indigenous communities, merchants, artisans, urban markets, transportation, and imperial institutions all mattered, and their relative weight varied by region and period. Silver stimulated agriculture and commerce; sugar built a plantation Atlantic; cities connected producers to consumers; and Pacific and Atlantic commerce tied Latin America to Asia and Europe.

These economies were also profoundly unequal. Land concentrated in large estates and in the hands of the Church; commercial credit and monopoly privilege favored merchant elites; and social privilege shaped access to office, guilds, and markets. Indigenous communities, smallholders, vendors, artisans, and enslaved workers contributed essential labor and production but received little of the wealth.

The period's economic changes created different forms of dependence at different scales. An Indigenous town might depend on nearby landowners for grazing access or employment; a small estate on a merchant's advance; a port on the arrival of regulated shipping; and the Crown on taxes and silver from American possessions. These chains were not identical to total economic stagnation. American merchants developed expertise and networks, rural households supplied growing cities, and regional production expanded as demand increased. Yet the ability to profit was distributed unequally, and coerced labor, monopoly privileges, and land concentration gave powerful actors advantages that others could rarely match.

In the late eighteenth century, imperial reform changed the regulatory framework. The Spanish Reglamento de comercio libre of 1778 expanded authorized commerce among designated imperial ports, gradually permitting trade between a larger number of Spanish and American ports and extending to New Spain and Venezuela in 1789, while retaining imperial rules, duties, and preferences. It represented greater commercial freedom within the Spanish empire, but not unrestricted international free trade. Portuguese reforms of the Pombaline era, discussed in Chapter 48, similarly reorganized commercial companies and administration.

The regulation of 1778 altered this structure without abolishing it. More Spanish and American ports obtained permission to exchange goods within the empire, potentially shortening some routes and encouraging additional shipping. The policy did not offer unrestricted access to merchants of every nation, nor did it necessarily remove taxes or local commercial privileges. Its effects differed by region, and some ports and merchant communities gained more than others. By the end of the century, commercial change interacted with population growth, changing consumer demand, and fiscal pressures associated with imperial warfare. The results offered both new opportunities and new grievances that later reformers and independence advocates would confront.

No single verdict about colonial economic performance captures these tensions. The extraction of wealth toward imperial centers was real, and so was the development of American markets with their own institutions and interests. A sound interpretation must ask whose activity generated wealth, who controlled its distribution, and what forms of legal and physical coercion secured returns. The chapter closes at the threshold of the history of enslavement because the extraordinary output of Atlantic plantations was inseparable from the people whose labor was compelled to sustain it.

The enduring interaction among land concentration, plantation production, regional specialization, urban development, and global commercial dependence shaped later history, as Volume III shows. The closing historical question is how colonial economies could generate extraordinary wealth and expanding commercial networks while leaving much of the population subject to insecurity, coercion, and exclusion. The question leads directly to Chapter 44, because plantation prosperity cannot be fully understood without examining the lives and experiences of the enslaved African populations whose labor supported much of it.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Extractive Dependencies or Economies with Internal Commercial Autonomy?

The question: Were colonial Latin American economies primarily extractive dependencies, organized to send silver and commodities to Europe, or did they develop substantial internal commercial autonomy?

Interpretation A: Extractive dependency. Some historians, influenced by dependency theory, emphasize that silver and plantation exports flowed to European and Asian markets, that imperial regulation channeled trade through the metropolis, and that colonial economies were structured by external demand and metropolitan interests. On this view, wealth was drained, and regions were left specialized and vulnerable.
Interpretation B: Substantial internal autonomy. Others emphasize that colonial economies supported large internal markets for food, textiles, livestock, and transport, that merchant elites and estates responded to regional prices, and that mining and plantations stimulated local economic growth. On this view, much colonial production circulated within the Americas and local actors shaped economic outcomes.

Evidence: Both views draw on customs registers, notarial records, estate accounts, price series, and studies of regional trade. Export registers support the first; studies of hacienda and market economies, such as Van Young's work on Mexico, and of merchants, such as Hoberman's, support the second.

Why it matters: The interpretation shapes how historians explain later inequality and underdevelopment: as a legacy of colonial extraction or of internal social structures. The two views overlap, since an economy can export heavily and also sustain a large internal market, and many scholars combine them.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation and identify the type of evidence that would best distinguish them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Indigenous Economic Systems and Colonial Transformations, c. 1500–1800

What Changed

  • Land passed in large part to Spanish and Portuguese estates and institutions
  • Tribute and labor were redirected to encomenderos, the Crown, and employers
  • New crops, livestock, and technologies changed production
  • Markets integrated Indigenous producers into colonial and global commerce

What Stayed the Same

  • Maize, beans, and potatoes remained staples, and household cultivation continued
  • Community landholding and local institutions survived in many regions
  • Indigenous markets, carriers, and artisans remained central

Who experienced change most directly: Communities near estates, mines, and cities, and those subject to labor drafts. Greater continuity: Remote communities and household production. Why the pattern matters: It shows that Indigenous economic life was transformed but not eliminated, and that Indigenous actors remained part of the colonial economy.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Settings: A Farming Household, a Sugar Estate, and a City Market

A rural farming household. In an Indigenous village of central Mexico or the Andes, a household typically farmed family plots of maize or potatoes, raised small animals, and perhaps wove cloth, with women, men, and children each having tasks. It owed tribute, labor service, and community obligations, and sold surplus at the weekly market. These descriptions reflect general patterns documented in colonial records, not a single recorded family.

A sugar-producing estate. On a Brazilian engenho during harvest, the work of cutting, hauling, milling, and boiling continued day and night, with workers assigned to dangerous tasks near mill rollers and furnaces. Enslaved workers lived in quarters near the mill and received food and housing provided by the estate or grown on their own plots where allowed. Skilled workers and overseers held higher status, and the owner's family lived in the main house. The experience of enslaved workers is examined in Chapter 44.

A city market vendor. A woman selling vegetables or cooked food in a market square rose early to buy or carry goods, paid fees or risked fines, negotiated with customers, and used credit from suppliers. Her income helped support her household. Such vendors, often Indigenous or of African or mixed descent, are poorly recorded in official sources, which means that their activity must be reconstructed from municipal regulations, court cases, and notarial records.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that colonial Latin America developed an interconnected economy based on land, agriculture, labor, commerce, transportation, and cities, and that Indigenous, African, European, and mixed-descent people all shaped it. Silver and sugar are the most famous products, but the system that sustained them was much broader.

Land was the foundation. Indigenous communities held land under varied systems, which the Spanish and Portuguese crowns overlaid with mercedes de tierras and sesmarias. Epidemic mortality, congregación, purchase, and composición transferred much land to colonists and institutions, yet Indigenous communities defended their holdings through petitions, titles, and courts. Colonial landownership was a product of conquest, negotiation, and conflict.

Estates took diverse forms. Haciendas, estancias, fazendas, and plantations differed in crops, labor, and markets, and they were assembled through grants, purchase, inheritance, debt, and the acquisition of Indigenous land. They coexisted with Indigenous communities, smallholders, tenants, and wage workers, and the labor arrangements (encomienda, repartimiento, mita, tenancy, debt) were distinct institutions, not synonyms. Agricultural production depended on many producers.

Food, livestock, and regional specialization supplied colonial society. Indigenous staples combined with imported wheat, cattle, sheep, and mules, and altitude, climate, and transport costs shaped regional economies. Cities and mining districts created demand that linked regions together.

Sugar was the first great plantation commodity. It expanded from Atlantic islands to the Caribbean and especially northeastern Brazil, where the engenho combined agriculture and industry, required heavy capital, and concentrated economic power. Plantation profitability depended on coerced labor and, in major regions, on the Atlantic slave trade, a subject Chapter 44 develops. Cacao, tobacco, indigo, and cochineal diversified exports and relied on different producers, including Indigenous communities.

Internal markets and institutions organized exchange. Mining hinterlands stimulated agriculture, transport, and trade; obrajes and workshops produced cloth and goods; merchants supplied credit and distribution and wielded political influence; and taxation, consulados, and guilds structured privilege. Regulation was real but incomplete, and evasion and negotiation were normal.

Overseas commerce connected the colonies to the world. The Spanish fleet system channeled trade through Seville and Cádiz, Veracruz, Cartagena, Portobelo, and Havana, while the Manila galleons linked New Spain to Asia. Portuguese Brazil tied sugar to Lisbon and Atlantic markets, and the Dutch occupation of Pernambuco (1630–1654) shows how competition could reorder a colonial economy. Contraband, smuggling, and privateering reflected the gap between mercantilist ambition and local economic need.

Cities were the places where these systems converged. They concentrated authority and commerce, drew on hinterlands, and created spaces where vendors, artisans, apprentices, servants, and women, including Indigenous and African-descended women, earned livelihoods. Documented cases show women acting as independent economic agents.

The economies were unequal. Land, credit, and privilege were concentrated, while many workers faced coercion and insecurity. Late-colonial reforms, including the 1778 Reglamento de comercio libre, expanded commerce within the Spanish empire but did not create international free trade.

The chapter's historiographical debate asks whether colonial economies were extractive dependencies or had substantial internal autonomy. The evidence supports elements of both. Students should also take away a method: reading land titles, notarial records, customs registers, and treatises critically, asking whose perspective each reflects. The chapter closes by pointing to the enslaved workers whose labor underpinned much plantation wealth, the subject of Chapter 44.

Key Terms

Merced de tierras
A Spanish royal or municipal grant of land to a settler, creating a legal claim whose boundaries and conditions were often contested.
Sesmaria
A Portuguese land grant, applied in Brazil by captaincy holders and officials, nominally conditioned on cultivation.

A Spanish policy allowing holders to regularize defective land titles by paying the Crown.

The resettlement of dispersed Indigenous populations into planned towns, which also opened land to colonists.

Indigenous community documents in central Mexico asserting ancient landholding, used in litigation.

A Spanish American landed estate producing grain, livestock, or other goods for regional markets with varied labor.
Estancia
A grant or estate for livestock, the usual term for cattle ranches in the Río de la Plata.

A Portuguese term for a farm or estate, applied to cattle, cotton, and other colonial enterprises (and, increasingly, coffee in later centuries).

A large agricultural-industrial unit producing a commercial crop for distant markets with a concentrated coerced workforce.

The Brazilian sugar mill and the whole estate that grew and processed cane.

An intensively farmed artificial plot on shallow lakes in the Basin of Mexico.

A grant of Indigenous tribute, and initially labor, to a Spaniard, limited after 1542 to mainly tribute.
Repartimiento
A state-supervised rotation of Indigenous workers to Spanish employers for wages.
Mita
An Andean labor draft adapting an Inca rotation, used chiefly in mining but also other work.

Obraje
A colonial textile workshop combining spinning, weaving, and finishing, sometimes with coerced labor.
Gremio
A craft guild regulating quality, prices, and apprenticeship.
Consulado
A corporate body of merchants with jurisdiction over commercial disputes and a voice in policy.

The regulated system of Spanish trade between Spain and the Americas, including convoys and licensed ports.
Flota and galeones
The convoys sailing to New Spain and to Tierra Firme, respectively.
Manila galleon
A ship in the trans-Pacific trade between Manila and Acapulco beginning in 1565.
Contraband
Trade conducted outside legal channels or in violation of prohibitions or duties.
Privateering
Attack on enemy shipping under a government commission, distinct from piracy.
Cabildo
A municipal council that regulated land, markets, prices, and urban life.
Mercantilism
A body of policies that sought to preserve colonial trade for the metropolis.
Cochineal
A red dye from insects raised on nopal cactus, produced largely by Indigenous communities in southern Mexico.

A Spanish regulation expanding authorized trade among designated imperial ports without opening the empire to foreign trade.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that colonial Latin America was not only a world of silver extraction and plantation exports. It was also a world of Indigenous agriculture, estate production, regional markets, commercial credit, artisans, and cities linked to Europe, Africa, and Asia. The silver of Potosí and the sugar of Pernambuco depended on a far broader set of producers and exchanges.

The chapter also reveals how wealth and power were concentrated. Land, credit, and privilege accumulated among estate owners, merchants, and institutions, while many workers faced coercion and insecurity. These patterns of land concentration, commodity dependence, and regional specialization shaped the economic conditions inherited by the independent states of Volume III.

The chapter connects regions often studied separately: Mexico and the Andes, the Caribbean and Brazil, the Atlantic and the Pacific. It also shows Indigenous and African-descended people, and women, as economic actors whose labor and decisions sustained the system.

Chapter 44 takes up the people whose labor underpinned much plantation prosperity. It examines the Atlantic slave trade, the societies enslaved Africans created, and the long history of Afro-Latin America, giving fuller account of what this chapter could only introduce as an economic necessity.
