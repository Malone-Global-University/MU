Chapter Opening: Citizenship from Below

Chapter 110 studies how Latin Americans used collective mobilization in the 2010s and 2020s to challenge institutions, expand rights, demand constitutional change, and redefine citizenship. It asks why some movements achieved lasting reforms while others met institutional resistance or political defeat.

The chapter begins with the feminist "Green Wave," follows Chile's 2019 social explosion and its two rejected constitutions, then examines Indigenous, environmental, student, Afro-Latin American, and LGBTQ+ movements. It closes by comparing outcomes as of 16 November 2026, a date after which some trajectories remain open.

Evidence includes statutes, court judgments, referendum results, UN and national human-rights reports, movement documents, polling, and journalism. Each has limits: crowd estimates differ, official statistics reflect definitions, and movement and government accounts diverge. The chapter belongs to Volume V, Part XXV, and leaves Central American gangs and security policy to Chapter 111.

At the center of this history is a distinction between the right to speak, the ability to organize, and the capacity to change government. Millions of people can enter the streets and alter the political agenda without agreeing on a bill, constitutional settlement, or the distribution of public resources. For historians, the reasons for participating in a march must be reconstructed from local settings and sources rather than inferred from its largest banner.

The green scarf worn in Argentine abortion-rights demonstrations illustrates how a locally organized campaign can acquire transnational recognition. Chilean students who evaded Metro fares in October 2019, by contrast, helped begin a dispute that became national and constitutional. Indigenous organizations in Ecuador and Chile brought questions of territorial autonomy, consultation, and rural livelihoods into debates usually dominated by urban parties. Environmental defenders showed that participation could involve grave personal danger as well as institutional innovation.

Each case therefore presents different evidence. Statutes show what legislatures authorized; court judgments show how legal claims were decided; election results document votes on specified questions; reports by human-rights monitors document findings and limits of investigation. Movement statements reveal objectives and experiences but do not by themselves establish every allegation. Government declarations explain official positions without automatically proving the legality of their actions. The chapter identifies those distinctions throughout its comparative analysis.

The historical cutoff is 16 November 2026. Important outcomes between 2024 and that date include Chile's 2025 pension reform and 2026 presidential transition, legal developments in the Marielle Franco murder case, and renewed contestation surrounding abortion access in Argentina. These developments are presented as dated records and not as a final resolution of ongoing struggles. The concluding synthesis asks what changed in law, what changed in practice, and what remained unresolved.

Section 110.1

The New Era of Latin American Social Mobilization

Third-wave democratization delivered competitive elections, but by the 2010s many citizens judged that it had not delivered security, dignity, or fairness. Latin America remained one of the world's most unequal regions. Expanded secondary and university enrollment had produced a large educated cohort facing precarious work, while the end of the commodity boom around 2014 squeezed household budgets. Survey series such as Latinobarómetro recorded persistent distrust of parties and legislatures.

Earlier mobilizations had centered on unions, peasant leagues, church base communities, and human-rights groups (Chapters 88 and 96). The movements of the 2010s were more often issue-specific and identity-based, organized horizontally, and fluent in digital media. They also moved between street, court, and legislature. This chapter treats them as varied actors with different leaderships, ideologies, and relations to parties, not as a single "generation" or a single "left."

The relationship between democracy and organized dissent was therefore more complicated than a simple contrast between governments and streets. Elections established procedures through which parties could be replaced; they did not settle disputes over the distribution of public resources, recognition of cultural communities, or the everyday conduct of police and hospitals. A citizen might vote regularly yet confront inaccessible reproductive care, a school system divided by household income, or a pension unable to support retirement. Activists described these problems as deficits of substantive citizenship: formally equal political membership accompanied by unequal capacity to exercise rights. Opponents sometimes argued that movements used disruptive methods to bypass representative institutions. Both claims became questions about legitimate channels of political influence, not just ideological positions.

Economic conditions varied markedly. Chile combined growth, institutional continuity, and persistent household indebtedness; Argentina experienced inflation and recurrent macroeconomic crisis; Ecuador faced fiscal vulnerability tied to petroleum and public spending. Such differences caution against treating the entire region as a society reacting to one identical economic policy. A fare increase could become a catalyst in a city with long-established student networks, whereas conflicts over mining emerged in regions where land tenure, prior consultation, and ecological livelihoods had become central. Historical causation requires matching the grievance to local institutions and political opportunity rather than attributing every protest to 'neoliberalism' or globalization without explanation.

Movements inherited organizational practices from earlier struggles. The Mothers and Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo demonstrated how public space and moral testimony could challenge state narratives of disappearance. Organized Indigenous communities in Ecuador and the Zapatista movement in Mexico demonstrated forms of collective authority outside conventional party competition. Trade unions supplied transport, meeting halls, legal expertise and strike capacity even when demonstrations appeared leaderless. Feminist groups spent years developing networks of lawyers, doctors, teachers and neighborhood organizers before their demands attracted mass demonstrations. Digital communication widened the audiences of established organizations, but it rarely replaced those organizations' capacity to negotiate and monitor policy implementation.

A useful distinction concerns the three stages of a movement's political influence. Agenda-setting occurs when a subject becomes unavoidable in national debate. Institutional conversion follows when courts, legislatures, ministries, or constitutional bodies adopt a proposal or recognize a claim. Implementation requires administrative budgets, trained personnel, judicial enforcement, and continuing political support. The stages may proceed in different orders. Strategic litigation can precede mass demonstrations; a failed bill can expand a coalition; a successful plebiscite to begin reform can be followed by rejection of the reform text. When students measure success, they should specify which stage and whose objectives they mean.

Participants also disagreed among themselves. Generational and geographic differences divided feminist constituencies, and rural Indigenous communities sometimes expressed different preferences about resource extraction. Some protesters entered parties, while others guarded autonomy from them. Established churches, professional associations and conservative citizens organized campaigns with their own claims to representation. Governments sometimes responded with negotiations, sometimes with concessions, and sometimes with force. This chapter examines those interactions without assuming that participation in a movement makes every claim accurate or every tactic legitimate. Its analytical subject is the changing relationship between organized citizens and public institutions.

Section 110.2

Ni Una Menos and the Struggle against Gender Violence

In May 2015 the killing of Chiara Páez, a pregnant fourteen-year-old in Santa Fe province, Argentina, prompted journalists and writers to call for a march. On 3 June 2015 large demonstrations took place in Buenos Aires and many other Argentine cities under the slogan Ni Una Menos ("Not One Woman Fewer"). The movement drew on decades of feminist organizing, including the annual Encuentros Nacionales de Mujeres, and on a regional vocabulary of femicidio and feminicidio.

Several states had already created femicide offenses (Costa Rica 2007, Guatemala 2008, Chile 2010, Argentina 2012). The movement's significance lay in treating violence long classed as private as a matter of public accountability. After the killing of Lucía Pérez in October 2016, Argentine women staged a national strike, and Ni Una Menos marches spread to Peru, Chile, Uruguay, Mexico, and Brazil. Exposure to violence was unequal: poor, rural, Indigenous, Afro-descendant, migrant, and trans women faced greater risk and weaker access to justice. Official femicide counts differ by national definition and cannot be compared without care.

The movement's rapid visibility was not the sudden beginning of Argentine feminism. Decades of work had already linked sexual and reproductive health, workplace inequality, domestic violence, and the disappearance of women. Annual women's meetings established cross-provincial networks that could move information and resources. Human-rights organizations had developed a tradition of marches that transformed individual testimony into a political indictment of institutional failures. When journalists reacted to Chiara Páez's killing, they could draw upon these practices and upon lawyers and local organizations capable of translating outrage into legislative demands. The slogan worked precisely because it condensed a broad and complex agenda into a claim understandable beyond activists' existing circles.

The terms femicide and feminicide need careful distinction. Different scholars and legal systems use them differently: both concern gender-related killings of women, but feminicide is sometimes used to emphasize state complicity or the failure to investigate and punish. Legal definitions determine which deaths become official cases, and police recording practices can change over time. A national increase in documented femicides may indicate higher violence, improved recognition, or a combination; an apparent decrease may conceal under-reporting. Comparisons require matching years, definitions, population denominators, and the institutional level at which deaths were verified. International data should not be read as a scoreboard detached from the societies producing it.

The marches also changed the political meaning of streets and public squares. Participants carried the names and photographs of victims and sought better investigations, protective measures, shelters, and institutions capable of preventing repeat violence. They challenged beliefs that partner violence was a private matter to be settled within families. Some emphasized women's autonomy in public space; others focused on the specific failures of police to respond to earlier warnings. They confronted the enduring obstacles of obtaining forensic evidence, protecting witnesses, and ensuring that poor or rural complainants could access courts. The broader debate concerned the state's duty to safeguard people at home as well as in public.

By October 2016 the movement could organize a women's strike linking gender violence to unpaid care work and economic vulnerability. The relationship was analytically important: women lacking independent income or safe housing may find it harder to leave violent relationships, while caregivers bear heavy costs of absent public services. Yet activists differed about whether abortion access, labor policy, trafficking, and broader economic models should be inseparable from anti-violence efforts. Coalition breadth enlarged demonstrations and also introduced disagreements over political priorities. Support from artists, unions, students, journalists and neighborhood groups did not amount to unanimity about every proposed reform.

Transnational diffusion spread recognizable symbols while retaining national variation. Demonstrators in Peru and Mexico confronted different criminal procedures, legal definitions, and forms of impunity; organizers in Chile connected violence to debates over gender and constitutional reform. Local events were not replicas of a Buenos Aires march. A historical account should trace who adopted a slogan, how it was adapted, and which demands changed. It should also ask about the limitations of visibility: public attention can shift political rhetoric before it changes police budgets, prosecutors' practices, shelter availability or the security of marginalized communities. Those limits help explain why subsequent feminist mobilization pursued more specific legislative and judicial goals.

Section 110.3

Argentina's Marea Verde: From Protest to Abortion Reform

The National Campaign for the Right to Legal, Safe and Free Abortion, founded in 2005, built a federation of feminist, human-rights, union, and health-professional groups. The green scarf, linked to the Campaign since the early 2000s, became a mass symbol in 2018. That year the Chamber of Deputies approved a bill on 14 June (129–125) but the Senate rejected it on 8 August (38–31). The defeat did not end the movement; it expanded it geographically and generationally, including among secondary-school students.

After Alberto Fernández took office, the executive sent a new bill. Deputies approved it on 11 December 2020 and the Senate on 30 December (38–29, with one abstention). Promulgated on 14 January 2021 and published on 15 January, Law 27.610 permits abortion on request through the fourteenth week (inclusive), and afterward in cases of rape or risk to life or health. It guarantees public-health coverage and allows conscientious objection under conditions. Implementation has varied by province, and the same movement that won the law has repeatedly documented access barriers (110.19).

The Argentine campaign became a model of long-term political organization rather than spontaneous legislative success. The National Campaign, which began in 2005, cultivated a network of local chapters that persisted through changes in presidency, parliamentary composition and economic policy. Activists developed legal proposals, gathered endorsements from health workers and human-rights figures, and maintained a visible place in marches and public debates. Green scarves, commonly worn around necks, backpacks and wrists, allowed widely separated participants to display affiliation without belonging to a single party. The symbol itself did not create a majority; organizational labor made it a vehicle for recognizable collective action.

The 2018 congressional confrontation showed why national legislation required coalition-building beyond Buenos Aires. Deputies and senators debated the public-health consequences of clandestine procedures, constitutional protections, religious convictions and the authority of the state to regulate reproduction. Defenders of legal abortion emphasized preventable harm, disparities in access to medical care, and the need for health services. Opponents emphasized fetal life, ethical obligations and alternatives to abortion, while some questioned particular provisions rather than reform as a whole. Voting did not divide perfectly along party lines. Members of major blocs took different positions, making individual legislators, provincial interests and committee work essential to the legislative outcome.

The Senate's rejection in August 2018 could have demobilized an electoral campaign narrowly framed around one vote. Instead, the movement converted defeat into a new period of recruitment and education. Secondary-school organizations discussed sexual education and bodily autonomy; provincial networks challenged the idea that reproductive health was only a metropolitan concern. Organizers analyzed who had voted against the bill and how subsequent coalitions could change the arithmetic. Social media carried congressional debates into homes, but in-person assemblies and professional advocacy helped sustain the campaign after the moment of mass spectacle passed.

In 2020 the legislative situation was different. President Alberto Fernández's government took responsibility for a bill, although the final passage still depended on legislators' votes. The pandemic complicated parliamentary procedure, health access and demonstrations; it did not eliminate mobilization. Passage by both houses in December produced a national framework under which a patient could request an abortion within the time limit without proving a special legal ground. Beyond that limit, statutory exceptions concerned rape and risks to life or health. The law also addressed time limits for care, health-sector coverage and conscientious objection subject to duties that prevent abandonment of patients. These provisions matter because a legal right that gives no instructions to institutions may remain inaccessible.

Implementation posed separate challenges. A formal national guarantee met the uneven distribution of providers across provinces, difficulties obtaining medication, obstacles for minors and rural residents, administrative resistance, and continuing political disagreement. A provider's objection was not identical to an institution's authority to refuse all service; the law specifies obligations that must be interpreted and enforced. Activist groups documented access while opponents continued to seek political or legislative reversal. Thus the 2020 statute was a crucial historical achievement, but the subsequent history concerns whether a national legal promise could be made real across Argentina's federal and socially unequal health system.

Section 110.4

The Green Wave across Latin America

The "green wave" was transnational, but legal change ran through different institutions, so it should not be reduced to Argentina. Uruguay legislated in 2012 (Law 18.987, twelve weeks). Colombia's Constitutional Court, which in 2006 (C-355) allowed three exceptions, decriminalized abortion through the twenty-fourth week in ruling C-055 of 21 February 2022, after litigation by the Causa Justa coalition. Mexico's Supreme Court ruled in September 2021 that absolute criminalization is unconstitutional and in September 2023 struck down federal penal-code provisions and required federal health institutions to provide services; because criminal law is mostly state law, change proceeded state by state.

Chile's Law 21.030 (2017) allows abortion on three grounds only (risk to the woman's life, fetal unviability, rape). Brazil remained restrictive, with the question pending before its Supreme Court. Students must distinguish four layers: a court judgment, a change in criminal law, a health-service obligation, and actual availability. The first can occur without the last.

The comparison begins before the Argentine victory. Uruguay's 2012 legislation combined early-pregnancy access with a defined consultation process and statutory rules. Its reform illustrates how legislatures can establish a framework through ordinary political bargaining, but the precise procedure and timing differ from Argentina's. Chile, by contrast, enacted a three-grounds law in 2017 that did not establish access on request. Both reforms changed prior restrictions, yet their legal scope was not equivalent. Simple descriptions such as 'legalized abortion' conceal questions about who may obtain treatment, at which stage of pregnancy, on what grounds, and in which health facilities.

Colombia provides a judicial pathway developed over many years. In 2006 its Constitutional Court recognized exceptions to criminal punishment in three situations, establishing a framework for cases involving health, fetal viability and sexual violence. Feminist litigants and medical organizations later challenged the continued burden of criminal law in earlier pregnancies. The 2022 C-055 ruling altered that balance by removing criminal liability through the twenty-fourth week, while retaining circumstances for lawful access afterward. It did not automatically create identical service availability in every municipality. Scholars should distinguish the reasoning of the Court, the scope of the order, and the administrative measures required of health authorities.

Mexico shows the importance of federalism. The Supreme Court's 2021 decision addressed a state criminal framework and articulated constitutional objections to absolute criminalization. Its 2023 First Chamber decision concerned federal penal-code provisions and federal institutions; states did not all acquire precisely identical laws on the date of the judgment. Legislatures, local courts, health agencies and rights organizations continued to shape implementation. Residents in different states might confront different statutory deadlines or administrative pathways. A claim that 'Mexico legalized abortion nationwide in 2023' therefore oversimplifies both constitutional doctrine and local practice. The appropriate study asks what was invalidated, who was bound, and what had to happen next.

Opponents of these reforms were themselves politically diverse. Catholic and evangelical organizations mobilized around protection of prenatal life and conscientious objection; some physicians raised questions about professional autonomy; other citizens who supported limited exceptions opposed access without specified grounds. Reform coalitions also included people with differing ethical positions who agreed that criminal punishment or lack of medical care produced unjust consequences. Historians should identify the arguments and institutions involved rather than assume that every supporter or opponent belonged to one political camp. Public opinion, legislative votes and court opinions offer distinct types of evidence and cannot substitute for one another.

The consequences of reform must be measured across several dimensions. Legal status can change on one day while medical training, distribution of medication, referral systems and public information change over years. Differences between cities and rural regions, between insured and uninsured patients, and between adult and adolescent access affect the practical meaning of a right. Researchers may use health-ministry service statistics, patient testimony, court enforcement records and budgets to investigate implementation, keeping the limitations of each source clear. A comparative history of the Green Wave is therefore a history of institutions as well as slogans: it studies how movements gained access to decision-making, how laws were interpreted, and what remained contested.

Feminicidio / Femicide

Femicide usually means the killing of a woman because of her gender. In Latin America feminicidio also carries a political claim that states fail to prevent or punish such killings.

Definitions in national laws differ, so statistics should not be compared across countries without checking them.

Section 110.5

Chile before 2019: Inequality, Education, and the Demand for Reform

Chile after 1990 combined stable democratic institutions and rapid poverty reduction with a social model inherited from the dictatorship (Chapter 85): a 1980 constitution amended in 2005, individually funded pensions run by AFPs, and health and education systems mixing public and private provision. Many retirees received pensions far below what the system had promised, and families carried education debt.

The 2006 "Penguin Revolution" of secondary students (named for their uniforms) and the far larger 2011 movement of university students, coordinated by CONFECH, demanded an end to profit in education and fairer access. Leaders such as Camila Vallejo, Giorgio Jackson, and later Gabriel Boric entered electoral politics. Tuition-free access (gratuidad) began in 2016, while the #NoMásAFP marches that year targeted pensions. These movements built the networks, vocabulary, and personnel that 2019 would draw on.

The Chilean democratic settlement after 1990 emerged from a negotiated transition rather than a clean institutional break. Civilian presidents governed within a constitutional and economic framework bearing the imprint of military rule, even as major reforms reduced appointed offices and expanded political participation. Governments of different coalitions used markets and social spending in changing combinations, often achieving substantial poverty reduction. Yet the distribution of wealth, uneven quality of services, and dependence of households on private debt remained politically sensitive. Growth and dissatisfaction could coexist because aggregate prosperity did not describe the experience of families paying for schools, transport, retirement, and health treatment.

The pension system became a particularly vivid illustration. Individual retirement accounts linked benefits to a worker's contribution history and investment performance. This arrangement could generate resources for formal workers with sustained contributions, but interrupted employment, low wages, long periods of caregiving and informal work often produced inadequate benefits. Women's employment histories made these weaknesses especially apparent. Demonstrators in the No+AFP movement challenged the assumption that private accounts alone could deliver adequate security in old age. Defenders of the system argued for reform that preserved individual savings and fiscal sustainability. The dispute therefore involved both the immediate adequacy of benefits and competing visions of intergenerational obligation.

Education offered another route into national politics. The 2006 secondary-school movement addressed transport passes, examination fees, school quality and the institutional legacy of municipalized education. Student occupations and assemblies demonstrated the capacity of young people, who were not yet national officeholders, to set a political agenda. By 2011 university students challenged tuition, loans, unequal opportunities and the role of profit in education. Many families experienced enrollment in higher education as an aspiration financed by long-term debt. Activists demanded public responsibility for access and quality, while others questioned how universal subsidies would affect fiscal priorities and whether all institutions should receive equivalent treatment.

Organizational consequences outlasted individual demonstrations. Chilean students learned to conduct national assemblies, coordinate local action, communicate with journalists, and negotiate with ministers. Leaders such as Camila Vallejo and Gabriel Boric later entered Congress and government, illustrating the porous boundary between street politics and representative institutions. That transition posed dilemmas: a movement can demand an ideal program more easily than an administration can finance or legislate it under divided authority. Many activists regarded institutional participation as a route to durable reform; others feared that elected office would weaken grassroots accountability. These tensions would reappear when former student leaders had responsibility for governing after 2022.

By 2019 this political inheritance intersected with other sources of dissatisfaction: high-profile accusations of elite misconduct, health-care waiting times, the cost of living, and the perception that policy consultation favored specialists over ordinary users. None automatically produced a national uprising. But they supplied interpretive frameworks through which a modest transit fare increase could symbolize broader injustice. The Metro was both a practical necessity and a visible public system connecting neighborhoods unequally served by the city. The accumulated student experience helps explain why fare evasion could escalate quickly into a wider crisis of trust.

Section 110.6

October 2019: The Estallido Social

Transport authorities announced a thirty-peso increase in Santiago Metro fares on 4 October 2019; the adjustment took effect around 6 October. From mid-October secondary students organized mass fare evasion. On 18 October several stations were burned and the system shut down. President Sebastián Piñera declared a state of emergency for the Metropolitan Region on 18 October; expanded emergency measures and curfews followed on 19 October. On 25 October, demonstrators filled Plaza Italia, renamed Plaza Dignidad; estimates of more than a million participants in Santiago come from organizers and observers and should be treated as estimates.

Demands ranged beyond the fare: pensions, health care, wages, debt, and accountability of elites. Most participation was peaceful, including neighborhood assemblies (cabildos) and the feminist performance "Un violador en tu camino" by the collective Las Tesis. Arson, looting, and attacks also occurred, and the police used force on a large scale. A UN human-rights mission documented serious violations, including unnecessary or disproportionate force, ill-treatment, and sexual violence, while also noting violence by some protesters. Numbers of dead and injured differ by agency and attribution; consult the INDH and OHCHR reports directly. Neither "peaceful uprising" nor "riot" captures the whole event.

The sequence must be reconstructed carefully because different parts of the emergency developed over several days. Chile's transport authorities announced a fare adjustment on October 4, 2019, with the revised fare taking effect around October 6. Student-led mass fare evasion intensified in the following week. October 18 brought widespread disruption, fires at Metro facilities and confrontations with police. The first state-of-emergency decree for the Metropolitan Region was issued that day, with subsequent extensions and curfews. Explaining these dates matters: an original policy decision, a movement's tactical response, the outbreak of violence, and the executive's resort to extraordinary authority were separate actions by different institutions and people.

Fare evasion began as a recognizable protest technique. It was disruptive but not identical to station arson or assaults. The movement expanded as wider crowds identified the fare rise with perceived indifference to the household costs of urban life. By the large October 25 march, participants voiced demands over health care, pensions, wages, the constitution, corruption and political accountability. The composition of the crowd was heterogeneous: students, professionals, retirees, trade-union members, neighborhood groups and people who had rarely joined protests. Estimates of crowd size should be attributed to the organizations or authorities producing them. Without systematic counts, a photograph of a filled square cannot establish a precise number of participants.

The government framed disorder and attacks on transport infrastructure as threats to safety that required extraordinary intervention. Some shops were looted, infrastructure was destroyed, and residents experienced serious disruptions. Others saw the deployment of military personnel and security forces as a reminder of dictatorship-era repression. The clash of perceptions did not make every interpretation equally supported. Human-rights investigators documented unnecessary and disproportionate force by officers, including severe eye injuries, ill-treatment and sexual violence, while also noting attacks and violence by some demonstrators. Students should examine the methods, dates and attribution rules in the United Nations report, and compare the government's formal reply with the investigation's findings.

Public order and the right to peaceful assembly are not mutually exclusive obligations. A state has duties to protect transit workers, residents, protesters and police; those duties constrain the kinds of force it may use. Collective responsibility is particularly hazardous in mass movements, because the acts of a minority cannot automatically be assigned to peaceful participants. Conversely, the presence of many peaceful demonstrators does not excuse documented attacks on others. The historical challenge is to separate acts, perpetrators and contexts and to understand how government responses influenced the further course of the demonstrations.

Neighborhood assemblies and cultural performance became important forms of participation alongside mass marches. Las Tesis's intervention challenged sexual violence through an easily translated public performance, subsequently repeated internationally. Local assemblies debated constitutional demands and the future of public services. These practices helped turn an economic grievance into an argument about the foundations of citizenship. Yet observers must not read later constitutional proposals back into the initial fare protests as though all participants had planned that route from the beginning. The possibility of a constitutional convention emerged through political decisions made after the uprising had destabilized ordinary arrangements for governing.

Section 110.7

From the Streets to the Constitutional Convention, 2019–2022

On 15 November 2019, parties from government and much of the opposition signed an Agreement for Social Peace and a New Constitution; the Communist Party and some left groups did not sign. It established a plebiscite and a two-thirds quorum rule for any text. Postponed by COVID-19, the plebiscite on 25 October 2020 approved a new constitution (78.28%) and an elected convention (approximately 79.0%).

The convention elected on 15–16 May 2021 had 155 members, an electoral correction that produced gender parity, and seventeen reserved seats for Indigenous peoples. Independents and the left dominated, and the right fell below the one-third needed to block proposals. Elisa Loncon, a Mapuche scholar, was elected its first president. Its draft of 388 articles proposed a social and democratic state of law, extensive social rights, environmental provisions, recognition of a plurinational and intercultural state, Indigenous autonomies and parallel justice systems, and a Chamber of Regions replacing the Senate. Publicized convention controversies reduced public trust in the body.

The November 15 agreement was a political response to a crisis of authority, not an act by the protest movement as a single corporate body. Party leaders sought a route that could channel competing demands into procedures recognized by electoral institutions. The agreement offered voters a choice about drafting a new constitution and the kind of body that should undertake the task. Not all parties signed, and many neighborhood groups considered the agreement inadequate, reflecting concern that established elites might reclaim control of an uprising that they had not initiated. Nevertheless, the proposed path introduced a mechanism with which citizens could accept, modify or reject the constitutional project.

The 2020 plebiscite took place amid the COVID-19 pandemic. It asked two distinct questions: whether to draft a new constitution and which institution should do it. The overwhelming preference for an elected convention did not mean that voters had agreed on the substance of a future charter. Some sought stronger social guarantees; others wanted to replace symbols and rules inherited from authoritarianism; others supported a new drafting process while expecting continuity on property rights, the political system or the unitary character of the state. This distinction became decisive when the final texts were later submitted for separate approval.

Election rules shaped the convention's composition. Measures providing gender parity and reserved Indigenous seats made the body institutionally distinctive, while the electoral success of independent lists signaled distrust of traditional parties. Elisa Loncon's election as president symbolized possibilities for Indigenous representation that previous constitutional arrangements had not offered. Yet representation alone did not resolve disagreements over what kinds of autonomy, jurisdiction or territorial rights should be constitutionalized. The convention had to combine ambitious rights language with an architecture of institutions able to carry out those promises. A two-thirds approval requirement encouraged bargaining but could not guarantee public acceptance of the final package.

The draft articulated a social and democratic state committed to wide-ranging social rights and environmental protection. It proposed institutional changes concerning the legislature and forms of recognition for Indigenous peoples, placing questions of justice and governance alongside rights to health, housing and education. Supporters argued that the document addressed historical inequalities and democratic exclusions. Critics feared uncertainty about courts, the balance of powers, the future of the Senate and how multiple legal systems would interact. Some concerns rested on particular provisions; others circulated in distorted or inaccurate form. Historians should consult the actual text rather than relying only on either campaign's explanation of it.

Meanwhile the convention itself became an object of political judgment. Public disputes, individual misconduct and disagreements over priorities fed perceptions of indiscipline. President Boric's administration, entering office in March 2022, supported the process but faced inflationary and security pressures beyond the convention's control. Opposition to the text could therefore express objections to clauses, unease about procedure or discontent with the government. The referendum ultimately measured a decision about one complete draft under compulsory voting. It did not ask citizens to evaluate each proposed social right separately or to pronounce a permanent verdict on constitutional renewal.

Section 110.8

Two Constitutional Rejections and the Limits of Political Transformation

On 4 September 2022, in the first plebiscite held under compulsory voting, 61.86% rejected the draft and 38.14% approved, with turnout near 86%. Researchers point to several factors: the text's length and perceived radicalism, the plurinational provisions, anxiety about security and the economy, misinformation, and falling confidence in the convention. Polls showed that many who rejected the text still wanted a new constitution. Boric, elected in December 2021, then negotiated a second process with an expert commission and an elected Council.

In May 2023 right-of-center parties won a majority in that Council, with Kast's Republican Party the largest force. Its draft, more conservative than the first, was rejected on 17 December 2023 (55.76% against, 44.24% in favor). The 1980 constitution, repeatedly amended, remained in force. Rejecting one text never meant rejecting all reform: the two defeats show that majorities that demanded change disagreed about its content. Constitutional change was displaced by ordinary legislation, notably a 2025 pension reform. Kast won the December 2025 runoff and took office on 11 March 2026; reporting through mid-2026 described no active constituent process, new student protests on 3 June 2026 against education cuts, and falling approval ratings for his government.

The first draft's fate raised methodological questions about referendums. When voters receive a long constitutional package, a 'no' vote can signify rejection of one controversial provision, dissatisfaction with the drafting body, mistrust of politicians or opposition to the package as a whole. A new agreement established a second process, with experts working alongside an elected Constitutional Council. Its party composition differed sharply from the 2021 Convention, with the Republican Party emerging as the largest force in the Council election. The second draft was more conservative in orientation. Some voters who had opposed the first draft objected to the second for different reasons; this was not a simple swing from one ideology to another. Questions about abortion, social policy, national identity, public security and the distribution of government powers reappeared in revised form. Each process had its own procedures, participants and political timing.

The December 2023 rejection left the amended 1980 Constitution in force. It did not erase prior changes, nor did it cancel ongoing demands for better services. Political energy shifted toward statutory reform and executive policy, including pension legislation. Those narrower routes required negotiation with legislative opponents, administrative capacity and fiscal resources. A constitutional text can articulate principles, but it cannot by itself create clinics, schools or pension contributions. The repeated rejection underscored a distinction between the aspiration to transform social relations and the difficulty of constructing an institutional bargain acceptable to a broad electorate.

By 2026 Chile had moved into another political setting. José Antonio Kast, the candidate who won the December 2025 runoff, began his presidential term on March 11, 2026. His administration faced protest and controversy over education and other policies, showing that contentious participation persisted after the constitutional pathway had stalled. It would be misleading to write that the period ended with a final triumph for either the left or the right. Political actors and public priorities continued to change. The lasting institutional lesson is that a constitutional process opened by mass protest can still be constrained by electoral majorities, public trust and the detailed content of the proposed settlement.

Section 110.9

Mapuche Mobilization and the Question of Plurinationalism

The Mapuche, who resisted Spain for centuries (Chapter 38), were incorporated by the Chilean state in the nineteenth century and confined to reductions of their former lands. Agrarian reform before 1973 returned some land, the dictatorship reversed this, and forestry plantations later expanded across the Araucanía. The 1993 Indigenous Law created CONADI but not constitutional recognition. Mapuche organizations range from negotiators and electoral actors to groups such as the Coordinadora Arauco-Malleco that endorse direct land recovery.

The state invoked its anti-terrorism law against some Mapuche defendants. The 2017 "Operation Huracán" case collapsed after evidence was found to have been fabricated, and the police killing of Camilo Catrillanca in November 2018 caused national outrage. Attacks on forestry property and rural residents also occurred, and Chile maintained states of exception with military support in the southern macrozone from 2022. The convention's plurinational proposals made these questions central to 2022, but Mapuche communities were not politically uniform, and legitimate claims to land and recognition must be distinguished from specific acts of violence. During 2026 the Kast government pursued a security-focused policy and discussed revisions of Indigenous land arrangements; proposals and consultations remained contested at the November 16 cutoff.

Historical dispossession underlies contemporary Mapuche politics. The Chilean state's military incorporation of Mapuche territories during the nineteenth century transformed patterns of landholding and imposed new forms of administration. Later policies of settlement, land division and forestry expansion altered the environment and economic opportunities available to communities. Memories of territorial independence, ancestral authorities and previous agreements remained significant, but contemporary claims vary. Some organizations seek return of particular lands and protection of language and cultural institutions; others pursue political autonomy or participation in national decisions. It is inaccurate to treat all people identifying as Mapuche as members of one organization with a common strategy.

Democratic governments after 1990 established agencies and mechanisms for Indigenous development, but constitutional recognition and land restitution continued to be contested. Conflicts were intensified by competing legal titles and the economic importance of timber plantations and forestry infrastructure. Local residents, Mapuche workers, forestry owners and farmers sometimes held differing views about employment, policing and territorial reform. An environmental account can show why plantation forestry is disputed, while a legal account traces the distinction between restitution claims, property titles and state compensation. The two approaches complement rather than replace one another.

The application of antiterrorism legislation raised questions about criminal-law safeguards and unequal enforcement. A terrorist label is a legal accusation requiring proof under the relevant law; it should not be used casually for all direct action. The collapse of the Operation Huracán case exposed serious questions about fabricated evidence and the credibility of police investigations. The killing of Camilo Catrillanca further undermined trust in security authorities. At the same time, attacks on property, workers and rural households created genuine security fears. A balanced account recognizes both the state's obligation to investigate violence and its responsibility to respect Indigenous rights and the presumption of innocence.

The constitutional convention made these debates nationally visible. Reserved seats enabled direct Indigenous participation in drafting, and discussions of plurinationalism forced voters to consider whether the Chilean state could recognize differentiated political identities while preserving equal citizenship. Supporters saw such recognition as a response to long-standing exclusion; critics feared unclear jurisdictions or threats to national unity. The first draft's failure cannot be assigned exclusively to these provisions, but they became central subjects in the public campaign. The historical issue is not simply cultural symbolism; it involves which authorities make decisions about education, justice, land and public revenue.

During 2026 the Kast administration pursued a more security-centered approach and discussed changes to Indigenous land and consultation arrangements. The status of proposals and consultation processes should be read at the November 16 cutoff rather than imagined as completed reforms. For historians, this case demonstrates that territorial disputes endure across changes of government because they combine historical claims, existing property institutions and divergent definitions of security. Understanding them requires differentiating negotiated community action, parliamentary representation, litigation, direct land recovery, and particular criminal acts, rather than assigning every development to a single undifferentiated 'Mapuche movement.'

Section 110.10

Ecuador's Indigenous Movements and National Protest

CONAIE, founded in 1986, has led national uprisings since 1990 and was central to the 2000 overthrow of President Mahuad. In October 2019 Lenín Moreno's decree ending fuel subsidies, part of an IMF-backed program, triggered transport strikes and an Indigenous march on Quito; the government temporarily relocated to Guayaquil. After about eleven days, mediation by the UN and the Catholic Church produced an agreement revoking the decree.

In June 2022 an eighteen-day mobilization under Leonidas Iza demanded fuel-price controls, debt relief for small farmers, and limits on mining and oil in Indigenous territories. Violence and several deaths occurred, and a negotiated agreement followed on 30 June. A further wave of protests and highway blockades began in September 2025 after President Noboa removed a diesel subsidy. CONAIE, under Marlon Vargas, announced an end to the road-blocking protests on 22 October 2025 while continuing to oppose the policy. CONAIE's influence has rested less on its party, Pachakutik, than on the capacity to paralyze roads and negotiate directly with presidents. It also reveals a limit: such leverage can block policies more easily than it can build lasting majorities.

Ecuador's Indigenous confederation is not merely an episodic protest coalition. CONAIE developed from decades of organizing in highland, Amazonian and coastal communities, bringing together distinct linguistic, territorial and political traditions. From the 1990 uprising onward, Indigenous organizations pressed demands for recognition, bilingual education, land protection and greater political participation. Their capacity to organize national road blockades and marches reflected established community institutions, not the temporary enthusiasm of unaffiliated crowds. This institutional depth helps explain why governments repeatedly negotiated with CONAIE despite electoral fortunes that varied among Indigenous parties and candidates.

The 2019 confrontation arose from a fiscal decree eliminating fuel subsidies, announced in a context of financial pressure and International Monetary Fund discussions. Transport-sector protests and Indigenous mobilization interacted, but their priorities were not identical. Rural producers feared higher transport costs and broader price effects; Indigenous leaders also raised longstanding questions about political recognition and economic fairness. The government's relocation of administrative functions and the scale of demonstrations indicated a national crisis. Mediation produced a reversal of the disputed decree, but reversing one policy did not resolve the long-term distribution of subsidies or the state's dependence on petroleum revenues.

The June 2022 uprising again combined immediate economic demands with territorial concerns. Rising fuel prices and debt burdens affected farmers and transport users, while proposed oil and mining projects raised consultation and environmental issues. Leonidas Iza became a prominent public face of the mobilization, but CONAIE comprised many organizations that could disagree about tactics and concessions. Confrontations, police actions, deaths and disruptions imposed costs on protesters, residents and businesses. The June 30 agreement created a negotiated route to some changes and further discussion; researchers need to distinguish the agreement's text from later implementation to establish what was actually delivered.

A third major conflict in 2025 demonstrated how recurring grievances could survive changes of president and Indigenous leadership. Following the removal of a diesel subsidy under President Daniel Noboa, road blockades affected parts of Ecuador. Reporting identified increased fuel costs for transport and agriculture as central concerns, along with wider opposition to the government's handling of rural economic policy. CONAIE, then led by Marlon Vargas, announced on October 22, 2025 that it was ending highway-blocking protests amid threatened military intervention. The termination of blockades did not amount to acceptance of the reform or to settlement of every underlying grievance; the organization described continued resistance through other channels.

The comparative significance of 2019, 2022 and 2025 is that different governments confronted a recurring institutional capacity for protest. Students can evaluate whether road blockades are an effective form of bargaining, how authorities should protect access to transport and medical supplies, and why negotiations sometimes fail to produce durable compromise. CONAIE's strength lay partly in its ability to coordinate territorially rooted constituencies; its constraints included internal differences and the difficulty of converting episodic concessions into stable policy. The case illustrates the tension between majoritarian government decision-making and organized minority claims to consultation, distributive justice and recognition.

Section 110.11

Environmental Justice and the Defense of Territory

Extractive projects created conflicts across the region. In Peru, the 2009 Bagua clashes over Amazonian decrees killed more than thirty civilians and police, and protests against the Conga and Tía María mines followed. ILO Convention 169 and the Inter-American Court's Sarayaku v. Ecuador judgment (2012) strengthened prior consultation, though states often treat it as procedure rather than consent. In Brazil, Belo Monte and the "temporal framework" dispute over Indigenous land became national issues. In Colombia, a 2017 local consultation in Cajamarca (Tolima) rejected mining.

In Ecuador, voters on 20 August 2023 approved ending oil extraction in Yasuní National Park's Block 43. In Panama, protests in late 2023 against the Cobre Panamá mine preceded a Supreme Court ruling that its contract was unconstitutional. Participants disagreed: some local residents sought jobs and revenue, others prioritized water and land, and Indigenous organizations insisted on consent. Lithium, Amazonian development, and Chinese investment are reserved for Chapter 114.

Environmental conflict is not simply a struggle between people who value nature and people who value economic development. Mining and petroleum generate jobs, taxes, electricity and export earnings, while their costs can be borne disproportionately by communities close to extraction sites. Contamination, pressure on water systems, displacement and changes in land use affect households differently from the national revenue benefits reported by governments. State officials may invoke public ownership of subsoil resources; local residents may invoke territorial rights, property, cultural survival or the right to consultation. These competing legal and economic claims help explain why apparently technical licensing decisions become matters of national politics.

In Peru the violent 2009 Bagua confrontation grew out of disputes over laws affecting Amazonian lands and the meaning of consultation. The deaths of police and civilians revealed severe risks when state authority and Indigenous mobilization collided. Later disputes around Conga and Tía María mines raised different issues, including agricultural water use and regional economic expectations. Scholars should not collapse these conflicts into one outcome. The location of a mine, the quantity of accessible water, local employment and the history of consultations all shape whether communities perceive proposed extraction as an opportunity or a threat.

Regional legal developments provided new standards but not simple answers. Convention 169 of the International Labour Organization requires governments to consult Indigenous peoples through appropriate procedures concerning measures that directly affect them, with the objective of obtaining agreement or consent. That obligation does not mean every ordinary policy decision automatically requires a universally defined veto; particular projects and peoples' rights may involve stronger requirements under additional legal standards. In the Sarayaku judgment, the Inter-American Court found that Ecuador had violated community rights in connection with oil exploration and insufficient prior consultation. The decision placed international attention on the timing and quality of state engagement with Indigenous institutions.

Environmental voters also exercised power through direct-democratic and judicial channels. In Ecuador, the August 2023 Yasuní referendum called for ending oil extraction from Block 43 in a protected part of the Amazon, while implementation encountered fiscal, technical and political disputes. Panama's massive protests against a copper-mining contract in 2023 were followed by a Supreme Court judgment finding the contract unconstitutional. The chronology matters: demonstrations could influence the political environment in which institutions acted, but a judicial ruling has its own legal reasoning and cannot simply be described as a protest victory without examining that reasoning. In each case the law's implementation required decisions about workers, existing infrastructure and future revenue.

Brazilian conflicts over hydroelectric dams, deforestation, Indigenous land demarcation and the so-called temporal framework demonstrate the interplay of legislatures, courts and executive agencies. Environmental defenders may support conservation while also disagreeing with outside organizations about who should control local land. Students should compare local testimony, environmental-impact assessments, official economic projections and court decisions, identifying the evidentiary limits of each. Detailed accounts of regional lithium geopolitics, commodity demand and China belong to Chapter 114; here the focus is on the politics of mobilization, consultation and institutional accountability.

Section 110.12

Indigenous Women, Environmental Defenders, and the Costs of Activism

Berta Cáceres, a Lenca leader and co-founder of COPINH, led the campaign against the Agua Zarca dam on the Gualcarque River in Honduras, for which she received the Goldman Prize in 2015. Foreign financiers withdrew, but she was murdered at home on 2 March 2016. In 2018 seven men were convicted, including employees and former military personnel linked to the company DESA, and in 2021 DESA's former president David Castillo was convicted as a co-perpetrator and sentenced to more than twenty-two years. Her family and COPINH argue that those who planned the crime at higher levels remain unprosecuted.

Cáceres's case illustrates wider patterns: Global Witness annual reports have repeatedly found Latin America to account for most recorded killings of land and environmental defenders worldwide. Defenders also face criminalization through lawsuits and charges. The Escazú Agreement (2018, in force 2021) is the first regional treaty with provisions protecting them. Women lead many such struggles because land, water, and household survival are tied together, as in the work of Francia Márquez, an Afro-Colombian environmental leader who became Colombia's vice president in 2022.

The history of environmental defense must be read at the scale of personal risk as well as institutional reform. Berta Cáceres helped build COPINH as a vehicle for Lenca community organizing, bringing land, cultural rights and public-service grievances into collective campaigns. The Agua Zarca project became emblematic because the contested river had material, cultural and spiritual significance for people in the region. Supporters of the project argued that electricity production and construction could support development. Opponents raised failures of consultation and threats to community control. International recognition, including the 2015 Goldman Environmental Prize, elevated Cáceres's profile but did not guarantee safety.

Her assassination in March 2016 was followed by investigations, international pressure and multiple trials. Seven defendants were convicted in 2018 for roles connected to the killing, and David Castillo, a former executive associated with the company, was convicted in 2021 and subsequently sentenced. The convictions should be described by offense and defendant rather than compressed into a claim that 'the company' was convicted of murder. Cáceres's family and COPINH continued to press for investigation of alleged higher-level responsibility; those allegations must be attributed and not treated as findings against people whom a court had not convicted. This distinction is essential to writing about political violence responsibly.

Threats to defenders can arise before any physical attack. Criminal complaints, surveillance, costly litigation, public stigmatization, loss of employment and gendered intimidation can make organizing prohibitively expensive. Women leaders may face threats aimed at children and relatives, while Indigenous and Afro-descendant organizers also confront racial discrimination and unequal access to legal protection. Annual tallies of killed environmental defenders, such as those published by Global Witness, can illuminate patterns but are subject to differences in reporting and incomplete investigations. Not every recorded death can be safely assigned a single economic motive, and an absence from a database does not prove safety.

The Escazú Agreement sought to strengthen access to environmental information, participation in decisions, and environmental justice, with particular provisions concerning human-rights defenders. Negotiated in 2018 and entering into force in 2021, it provides an important example of local concerns being expressed through a regional legal instrument. Yet treaties require ratification, implementing regulations and effective domestic institutions; participation in a treaty does not automatically prevent threats on a particular road or river. Comparing countries that adopted the agreement with those that did not requires attention to broader legal and political conditions rather than an assumption of immediate causation.

Francia Márquez's activism in Colombia offers a different pathway. Her work around mining and environmental protection connected Afro-Colombian territorial rights with national electoral politics, leading to service as vice president from 2022. That transition did not eliminate disagreements within Afro-Colombian or environmental constituencies; some advocates feared their priorities could be diluted when represented inside government. Across the region, women defenders combined community care, local knowledge and sophisticated legal argument. Their experiences show why the human cost of mobilization cannot be measured solely by the number of statutes amended or the size of a demonstration.

Section 110.13

Student Movements and Youth Political Participation

Chilean and Colombian students faced similar problems of cost, debt, and unequal quality. In Colombia the 2011 student coalition (MANE) forced the withdrawal of a higher-education reform, and student protests recurred in 2018. The national strike that began on 28 April 2021 was triggered by a tax proposal under President Duque and grew into a two-month mobilization led by unions, students, Indigenous and Afro-Colombian groups, and youth "front lines" in cities such as Cali. Human-rights groups and the UN documented serious abuses by security forces; counts of the dead differ by source. The government withdrew the tax reform.

Young people were not ideologically uniform. Many supported movement candidates, and many others later voted for the right in Chile and Argentina; young men and women often diverged in preference. Student organizations proved effective in mobilizing but less effective in producing lasting governing coalitions, a lesson Chile's former student leaders learned inside government.

Student activism has a distinctive institutional setting. Schools and universities concentrate young people, create shared schedules, provide spaces for public discussion and connect participants through academic and social networks. Educational rules also make the object of protest highly visible: tuition, grants, school access, examination costs and debt all connect individual experience to government budgets. Yet students are not a uniform constituency. Secondary students may lack voting rights; university students face varied financial pressures; and young people outside formal education may not identify with campus organizations. Evaluating a movement's representative claims therefore requires asking who was able to participate and who remained outside the institutional network.

Colombia's 2011 campaign against higher-education reform drew upon these conditions. The Mesa Amplia Nacional Estudiantil organized demonstrations and debate over university autonomy, financing and proposed legal changes. The government's withdrawal of its proposal illustrated the capacity of sustained student coordination to affect policy before a final law was enacted. Later mobilization in 2018 emphasized resources for public universities and the conditions of students entering higher education. Those achievements differed from legislation permanently restructuring the entire system: temporary budgetary settlements could be revisited by subsequent administrations.

The 2021 national strike in Colombia was broader than student politics. A controversial tax proposal provided an initial trigger, but demonstrations quickly encompassed inequality, pandemic-related hardship, policing, employment and political representation. The government withdrew the tax initiative, while strikes and mobilization continued. In Cali and other cities, young protesters established front-line groups that faced police, built barriers and sometimes engaged in confrontations. Human-rights bodies documented abuses, including excessive force, and investigated reports of killings and disappearances. A careful account distinguishes verified deaths, allegations undergoing investigation and figures produced by different monitoring organizations.

Regional and racial geography shaped participation. In Cali, Afro-Colombian communities, urban peripheries and young people facing precarious work appeared in a movement often described too narrowly as student-led. Indigenous groups organized their own participation and demands. Local businesses and residents could oppose blockades without necessarily opposing tax reform or social rights. Demonstrators might disagree about whether negotiations should be carried out by unions, community assemblies or elected representatives. The history therefore concerns both the content of grievances and the problem of determining who had authority to speak for an unusually broad coalition.

Young activists sometimes transformed into elected officials, while other groups rejected affiliation with parties. Chile's earlier student leaders illustrate one path; Colombian organizers followed varied careers in local government, advocacy and independent movements. Evidence about youth ideology must avoid sweeping generational generalizations: age, gender, neighborhood, religious background and socioeconomic circumstances produced different electoral choices. The most enduring contribution of student mobilization may be its capacity to train participants in organization, public argument and engagement with institutions, even when a particular legislative demand remains unresolved.

Section 110.14

Housing, Pensions, Public Services, and Everyday Economic Citizenship

Many protests were about the cost of ordinary life. Brazil's June 2013 protests began over a twenty-centavo bus-fare rise in São Paulo and spread nationwide. Chile's 2019 movement drew on pensions, health costs, and debt. Housing shortages produced land occupations and informal settlements. Even where macroeconomic indicators improved, households relied on credit and informal work: the ILO estimates that roughly half of the region's workers hold informal jobs, without secure pensions or health coverage.

This explains why democratic elections and growth did not automatically produce a sense of security. Protesters demanded an economic citizenship made of dependable services, transport, and old-age income. Chile's 2025 pension reform, which added employer contributions and a guaranteed benefit, showed that movement pressure could affect ordinary legislation, though not necessarily in the way protesters had imagined.

Social rights became politically powerful because they referred to services used every day. A household may encounter the state through a crowded bus, a public hospital, a school admissions office or a pension payment long before it studies a party manifesto. Differences between expected and actual service quality can undermine confidence in government even when economic growth is positive. Conversely, a policy that appears to reduce expenditure in a national budget may shift costs onto families through travel time, unpaid care, debt or loss of wages. Social movements often brought those otherwise dispersed costs into public debate.

The Brazilian transport protests of June 2013 illustrate how scale can change political meaning. Initial actions in São Paulo opposed an increase in bus fares and were connected to long-term organizing for affordable transit. Police responses and viral images attracted wider attention; subsequent demonstrations expanded to encompass corruption, public investment and dissatisfaction with political elites. Different groups then offered conflicting interpretations of what the protests represented. A historian should resist drawing a direct uninterrupted line from the fare campaign to every later development in Brazilian politics. Movement coalitions can expand quickly and then fragment because participants do not agree about the next objective.

In Chile pensions condensed disputes over the meaning of work and social responsibility. A retiree who had spent decades in informal employment or caring for family could receive a small benefit under an account-based system. Activists sought more collective financing and reliable minimum support, while defenders emphasized the fiscal cost and importance of retaining accumulated savings. The 2025 pension legislation altered contribution rules and benefit arrangements through parliamentary negotiation. Its historical significance lies not only in what was enacted but also in what organizers considered insufficient and how new institutions would implement the change. A reform's effect on recipients requires more time and data than the date of enactment provides.

Housing adds a spatial dimension. High land prices, informal settlements, rent burdens, long commutes and uneven access to services can concentrate disadvantage at metropolitan edges. Residents may organize to secure water, sanitation and legal tenure rather than a national constitutional change. Informal-sector workers similarly face unstable earnings, limited employer contributions and exposure to sudden income loss. Their forms of collective action—street-vendor associations, neighborhood committees, transport unions and caregivers' groups—may not resemble the iconic demonstrations in a capital square but can be decisive for local governance.

The idea of economic citizenship therefore complements legal citizenship rather than replacing it. Individuals can formally possess a right to health care or education while lacking the resources to exercise it. Public finance imposes real trade-offs, making debates about who pays for reform unavoidable. Students should compare a protester's statement of need, a government's fiscal estimate and an official record of service performance as different types of evidence. A movement may win recognition for a problem, obtain a new benefit or reshape expectations without achieving its entire agenda. The relationship between public services and legitimacy is more historically precise than a general claim that people protested because they were 'angry.'

Section 110.15

Afro-Latin American, LGBTQ+, and Intersectional Movements

Afro-Brazilian organizing runs from the Unified Black Movement (1978) through the recognition of quilombo land rights in the 1988 Constitution to the 2012 quota law for federal universities. Black feminists such as Lélia Gonzalez argued that race, gender, and class operate together. Marielle Franco, a Black lesbian socialist councilwoman who grew up in Rio's Maré favelas, was elected in 2016 and criticized police killings. She and her driver Anderson Gomes were shot dead on 14 March 2018.

The two men who carried out the killing were convicted in 2024. On 25 February 2026, a panel of Brazil's Supreme Federal Court unanimously convicted Domingos and Chiquinho Brazão as the masterminds, each sentenced to 76 years and 3 months, and former Rio civil-police chief Rivaldo Barbosa to 18 years for corruption and obstruction; the court did not find his direct participation in the murders; a June 2026 report indicated that the panel upheld the sentences. LGBTQ+ campaigns won marriage equality in Argentina (2010), gender-identity rights (2012), and court rulings in Brazil, Colombia, and elsewhere. These movements share goals but not a single agenda, since race, sexuality, class, and territory produce different priorities.

Afro-Latin American movements emerged from histories of enslavement, emancipation and persistent inequalities that did not end when constitutions declared equality. In Brazil the Movimento Negro Unificado and subsequent networks challenged discriminatory policing, limited educational opportunities and the absence of Black perspectives in national history. Laws recognizing quilombola land rights and establishing university admissions policies marked important changes, but they also generated debates about criteria, implementation and the reach of affirmative action. Afro-descendant constituencies differ by region, class, religious tradition and political outlook; no single organization speaks for all of them.

Black feminist writers and organizers argued that gender inequality could not be measured without considering race and class. Domestic employment, unequal pay, reproductive health and police violence affected groups differently, making one-size-fits-all accounts of 'women's experience' inadequate. The intellectual contribution often described as intersectionality was a method of investigating connected institutions, not simply a catalogue of identities. Historians should analyze the concrete settings in which those connections operated: a public hospital, a neighborhood affected by police raids, a university admissions office or a campaign for access to land.

Marielle Franco's political career made several of these issues visible. Raised in the Maré complex of Rio de Janeiro, she worked on human rights and served on the city council, criticizing security policies and defending residents of favelas. Her killing together with driver Anderson Gomes in March 2018 provoked national and international mobilization. Public demonstrations sought justice while honoring the wider political causes associated with her work. It is essential to distinguish the convictions of the gunmen from the later judicial findings against other defendants, and to avoid attributing motives beyond what evidence and verdicts establish.

In February 2026 the Supreme Federal Court's First Panel convicted Domingos and Francisco 'Chiquinho' Brazão, assigning each a sentence of 76 years and three months in the murder case; other defendants received different sentences. For former police official Rivaldo Barbosa, the court reclassified the accusations and convicted him of corruption and obstruction rather than direct participation in the murders. The court rejected specified defense appeals in June 2026. These results demonstrate how a mobilization demanding accountability can intersect with lengthy investigations and judicial procedure. They do not prove that the underlying structures of organized political violence had been dismantled.

LGBTQ+ activism intersected with but was not identical to Afro-descendant or feminist organizing. Argentina's 2010 marriage-equality law and 2012 gender-identity law provide legislative examples; other countries used high-court judgments, administrative recognition or local ordinances. Transgender activists and gay and lesbian groups sometimes emphasized different needs concerning medical treatment, identity documents, employment and safety. Opponents invoked religious, family and legal arguments, while supporters appealed to equality and autonomy. The chapter should treat these as concrete institutional disputes and show how urban access to rights often differed from conditions in rural and marginal neighborhoods. The central lesson is that equality is negotiated across several, sometimes overlapping, public institutions.

Section 110.16

Digital Activism, Protest Networks, and Political Backlash

Hashtags, WhatsApp groups, and smartphone video lowered the cost of coordination and documentation: images of police ocular injuries in Chile and the viral spread of Las Tesis are examples. The same tools spread misinformation, as in false claims about the 2022 Chilean draft, and enabled surveillance, such as the Pegasus spyware found by researchers on phones of Mexican journalists and activists in 2017. Digital networks mobilize quickly but are fragile; unions, neighborhood assemblies, Indigenous organizations, and feminist networks sustain longer commitments.

Opposing mobilizations used similar methods. Evangelical and Catholic organizations led the "Save the Two Lives" marches in Argentina in 2018, the "Don't mess with my children" campaigns in Peru and Mexico against gender education, and conservative coalitions in Brazil. These countermovements were lawful, democratic participants whose arguments rested on moral convictions and defense of the family.

Digital tools changed the circulation of protest information, but not every aspect of power. Smartphones made it easier for participants to broadcast events without waiting for national television, and messaging services facilitated rapid coordination across neighborhoods. A video can challenge an official account of a particular arrest, but it cannot on its own establish the total number of incidents across a city or determine the intentions of every person shown. Researchers must verify time, location, sequence and whether footage has been edited. These evidentiary cautions became especially important during Chile's 2019 confrontations and Colombia's 2021 demonstrations, when competing images circulated faster than formal investigations.

Hashtags operate as organizing labels rather than membership registries. A person sharing Ni Una Menos material may support an anti-violence campaign without endorsing every proposed abortion reform. Likewise, a post about the Chilean constitution can be supportive, hostile, satirical or simply informational. Online prominence does not automatically reveal regional participation, gender composition or electoral strength. Platform algorithms privilege some forms of communication, and people without reliable access or who prefer local-language meetings may be underrepresented. The digital record therefore makes part of political life unusually visible while leaving other forms of organization obscure.

Traditional institutions remained essential. Unions could call stoppages and negotiate employment terms; community assemblies could deliberate over neighborhood priorities; professional associations supplied technical expertise on health care or law. Indigenous organizations depended on territorial decision-making and established authority structures that could not be replaced with national hashtags. Feminist activists carried proposals from local meetings into parliamentary committees. These relationships help explain why some campaigns survived after attention faded while others dispersed. Lasting influence required the ability to maintain contact, raise resources, produce credible evidence and deal with institutions that moved on legislative or administrative schedules.

Digital technology also enabled coercion. Surveillance and spyware disclosures in Mexico raised concerns about monitoring journalists and human-rights defenders. Individuals faced harassment and doxxing, including threats based on gender, race or sexual identity. False claims about Chile's constitutional draft traveled alongside accurate criticisms, making an independent reading of the legal text particularly important. Governments and movement organizers faced different incentives to highlight, suppress or interpret evidence. None of these problems is unique to one ideology or one country, and ethical analysis should distinguish proven surveillance practices from allegations requiring further investigation.

Countermovements used many of the same communication channels and public-space tactics. Religious communities, secular conservatives, neighborhood groups and professional organizations disputed reproductive reform, gender education and constitutional proposals. Their positions differed: some supported limited exceptions to abortion restrictions, some opposed all liberalization, and others focused on implementation or parental authority. The existence of these movements underlines the democratic possibility of public disagreement. The line between legitimate opposition and unlawful suppression is crossed when actors threaten participants, falsify evidence, or use coercion against peaceful political activity. Studying the full field of mobilization is more informative than imagining that digital tools naturally advance only one set of values.

Section 110.17

When Protest Becomes Law: Institutional Pathways to Reform

Mobilization reached law by different routes. In Argentina it passed through the legislature after a defeat. In Colombia and Mexico, strategic litigation by feminist coalitions produced judicial decisions. In Chile, mass protest was channeled through a negotiated agreement and referendums. In Ecuador, mobilization yielded negotiated reversals, and the Constitutional Court in April 2021 decriminalized abortion in rape cases; a popular vote on Yasuní followed in 2023.

Legal victory does not guarantee equal access. Rights depend on budgets, trained providers, rural clinics, provincial or state compliance, and the use of conscientious objection. Reforms by court can be difficult to enforce, reforms by statute can be repealed, and negotiated settlements can be reversed after elections. A movement's task therefore continues after its victory.

The path from collective action to enforceable policy is rarely direct. Demonstrations can change the costs of political inaction by attracting media attention, revealing organized constituencies and encouraging legislators to reconsider their positions. But a campaign that fills streets may fail in a legislature where coalition partners cannot agree on a bill. Similarly, a movement can win a favorable judgment yet confront administrators who lack funding or interpret the decision narrowly. These outcomes should not be grouped as simply 'victory' or 'defeat.' Different institutions create distinct opportunities and constraints for organized citizens.

Argentina's national abortion law illustrates a legislative path. Years of advocacy produced legal text and institutional allies, then a 2018 defeat, followed by a new coalition and successful votes in 2020. The resulting statute established operational duties for health providers, making subsequent administrative implementation part of the same political history. Colombia and Mexico illustrate judicial paths in which advocates developed constitutional arguments and brought cases that altered the legal standards governing punishment and access. Courts do not function as legislatures: their holdings answer defined legal questions, while agencies and later rulings determine much of their practical reach.

Chile's post-2019 pathway differed because the political agreement offered a process to rewrite the basic constitutional framework. Referendums gave voters binding opportunities to reject the specific texts produced. The first process generated an innovative convention and ambitious proposals, but its final product failed; the second, with different composition and priorities, also failed. This demonstrates that a movement can win institutional recognition of its demand for change without being able to secure acceptance of a comprehensive solution. A constitution also differs from an ordinary law: voters may agree with some social rights and disagree with the proposed distribution of state powers.

Ecuador offers examples of negotiated reversal and direct democracy. Road blockades compelled governments to bargain over fuel policy, while the Yasuní vote created a different kind of public instruction about extraction. Negotiation involves the authority of representatives and the risk that agreements later prove difficult to implement. A referendum has a specified electorate and ballot question but cannot predict the practical consequences of compliance. In either case the formal outcome must be followed into the agencies, budgets and courts responsible for carrying it out.

Comparative assessment should therefore specify indicators. Was a law passed, a criminal penalty removed, a benefit funded, a constitutional draft adopted, or a prisoner released? Did the decision affect rural populations as well as capital cities? Did the policy survive an election or generate a new controversy? These questions turn movement history into a study of public institutions over time. They also prevent the mistaken assumption that protest automatically commands democratic legitimacy or that an electoral defeat erases the historical significance of the issues a movement raised.

Section 110.18

Polarization, Countermovements, and the Limits of Consensus

Disagreement concerned the moral status of the fetus, the role of religion, the legitimacy of unelected courts, Indigenous autonomy and national unity, and public security. Critics of the Chilean drafts argued that the first was excessively long and partisan and that the second was insufficiently inclusive. Rural residents and forestry workers in the Araucanía described fear of violence. Defenders of abortion reform argued that clandestine abortion harmed poor women; opponents argued for legal protection of unborn life and for democratic debate.

A distinction is necessary between political disagreement and efforts to suppress lawful participation. Documented excessive force against protesters, killings of defenders, and criminalization of dissent are rights violations regardless of the cause, and so are attacks on people and property by armed actors. Fair treatment of competing views does not require equating these.

Political disagreement is not an interruption of social-movement history; it is one of the processes that movements generate. A campaign demanding new rights may draw supporters who see an overdue correction of injustice and opponents who fear a loss of values, resources or legal certainty. On abortion, some defended autonomy and argued that criminalization placed disproportionate burdens on poor women. Opponents argued that law should protect prenatal life and that conscience or religious liberty should be respected. Those arguments were developed in churches, courts, congresses and civil society, not just on social media. Neither side should be caricatured as having no ethical reasoning.

The institutional forum changes what kind of argument carries authority. Legislators balance competing interests and party commitments; judges apply constitutional provisions to particular legal controversies; public-health agencies must provide services under the law; referendums reduce complex proposals to a ballot choice. Actors who lose in one forum may seek another. After the Argentine law passed, opponents could advocate repeal or stricter implementation conditions. After Colombia's court ruling, debate continued about providers' obligations and public health. This is an ordinary feature of pluralist politics, although attempts to intimidate health workers or patients raise a different problem from lawful advocacy.

Chile's two constitutional campaigns illustrate how constitutional disagreement can be broader than left against right. Citizens who supported replacing the 1980 charter could oppose features of the 2022 text, and voters who rejected that draft could also oppose the more conservative 2023 version. Different coalitions made distinct appeals about national identity, political representation, gender, property and social welfare. Survey interpretation must be cautious: a vote against a constitutional package is not an item-by-item survey, and every campaign contains strategic messaging. Nevertheless, the election outcomes imposed binding political constraints on the proposed texts.

Indigenous autonomy and public security present another contested field. State officials and residents have legitimate interests in protection from violent attacks; Indigenous communities have legitimate interests in land claims, equal treatment and lawful consultation. People can disagree about proposed jurisdictional autonomy without endorsing discrimination. At the same time, particular allegations of police misconduct or violence by private actors must be evaluated according to evidence and legal process. The moral seriousness of a movement's cause does not establish the innocence of any participant, just as criminal allegations against individuals do not establish the guilt of a community.

A workable analytical framework separates disagreement, discrimination, coercion and adjudicated wrongdoing. Demonstrations and counterdemonstrations are ordinarily protected participation. Violence, threats and interference with lawful assembly require independent scrutiny regardless of who commits them. Court convictions establish particular legal findings, while unresolved allegations remain contested. These distinctions preserve fairness without creating false equivalence between documented abuses and unsupported claims. They also clarify the historian's task: explain why opposing groups mobilized, what they demanded, which institutions responded and how evidence supports—or fails to support—conclusions about the outcomes.

Argentina's Law 27.610 had not been repealed, although access conditions and national health policies were disputed. Changes in medicine procurement or clinic staffing require dated administrative and health-sector evidence; a report of reduced provision in one period should not be treated as proof that legal entitlement disappeared. The February 5, 2024 repeal initiative filed by deputy Rocío Bonacci is a documented parliamentary proposal, not evidence that the executive repealed the law. Later proposals should be identified by their own docket and date rather than merged with the 2024 filing.

Argentina's decision to sign the Geneva Consensus Declaration in August 2026 also requires a careful distinction. The declaration is an international political statement; it does not itself amend the country's abortion statute or erase existing court responsibilities. The government, individual legislators, opposition parties, health professionals and feminist organizations occupied different institutional positions and sometimes disagreed even within larger political alignments. For students, the relevant questions concern the legal force of an instrument, its participants and what domestic implementing action would be necessary to change patient rights.

Colombia's 2022 judicial decision remained a foundation for reproductive-rights litigation and service delivery, but implementation disputes could continue under future administrations. Mexico's federal constitutional decisions likewise required attention to state legislation and health institutions. Regional campaigns did not move in a single direction: in some states court rulings advanced rights while in others legislative restrictions and administrative barriers remained central concerns. A reliable summary through 2026 should date legal status and avoid projecting current debates into permanent victory or loss.

Chile's constitutional process had produced two rejected drafts and no accepted replacement by the cutoff. The 2025 pension reform and later debates about education represented separate institutional arenas in which social demands continued. The Kast government, inaugurated in March 2026, created a changed political environment for movements and countermovements. Indigenous land and security policy remained contested; documentation should distinguish proposals, consultations, enacted laws and police operations. The return of student demonstrations showed that the defeat of constitutional texts did not end organized youth participation.

Ecuador's 2025 fuel protests ended their highway blockade phase in October of that year, leaving questions about subsidies and Indigenous representation unresolved. Environmental disputes over Yasuní and other projects continued to test the effectiveness of court decisions, referendums and consultation procedures. Brazil's February 2026 convictions in the Marielle Franco case, followed by rejected specified appeals in June, represented a significant judicial outcome without proving that political violence or inequality had ceased. The legacy of Berta Cáceres likewise required distinguishing completed convictions from ongoing demands for further accountability. The record through late 2026 reveals partial changes, persistent conflict and new political alignments, not a settled destination.

Review and Historical Synthesis: Citizenship from Below

The movements studied here shared a claim to be included in defining citizenship, but their outcomes depended on political opportunity, organizational depth, access to institutions, coalitions, and the strength of countermovements. Argentina's feminist coalition won law after losing once; Colombian and Mexican activists won through courts; Chilean protesters opened a constitutional process that voters rejected twice; Indigenous organizations used disruption to bargain; environmental defenders linked local survival to international law at high personal cost.

Together these cases show that protest can change agendas and sometimes law, but cannot by itself build stable majorities or guarantee enforcement. Chapter 111 turns to Central America after its civil wars, where postwar institutions, gangs, migration, and security policy posed different questions about the state's capacity to protect its citizens.

The comparative analysis returns to the distinction among visibility, institutional change and implementation. Ni Una Menos made gender violence a national and transnational issue, while the Green Wave built a legal campaign capable of surviving a legislative defeat. Chile's 2019 demonstrations changed the political agenda so profoundly that parties agreed to a constitutional pathway, yet voters rejected two resulting drafts. Ecuadorian Indigenous organizations demonstrated the negotiating power of territorially embedded associations; environmental defenders showed how costly such participation could become. No single definition of success captures these different historical trajectories.

Organization mattered as much as public enthusiasm. Feminist networks linked lawyers, physicians, teachers and students to legislative committees. Chilean students developed political leaders and coalition-building skills years before the 2019 uprising. CONAIE mobilized through community and regional bodies rather than relying only on party structures. Environmental organizations linked local experience with international legal standards and human-rights advocacy. These varied institutions gave protests memory, continuity and resources. They also introduced internal disputes about strategy, electoral participation and the acceptable terms of negotiation.

Political opportunity mattered too. A receptive president and a narrow parliamentary majority made possible Argentina's 2020 statute; constitutional litigation provided a different opening in Colombia and Mexico. Chile's referendums showed the veto power of a broad electorate faced with comprehensive legal packages. Ecuador's road blockades created pressure for bargaining, while judicial institutions determined the legal significance of territorial and environmental claims. Each setting raises questions about representation: whom do activists speak for, whom do elected bodies answer to, and who bears the costs of an outcome? None can be settled solely by counting demonstrators or displaying a legal text.

A historical explanation must also preserve the agency of opponents. Religious organizations, skeptical voters, business associations, public officials and residents affected by disruptions could resist particular proposals for principled or material reasons. Their concerns sometimes changed the content of laws or prevented consensus. Countermobilization is not evidence that movements were meaningless; it demonstrates that citizenship is contested among multiple organized constituencies. Yet the protection of lawful participation places limits on all sides. Torture, threats, attacks on civilians and unlawful repression remain subjects for evidence-based accountability, not matters to be justified by agreement with a cause.

Finally, social rights are not self-executing. An enacted law needs trained professionals and accessible institutions; a favorable court judgment needs compliance; a constitutional right requires government capacity and often funding; a negotiated agreement needs monitoring. The deepest continuity across the movements is the recurring effort to translate claims about dignity, equality, identity and material security into practical state obligations. Their histories through November 2026 remain open, but their effect on the public vocabulary of citizenship is established. Chapter Summary

The democratization of Latin American states expanded electoral competition without resolving all social inequalities. Campaigns emerging in the 2010s therefore pressed for forms of recognition, security and material access that elections alone did not deliver. Movements differed substantially by national political institutions, geography and membership.

Ni Una Menos converted long-standing feminist concerns about gender-based killings into visible claims of public accountability. Its significance arose both from mass mobilization and from older networks of organizers, lawyers, journalists and human-rights advocates. The movement demonstrated how violence in homes and neighborhoods could become a national political question.

The Green Wave translated transnational feminist organizing into diverse domestic legal pathways. In Argentina a legislative coalition achieved Law 27.610 after the Senate rejected an earlier bill in 2018. In Colombia and Mexico constitutional litigation played central roles, while Uruguay and Chile adopted different statutes with different legal scope.

Comparing laws requires distinguishing a right declared by a court, a prohibition removed by statute, duties imposed on health institutions and the actual ability of patients to obtain care. Administrative capacity, geography and political opposition affect implementation even after national legal change.

Chile's October 2019 social explosion arose from a transit-fare dispute that interacted with deeper grievances over pensions, education, debt and legitimacy. Mass peaceful participation coincided with violence and documented human-rights violations. The government's responsibility to preserve public order did not remove its obligation to protect peaceful demonstrators.

The constitutional agreement of November 2019 and October 2020 plebiscite created an institutional path to change. The 2022 and 2023 referendums subsequently rejected two different proposed texts. Those decisions illustrate the difference between supporting the process of constitutional renewal and approving particular institutional arrangements.

Mapuche movements connected land, identity and consultation to the history of Chilean territorial incorporation. Ecuadorian Indigenous organizations repeatedly forced national negotiations over fuel and economic policy. Both cases demonstrate organizational depth as well as internal diversity and tensions concerning law and security.

Environmental defenders confronted disputed mining, hydroelectric and petroleum projects, often under considerable personal risk. The Sarayaku judgment, ILO Convention 169, Yasuní referendum and Escazú Agreement show distinct forms of rights, consultation and public participation. Legal documents must be followed by evidence of implementation.

Students, informal workers, pensioners and urban residents mobilized around the everyday cost of education, transportation, health care and housing. Social movements linked formal citizenship to practical access to public services. Their objectives could produce statutory reform without producing a broad consensus on the economic model.

Afro-Latin American, Black feminist and LGBTQ+ organizations challenged forms of exclusion that were not reducible to a single class or national identity. The Marielle Franco case demonstrated the importance of distinguishing public accusations, criminal convictions and continuing questions about structural violence.

By November 16, 2026 the overall record was neither uninterrupted progress nor decisive defeat. A law could survive new opposition, a constitutional initiative could fail, a court conviction could bring accountability without ending systemic harm, and a movement could adapt its tactics. The durable historical issue is how organized citizens negotiate the meaning and enforcement of public obligations.

Chapter Reflection

These movements show that citizenship is contested from below as well as defined by constitutions. Their victories came through legislatures, courts, negotiations, and referendums, and their setbacks came from the same institutions.

The movements shaped later politics: Chile's former student leaders governed, and countermovements and new governments responded. Many questions remain open as of November 2026.
