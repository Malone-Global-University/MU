Chapter Opening: La Higuera, 9 October 1967

On 9 October 1967, in a small schoolhouse at La Higuera in southeastern Bolivia, Bolivian army officers held a prisoner captured the previous day. By that afternoon he was dead, killed on orders that originated with Bolivian military authorities.

The prisoner was Ernesto “Che” Guevara. Nine years earlier he had helped bring down the Batista dictatorship in Cuba. Now he led a small, isolated force in a country where the political support he expected had not appeared. The distance between Cuba’s victory in 1959 and Bolivia’s defeat in 1967 frames this chapter.

The chapter asks why the Cuban Revolution encouraged a generation of Latin American revolutionaries to pursue armed struggle, and how national political conditions, popular participation, military power, foreign intervention, and rival strategies shaped their successes and failures. It separates inspiration from command: some movements received Cuban training or aid, while others grew from local conflicts and borrowed selectively.

The evidence is uneven. It includes Guevara’s own writings and diaries, movement manifestos, declassified U.S. documents, conference records, memoirs, and later scholarship. Each carries a purpose: guerrilla texts advocate, military and intelligence records serve governments, and memoirs are shaped by defeat. Chapter 81 covers Cuba’s internal transformation and the 1961–62 crises; Chapter 83 covers Colombia; Chapters 84–91 cover the dictatorships and Central American wars.

Learning Objectives and Guiding Questions

Students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Cuban victory changed political expectations across Latin America.
  2. Analyze Guevara as a political thinker and organizer.
  3. Explain foco theory and distinguish Guevara’s views from Debray’s.
  4. Compare guerrilla movements in Guatemala, Venezuela, and Peru.
  5. Distinguish Cuban inspiration, support, and direction.
  6. Trace Guevara’s Argentine, Congo, and Bolivian ventures and explain their failure.
  7. Contrast rural and urban insurgency in Uruguay, Brazil, and Argentina.
  8. Assess women’s and civilians’ roles and the human costs of political violence.
  9. Evaluate U.S. policy and its interaction with Latin American governments.
  10. Distinguish the historical Guevara from his later image.

Guiding Historical Questions

  1. Why did the Cuban example persuade some groups, but not others, to take up arms?
  2. Did Cuba command these movements, or did they use Cuba?
  3. Why did strategies that worked in one place fail elsewhere?
  4. What separates support for reform, support for revolution, and joining an armed group?
  5. How did governments, with and without U.S. help, defeat insurgencies?
  6. How reliable are sources produced by insurgents, militaries, and intelligence services?
  7. Why did Guevara’s death mark a turning point without ending revolutionary movements?

Historical Context

By 1959 much of Latin America combined deep inequality, concentrated landholding, limited political participation, and powerful militaries. Leftist politics was divided among reformers, Soviet-aligned communist parties that favored patient organization, nationalist movements, and dissident students and officers. Guatemala’s 1954 overthrow of Jacobo Árbenz (Chapter 79) taught many that peaceful reform could be crushed. Bolivia’s 1952 revolution (Chapter 78) showed that revolution could also produce an unstable settlement. Cuba’s victory (Chapter 80) arrived in this setting, and the Cold War shaped how every actor interpreted it.

Methodological boundary: “Cuban influence” can mean the prestige of a successful revolution, personal political relationships, training, material support, or direct operational direction. Each is a separate historical claim requiring separate evidence. The 1959–1973 core chronology is kept distinct from the later Colombian and Central American civil wars and from military-dictatorship histories treated in subsequent chapters.

Section 82.1

82.1 The Cuban Revolution as a Continental Political Example

January 1959 persuaded many Latin Americans that a well-armed, U.S.-supported dictatorship could be defeated. The victory appealed to students, intellectuals, radical nationalists, labor activists, and rural organizers. It also complicated life for the left: constitutional reformists, communist parties, revolutionary nationalists, and new armed activists read Cuba differently. Cuba became a symbol well before Havana built systematic ties with foreign movements, and that symbolic power is why inspiration must not be read as direction.

The Cuban precedent was powerful because it contradicted prevailing assumptions about the resilience of established governments. Fulgencio Batista possessed an army, police apparatus, recognized diplomatic relations, and access to military supplies, yet his regime collapsed at the end of 1958. The guerrillas in the Sierra Maestra were only one component of that outcome. Urban underground networks, student demonstrations, labor unrest, disaffection within the armed forces, and the dictatorship's own loss of legitimacy contributed to victory. To present January 1959 as the triumph of a handful of armed men over an otherwise stable state would be to convert a complicated social and political history into a reassuring legend. Indeed, the difference between what happened in Cuba and what outsiders believed had happened became a central problem for the revolutionary movements of the following decade.

The chronology of Cuban consolidation also mattered. Revolutionary unity against Batista did not survive intact after his fall. The government pursued land reform, nationalizations, political trials, and institutional reorganization, while relations with Washington deteriorated and relations with Moscow intensified. Some admirers elsewhere saw an example of social justice pursued against entrenched economic power; opponents saw confiscation, repression, and an increasingly one-party political system. Revolutionary groups had to decide whether they sought Cuba's social program, its armed road to power, its international alignment, or some combination. An activist sympathetic to agrarian reform could nevertheless reject the imprisonment of opponents or the substitution of armed command for electoral choice. Those distinctions became important when Cuban language was used in countries with functioning elections as well as in countries ruled by military dictators.

The demonstration effect spread through travel, radio, student associations, newspapers, exile communities, and personal contacts. Journalists and delegations visited Havana; young intellectuals read Guevara and Castro; local organizers argued about whether mountain guerrillas could overcome armies in societies different from Cuba. Yet information circulated selectively. The victory's dramatic closing episode attracted more attention than several years of organizing in towns, the widening opposition to Batista, and decisions by different sectors of Cuban society. Later militants sometimes assumed that the essential ingredient was determination rather than a favorable political coalition. This selectivity was not unique to revolutionaries: anti-communist officials also reduced complex local grievances to an externally directed Cuban conspiracy.

The political range was broad. Some governments attempted reform to avert insurrection. Communist parties divided between those emphasizing trade unions and electoral work and militants who considered gradualism exhausted. Christian-democratic, nationalist, and social-democratic movements drew different conclusions. Cuba did not create Latin America's land inequality, dependent economies, or disputes over representation; it made an armed outcome appear possible within that setting. The chapter therefore treats the Cuban Revolution less as the singular cause of subsequent uprisings than as a transnational event that changed how actors evaluated existing national conflicts and their possible futures.

This distinction also guards against retrospective inevitability. A young student in Caracas in 1961, a Guatemalan officer after 1960, and an Argentine Peronist confronting proscription could all invoke Havana while pursuing projects grounded in different institutions. Their decisions require country-specific explanation. Some movements obtained Cuban instruction or assistance, but others appropriated the example without requesting permission. The test of Cuban influence is documentary evidence of actual relationships, not simply the resemblance of slogans or the presence of a portrait of Guevara.

Section 82.2

82.2 Che Guevara: Internationalism, Marxism, and National Liberation

Born in Rosario, Argentina, in 1928, Guevara trained as a physician and traveled through Latin America. He witnessed Guatemala’s 1954 overthrow, which reinforced his belief that reformist governments could not survive U.S.-backed opposition. In Cuba he held posts in the revolutionary government, including in industry and banking. He argued that imperialism and economic dependence linked national liberation to international socialism, and he stressed moral commitment and sacrifice. His period in government also involved coercion and the problems of administration. By 1965 he resolved to fight abroad, leaving public office to do so.

Guevara's life before Cuba connected several experiences that later appeared in his political thinking. Travel through South America exposed him to unequal landholding, mining labor, Indigenous marginalization, and governments dependent on commodity exports. His response to the 1954 overthrow of Jacobo Árbenz in Guatemala was especially consequential: he concluded that institutional reform could be defeated by a coalition of domestic opponents and foreign power even when a government had won office legally. This conclusion should be understood as Guevara's interpretation, not as proof that no peaceful reform could succeed in Latin America. It helped explain his subsequent preference for revolutionary transformation and his distrust of U.S. declarations of hemispheric partnership.

In Mexico Guevara met Fidel Castro and joined the expedition that sailed aboard the Granma in late 1956. His early function as a physician did not exclude combat or command; he became one of the rebel army's leading officers. Later accounts of his competence were shaped both by the prestige of victory and by the political needs of a new government. His own written descriptions placed unusual weight on discipline, sacrifice, and the formation of a revolutionary character. But revolutionary discipline could include coercion, compulsory labor, and punishment for perceived betrayal. A scholarly biography must consider these alongside his intellectual interests and his capacity to inspire collaborators. Heroic and prosecutorial portraits alike are inadequate when they treat moral evaluation as a replacement for historical investigation.

Between 1959 and 1965 Guevara became more than a guerrilla commander. He served in important economic posts, including leadership of the National Bank and the Ministry of Industries. He participated in debates over planning, industrialization, financial incentives, and the proper role of consciousness in socialist economic development. His advocacy of moral rather than predominantly material incentives reflected a conviction that the revolution should produce different social relations, not merely redistribute profits. Economic shortages, a constrained industrial base, and increasingly close Soviet ties presented practical limits to his vision. These experiences influenced his internationalism: he believed national revolutions could be threatened by dependence on world markets and great powers, including sometimes by the priorities of socialist allies.

His international speeches did not simply repeat Soviet policy. In Algiers in February 1965, he criticized inequitable economic relations between socialist and poorer countries, even while insisting on solidarity among anti-imperialist movements. The address demonstrates a tension between Cuba's material reliance on the Soviet bloc and Guevara's broader aspiration for revolutionary independence. It does not establish that Guevara had already broken decisively with Castro or with every aspect of Cuban state policy. Interpretations of his departure in 1965 differ, drawing on the uneven survival of Cuban internal records, later testimony, and his farewell letter.

Guevara's continental vision developed alongside a personal belief that a revolutionary leader should accept direct physical risk. He left ministerial office and eventually pursued campaigns abroad rather than remaining the symbolic embodiment of Cuba's success. The decision made his ideas unusually susceptible to empirical evaluation: military outcomes in the Congo and Bolivia could now be measured against the political assumptions of his writings. A historian should ask which failures stemmed from local conditions, which from faulty information, which from political disputes, and which from Guevara's own decisions. It is equally necessary to examine the experiences of the people among whom he fought, whose interests cannot be inferred automatically from his declared intentions.

Section 82.3

82.3 Guerrilla Warfare (1960), Foquismo, and Revolutionary Insurgency

In La guerra de guerrillas (1960) Guevara argued that popular forces could defeat a regular army, that revolutionaries need not wait for every condition to mature because an insurrectionary nucleus could create them, and that rural terrain was the main arena. These claims were modified in his later writings, so foco theory was not a fixed doctrine. Régis Debray’s Revolution in the Revolution? (1967) gave it a sharper and more rigid form, subordinating party to guerrilla vanguard. The practical limits were political legitimacy, local support, logistics, and organization, which the model tended to underestimate.

The famous three propositions associated with Guevara's 1960 book emerged from a particular revolutionary history. They suggested that popular forces could defeat an army, that insurgents might help create revolutionary conditions rather than merely wait for them, and that the countryside offered decisive terrain in much of underdeveloped Latin America. His text nonetheless assumed interaction with a population rather than the magical effect of armed will. Guerrillas needed supplies, information, safe relations with residents, and a cause that could attract wider support. The proposition that a small force could begin a process was not equivalent to saying that all prerequisites were irrelevant. Later summaries often erased the qualifications and converted an argument about political initiative into a promise of automatic victory.

The term foquismo itself needs historical care. A foco literally signifies a focus or nucleus; scholars have used the word to group a family of insurgent theories, but Guevara's ideas evolved between 1960 and his last campaigns. Matt D. Childs traces changes in his writings in relation to Cuban political circumstances and international strategy. Régis Debray's 1967 Revolution in the Revolution? presented a sharpened case for the guerrilla nucleus as the leading political institution. Debray's interpretation sometimes displaced the role of existing parties, urban networks, and organized workers more radically than Guevara's earlier positions. Calling every Latin American insurgent a foquista therefore conceals real disagreements over party leadership, urban work, peasant recruitment, and the timing of armed action.

There were historical reasons the model appealed to activists. Electoral exclusion, military coups, repression of labor movements, and the repeated failure of reform proposals could make the ordinary political system appear closed. Organizers who had watched the fall of Árbenz or the marginalization of communist parties might conclude that patient coalition-building only exposed their supporters to surveillance. They also saw in Cuba a way to act without waiting for a revolutionary majority that might never materialize. These were politically intelligible judgments, but intelligibility did not guarantee accuracy. A regime capable of absorbing protest, an army with strong rural intelligence, or a countryside with different property relationships could prevent a foco from generating the coalition it needed.

The contrast between a theoretical model and the record of campaigns reveals a recurring problem of evidence. Guerrilla memoirs often highlighted courage and betrayal, while state communiqués emphasized public order and depicted rebels as foreign agents. Accounts written after defeat sometimes explained failure by reference to insufficient peasant consciousness or incompetent local leadership. Such conclusions may be partly right, yet they can also protect the theory from criticism: any failure becomes an argument that the correct model was not properly applied. Comparative history instead asks what occurred across several cases even when the participants themselves interpreted their experience differently.

A rigorous comparison must therefore analyze at least four independent variables: the government's political legitimacy, established networks of social organization, the capacity and conduct of security forces, and relations between insurgents and civilians. Terrain matters but cannot substitute for politics. The same mountain setting may protect a small force for months while preventing recruitment and resupply. A city may offer publicity and concealment while exposing members to surveillance. The chapter returns to these variables repeatedly so that Cuba's success is not treated as a universal formula and later failures are not reduced to a single mistake.

Section 82.4

82.4 Students, Workers, Peasants, Communist Parties, and Recruitment

Insurgents came mainly from universities, dissident officers, labor activists, party dissidents, and some rural organizers, rarely from the poorest peasants. Soviet-oriented communist parties often preferred electoral and union work and clashed with younger militants. Closed electoral systems, land concentration, repression, and disappointed reform raised the appeal of arms. Students should separate three questions: why people backed reform, why some chose revolutionary politics, and why only a few took up arms. Poverty alone does not answer any of them.

Revolutionary participation was shaped by organizations that existed long before guerrilla bands appeared. University federations offered meeting spaces and print networks; trade unions carried grievances over wages and repression; campesino associations organized disputes over tenancy, access to land, and government services. These networks could sustain legal mobilization without endorsing armed insurrection. Where governments permitted elections, some activists preferred party campaigns or strikes because they promised broader participation and less exposure to violence. Where soldiers overthrew elected governments or authorities banned established parties, younger militants increasingly questioned whether those approaches remained possible. The same structural inequality could therefore generate different political choices in neighboring countries.

The social composition of guerrilla leadership is particularly revealing. Many prominent organizers were students, educated professionals, party dissidents, journalists, or former officers with the ability to travel, read political literature, and maintain transnational contacts. Rural recruits were essential in some campaigns but frequently fewer than organizers had predicted. The rural poor were not a homogeneous audience. Tenants, smallholders, landless workers, Indigenous communities, and prosperous farmers had different interests and prior experiences with state institutions. A campesino who supported land redistribution might reasonably fear that helping an unknown armed column would bring reprisals, jeopardize a harvest, or damage a household's relations with neighboring communities.

Communist parties were similarly varied. Some followed international strategies shaped by Soviet diplomacy, but they also had national histories of labor organizing, clandestine survival, and electoral alliances. Conflict with younger guerrillas often involved actual disagreements over the most effective way to transform society. Party leaders might ask who would represent workers if an isolated foco provoked mass repression; militants might answer that waiting for a revolutionary situation indefinitely preserved the status quo. These disputes could fracture campaigns over control of resources, political messages, and relations with local supporters. The Bolivian confrontation between Guevara's circle and Mario Monje was therefore an extreme example of a wider dilemma, not an inexplicable quarrel between personalities.

Gender altered both participation and remembrance. Women organized clandestine communication, provided medical assistance, raised funds, forged political relationships, and sometimes fought as armed members. Yet movement narratives frequently celebrated male commanders, and security records often described women primarily as companions or suspects. Family care, pregnancy, access to housing, and gender-specific exposure to interrogation also shaped who could participate and at what personal cost. To recover these experiences historians must read memoirs against testimonies, court files, photographs, and later oral histories rather than assuming that a woman absent from a command roster played no historical role.

Recruitment itself was a process, not an automatic response to poverty. People moved between sympathy, logistical assistance, underground membership, armed action, disillusionment, and withdrawal. Each step involved different risks and commitments. A social-history approach asks how individuals weighed family responsibilities, state repression, religious or national identities, and their assessment of political alternatives. Such distinctions are essential for evaluating the claim that revolutionary movements acted on behalf of the people: political representation must be demonstrated through participation and accountability, not merely asserted by those who carried weapons.

Section 82.5

82.5 Guatemala: MR-13, the FAR, and Renewed Armed Resistance

After Árbenz’s fall, a rebellion by junior officers against President Ydígoras Fuentes on 13 November 1960 failed but produced the Movimiento Revolucionario 13 de Noviembre (MR-13) under Marco Antonio Yon Sosa. Luis Augusto Turcios Lima helped form the Fuerzas Armadas Rebeldes (FAR) in 1962, linked with the Guatemalan Labor Party (PGT) and students. Cuba’s influence was real but the grievances were Guatemalan. Counterinsurgency, factional division, and thin ties to rural communities weakened the groups. The civil war proper and its atrocities belong to Chapter 91.

Guatemala's early armed movements arose from an exceptionally contentious national history. The 1944 revolution had expanded labor and political participation, and the agrarian reform of Jacobo Árbenz challenged concentrated landownership before his overthrow in 1954. The resulting government of Carlos Castillo Armas and its successors reversed important reforms and suppressed leftist politics. Yet the origins of the MR-13 were not simply a spontaneous rural uprising. Young officers revolted against President Miguel Ydígoras Fuentes on 13 November 1960 amid discontent within the armed forces and anger at the government's course. After defeat, surviving rebels sought political allies, developed rural bases, and increasingly embraced revolutionary language. Their movement crossed the boundary from military dissent into a wider struggle over social and political authority.

Marco Antonio Yon Sosa, Luis Augusto Turcios Lima, and other organizers faced difficult questions of leadership and strategy. The Guatemalan Labor Party, known as the PGT, possessed experience in clandestine political organization, while student groups brought contacts in the capital. A coalition called the Fuerzas Armadas Rebeldes took shape in December 1962, with more public organization during 1963. But this union did not erase the independence of its components. Disputes arose over the division between political and military authority, the relation of existing parties to armed fronts, and how much emphasis to place on rural campaigns. The names MR-13 and FAR accordingly denote changing organizational relationships rather than an unbroken chain of command.

The Sierra de las Minas, Izabal, and eastern Guatemala offered terrain in which small units could conceal themselves, but the government's presence along roads and among local officials limited the insurgency's freedom. Peasants had grievances over land and labor, but many feared repression or found guerrilla political programs distant from immediate concerns. Organizers struggled to build reliable communications among different fronts. The movement's promise of national revolution could be undermined by the practical challenge of sustaining a small armed group without the local institutions that would make its political claims credible. It is misleading to infer peasant allegiance from the physical location of guerrillas in a rural district.

The rupture between Yon Sosa and Turcios Lima became explicit in the mid-1960s. Turcios Lima's March 1965 open letter has significance precisely because it documents an internal argument over leadership and the direction of the struggle. The letter should be studied through the edited reproduction discussed by Arturo Taracena Arriola, with its provenance made clear; it must not be assigned invented words. When Turcios died in an automobile accident in October 1966, the insurgency lost an organizer whose ability to mediate among factions had already been tested. Government campaigns intensified in the east, and the wider conflict entered a new phase. Chapter 91 will trace Guatemala's prolonged civil war and mass atrocities; this section explains how its earlier revolutionary organizations formed, divided, and failed to achieve a rapid Cuban-style victory.

Section 82.6

82.6 Venezuela: MIR, Communists, the FALN, and Democratic Revolution

After Pérez Jiménez fell in 1958, the Punto Fijo settlement and President Rómulo Betancourt built a contested electoral democracy. The Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria (MIR) broke from the governing party, and with the Communist Party it backed the Fuerzas Armadas de Liberación Nacional (FALN). The May 1962 Carúpano and June 1962 Puerto Cabello uprisings were military rebellions, distinct from the guerrilla and urban campaigns that followed. Government repression, an electorate that still voted, and divisions on the left led to decline. The Cuban model struggled where a restrictive but functioning electoral system existed.

Venezuela demonstrated a particularly important limit to revolutionary imitation: the existence of a competitive, if unequal and contested, electoral system. The overthrow of Marcos Pérez Jiménez in 1958 opened a political settlement associated with the Punto Fijo agreement among major civilian parties. Rómulo Betancourt's government faced serious social demands, factional exclusion, and Cold War tensions, but many Venezuelans regarded elections and party participation as meaningful achievements after dictatorship. An insurgency seeking to overthrow such a government had to explain why armed action was more legitimate than available constitutional competition. This did not make the system perfectly democratic; it made the politics of revolutionary recruitment very different from Batista's Cuba.

The Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria emerged from a division within the governing Acción Democrática, while the Communist Party of Venezuela possessed its own electoral and organizational history. Radical militants interpreted inequalities and government repression as evidence that democratic reform was inadequate. Military dissidents participated in the Carúpano revolt on 4 May 1962 and the Puerto Cabello revolt on 2 June, but these failed barracks uprisings should not be confused with all subsequent rural guerrilla operations. After their defeat, parts of the left shifted toward rural insurgency under arrangements associated with the FALN, while other activists favored urban clandestine work. Contemporary party records and later testimonies reveal disagreements over whether military initiative should precede the construction of a political majority.

The Venezuelan state combined armed suppression with continuing electoral competition. Military operations, arrests, and security surveillance damaged insurgent formations, sometimes accompanied by abuses against civilians and political prisoners. Yet ordinary voters also had reasons to reject a new civil war, and legal leftist politicians could argue that elections remained preferable to armed isolation. Rural populations were diverse, including communities whose expectations of land reform or local services did not translate into willingness to support insurgent units. Revolutionary organizations sometimes interpreted this response as inadequate political consciousness, thereby obscuring the possibility that residents were making rational choices about danger and available alternatives.

Cuba's role further complicated domestic politics. Havana expressed solidarity and supplied varying forms of assistance, but Venezuelan party leaders and local militants maintained their own calculations. The Venezuelan Communist Party's later reconsideration of armed struggle illustrated how electoral opportunities and failures in the field could revise a strategy that initially appeared plausible. By the late 1960s many participants had abandoned or modified the insurgent route, although not all radical politics disappeared. Venezuela thus provides a comparative case in which state coercion cannot be ignored, but neither can the resilience of civilian party organization. It shows why the attraction of Havana depended on the institutional context in which the example was received.

Section 82.7

82.7 Peru: MIR, ELN, and the Campaigns of 1965

Peru’s Andean highlands had acute rural inequality and recent land occupations. Luis de la Puente Uceda, a dissident of the Aprista party, led the MIR; Héctor Béjar led a separate Ejército de Liberación Nacional (ELN), which had failed in 1963. Both launched campaigns in 1965 in areas including Cusco, Junín, and Ayacucho, and both were defeated within months. Existing campesino land struggles were not the same as armed insurrection. Agrarian grievances persisted, and the military government of Juan Velasco Alvarado began agrarian reform in 1969, which was not a guerrilla victory.

Peru's insurgencies emerged from overlapping struggles over land, social hierarchy, and the limits of elected government. In the early 1960s campesino mobilization challenged estates in the southern highlands and other regions, while disputes within the American Popular Revolutionary Alliance, or APRA, prompted younger militants to criticize its parliamentary compromises. Luis de la Puente Uceda and associates developed the Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria from the Aprista Rebelde tendency. Other militants pursued a separate organization, the Ejército de Liberación Nacional. Both invoked continental revolution, but their membership, prior experiences, command arrangements, and regional contacts differed. Describing them as a single Cuban-backed rebellion would erase the political disagreements that shaped their campaigns.

The ELN's attempted entry in 1963 failed in Madre de Dios, and the poet Javier Heraud died during that episode. Its later effort in 1965 was a renewed campaign rather than the uninterrupted continuation of a successful insurgent front. In that year the MIR established fronts in Cusco, Junín, and Piura, while the reconstituted ELN operated around La Mar in Ayacucho. The exact locations matter: the Peruvian Andes and Amazonian approaches were not a single social environment. Landholding systems, transport, ethnic relations, local political authorities, and links to existing agrarian unions varied substantially. The Instituto de Estudios Peruanos chronology provides a useful overview of these distinctions.

The insurgents encountered a government capable of bringing significant military force to bear. President Fernando Belaúnde Terry presided over a constitutional administration, though reform disappointed many rural activists. The army adapted its operations and defeated both major campaigns within months. The military response created its own questions concerning coercion and civilian harm; defeat should not be narrated solely through the competence of soldiers or the failure of insurgent commanders. Leaders also struggled to translate a revolutionary program into reliable support among villagers whose central concern might be land title, protection from estate authorities, or the continuation of their family's agricultural work.

Peru's later trajectory provides a warning against equating the guerrillas' failure with the resolution of agrarian conflict. In 1968 a military government displaced Belaúnde, and Juan Velasco Alvarado instituted extensive agrarian reform in 1969. The reform had its own origins in social mobilization, military planning, and political calculations; it was not a direct victory awarded to the defeated MIR or ELN. Later revolutionary movements in Peru followed different organizations and circumstances. For this chapter the central question is comparative: why did a strategy inspired by Cuba fail to consolidate a broad rural coalition even where grievances over land and inequality were demonstrably real? The answer lies in political organization and state response as well as geography.

Section 82.8

82.8 Havana, Training Networks, the Tricontinental, and OLAS

Cuban relations with movements ranged from ideological influence and diplomacy to training and material aid, and documentation varies by case. Cuba stayed economically and militarily dependent on the Soviet Union. The Tricontinental Conference (Havana, January 1966) created OSPAAAL, linking Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The Organización Latinoamericana de Solidaridad (OLAS) held its first conference in Havana in July–August 1967. Interpretations vary: anticolonial solidarity, revolutionary internationalism, or promotion of armed movements against existing governments. Havana did not direct every insurgency.

Havana developed several kinds of international relationship that historians must keep distinct. Public support for anti-imperialist movements was visible in speeches, newspapers, and conferences. Diplomatic links involved governments as well as organizations. Training, travel, and material assistance required more specific arrangements and generated records of unequal completeness. There was no single Cuban command center that mechanically determined every decision taken by guerrillas in Guatemala, Venezuela, Peru, Argentina, or Bolivia. Local political struggles predated many contacts with Havana, and recipients sometimes disagreed openly with Cuba's preferences. The difference between ideological prestige and operational direction is therefore not an apology for covert aid; it is a condition of accurate attribution.

In January 1966 Havana hosted the Tricontinental Conference, which brought together delegates and organizations concerned with political liberation in Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Its creation of the Organization of Solidarity with the People of Asia, Africa and Latin America, commonly known as OSPAAAL, supplied a public institutional framework for publications, conferences, and political messaging. The organization helped circulate distinctive posters, journals, and images across language and regional boundaries. Printed culture mattered because most admirers of Cuba would never receive guerrilla training or personally meet its leaders. They could nevertheless imagine a shared international struggle through slogans, artwork, and reports about events in distant countries.

The Organización Latinoamericana de Solidaridad, or OLAS, held its first major conference in Havana from 31 July to 10 August 1967. Participants debated the place of armed liberation in continental politics while governments and communist parties outside the conference assessed the consequences. Contemporary conference proclamations can demonstrate what delegates endorsed publicly, but they do not by themselves reveal the extent of clandestine assistance, prove that every participant held identical views, or establish that declarations became policy in every country. The public poster associated with OLAS provides evidence about the cultural presentation of revolution, not about the operational success of particular guerrilla columns.

Cuban internationalism also had limits imposed by state survival. The failed invasion at the Bay of Pigs, the 1962 missile crisis, economic pressures, and reliance on Soviet support affected Havana's capacity to act abroad. Its relationships with national communist parties were not uniformly harmonious, and its leaders sometimes adjusted tactics in response to shifting international conditions. Guevara's April 1967 message urging wider resistance to imperialism demonstrates the high aspirations of the project, but it also emerged as his Bolivian expedition encountered severe difficulty. The conference narrative should thus be read against defeats already suffered in Peru, Argentina, and the Congo. An international movement could produce enduring symbols even when the military theories invoked at its meetings performed poorly in practice.

82.9 Masetti and the Ejército Guerrillero del Pueblo

Jorge Ricardo Masetti, an Argentine journalist who had befriended Guevara, led the Ejército Guerrillero del Pueblo (EGP) in northern Argentina in 1963–64. Guevara supported and helped plan the venture but did not take part physically. The group found little local support, struggled with clandestine cross-border logistics, and was broken up by security forces; Masetti disappeared. Bolivia’s problems were thus foreshadowed.

The Ejército Guerrillero del Pueblo represented an early and especially revealing attempt to use northern Argentina as a starting point for continental revolution. Its leader, Jorge Ricardo Masetti, had covered the Cuban insurgency as a journalist in 1958 and established close relations with Castro and Guevara. After the Cuban victory he participated in revolutionary communications projects, most notably the development of Prensa Latina. He later chose to lead an expedition rather than remain a journalist. In 1963 Masetti, known as Comandante Segundo, entered the forests of Salta with a small contingent. His title implied the presence of a higher commander, and Guevara's supporters saw the project as preparation for a larger campaign, but Guevara himself did not fight there.

Argentina presented obstacles distinct from those in Cuba. The outlawing of Peronism after 1955, military interventions, labor militancy, and the complex loyalties of organized workers shaped opposition politics. Northern rural districts contained grievances, yet armed outsiders could not presume that isolated communities wanted to participate in an insurrection. The expedition had limited relations with local union organizations or broad political parties, and its secrecy complicated recruitment. Its members struggled with illness, shortages, internal discipline, and communication. Recollections about the movement's ideological commitment cannot substitute for evidence that residents accepted its claims to leadership or regarded its presence as a source of protection.

Argentine security forces discovered and dismantled the group in early 1964. Several members were arrested, others died, and Masetti disappeared; his remains have not been recovered. The Comisión Provincial por la Memoria has published records showing surveillance of the movement by Argentine intelligence bodies. Such documents establish the state had its own surveillance and coercive machinery, but they do not provide an impartial reconstruction of every encounter. Former guerrilla testimony similarly requires comparison with records made at the time. The movement's collapse occurred before Guevara's Bolivian venture and demonstrates why Bolivia cannot be treated as the first unsuccessful application of continental guerrilla theory.

The EGP also exposes an important chronology of revolutionary memory. Masetti's disappearance has been commemorated through competing political narratives: in one, the movement was a heroic precursor; in another, an isolated intrusion that endangered its own members and offered ordinary Argentines no credible political alternative. Neither interpretation should replace investigation. Historical analysis asks why a clandestine movement with international ties, intellectual commitment, and experienced organizers nevertheless failed to mobilize a national constituency. The discrepancy between external revolutionary prestige and local political reach would recur in other campaigns, including Guevara's own final expedition.

Section 82.10

82.10 Guevara in the Congo, 1965

After Congolese independence, Patrice Lumumba’s assassination and the Simba rebellion left several rebel leaders contending, including Laurent-Désiré Kabila. Guevara led a Cuban contingent to eastern Congo in 1965. His diary records poor coordination, weak local leadership, language barriers, and logistical failures, set against Western-backed forces, mercenaries, and shifting African diplomacy. The Cubans withdrew in November 1965. The experience tested, and cast doubt on, his assumptions about revolutionary strategy.

Guevara's Congo expedition was an African campaign undertaken by a Latin American revolutionary in the midst of a profoundly local conflict. The Congo's independence in 1960 had been followed by secession, international intervention, the removal and assassination of Patrice Lumumba, and rival claims to state power. By 1965 the Simba rebellion and resistance factions opposed a shifting coalition of government forces, foreign military assistance, and mercenaries. Guevara arrived with a Cuban contingent in eastern Congo near Lake Tanganyika, hoping to contribute to an international anti-imperialist front. The expedition must be situated within Congolese political history rather than portrayed as a remote rehearsal for his later death in Bolivia.

The Cuban volunteers encountered substantial difficulties in coordination with local forces. Language differences hindered command, and relations among Congolese leaders were affected by disagreements over priorities and authority. Guevara's own diary offers sharply critical assessments of some of his allies, including Laurent-Désiré Kabila, but historians should not treat those judgments as the only valid measure of Congolese aspirations. Guevara entered a conflict with its own leadership rivalries, community grievances, and shifting international relationships; local actors were not simply the raw material of a Cuban revolutionary strategy. Piero Gleijeses's archival research demonstrates the importance of the international context and the independent calculations of African participants.

The operational difficulties went beyond the character of any leader. The Cubans struggled to build reliable coordination, maintain supply, and align their efforts with a political program capable of recruiting local support. Government and foreign-backed adversaries could also draw on resources and networks the expedition lacked. Guevara's writings recorded frustration with illness, disorganization, and the limits of a small foreign force in an unfamiliar landscape. Yet the evidence cannot support a simplistic generalization that African fighters were incapable of discipline or political commitment. Such judgments would repeat the biases of a frustrated outsider rather than explain why particular alliances failed.

The Cubans withdrew in November 1965. The failure mattered personally to Guevara because it challenged assumptions he had developed from Cuba's success, but it did not terminate Cuba's involvement in Africa. Cuban policy later expanded in other settings, including Angola, under circumstances and command structures that differed greatly from Guevara's venture. The chapter therefore distinguishes the Congo expedition's specific defeat from any sweeping conclusion about the possibility of transnational military solidarity. A source study of Guevara's Congo diary should ask what the journal reveals about his expectations, which voices it excludes, and how publication and later editing shaped readers' access to the text. The episode foreshadowed Bolivia through problems of local relationships and coordination, not because every foreign expedition was destined to repeat an identical outcome.

82.11 Bolivia, 1966–1967: Ñancahuazú

Bolivia’s 1952 revolution had weakened under the military government of René Barrientos. Guevara entered secretly in 1966 and established a base at Ñancahuazú, hoping it would anchor wider revolution in South America. The Ejército de Liberación Nacional (ELN) included Cubans, Bolivians, and others. Communist leader Mario Monje refused to cede control. Rural Bolivians were not a uniform revolutionary constituency: language barriers, wariness, and the legacy of the 1952 land reform limited recruitment, while supply and communications problems mounted.

Bolivia appeared attractive to Guevara's circle because of its central South American location and history of radical politics. The 1952 National Revolution had transformed the army, nationalized important mining interests, and begun agrarian change, yet the settlement produced new disputes over political control and economic distribution. By 1966 René Barrientos governed through a military regime with significant rural relationships and the ability to seek outside assistance. A revolutionary organizer reading Bolivia through the broad pattern of continental inequality risked overlooking how campesinos understood the agrarian reform already achieved and what they expected from the state. The existence of peasant grievances did not mean villagers would welcome a new armed campaign.

Guevara reached Bolivia clandestinely in late 1966 and established a camp near the Ñancahuazú region. The contingent brought together Cuban veterans, Bolivian militants, and other volunteers. The plan aspired to create a base from which revolution might spread across borders, but that continental ambition amplified the demand for secrecy and reliable external communication. Local support proved narrower than hoped, and many residents either avoided the guerrillas or shared information with government forces. Language, distance, different political memories, and fear of armed reprisal compounded the isolation. The diary records these difficulties from the perspective of a commander whose expectations had become increasingly distant from local circumstances.

The dispute with Bolivian Communist leader Mario Monje became a major political obstacle. Monje's party had its own strategy, organizational interests, and interpretation of the country's revolutionary situation. Accounts differ about who promised what and when, but the essential problem was that neither side accepted a stable relationship between the existing party and the foreign-led guerrilla command. Such a dispute was not merely a personal insult; it exposed the unresolved question of who had the legitimacy to direct a Bolivian revolution. Guevara's determination to act without waiting for party approval did not solve the problem of creating political institutions capable of sustaining the armed group.

Early encounters with government forces initially suggested the guerrillas could fight effectively, but battlefield success did not produce a mass movement. Communications with urban sympathizers weakened; the column divided; supplies became harder to secure; and the government's understanding of the region improved. U.S. assistance included training and advisers for Bolivian troops, but the Bolivian state and its armed forces retained responsibility for their own decisions. A May 1967 CIA report described intelligence derived in part from captured participants, illustrating how foreign agencies followed the campaign. Intelligence documents must not be mistaken for omniscient accounts: agents collected fragmentary reports, evaluated uncertain testimony, and sometimes revised earlier assessments. The Bolivian case therefore requires simultaneous attention to Guevara's own errors, national politics, military adaptation, and the limits of external intervention as an explanation for the outcome.

82.12 Defeat, Capture, and Execution

In 1967 the guerrilla column split, lost contact, and found no peasant uprising. Bolivian forces, trained and advised with U.S. assistance and with CIA personnel present, closed in. Guevara was captured at the Quebrada del Yuro on 8 October and moved to La Higuera. A declassified CIA memorandum of 11 October confirms he was alive when captured and killed the next day on Bolivian military orders, contradicting the initial official claim that he died of battle wounds. His death ended the campaign and shaped his legend.

In the months before October 1967, the Bolivian guerrilla campaign deteriorated through cumulative losses. The division of the column deprived Guevara of contact with important comrades and support networks. The army intensified operations in the southeast, and residents living in the zone had to weigh the danger of contact with armed strangers against the danger of suspicion by security personnel. Guerrilla deaths, shortages, and declining prospects for recruitment became mutually reinforcing. A revolutionary expedition that had expected to widen its social base instead found itself concentrating on survival. These conditions help explain defeat but should not obscure the responsibility of named actors for the final execution.

On 8 October Bolivian troops engaged the remaining group in the Quebrada del Yuro. Guevara was wounded and captured alive, then moved to the small schoolhouse at La Higuera. The next day he was killed while held prisoner. Contemporary official explanations initially suggested that he had died of battle wounds, but evidence available within days contradicted that assertion. In his memorandum of 11 October 1967, CIA Director Richard Helms reported that Guevara had been captured alive and subsequently executed under Bolivian military orders. The distinction is decisive: death in combat and the intentional killing of a captive are different events, carrying different historical and legal implications.

U.S. military assistance and CIA involvement in the campaign are documented, including training of the Bolivian Second Ranger Battalion and the presence of personnel seeking intelligence about Guevara. The evidence should not be stretched into a claim that the United States itself issued the particular order to execute him. Historians continue to compare Bolivian testimony, intelligence records, photographs, and recollections about the decision. Some accounts recorded decades later disagree over conversations inside the schoolhouse. A careful narrative distinguishes information supported by near-contemporary documentary records from disputed later dialogue. It also recognizes that the Bolivian government sought political advantage from announcing the death of a globally recognized revolutionary.

After the killing, photographs of the body in Vallegrande and disputes over its burial became central to the creation of a revolutionary icon. The image could suggest sacrifice and martyrdom, yet the photograph was also produced in the context of a military victory and an effort to establish identification. It should be read as evidence with a creator, audience, and purpose, not as a transparent window onto Guevara's political legacy. His final diary and later memoirs offered different explanations for failure. Those sources illuminate command decisions but remain incomplete without the experiences of Bolivian campesinos, soldiers, relatives, and political organizers. October 1967 ended Guevara's expedition; it did not end armed revolutionary movements throughout Latin America, nor did it resolve the debate over political violence.

82.13 Uruguay: The Tupamaros

Raúl Sendic’s rural sugar-worker organizing led to the Movimiento de Liberación Nacional–Tupamaros (MLN-T), which became an urban clandestine group in the mid-1960s amid economic stagnation and political crisis. Using publicity, robberies, and kidnappings, it sought sympathy from workers and students while provoking escalating repression. The state’s security forces defeated it by the early 1970s. Violence cost victims and society, and the dictatorship that followed belongs to Chapter 87.

Uruguay was one of the most urbanized societies in Latin America, with a long tradition of constitutional politics and social legislation. During the 1960s economic stagnation, inflation, labor conflict, and disillusionment with established parties weakened the confidence that earlier prosperity had sustained. The Movimiento de Liberación Nacional–Tupamaros grew from diverse activist networks, including Raúl Sendic's work among sugar workers in the north, but it became known principally for clandestine urban operations. Its organizers believed Uruguay's cities offered political visibility and opportunities to expose the limitations of existing institutions. Their strategy did not depend on duplicating the Sierra Maestra in an unsuitable rural landscape, even while the Cuban revolution remained an important ideological reference.

Tupamaro activities included expropriations described by members as revolutionary financing, occupations, kidnappings, prison escapes, and publicity operations. Certain actions attracted sympathy from citizens frustrated by inequality or official corruption, but the movement's coercive methods also produced victims and alarmed people who favored reform without violence. It would be misleading to measure public support simply by the popularity of a successful publicity operation. A city-based clandestine organization needed secrecy, safe housing, sympathetic contacts, and the ability to operate without isolating itself from ordinary political life. Intensified policing and intelligence work gradually exposed those networks.

The political response changed as the movement expanded. Emergency powers, arrests, torture, censorship, and the growing role of the armed forces narrowed space for lawful dissent and increased the costs of clandestine participation. The early 1970s saw a sharper cycle of confrontation, culminating in the effective military defeat of the Tupamaros in 1972. Some surviving members spent years imprisoned under abusive conditions. The constitutional order continued to erode and gave way to dictatorship in 1973, a development examined at length in Chapter 87. The sequence must not be simplified into a claim that guerrilla violence alone caused dictatorship: economic crisis, political choices by presidents and military commanders, anticommunism, and institutional breakdown all require independent analysis.

Uruguay is an instructive counterpoint to rural foquismo. Its urban militants attempted to turn publicity and political theater into revolutionary momentum, while state security institutions adapted to the organizational vulnerabilities of clandestine cells. Recent scholarship emphasizes contradictions between original political objectives and the increasingly militarized practice of the movement between 1970 and 1972. The broader social history includes unionists, voters, relatives of prisoners, journalists, and residents caught between insurgent and state violence. An academically sound account must distinguish their experiences from both the romantic memory of the Tupamaros and the government's self-justifying claims of restored order.

82.14 Brazil: Marighella and the ALN

After the 1964 coup, Carlos Marighella broke with the Brazilian Communist Party and founded the Ação Libertadora Nacional (ALN), favoring urban struggle. His 1969 Minimanual of the Urban Guerrilla is a historical document of that strategy. The ALN worked with the MR-8 in the September 1969 kidnapping of U.S. ambassador Charles Burke Elbrick. Marighella was killed in November 1969, and repression crushed most armed opposition. The wider dictatorship is the subject of Chapter 84.

Brazil's armed left emerged within the specific circumstances of the 1964 overthrow of President João Goulart. The military regime constrained party politics, suppressed opposition organizations, and gradually developed a much more extensive machinery of surveillance and coercion. Carlos Marighella, a veteran communist organizer, disagreed with the Brazilian Communist Party's approach to dictatorship and favored revolutionary armed action. He became associated with the Ação Libertadora Nacional, known as the ALN, which operated through clandestine networks rather than a single remote rural base. His move toward armed struggle was shaped by Brazilian political experience, not simply by obedience to Cuban direction.

Marighella's 1969 Minimanual of the Urban Guerrilla should be treated as a primary source advocating a political strategy. It presents an attempt to explain why clandestine action in cities might challenge the regime, disrupt established authority, and communicate revolutionary messages. A historical study need not reproduce the manual's operational prescriptions. The more important analytical question is what assumptions it made about repression, publicity, the social base of opposition, and the capacity of violence to create mass political change. These were claims by a participant, not tested conclusions. Brazil's large cities contained industrial workers, students, professionals, and unequal neighborhoods with different orientations toward the military government.

The September 1969 kidnapping of U.S. ambassador Charles Burke Elbrick became an internationally visible confrontation. The ALN and Movimento Revolucionário 8 de Outubro, or MR-8, claimed responsibility and demanded publicity for a manifesto and the release of political prisoners. Contemporary U.S. diplomatic records show how officials understood the crisis as it unfolded. The operation helped publicize the resistance but also contributed to intensified state security measures. A major qualification concerns Marighella's personal role: organizational involvement by the ALN does not prove that he approved the specific plan in advance, and historical accounts report that he was surprised by the kidnapping. Historians must keep the distinction between an organization's action and an individual leader's decision.

Marighella was killed by police in São Paulo in November 1969. Repression subsequently weakened the ALN and other armed organizations, while the dictatorship consolidated its capacity to investigate, detain, and intimidate opponents. The record contains evidence of torture and illegal state violence as well as attacks carried out by underground groups. A balanced account must not equate coercion by state institutions with every action of a clandestine organization, since their resources and legal responsibilities differed; neither should it erase those harmed by insurgent violence. Chapter 84 examines the Brazilian dictatorship comprehensively. Here Brazil demonstrates how urban revolutionary strategy interacted with an increasingly authoritarian security state and with a diverse society whose political resistance extended far beyond armed groups.

82.15 Argentina: Peronist Militancy, the ERP, and the Montoneros

Perón’s 1955 overthrow and the proscription of Peronism left a politics of military intervention and labor conflict. The Ejército Revolucionario del Pueblo (ERP), tied to the Marxist PRT, and the Peronist Montoneros both emerged in 1970. They were not interchangeable, and the later Dirty War’s violence should not be retroactively assigned to the entire left. Cuba influenced them, but Argentine history shaped them. The 1976 dictatorship belongs to Chapter 86.

Argentina's revolutionary left cannot be explained without the contested legacy of Peronism. After Juan Domingo Perón's overthrow in 1955, the proscription of Peronist parties and movements, recurring military intervention, and struggles over labor organization created a political environment in which electoral participation was repeatedly restricted. Workers' activism and student radicalization expanded during the 1960s, culminating in major protest episodes such as the Cordobazo in 1969. These mobilizations were not themselves equivalent to guerrilla insurgency. They showed the existence of social grievances and capacities for collective action that armed organizations tried to interpret and sometimes appropriate, but unions and students disagreed about methods and desired political outcomes.

The Ejército Revolucionario del Pueblo emerged in 1970 from the Marxist Partido Revolucionario de los Trabajadores and pursued a socialist revolutionary project. The Montoneros, who also appeared publicly in 1970, identified with currents of revolutionary Peronism. Their differing intellectual foundations shaped how each viewed Perón, labor, socialism, and the possibility of mass political legitimacy. Treating them as interchangeable Cuban-inspired guerrillas obscures a central feature of Argentine politics: armed militants could disagree sharply about the nature of the nation and the social coalition capable of transforming it. Both movements also contained internal debate, and individual participation changed as political opportunities and repression shifted.

The return of elections in 1973 and Perón's return complicated the premise that politics was permanently closed. Some militants expected renewed constitutional politics to provide an opening, while factional struggle within Peronism became increasingly violent. Violence by armed organizations, far-right groups, and state-linked forces intensified, but they occupied different institutional positions and exercised different degrees of power. The chronology of the later military dictatorship and systematic repression after March 1976 belongs to Chapter 86; it cannot simply be projected backward onto every activist organization formed earlier. Nor can the crimes of later state authorities be explained solely as a reaction to guerrilla operations.

The Argentine case reveals an additional danger in using the label foquismo too broadly. The ERP and Montoneros developed amid major urban labor and political conflicts, not in a vacuum of peasant organization. Their members debated mass politics, party relations, clandestine structures, and the morality of coercion in ways that diverged from Guevara's original rural emphasis. Cuban symbolism mattered, but the actions of Argentine students, workers, Peronist factions, socialist organizers, and military governments gave each organization its particular shape. A comparative account should ask what forms of legitimacy they claimed, what institutions they could actually influence, how civilians experienced violence, and why armed militancy failed to create a broadly accepted route to democratic transformation.

Section 82.16

82.16 Rural and Urban Revolution: Strategy, Women, and Civilians

Movements disagreed on whether mass organization should precede armed struggle, and how to deal with unions, students, Indigenous communities, and parties. Clandestine groups often stifled internal dissent. Women served as organizers, couriers, journalists, leaders, and combatants, not only as support. Families and communities in conflict zones bore pressures that leaders’ statements rarely record.

The distinction between rural and urban insurgency was never simply a choice of physical terrain. Rural groups hoped that geographic distance from the capital could sustain armed activity and foster close relations with campesinos. Urban groups sought visibility, access to institutions, and the possibility that strikes or student mobilizations might develop into a broader confrontation with the government. Both models faced an underlying problem: political influence cannot be inferred from clandestine capability. A small group might survive in a remote region or stage a dramatic event in a city without convincing the population to accept its authority or aims. Success in avoiding arrest was not equivalent to gaining a legitimate mandate.

Urban movements such as the Tupamaros and the ALN developed amid dense social networks but were exposed to surveillance and police penetration. Rural movements in Guatemala, Peru, and Bolivia confronted travel distances and the need to establish trust with communities whose daily interests did not necessarily align with continental revolutionary schemes. In either setting, repression could strengthen radical grievances while simultaneously destroying the very organizations needed to organize a political majority. Violence also changed local calculations. Civilians could fear both insurgent reprisals and government suspicion, making public support difficult to measure. Movement literature that claimed popular backing must be read alongside testimony from people who declined to participate.

Women appear across these histories as political theorists, activists, couriers, negotiators, logistical organizers, family supporters, and armed participants. Some challenged gender hierarchies inside revolutionary movements, while others found those hierarchies reproduced under the rhetoric of liberation. Security archives frequently reduced women to the status of associates of men, and later heroic memory sometimes repeated that erasure. Historical reconstruction requires attention to oral histories, prison records, autobiographies, community testimony, and the unequal risks faced by women with caregiving responsibilities. A movement's public commitment to equality did not guarantee equal influence over its internal decisions.

The experience of noncombatants similarly cuts across ideological divisions. Peasants, shopkeepers, children, teachers, miners, students, and workers lived with interruptions to schooling, arrests, changing local authority, and suspicion directed at entire neighborhoods or villages. Armed organizations sometimes coerced support or targeted civilians; security forces could impose detention and violence on people with no insurgent connection. The scale, purposes, and responsibilities associated with these acts must be investigated rather than flattened into a general claim that all sides behaved identically. Studying everyday life makes visible the limits of military-centered histories: revolutions are fought in societies whose members have their own political ambitions and survival strategies, not merely on battlefields between rival armed groups.

Section 82.17

82.17 The United States, the Alliance for Progress, and Counterinsurgency

President Kennedy announced the Alliance for Progress on 13 March 1961, a ten-year development program that mixed reform goals with Cold War aims. Alongside it, the U.S. expanded military aid, training, and intelligence cooperation. Latin American governments pursued their own interests and were not passive tools. Development aid, diplomacy, clandestine action, and direct military assistance must be distinguished by country and by evidence.

The Alliance for Progress developed from a hemispheric history of competing approaches to inequality and political legitimacy. Latin American governments had sought development finance and a more favorable economic relationship with the United States before the Cuban Revolution. In March 1961 President John F. Kennedy proposed a ten-year cooperative program involving growth, education, housing, health, taxation, and reform. The initiative had a social language, promising improvements for populations living with poverty and poor public services. It also emerged in a Cold War context in which Washington feared that revolutionary movements would gain followers where existing governments appeared indifferent to inequality. Economic development and containment were therefore linked, but the program was not merely a camouflage for military policy.

Implementation depended on Latin American governments and societies as much as on Washington. Governments sought aid for infrastructure, schools, and fiscal stabilization; elites differed over agrarian and tax reforms; civic organizations demanded more equitable results. Projects could create tangible improvements, yet funding obstacles, local corruption, institutional weakness, and unequal economic growth limited the initiative's ambitions. A ten-year pledge was not equivalent to a single bank transfer, and official announcements did not establish that residents received promised benefits. The Kennedy address of 13 March 1961 is valuable precisely as a statement of aspiration and political priorities, which should be compared against later evidence about implementation.

Security cooperation followed a related but distinguishable path. U.S. military assistance, training programs, intelligence contacts, and diplomatic pressure expanded in several countries where officials regarded insurgency as a threat. Some governments had already developed coercive doctrines and counterinsurgency institutions before their cooperation intensified. It would be inaccurate to represent Latin American officers simply as instruments responding to commands from the United States. They pursued their own political projects, frequently had domestic opponents, and could initiate repression without outside prompting. Conversely, an emphasis on national agency must not minimize the material, diplomatic, and sometimes operational support Washington provided to authoritarian partners.

The relationship between reform and security was contradictory. Development programs claimed to widen democratic participation while security partners sometimes restricted parties, unions, or civil liberties. Some officials considered reform an alternative to revolution; others feared that concessions would empower radicals. Revolutionary organizations used the same contradictions to argue that constitutional change was impossible, even where electoral arrangements remained viable. Country comparison is essential: Venezuelan democratic competition, Guatemalan repression, Peruvian military reform, and Brazil's post-1964 dictatorship produced very different combinations of American assistance and domestic politics.

A rigorous account therefore asks about the recipients of assistance, the conditions attached, the institutions strengthened, and the experiences of communities affected. Kennedy's speeches cannot by themselves explain the outcome of a guerrilla campaign; nor do later records of training prove that every security operation was directed from Washington. The Alliance for Progress belongs in this chapter because it was one of several competing responses to the continental political challenge posed by Cuba, demonstrating that the struggle over Latin America's future involved development policy and political representation as well as armed force.

Section 82.18

82.18 Counterinsurgency, Repression, and the Problem of Evidence

Governments used military operations, emergency laws, intelligence, detention, and censorship, and civilians with no insurgent ties suffered killings, torture, disappearances, and displacement. Insurgents also committed kidnappings and attacks on civilians; scale, purpose, and institutional responsibility differed between state and nonstate violence. Sources are shaped by propaganda, clandestine records, memoirs, and military archives, so historians must separate combatants, civilians, opponents, and suspected supporters.

Counterinsurgency developed through the interaction of national militaries, police agencies, intelligence services, foreign advisers, and changing political institutions. Governments distinguished between conventional defense and the task of identifying clandestine networks embedded in cities and rural communities. They did not always respect the distinction between armed insurgents and civilian sympathizers, nor between political opposition and participation in a criminal act. In several countries emergency powers expanded, military institutions acquired internal-security responsibilities, and legal safeguards weakened. Historical explanation must treat these changes as political choices with consequences for the entire population, not as neutral responses automatically required by the existence of guerrillas.

Guatemala's armed forces undertook major rural campaigns in the eastern departments during the 1960s. Venezuela used military and police operations while retaining electoral institutions, and Peru deployed its army against insurgent fronts in 1965. Brazil's military government built an expanding security system after 1964; Uruguay moved toward direct military participation as the Tupamaros campaign intensified. Each state had a different constitution, political coalition, and public justification for coercion. The resulting record of detention, torture, intimidation, and sometimes unlawful killing requires attention to the agencies responsible and the chronology of each country's conflict. A single regional label should not conceal differences in accountability or scale.

Insurgent organizations also exercised coercion. Kidnappings, assassinations, intimidation, and attacks on public officials could harm civilians or restrict political freedoms. Some acts were presented by militants as punishment for collaboration or as exchanges for imprisoned members, but their proclaimed political aims did not erase the victims' experience. Historians should distinguish deliberate targeting, incidental harm, legal punishment, and unlawful state violence rather than treating every death as an interchangeable casualty of ideological confrontation. The state possessed constitutional obligations and vastly greater institutional resources, so evaluation of its conduct involves responsibilities that do not disappear merely because its opponents also committed abuses.

Evidence from secret conflicts is especially difficult. Military intelligence reports can preserve valuable dates and names but may reproduce untested allegations, interrogation-derived claims, or official assumptions. Guerrilla communiqués may accurately describe organizational aims while exaggerating support or battlefield success. Newspaper coverage can be limited by censorship, fear, or partisanship. Declassified U.S. records provide another important perspective, yet they reflect what particular American officials were told and believed, not necessarily everything that happened. Testimonies collected decades later can recover marginalized experiences while raising questions of memory and retrospective interpretation. The historian's task is to compare sources rather than promote a preferred institution's documents into an unquestionable record.

The social costs were not merely side effects. Fear changed decisions about travel, employment, political membership, and the reporting of abuses; children and relatives could endure consequences from actions they did not choose. Legal political movements sometimes lost space as governments equated dissent with subversion. These developments shaped later dictatorships and internal wars, which the next chapters examine in their own national settings. Chapter 82's purpose is to explain how an early wave of armed movements and the state response altered the balance between revolutionary hope, civil liberties, and organized coercion.

Section 82.19

82.19 Guevara’s Legacy, the Bolivian Diary, and Historical Memory

After October 1967 his Bolivian Diary circulated widely, along with photographs, posters, and speeches. His April 1967 Message to the Tricontinental called for creating “many Vietnams”; admirers saw sacrifice and solidarity, critics saw human costs, coercion, and failed campaigns. His writings must be separated from later cultural images. Cuba’s international position after 1967 showed the limits of exporting revolution and its continuing reliance on the Soviet Union.

Guevara's death created an unusually powerful historical image because its meaning was contested almost immediately. Photographs of his body in Vallegrande circulated widely; later posters reused his face as a symbol detached from the specific military defeat in Bolivia. Supporters treated him as the embodiment of courage, international solidarity, and willingness to share the hardships of the poor. Critics emphasized political coercion, failures of military judgment, and the danger of romanticizing violence. Neither claim makes the Bolivian expedition disappear, and neither permits historians to skip analysis of Guevara's own recorded decisions. A symbol that functions effectively in political culture is not necessarily an accurate account of the person represented.

The circulation of the Bolivian Diary was itself a political event. The diary was captured by Bolivian forces and published in transcription in 1968; later facsimile publication provided additional means of comparing editions and assessing editorial intervention. A declassified American memorandum from August 1968 demonstrates that the unauthorized transfer of a copy to Cuba produced a political crisis in Bolivia, illustrating that control over historical evidence was a matter of state authority as well as scholarship. Students must identify which edition they are reading, who prepared the translation, and whether its annotations were written by Guevara or supplied later. A diary is contemporaneous testimony about what a participant believed, not an exhaustive history of all participants in a campaign.

The 1967 OLAS poster held by the Library of Congress provides a second kind of source. Its slogan presents revolution as a duty, while the catalog identifies the object as a Cuban political poster associated with the organization's first conference. The record notes bullet-hole imagery and specifies a collection and physical call number. Such details allow students to discuss how political violence, legitimacy, and urgent action were represented visually. The artwork does not demonstrate that an insurgent group controlled territory or enjoyed popular support. Moreover, the Library of Congress warns that publication may be restricted; this textbook should analyze the authenticated record and link to it without embedding a potentially rights-restricted reproduction.

Revolutionary memory continued after the first wave of guerrilla failures. Activists in different countries invoked Guevara while choosing substantially different forms of organization, and commercial circulation turned his portrait into a design encountered far outside socialist politics. The change from revolutionary document to international icon illustrates how imagery can outlive the political strategies that originally gave it meaning. Students should compare at least three bodies of evidence: Guevara's writings, contemporary reports about the campaigns, and later commemorative objects. Each speaks to a different question—belief, event, or remembrance—and none should substitute for the others.

Section 82.20

82.20 Revolutionary Movements after Cuba: Synthesis

Strategies fitted to one society often failed in another because local support, social organization, legitimacy, state capacity, geography, and repression differed. The foco worked poorly as a general model. Alternatives included electoral socialism and reformist military governments (Chapters 85 and Peru above). Nicaragua’s 1979 victory required its own explanation (Chapter 89). Cuba showed that a dictatorship could be overthrown by a broad movement yet gave no universal template. Colombia, shaped by partisan violence, rural conflict, and exclusion, developed an unusually durable guerrilla conflict, the subject of Chapter 83.

The movements examined in this chapter arose from overlapping political crises, but they cannot be reduced to a single continental insurgency. Guatemala's post-1954 repression, Venezuela's contested electoral democracy, Peru's rural land conflicts, Argentina's proscribed Peronism, Uruguay's urban crisis, and Brazil's military dictatorship offered distinct conditions for revolutionary mobilization. Guevara's campaigns in Argentina, the Congo, and Bolivia confronted similarly different societies. An intellectual model developed from Cuba's overthrow of Batista could inspire each movement without adequately explaining why it attracted followers in one district and failed in another. This diversity is the starting point for the chapter's conclusions rather than an inconvenient exception to theory.

Comparative scholarship suggests that lasting insurgencies depended on more than a committed nucleus. They needed durable social relationships, political institutions that could represent constituencies, and a military organization capable of surviving state pressure without destroying the communities on which it depended. Governments differed in legitimacy and coercive capacity, and activists varied in the quality of their local knowledge. The same armed action might attract solidarity in one political setting and alienate supporters in another. Women, labor organizations, campesino groups, and civilian families were not passive background characters: their participation, withdrawal, and responses to coercion shaped what revolutionary movements could actually accomplish.

The first wave's defeats did not resolve the struggles over land, inequality, foreign influence, and democratic rights that helped produce it. A later wave of insurgency in parts of Central America drew from different strategies and more extensive organizing; Colombia's long conflict developed from its own history of partisan violence and institutional exclusion. These later chapters should not be read as delayed fulfillments of Guevara's program. Timothy Wickham-Crowley's comparative work emphasizes that movements emerging under different regimes adopted different relationships to mass organization and prolonged struggle. The point is historical change: revolutionary movements learned, adapted, fragmented, and sometimes disappeared as national conditions shifted.

The end of the chapter also resists two retrospective simplifications. The defeat of several guerrilla movements did not prove that existing states were just or fully democratic, and the injustice of some states did not make every armed strategy politically viable or ethically defensible. Insurgents and officials made decisions with effects on civilians who often had little control over either side. The most important analytical categories are political legitimacy, local participation, institutional capacity, external assistance, and the changing moral and material costs of violence. They connect the history of movements to the history of societies.

Chapter 83 turns to Colombia, where La Violencia, the National Front, rural mobilization, and later guerrilla organizations produced a different chronology of conflict. Subsequent chapters address military dictatorships, the Chilean road to socialism, and Central American revolutions. Readers should carry forward a method rather than a universal verdict: distinguish a movement's declared ideals from its organizational practices, test its claims against local testimony, and keep separate the moment of historical action from the memory created afterward. The Cuban Revolution transformed expectations across Latin America, but the history of revolutionary change remained irreducibly national, social, and contested.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary: Foco / Foquismo

A foco is a small armed nucleus meant to ignite wider revolution. “Foquismo” is the label for the strategy, largely coined by critics and Debray’s systematization. It matters because Guevara’s own views changed, so the term should not be treated as a single fixed doctrine.

Historical Vocabulary: Urban Guerrilla

Insurgency in cities using clandestine cells, publicity, and targeted violence. It was not a rural foco moved to town: it relied on different organization and assumptions about state power and civilian audiences.

Historical Vocabulary: Internationalism

The principle that revolutions in separate countries were linked. In Cuban policy it ranged from rhetoric and diplomacy to training and volunteers, and did not equal command.

Historical Debate: Innovation or Inappropriate Generalization?

The question: Was foco theory an innovative strategy or an overgeneralization of Cuba’s experience?

Interpretation A: It adapted guerrilla war to Latin America, rejected passivity, and showed that revolution did not have to wait on conditions.

Interpretation B: It misread Cuba’s success, which depended on Batista’s weakness and broad civilian support, and ignored political organization.

Evidence: Guevara’s writings, Debray, the diaries, comparative studies such as Wickham-Crowley, and the record of failures. Scholarship also notes the theory evolved, so both positions overlap.

Why it matters: It shapes whether defeat is blamed on theory, execution, or circumstance.

Continuity and Change Over Time: Before and After 1967

What changed: the rural foco lost credibility; urban groups, labor, and political parties gained prominence; repression intensified. What stayed continuous: inequality, political exclusion, and Cuban solidarity rhetoric. Who experienced change most: insurgents, and communities in conflict zones. Greater continuity: landholding structures. Why it matters: 1967 was a turning point in credibility, not an end to revolutionary movements.

The longer perspective requires care: the first wave of rapid foco attempts declined after repeated defeats, while later movements sometimes reorganized around long-term political and community work. The 1967 death of Guevara was not the sole cause of that change, and national turning points rarely occurred simultaneously.

Cause and Effect: Cuban Influence and Insurgency

Long-term causes: inequality, exclusion, Cold War politics. Short-term causes: Cuba’s 1959 victory, Guatemala’s 1954 lesson. Event: a wave of guerrilla movements. Immediate effects: U.S. counterinsurgency, Alliance for Progress, repression. Long-term consequences: defeat of most movements, militarized states, revolutionary memory. Complexity: no single cause; Cuban influence acted through local conditions.

Causal distinction: structural grievances can help explain the appeal of revolutionary claims without explaining the timing of a specific armed campaign. The Cuban demonstration effect helps explain choice of strategy; national electoral rules, rural relations, local political organizations, and state response help explain subsequent outcomes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Rural households in Andean and Bolivian highlands and lowlands faced the arrival of armed strangers, army pressure, and loss of labor. Students and workers balanced classes and jobs with clandestine meetings. Clandestine activists lived under false identities and surveillance. Families of insurgents and suspects experienced searches, detention, and uncertainty, and the surviving sources record them thinly.

This feature should never be treated as an invented representative diary. Use contemporary letters, union records, testimonies, memoirs and court files to test the effect of political violence on ordinary life, and record the social position, location and period of each witness.

Chapter Summary

The Cuban Revolution of 1959 changed political expectations and encouraged groups seeking radical change to pursue armed struggle. Their strategies differed, and Cuban inspiration did not mean Cuban command.

Guevara combined Marxism, anti-imperialism, and moral commitment, and in Guerrilla Warfare argued that an armed nucleus could create revolutionary conditions. His ideas evolved, and Debray’s version was more rigid.

In Guatemala, Venezuela, and Peru, insurgencies of 1960–65 grew from local conflicts, struggled to win sustained civilian support, and were defeated or fragmented.

Havana built solidarity institutions, notably the Tricontinental of 1966 and OLAS in 1967, while staying dependent on the Soviet Union.

Guevara’s ventures in Argentina’s EGP, the Congo, and Bolivia failed. In Bolivia he was captured on 8 October 1967 and executed the next day on Bolivian military orders.

Urban movements in Uruguay, Brazil, and Argentina used different methods and faced severe repression.

The U.S. responded with the Alliance for Progress and security cooperation, interacting with Latin American governments’ own interests. State and insurgent violence both harmed civilians.

Guevara’s image outlived the campaigns. The foco failed as a general model, but revolutionary memory persisted. Colombia’s distinct conflict follows in Chapter 83.

Comparisons across the countries studied demonstrate that a movement’s declared ideology does not explain its reception by workers, students, and campesinos. Existing parties, electoral institutions, land tenure, and armed forces shaped what people regarded as politically possible.

In Guatemala the MR-13, FAR and PGT disputed organizational control; in Venezuela elections remained politically meaningful to many citizens despite repression; in Peru the MIR and ELN failed to develop sustained alliances with existing campesino struggles.

Argentina’s EGP and Guevara’s Congo expedition exposed the limits of foreign prestige and clandestine initiative. The Bolivian venture repeated difficulties of local legitimacy, communications, organization, and national political context.

Uruguay, Brazil and Argentina developed different urban movements whose politics cannot be collapsed into a single expression of foquismo. In each country political institutions, state repression, and civilian choices shaped the outcome.

Official and insurgent documents require cross-examination. The Helms memorandum corrects the claim that Guevara died in battle; the Bolivian diary documents Guevara’s observations, while the OLAS poster documents the political construction of revolutionary identity.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how an event in one country became a continental reference point, and how ideas change when transplanted. Local conditions, not theory alone, determined outcomes.

Its legacies include militarized states, revolutionary memory, and a lasting debate about armed struggle versus electoral paths. Chapter 83 turns to Colombia, whose conflict endured by a different logic.
