Juan and Eva Perón and the Rise of Peronism

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: The Crowd in the Plaza

On the evening of 17 October 1945, a very large crowd filled the Plaza de Mayo in Buenos Aires to demand the release and return of a colonel the military government had pushed out of office eight days earlier. The question this chapter pursues is how organized workers, many of them recent migrants to the capital's industrial belt, became the foundation of the most durable mass political movement in modern Argentine history.

Reports of the day agree on its outline. Workers from the meatpacking plants of Berisso and Ensenada, from the factories and docks of Avellaneda, and from other districts south and west of the capital crossed the Riachuelo and converged on the city's center. Some came because union leaders called them, some because co-workers or neighbors did, and some because they feared the loss of recent gains. The man they were calling for, Colonel Juan Domingo Perón, had run the Secretariat of Labor and Welfare for two years. By nightfall he was on the balcony of the Casa Rosada, and within months he was a candidate for the presidency.

The movement that took his name is the subject of this chapter, and so is the woman who became its second great figure. Eva Duarte, an actress from the provinces, married Perón within days of the October mobilization and, as Eva Perón, became one of the most influential and polarizing women in the history of the Americas. She led no army and held no cabinet post. She nonetheless built a vast welfare organization, campaigned for women's suffrage, organized the first mass political party of Argentine women, and became the emotional center of Peronist political culture. The chapter treats her as a political leader in her own right and not as an adjunct to her husband, while refusing the later legend that she organized the events of October 1945.

The chapter pursues two developments at once. Between 1943 and 1955 social and political participation in Argentina expanded dramatically: unions gained legal recognition and bargaining power, wage earners gained paid leave, pensions, and legal protections, women gained the vote, and large groups long treated as marginal gained a sense of public recognition. During the same years governing authority concentrated in the presidency, opposition parties and newspapers faced restrictions, universities and courts lost independence, and the state tried to fuse itself with a single movement. Peronism was both. Treating it as only a democratic social revolution or only an authoritarian project mistakes a historically distinctive movement for a simpler thing.

Argentina was an unusual setting for such a movement. By the 1940s it was among the most urbanized, literate, and export-prosperous countries in Latin America, with a large European-descended immigrant population and an industrial sector that had grown during the Depression. Chapter 75 explained how the collapse of export markets encouraged import-substituting industry across the region, and Chapter 76 examined how Getúlio Vargas built a corporatist labor state in Brazil. Peronism belongs to that family of developmental and populist regimes, but it also grew from Argentina's own labor movement, its crisis of party politics after 1930, and its military's debates about nation, industry, and order.

Evidence needs care from the first page. Peronism generated an enormous documentary record: laws, decrees, speeches, newspapers, radio, film, photographs, and the vast correspondence addressed to Eva Perón. State propaganda shaped much of that record. After 1955 the successor regime and the opposition produced a rival literature that condemned Peronism, while Peronist memory preserved a different one. Historians have in recent decades turned to union archives, oral histories, and local studies to recover what workers, women, and rural communities themselves understood. Each kind of source has a purpose, and the chapter signals those purposes as it goes.

The chapter runs from the political crisis of 1930 to the overthrow of Perón in 1955, concentrating on 1943–1955. It does not narrate the long history of Argentina after 1955, though it notes Peronism's survival and Perón's return to power in 1973. It also leaves to other chapters the Bolivian revolution of 1952 (Chapter 78) and the Guatemalan crisis of 1954 (Chapter 79), which occurred while Perón governed and which the Argentine government watched with interest. The task here is to explain how a government that delivered substantial social reform, won repeated elections, and commanded deep working-class loyalty could nonetheless polarize the country so profoundly that it fell to a military rebellion.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the political system of the Infamous Decade struggled to represent Argentina's changing urban and industrial society.
  2. Trace Juan Perón's rise from the 1943 military government to the Secretariat of Labor and Welfare, distinguishing documented organization from later claims about participants' motives.
  3. Analyze the relationship between Perón and organized labor while recognizing that Argentine unions had their own leaders, traditions, and agency before 1943.
  4. Evaluate the causes and consequences of the October 17, 1945 mobilization, distinguishing Eva Duarte's documented activities from retrospective exaggerations of her role.
  5. Assess the labor, economic, and welfare policies of the first Peronist government, including real gains for workers and limits on independent union leadership.
  6. Explain the First Five-Year Plan, the IAPI, and the nationalizations, and analyze tensions among exporters, workers, and industrialists.
  7. Examine Eva Perón's political leadership, the Foundation, Law 13.010, and the Women's Peronist Party as distinct components of her public career.
  8. Distinguish the constitutional expansion of social rights in 1949 from the growth of presidential authority.
  9. Interpret Peronist political culture and the evidence for popular participation as well as state orchestration.
  10. Assess restrictions on opposition parties, the press, and universities without erasing the electoral legitimacy of Peronist majorities.
  11. Explain the economic difficulties of 1949–1952 and the later recovery, rejecting a simple narrative of continuous decline.
  12. Connect the Church–State conflict, the 1955 bombing of the Plaza de Mayo, and the September 1955 coup to Peronism's survival as a political and social force.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the political order created after 1930 lose the capacity to incorporate Argentina's growing working class?
  2. How should historians weigh Perón's initiative against the independent agency of union leaders and rank-and-file workers in explaining labor's support for him?
  3. What does the October 1945 mobilization reveal about how political loyalty is formed, and why do later accounts of it differ so sharply?
  4. In what senses did Peronism expand citizenship, and in what senses did it narrow the space for pluralism?
  5. How did Eva Perón's informal authority differ from formal political office, and why did that difference matter?

Historical Context

Argentina's twentieth-century politics grew out of a nineteenth-century success story. From the 1880s the country had tied its prosperity to the export of beef, wheat, and wool from the fertile pampas, financed largely by British capital and carried by British-built railways to the port of Buenos Aires. Millions of European immigrants, especially Italians and Spaniards, arrived between the 1880s and the First World War, and by 1914 foreign-born residents made up a very large share of the population of the capital. The Sáenz Peña electoral law of 1912 introduced secret, compulsory male voting, and in 1916 the Radical Civic Union (UCR) under Hipólito Yrigoyen won the presidency, ending decades of control by a landed conservative elite. Radical governments broadened political participation among the middle classes but largely did not alter the export economy.

The labor movement was already old. Anarchist, socialist, and syndicalist currents had organized immigrant workers since the 1890s, and strikes in 1919 (the Semana Trágica) and in Patagonia in 1920–1922 were met with severe repression. Unions in the railways, the ports, and the meatpacking plants developed experienced leaderships. A national confederation, the General Confederation of Labor (CGT), was formed in 1930, shortly before the military coup that opened the period examined in Section 77.1. Women worked in textiles, food processing, domestic service, and the needle trades, and Argentine feminists, many of them socialists, had campaigned for civil and political rights since the early century, as Section 77.11 explains.

The world economy then changed the country. The Depression devalued Argentine exports, and the Second World War cut off many imported manufactures. Domestic industry, especially textiles, food processing, metalworking, and consumer goods, expanded behind these barriers, and the industrial labor force grew. A growing migration from the interior provinces to Buenos Aires added a new population, often described in contemporary slang as cabecitas negras, a term that carried class and racial condescension. These newcomers were frequently of mixed Indigenous, Spanish, and European ancestry and met prejudice from an older urban population that thought of itself as European. Their arrival is essential to understanding both the base of Peronism and the hostility it provoked.

The armed forces were the third element. The Argentine army, professionalized in the early century with German-influenced training, had intervened in politics in 1930 and increasingly saw industry, steel, and fuel as questions of national defense. Some officers admired European authoritarian regimes in the 1930s, others were conservative nationalists, and others were liberal or pro-Allied, so the military was never a single political bloc. World War II sharpened these divisions, because Argentina remained formally neutral long after most Latin American republics aligned with the United States. This neutrality, and the suspicions it aroused in Washington, became part of the political atmosphere in which Perón rose.

Historians have long debated how to explain Peronism. An earlier sociological tradition associated with Gino Germani emphasized the arrival of a new, politically inexperienced working class from the interior, available for manipulation by a charismatic leader. A later revision, associated especially with Juan Carlos Torre and Daniel James, stressed the role of experienced union leaders and the real material and symbolic gains that workers received. A more recent cultural and social history, represented by Mariano Plotkin, Mark Healey, and Eduardo Elena, examines how Peronism shaped identities, consumption, and daily life. This chapter draws on all three while treating none as complete.

The Infamous Decade, Conservative Restoration, and the Crisis of Argentine Democracy

On 6 September 1930 General José Félix Uriburu led troops into Buenos Aires and overthrew President Hipólito Yrigoyen, who was elderly, unpopular with many elites, and weakened by the Depression. The coup had broad support among conservatives, nationalists, and sections of the press, and it set a precedent: the armed forces had removed a constitutionally elected president with little resistance. Uriburu's own corporatist and authoritarian ambitions found limited backing, and when his government held provincial elections in April 1931 and the Radicals won in Buenos Aires province, the results were annulled. The experiment signaled how the new order would treat the ballot when it produced the wrong result.

General Agustín P. Justo became president in 1932 through an election from which the Radicals, who had not yet decided to participate, were largely absent, and he governed with a conservative alliance known as the Concordancia. His successors, Roberto Ortiz and Ramón Castillo, ruled under a political system marked by electoral fraud, patronage, and the manipulation of provincial machines. Writer José Luis Torres coined the phrase Década Infame, the Infamous Decade, which became the standard shorthand. Some historians caution that the label carries the moral judgment of its coiners and that the period also saw economic recovery, public works, and the start of state intervention in the economy. The fraud, however, was real and was widely acknowledged at the time. President Ortiz tried to restore clean elections before illness removed him from power in 1940, and his failure reinforced the sense that the system could not reform itself.

Foreign economic dependence heightened the sense of national humiliation. Under the Roca–Runciman Agreement of 1 May 1933, Britain undertook, subject to specified price and consultation conditions, to maintain imports of Argentine chilled beef broadly against the depressed 1932 benchmark. In return Argentina made concessions affecting sterling exchange, tariffs, and British commercial and utility interests. The treaty did not guarantee Britain a share of Argentine beef purchases: it concerned British purchases of Argentine beef, and critics condemned the advantages secured by foreign meatpackers and British interests. A Senate investigation of the meatpacking trade led by Lisandro de la Torre ended in 1935 when a gunman in the chamber killed Senator Enzo Bordabehere, a colleague of de la Torre. The affair became shorthand for corruption and impunity, and later Peronists would invoke it as evidence of what they were replacing.

Meanwhile the social structure was changing faster than the political one. Import-substituting industry expanded in textiles, food, metalwork, chemicals, and consumer goods, and the manufacturing labor force roughly doubled between the early 1930s and the mid-1940s, according to commonly cited estimates. Roughly a million or more people moved from the interior provinces to the capital region over the 1930s and 1940s. Factory workers, many of them women and young people, lived in the working-class suburbs of Greater Buenos Aires, where housing was often self-built and infrastructure thin. Their workplaces were frequently unorganized, wages were low, and employers often ignored the labor laws that did exist.

Existing unions did not represent most of these workers. The CGT, formed in 1930 by the union of socialist and syndicalist federations, split in 1935 and again over political strategy, and its strongest affiliates were in transport and commerce, where Socialist leaders dominated. Communists organized in construction, meatpacking, and textiles and led some of the most militant strikes of the period. Neither the Socialist nor Communist parties, however, could convert union strength into electoral success among the new working class. The Radical Party, under Marcelo T. de Alvear and then others, returned to elections in 1935 and fought fraud, but its middle-class leadership had little organic connection to the newer industrial workforce. The conservative parties governed through patronage in the provinces. None of the parties spoke effectively to the new working class.

The result was a political system increasingly cut off from the society it governed. A conservative order dependent on fraud and military goodwill faced a growing urban industrial population that existing parties and unions only partly represented. This gap did not make Peronism inevitable, and other outcomes were possible. But it created an opening for a leader who offered the new working class recognition, material benefits, and organizational access, and for a military that increasingly saw the old order as discredited.

Section 77.2

The Military Coup of 1943 and Juan Perón's Emergence in Government

On 4 June 1943 army units marched on the Casa Rosada and removed President Castillo, whose government was widely expected to manipulate the coming election in favor of a conservative successor. The coup came from within the army and had no single program. Its participants included conservative nationalists, officers sympathetic to the Axis, others with Catholic and anticommunist priorities, and some who wanted above all an end to fraud and to Argentina's diplomatic isolation or humiliation. General Arturo Rawson held the presidency for a day or so, and General Pedro Pablo Ramírez then assumed power, followed in 1944 by General Edelmiro Farrell. The new government dissolved Congress, banned political parties, intervened in universities, and imposed Catholic religious instruction in public schools.

Argentina's neutrality in the Second World War shaped the politics of the new regime. The United States pressed Buenos Aires to break with the Axis, while military nationalists and some pro-German officers resisted. Argentina severed relations with Germany and Japan in January 1944, after which Ramírez was pushed from office, and it declared war on the Axis in March 1945, late enough that critics regarded the move as opportunistic. The episode left Washington deeply suspicious of the officers who governed Argentina, and Colonel Perón figured prominently in its suspicions. That hostility would become a central theme in the campaign of 1945–1946.

Juan Domingo Perón, born in 1895 in Lobos, Buenos Aires province, was a career officer with experience as a military attaché in Chile and a period of study and observation in Italy in 1939–1941, where he saw Mussolini's regime and the wartime European situation at first hand. He taught military history, wrote on strategy, and was known in the army as a skilled organizer and speaker. Historians disagree about how far the Italian experience shaped his later politics. He plainly absorbed an interest in organizing the nation for industrial and military strength, but Peronism was not a copy of Italian Fascism, and the movement grew out of Argentine conditions and Perón's improvisations.

Perón was a leading member of a secret lodge of officers called the Group of United Officers (Grupo de Oficiales Unidos, GOU), which circulated manifestos and helped plan the 1943 coup. The GOU's membership, internal operations, and even the meaning of its initials are disputed, because evidence was destroyed or reconstructed from memoirs, and later claims about the beliefs of every participant should be treated cautiously. What is documented is that a network of colonels shared concerns about neutrality, industry, order, and the weakness of the old political class, and that Perón was one of its energetic organizers. He did not lead the coup and held at first a modest post, but he proved adept at turning that post into a power base.

His critical choice was to seek control of the National Labor Department, a small and neglected agency. The military government had at first treated labor primarily as a security problem, repressing communist-led unions, closing some union offices, and arresting leaders. Perón took a different view. In late 1943 he took over the Labor Department and had it raised to the Secretariat of Labor and Welfare, giving it cabinet-level weight. He used it to open channels to union leaders, mediate disputes, enforce existing labor law that employers had ignored, and issue new protections. While Perón rose, he also served as war minister and in July 1944 became vice president, accumulating three posts that gave him access to both soldiers and workers.

Section 77.3

Labor Reform, the CGT, and the Construction of Perón's Working-Class Coalition

Perón did not create Argentine labor. The railway, commerce, municipal, and meatpacking unions he courted had decades of organizing behind them, experienced leaders, and distinct political traditions. Joel Horowitz's studies of the pre-1943 unions show that many leaders were pragmatic and open to alliance with the state if it delivered concrete benefits. Some leaders of the older Socialist and syndicalist unions, such as Ángel Borlenghi of the commerce workers, saw in the Secretariat an unprecedented chance to win gains that employers and conservative governments had refused. Others, like Luis Gay of the telephone workers and leaders of the railway unions, took similar views. Their willingness to work with a military government was a choice they made for reasons of their own.

The Secretariat delivered. Perón's agency intervened in strikes and typically brokered settlements that raised wages. It extended retirement coverage to categories of workers who had lacked it, notably those in commerce and industry. It created labor courts and enforcement mechanisms in 1944. It issued the Rural Worker's Statute (Estatuto del Peón) in October 1944, setting minimum wages, hours, and conditions for agricultural laborers and infuriating many landowners. It froze rents and restricted evictions of tenant farmers. Vacation and severance rules, employer obligations for workplace safety, and the annual bonus known as the aguinaldo, which became general in December 1945, were all measures that translated state power into concrete household benefits.

The government also set new rules for unions themselves. A 1945 decree, discussed in Section 77.6, favored one recognized union per sector and tied legal privileges to government recognition. This arrangement strengthened large unions allied with the Secretariat and weakened communist, anarchist, and independent rivals. The effect was double-edged, as it would be throughout the Peronist period: unions gained legal standing and bargaining power, and the state gained leverage over which unions would be recognized and who would lead them. Communist-led unions, in particular, were repressed or absorbed, and some of their activists were arrested, while some Communist leaders also saw the logic of working within the new arrangement.

Not all labor supported Perón. Communists and many Socialists opposed what they saw as a military dictator's manipulation of workers, and some strikes in 1944–1945 targeted the government. Anarchist and independent unionists distrusted state-sponsored organization. In 1945 a number of employer associations and middle-class parties denounced the Secretariat's measures as demagogy. For many employers, the issue was not only wages but authority on the shop floor: the labor courts and inspections limited managers' discretion. This resistance matters because it shows that Peronism's labor base was built through choices and conflicts among workers, not given in advance.

By the autumn of 1945 Perón had assembled a coalition of union leaders, rank-and-file workers who had gained directly, sections of the military, and some Catholic and nationalist currents. It was a coalition of unequal partners with different motives, and its cohesion was untested. Its opponents were also assembling: employers, landowners, most political parties, much of the press and university community, and a large part of the middle classes were turning against a regime that appeared to be building a mass movement on a military foundation. The crisis of October 1945 would test both coalitions.

Primary Source Window

Labor Legislation of 1944–1945: The Rural Worker's Statute, the Aguinaldo Decree, and the Professional Associations Decree

Creators: The Secretariat of Labor and Welfare and the military government · Dates: 1944–1945 · Type: Decrees published in the national official bulletin

Context: These decrees were issued by an unelected military government under Perón's direction at the Secretariat. They became the legal foundation of the Peronist labor settlement and were later given the force of law by Congress after the 1946 election.

Paraphrase of the decrees' central provisions: the Rural Worker's Statute set minimum wages, working hours, housing and food conditions, and employer obligations for agricultural laborers; the December 1945 aguinaldo decree required employers to pay an annual supplementary salary; and the associations decree established conditions under which unions could gain legal recognition and bargaining rights.

Analysis: The decrees show concrete benefits delivered through state authority, which helps explain labor's support. They also show how legal recognition gave the state leverage over unions. Their language of rights and obligations tells historians what the government wanted workers and employers to believe, but it does not show how consistently the laws were enforced in particular workplaces, especially on isolated rural estates where landowners held local power.

  1. Which groups of workers gained most directly from these decrees, and which employers lost authority?
  2. Why might a military government issue such labor legislation, and who is the audience for it?
  3. What can the decrees tell historians about enforcement in practice, and what cannot?
  4. How might a union leader and a landowner have read the same decree differently?

Section 77.4

Eva Duarte, the October 17 Mobilization, and the Birth of Peronism

Eva Duarte was born in 1919 in Los Toldos, a small town in Buenos Aires province, the youngest of five children of a mother who raised them without a legally recognized father. Records of her birth were later altered, and some details of her childhood have been shaped by both hostile and devotional retelling. In 1935 she moved to Buenos Aires, where she worked in small theater companies, radio dramas, and minor film roles. By the early 1940s she had become a recognizable radio performer. Her upbringing in poverty and her experience of the capital's condescension toward provincial women were part of her later political language, though students should note that she shaped her own autobiography for political purposes.

She met Perón in January 1944 at a benefit in Buenos Aires for victims of the San Juan earthquake, an event the Secretariat helped organize. Their relationship developed through 1944 and 1945. Her influence in these early months is documented mainly through later recollections, and historians disagree about how far she acted as a political confidante before October 1945. What is certain is that her radio work and her connections among actors and broadcasters gave Perón useful contacts, and that some officers disliked the relationship.

Perón's position had weakened by autumn 1945. The Allied victory made Argentina's wartime record an embarrassment, the United States pressed for democratization, and the opposition grew bolder. In September a large demonstration of the Democratic opposition marched in Buenos Aires. Army officers, led by garrison commanders including General Eduardo Ávalos at Campo de Mayo, pressed Farrell to remove Perón. On 9 October he resigned his posts, and on 12 October was detained and sent to Martín García island in the River Plate. Some of his supporters in the unions and in the Secretariat feared that the gains of two years would be rolled back, and Perón's departure left the CGT and the labor leaders without their ally.

What followed was a spontaneous and organized mobilization that scholars have reconstructed through newspapers, union records, and oral history. Leaders of unions in the meatpacking plants of Berisso and Ensenada, including activists around the union leader Cipriano Reyes, began calling for workers to march. In Avellaneda, in the working-class suburbs south of the capital, and elsewhere, crowds began forming on 16 October and the morning of 17 October. The CGT's leadership debated, divided over whether to call a general strike, and eventually voted for one on 18 October, but by then workers had already stopped work and moved toward the center. Participants crossed the Riachuelo, the river that separates the capital from the southern suburbs, on foot and by whatever transport was available, and filled the Plaza de Mayo through the afternoon and evening.

The size of the crowd is uncertain; estimates run from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand. The government, wary of violence, did not attempt to disperse it. Perón had been moved from Martín García to a military hospital, and negotiations among officers and union leaders secured his reappearance. Late that night he addressed the crowd from the balcony of the Casa Rosada, thanking workers and calling for calm and unity. The image of workers cooling their feet in the plaza's fountains fixed itself in opponents' accounts, where they mocked the crowd as descamisados, "the shirtless," a label the movement embraced.

Eva Duarte's role requires precision. A popular legend holds that she organized the mobilization personally, rallying the unions and driving the crowds to the plaza. Contemporary evidence does not support this. The decisive organizers were union leaders and workers, together with military and political negotiators, and Eva's own activities in these days are poorly documented. She was in Buenos Aires, she was Perón's partner, and she later became a symbol of the day, but she was not its architect. Perón and Eva married in a civil ceremony on 22 October 1945, and the legend of her role was largely built afterward, partly by her own retellings. Treating her as a political actor in her own right, as later sections do, does not require crediting her with 17 October.

Historical Vocabulary
Descamisados

The word descamisados ("the shirtless ones") was used by opponents in 1945 to mock the working-class crowds who demonstrated for Perón, implying poverty, disorder, and lack of refinement. Peronists adopted the label as a badge of honor, and it came to name the movement's working-class supporters, whom Perón and Eva addressed directly.

The term shows how political vocabulary can be reclaimed. It also reflects the class and regional prejudice, expressed in related words such as cabecitas negras, that surrounded the movement's early years. Students should not treat the word as a neutral description of a social class.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The October 1945 Mobilization and the Rise of Perón

Long-term causes

The post-1930 crisis of representation, industrial growth and internal migration, a long union tradition, and the failure of existing parties to incorporate the new working class.

Short-term causes

Labor gains under Perón's Secretariat, his forced removal on 9 October 1945, fear of losing those gains, and divisions within the military and the opposition.

Event or transformation

Workers mobilized in Greater Buenos Aires and filled the Plaza de Mayo on 17 October, securing Perón's return to public life.

Immediate effects

Perón reappeared and announced his candidacy; unions and the Labor Party prepared an electoral campaign; the opposition consolidated against him.

Long-term consequences

A mass political identity formed around Perón; 17 October became the movement's founding day; workers became a central electoral force.

Historical complexity

The mobilization depended on union leaders, rank-and-file initiative, military bargaining, and Perón's earlier work. No single actor, including Eva Duarte, created it.
Division II — The First Peronist Government · Section 77.5

The Presidential Election of 1946 and the Formation of the Peronist Government

The election of 24 February 1946 was among the cleanest in Argentina since 1930, and it was bitterly polarized. Perón ran on the ticket of the Labor Party, founded in October 1945 by union leaders including Cipriano Reyes, together with dissident Radicals and other groups, with a Radical defector, Hortensio Quijano, as his running mate. His opponent was José Tamborini, a Radical, heading the Democratic Union, an alliance of Radicals, Socialists, Communists, and Progressive Democrats that had united to block a military candidate. The alliance spanned an unusual range of positions, and its inclusion of the Communist Party and its backing from much of the business and landowning world gave Perón material for his claim that the opposition represented the old oligarchy.

International politics entered the campaign directly. In February 1946, a few weeks before the vote, the U.S. State Department, under the influence of Assistant Secretary Spruille Braden, published a document known as the Blue Book that accused Argentine officials, Perón among them, of collaboration with the Axis. Perón turned the publication to his advantage, presenting the election as a choice between Argentina and foreign interference, in the slogan Braden o Perón. Many historians conclude that the Blue Book helped rather than hurt him, though some voters were no doubt moved by its contents and by the Democratic Union's own appeal for democratic restoration.

Perón won with about 52 to 53 percent of the vote, against roughly 42 to 43 percent for Tamborini, and a large majority in the electoral college. His support was strongest among industrial workers, in the working-class districts of Greater Buenos Aires, and in many interior provinces; the Democratic Union did best among the urban middle classes and in parts of the central pampas. The results do not support a simple division between rich and poor, since many workers voted for the opposition and some middle-class and rural voters supported Perón, but the class pattern was marked. The result was an electoral mandate that Perón's opponents had difficulty accepting and that Peronists thereafter cited as the basis of their legitimacy.

Perón took office on 4 June 1946, the third anniversary of the coup that had launched his career. He moved quickly to consolidate a governing movement. The Labor Party, whose leaders had expected to retain autonomy, was dissolved by decree into a single movement and then, in 1947, the Peronist Party. Cipriano Reyes, who resisted, broke with Perón and was later imprisoned for years on charges of plotting against him, which Reyes denied. The episode illustrates the choices facing independent labor leaders: those who cooperated gained positions, and those who insisted on autonomy faced marginalization or worse.

Perón also reshaped the institutions around him. The Supreme Court was reorganized through impeachment of most of its members in 1947, in a process opponents regarded as political and supporters defended as a reckoning with a court that had legitimized the 1930 coup. Governors and provincial legislatures aligned with the presidency, and the army was courted with budgets and promotions. The CGT came to function as a pillar of the government, with a growing role in selecting candidates.

The first Peronist government therefore illustrates a feature that would define the whole period: its legitimacy rested on a genuine electoral majority, and its governing practices increasingly narrowed the space available to those who had not voted for it. The two things were not contradictory in the minds of its supporters, who believed the majority had a right to govern, but they would become increasingly contested in the years that followed.

Section 77.6

Organized Labor, Union Power, Social Rights, and the Peronist Social Coalition

Under Perón, organized labor became a central institution of the Argentine state and society. Union membership grew from roughly half a million in the mid-1940s to a figure commonly estimated in the range of two million or more by the early 1950s. Collective bargaining agreements, which covered a limited share of workers before 1943, expanded rapidly, and the government supported the principle that wages should rise and that workers should share in national income. Estimates of the wage-earners' share of national income increased substantially in the late 1940s, though precise figures depend on methods and sources, and historians treat the numbers with care.

Social rights expanded beyond wages. Paid vacations, severance protections, the annual aguinaldo, accident compensation, and the extension of retirement pensions to most categories of workers transformed what employment meant. Labor courts allowed workers to bring complaints against employers with more chance of success than before. Many workers recalled the new tone of the workplace as important, since foremen and managers could no longer dismiss complaints without consequence. These changes were not abstract: for many families they meant secure income in old age, holidays away from the city for the first time, and a sense of legal protection.

The CGT's political role grew in parallel. Its leaders sat on policy bodies, helped draft and administer labor legislation, and supported the Peronist Party's candidates. The confederation also became a vehicle through which the government supervised unions. Leaders of recognized unions were expected to support the government, and the CGT acted at times as a disciplinary body. Luis Gay led the CGT in 1946–1947 but was pressed out amid conflict over trade-union independence and Perón’s political consolidation. Aurelio Hernández briefly succeeded him; José Espejo held the general secretaryship from late 1947 until 1952, followed by Eduardo Vuletich. Eva Perón developed close relationships with CGT leadership, but this chronology should not be reduced to a single appointment made by her. Union officials who challenged government direction faced pressure and, in some cases, removal.

The limits of union independence appeared in conflicts. The railway strike of 1950–1951, in which workers protested wages, was declared illegal, and the government drafted strikers into the military and imprisoned leaders. The sugar workers of Tucumán, represented by the FOTIA federation, struck in 1949 and faced repression. These episodes show that Peronism did not simply surrender to labor demands. When strikes threatened the government's plans, particularly when they challenged the leadership of the CGT, the state treated them as threats. Historians such as Daniel James emphasize that workers nonetheless retained significant influence at the workplace through shop stewards, comisiones internas, who defended local interests and sometimes defied national leaders.

The result was a double reality. Workers gained concrete and lasting material benefits, along with a new public dignity, and many experienced Peronism as the first government that took them seriously. At the same time, the price of institutional access was loyalty to a government increasingly intolerant of dissent within the labor movement. Both facts are necessary to understand why Peronism produced fervent loyalty among workers and fierce opposition from rival labor currents.

Historical Vocabulary
Personería gremial

Personería gremial (roughly, "union legal standing") was the legal recognition under Argentine law that allowed one union per sector to negotiate collective agreements and represent workers, established in the 1945 decree on professional associations and consolidated under Peronism.

The mechanism helps explain both the power and the dependence of Peronist unions: recognition gave large unions bargaining authority and funding, and it gave the state influence over which union received it. The legacy endured long after 1955 and remains a feature of Argentine labor relations.

Section 77.7

The First Five-Year Plan, the IAPI, and the Economic Foundations of Peronism

When Perón took office, Argentina's economy was unusually favorable. The war had left the country with large foreign-exchange holdings, mostly in blocked British sterling, and with agricultural exports in high demand as a hungry Europe sought food. The government nationalized the Central Bank in 1946, giving the state control over credit, and created the Argentine Institute for the Promotion of Trade (Instituto Argentino de Promoción del Intercambio, IAPI) in May 1946 to monopolize the purchase and export of key agricultural products.

The IAPI bought grain and other products from farmers at fixed domestic prices and sold them abroad at higher world prices, using the difference to finance industrial development, subsidize consumption, and fund the state. For urban workers the system helped keep food cheap and wages purchasing-power high. For agricultural exporters, especially large pampas landowners, it meant that a share of the earnings from the postwar boom was captured by the government. Many farmers resented the policy and reduced planting, and over time this affected export volumes, as Section 77.17 discusses. The IAPI also had wider uses, including managing trade agreements with European buyers, and it became a symbol of Peronist economic nationalism.

The First Five-Year Plan, announced in 1946 and approved by Congress in 1947 to run through 1951, was a program of public works, industrial expansion, energy development, housing, health, and education. It aimed to reduce dependence on imports, expand heavy and light industry, and raise domestic consumption. Like development plans elsewhere in the world at the time, it was less a rigorous schedule than a declaration of intentions and priorities. It funded hospitals and schools, supported credit for industrial firms, and guided state investment in steel, energy, and transport. Industrial output grew, particularly in consumer goods such as textiles, appliances, and food processing, and employment rose with it.

On 9 July 1947, Perón symbolically proclaimed Argentina's economic independence in the city of Tucumán, where independence had been declared in 1816. The government used the occasion to announce the repayment of the foreign debt and the nationalization of foreign-owned services, as the next section describes. The imagery linked Peronist economic policy to the founding national narrative, reinforcing the movement's claim to complete the work of independence.

The model rested on tensions. Agricultural exporters, who still earned the foreign exchange on which imports depended, saw their incentives reduced. Industrialists benefited from protection and credit but chafed at labor rules and government direction. Workers gained consumption and security but faced the risk that inflation would erode wages. The government's task was to balance these interests, and in the boom years of 1946–1948 it managed to do so. When international conditions turned against Argentina, the balance became harder to hold.

Section 77.8

Nationalization, Infrastructure, Housing, and the Expanding Economic State

The Peronist government extended public ownership into sectors long dominated by foreign companies. The railways, built largely with British capital, were the most important. After negotiations, which were complicated by the blocked sterling Argentina held in London and by the conditions of the postwar world, the government purchased the British-owned lines, and they passed into state hands on 1 March 1948, a date commemorated as a national triumph. The price, often put at roughly 150 million pounds sterling, was considered by critics to be too high for a system that had deteriorated, and by defenders to be a reasonable cost for ending foreign control.

Other services followed. The government purchased the telephone system from the American firm ITT in 1946, took over gas supply, ports, and a merchant fleet, and in 1950 consolidated air carriers into Aerolíneas Argentinas. Public enterprises were created in steel, energy, and shipbuilding, and the state expanded its role in credit through the Central Bank and the Industrial Credit Bank. The petroleum company YPF, founded in 1922, was a prior model of public enterprise that Peronist officials invoked. These measures reflected broad currents in the mid-century world, where governments in many countries, including democracies in Europe, nationalized railways and utilities.

The government also built. The health ministry, led by Ramón Carrillo, expanded hospital capacity dramatically; figures often cited suggest that the number of hospital beds roughly doubled, though students should treat such numbers as approximate. Carrillo's campaigns against malaria, tuberculosis, and other diseases made measurable differences in rural and impoverished areas. The state financed housing through public credit and construction programs, and the Eva Perón Foundation added its own housing projects, discussed in Section 77.10. Schools, roads, and dams were built, and the government pursued investment in the interior as well as the capital.

The costs were real. The railways required modernization that the government was slow to finance, and the new state firms often ran deficits, absorbing subsidies. Nationalized services kept prices low for consumers and provided jobs, but they strained public finances once export earnings declined. Critics, including many economists, argued that the purchase of the railways spent foreign exchange that might have supported industrial equipment, while supporters stressed the value of ending foreign ownership of strategic assets. Both arguments have continued to shape Argentine debates.

The expansion of the economic state transformed Argentine life in ways that outlasted Perón. Public ownership of railways and utilities, state-owned enterprises, a large public health system, and active state credit became features of the country's institutional landscape. Subsequent governments of different orientations altered or reversed pieces of the system, but none could ignore the public expectations that Peronist policy had created.

Everyday Life and Social History

Working-Class Households: Wages, Consumption, Housing, and Gender

For many working-class families the Peronist years brought tangible changes. Rising wages, the aguinaldo, and subsidized food allowed households to buy more clothing, furniture, radios, and appliances, and historians such as Eduardo Elena have studied how Peronism encouraged a new idea of dignified consumption as part of citizenship. Vacations organized through unions and the Foundation took families to seaside and mountain resorts, including facilities created for workers, in a country where leisure travel had been a middle-class prerogative. Many families still lived in self-built houses in suburbs lacking paved streets and sewers, and the housing shortage remained severe despite public programs.

Gender shaped these experiences. Men's wages were treated as the family wage, and Peronist social policy often assumed a male breadwinner and a domestic mother, even as many women worked in factories, workshops, and homes. Women's industrial employment remained significant, and domestic service stayed a major occupation, often with weak protection until later statutes. Eva Perón addressed women both as mothers and as political actors, a tension examined in Section 77.12. The sources record household life unevenly, since official and Peronist publications emphasize achievement and opposition publications emphasize manipulation, and historians turn to oral histories, union records, and household budgets to see more.

Division III — Eva Perón and Women's Citizenship · Section 77.9

Eva Perón as a Political Leader: Labor, Public Mobilization, and the Descamisados

Eva Perón's political career began after Perón took office, not in October 1945. As the president's wife she held no official position, but she quickly established an office in the Secretariat of Labor and Welfare, where she met union delegations, heard petitions, and negotiated disputes. She cultivated relations with union leaders and, increasingly, intervened in their internal affairs. Her influence with the CGT was considerable, though its extent and mechanisms are debated: some scholars see her as a mediator who held the movement together, others as a powerful patron who enforced loyalty.

Her public speeches, delivered over radio and at mass rallies, developed a distinctive political language. She spoke as a woman of humble origin who identified with the descamisados and attacked the oligarchy and what she called the enemies of the people. Her style was emotional and personal, in contrast to Perón's more measured oratory, and she presented herself as a bridge between the leader and the masses. She was widely known as Evita, a diminutive that supporters used affectionately and that she sometimes used for herself when addressing workers. Her account of her purposes in the 1951 book La razón de mi vida emphasizes devotion to Perón and the people, and students should read it as political self-presentation as well as testimony.

Eva's distinctive power lay in her informal channels. People wrote to her by the thousands, asking for housing, medical care, jobs, sewing machines, and other help, and her staff responded to many of these requests. She received visitors daily, and her direct, personal responses to petitioners created intense bonds. These networks bypassed bureaucracy and gave her access to local needs, but they also made assistance depend on her personal favor. Historians such as Donna Guy and Marysa Navarro have analyzed these archives as evidence of both the movement's reach and the relations of patronage that sustained it.

Her formal responsibilities were limited, which is crucial to evaluating her role. She was, successively, head of the Foundation and, from 1949, of the Women's Peronist Party, but she was never a minister and never held elective office. Her authority was personal and derived from her relationship to Perón and her popularity. She was also deeply resented by opponents, who attacked her origins, her acting career, and her ostentatious displays of jewelry and clothing, often in sexist and classist terms. Understanding this hostility requires seeing how it combined legitimate criticism of her unaccountable power with prejudice against a woman of her background.

In 1947 Eva traveled to Europe on a widely publicized tour, meeting Franco in Spain, the pope in Rome, and other leaders. The "Rainbow Tour," as it was later called, was intended to burnish Argentina's image and her own, and its reception was mixed, with warm welcomes in Spain and Italy and cooler ones elsewhere. Back in Argentina, the tour added to her prestige among supporters and her notoriety among critics.

Section 77.10

The Eva Perón Foundation, Social Welfare, and the Politics of Assistance

Eva's most substantial institutional creation was a welfare foundation, established in 1948 as the Foundation for Social Aid and later renamed the Eva Perón Foundation. Its origin lay in a conflict with the Society of Beneficence, a traditional charitable body run by women of elite families, which had received state funding and which, according to the usual account, did not offer her its customary presidency in 1946. The government took over the Society's functions, and Eva's Foundation expanded to fill the space. The Foundation worked outside normal governmental channels and was never fully subject to public budgetary oversight.

Its funding came from several sources: donations from unions and employers, contributions from workers, lottery proceeds, and government transfers. Critics noted that donations were often not wholly voluntary, since unions and businesses faced pressure to contribute. The Foundation's accounts were not published in detail, and the lack of transparency has fed accusations of misuse, though investigations after 1955 found less evidence of personal enrichment than opponents had asserted, while confirming a lack of normal accountability.

Its programs were extensive and concrete. The Foundation built and ran hospitals and clinics, including the mobile "health train" that carried medical services to remote regions. It built schools, children's homes, and a "children's city" for the poor, together with vacation facilities, student residences, and housing, including entire neighborhoods. It distributed clothing, food, toys, sewing machines, and other goods, and organized sports competitions for children and young people. Its homes for elderly people and for women arriving in the city from the provinces served groups that public institutions had largely ignored. For many beneficiaries the Foundation's aid was real and life-changing.

The Foundation also served political purposes. Assistance was distributed through networks of Peronist activists, and recipients were encouraged to see aid as the gift of Eva and Perón and not as a right, a contrast with the language of social rights used elsewhere in Peronist doctrine. Opponents, especially Socialists, argued that charity of this kind created dependence and substituted personal favors for institutional rights. Defenders replied that the state's own institutions were inadequate and that the Foundation addressed immediate need. Scholars such as Donna Guy have noted that the Foundation also employed many women and professionalized parts of social work, and that it combined elements of both charity and the welfare state.

Evaluating the Foundation requires holding together its concrete achievements, its patronage functions, and its dependence on Eva's personal authority. After her death it declined, and after 1955 it was dissolved, but the memory of its work remained central to her legacy.

Section 77.11

Women's Suffrage, Law 13.010, and the Expansion of Argentine Citizenship

The campaign for women's political rights in Argentina long predated Eva Perón. Feminists, many of them socialists, anarchists, and professional women, organized from the early twentieth century. Julieta Lanteri, a physician, voted in a municipal election in Buenos Aires in 1911 after successfully claiming citizenship, and later ran for office. Alicia Moreau de Justo, Elvira Rawson de Dellepiane, Sara Justo, and others built organizations and publications. A suffrage bill passed the Senate in 1932 but died in the lower house, and the province of San Juan granted women the vote in provincial elections in 1927. Argentina lagged behind neighbors such as Uruguay (1932) and Brazil (1932) in extending the vote nationally.

Perón and Eva both pledged support for the vote. Eva spoke publicly and over radio in 1946 and 1947, pressing Congress to act. The Senate approved a bill in 1946, and the Chamber of Deputies gave final approval on 9 September 1947. The law, numbered 13.010, was promulgated on 23 September 1947 at a mass gathering at which Eva spoke, and was published in the official bulletin on 27 September. These dates should be kept distinct: passage, promulgation, and publication are different legal steps. The law established equal political rights for men and women and made voting obligatory for both.

Women's first participation in a national presidential election came on 11 November 1951. Registration drives, led by the Women's Peronist Party, enrolled millions of women, and turnout was very high, reportedly around 90 percent. Women were elected to Congress in that election, in numbers that made Argentina's legislature, briefly, one of the more gender-diverse in the world: about two dozen deputies and several senators, nearly all of them Peronists. The results should be read with care: women who entered the legislature were chosen by the party leadership, often as loyal representatives, and they did not form an autonomous women's bloc.

Interpretations differ on how to credit the achievement. Peronists emphasized Eva's role and sometimes presented her as the sole author of women's suffrage. Feminists associated with the pre-Peronist movement argued that their decades of work made the law possible and that the Peronist government appropriated a cause it had not begun. Historians generally acknowledge both: the long campaign created the conditions, the Peronist majority and Eva's mobilization gave the decisive push, and the law had very different meanings for women who were activists and those who were new to politics.

Suffrage did not end gender inequality. The 1949 Constitution recognized equality between men and women in marriage and in political rights, but the Civil Code kept wives under legal limitations, and Peronist discourse often stressed motherhood and family. Women remained underrepresented in leadership, wages for women lagged men's, and the Women's Peronist Party was organized in subordination to Perón. Yet the extension of citizenship was real: for the first time women voted in national elections and held office, and a generation of women, especially working-class women, entered political life.

Section 77.12

The Women's Peronist Party, the 1951 Vice-Presidential Crisis, and Eva Perón's Death

In July 1949 Eva founded the Women's Peronist Party (Partido Peronista Femenino) as a separate branch of the movement, with herself as president. The party built a network of local units across the country, run by women who registered voters, organized meetings, and delivered Foundation services. Many of its organizers were women with little previous political experience. Scholars such as Susana Bianchi, Norma Sanchís, and Carolina Barry have studied how the party offered women a path into politics while keeping them under Eva's and Perón's authority. The party also reproduced an ideal of women's political role as moral and supportive, a more conservative framing than that of earlier feminists.

As the 1951 election approached, Eva's supporters in the CGT and the movement pressed for her to run as Perón's vice president. On 22 August 1951 the CGT staged a huge open meeting, called the Cabildo Abierto, in Buenos Aires, where speakers demanded that she accept the candidacy and the crowd chanted for it. She asked for time. The proposal alarmed much of the military, whose senior officers could not imagine taking orders from her, along with conservatives, the Church, and many within the movement. Perón's own position remains uncertain: sources suggest he was ambivalent and possibly wary.

On 31 August 1951 Eva announced her renunciation on radio, saying she would not accept the candidacy and would continue to serve Perón and the people. Historians still debate whether she renounced freely, under pressure from military and political leaders, or because of her health. She was already gravely ill with cancer, which was diagnosed and treated surgically in the autumn of 1951. She appeared in public on election day, voting from her hospital bed, and was reported to be losing strength.

She died on 26 July 1952 at the age of 33. Congress had conferred on her the title of Spiritual Leader of the Nation, and the country declared a period of mourning. Her body, embalmed by a Spanish pathologist, Pedro Ara, lay in state for about two weeks as an immense crowd filed past, and her funeral drew hundreds of thousands. Official commemoration, the compulsory use of her memoir in schools, and the proliferation of her images deepened the cult around her. Opponents regarded the display as an exercise in manipulation and the grief of her supporters as proof of the movement's irrationality, a view that many historians now reject as condescending.

The competing memories of Eva began immediately and have never ended. For her supporters she was a saint of the poor who gave her life for them; for her opponents she was a demagogue who corrupted the working class; for feminists she is a contested figure who gave women the vote and enforced their subordination. Her body, taken by the military after 1955, was hidden for years, buried abroad under a false name, and returned to Perón in 1971 before finally being placed in the Duarte family tomb in the Recoleta cemetery in Buenos Aires in 1976. That strange afterlife is itself evidence of how much political weight she carried.

Division IV — Political Culture, Constitution, and Opposition · Section 77.13

The Constitution of 1949, Social Rights, and Presidential Authority

The Constitution of 1853, with its liberal guarantees of property, speech, and representation, still governed Argentina when Perón took office. Peronists argued that it reflected the world of the nineteenth-century landowning elite and that a country of organized workers needed a charter recognizing social rights. A constitutional convention met from January to March 1949, and a new text was promulgated on 16 March. The reform required a two-thirds vote in Congress to declare the need for amendment, and the Peronist majority obtained it in a process that opposition deputies disputed, since the procedure for counting the required majority was contested. The Radical delegates withdrew from the convention in protest, and the text was adopted by the Peronist majority alone.

The social content was substantial. Article 37 enumerated rights of workers, including the right to work, a just wage, training, decent conditions, social security, protection of the family, and freedom to organize for the protection of professional interests. Article 37 did not expressly guarantee a right to strike. That omission is historically significant because the government emphasized workers’ social rights while also restraining some strikes and the autonomy of union leadership. It also set out rights of the family, of children, and of the elderly, and provided for education and culture. Other articles declared that property had a social function, authorized state ownership or control of mineral resources, energy, and public services, and restricted foreign ownership of strategic assets. The text affirmed equality of the sexes in political rights and the legal equality of spouses. These provisions gave constitutional form to the claims of the labor movement and were among the most advanced of their kind in the hemisphere at the time.

The reform also strengthened the presidency. The most controversial change was the removal of the prohibition on immediate reelection, which allowed Perón to run again in 1951 and potentially thereafter. Opponents argued that the amendment was designed to prolong the rule of a single man, and they pointed to the manner of adoption as evidence of procedural manipulation. Supporters replied that other democracies allowed reelection and that the majority had the right to amend the constitution. The reform further altered the structure of the executive and the relationship between the president and the provinces in ways that increased central authority.

This pairing of social rights with expanded executive power is central to evaluating the Constitution. The two changes need not be judged identically. A constitution can broaden social protection while also weakening checks on the executive, and historians have concluded that the 1949 text did both. Its social clauses were popular and retained meaning for many Argentines long after the document itself was abolished. The 1955 de facto government, claiming that the 1949 text lacked legitimacy, annulled it and restored the 1853 text in 1956, and a new convention in 1957 added a social-rights clause, Article 14 bis, that preserved much of the earlier content in a different form.

Section 77.14

Peronist Political Culture: Radio, Cinema, Education, Sports, and Mass Rituals

Peronism built a political culture as well as a political machine. The government expanded its control over radio, which reached most households, and over newsreels and cinema, and an undersecretariat of information coordinated propaganda. Radio programs presented the achievements of the government, speeches by Perón and Eva were broadcast widely, and by the early 1950s most radio networks and many newspapers were owned by the state or by Peronist allies. Cinema was subsidized and censored, with films promoting national and Peronist themes. Mariano Plotkin's study of Peronist propaganda shows how schools, ceremonies, and mass media were used to build identification with the leader.

Education was a central site. After 1948 textbooks and curricula introduced Peronist doctrine, and Eva's memoir was required reading in schools. Teachers faced pressure to display loyalty, and students were encouraged to join Peronist youth organizations, including a secondary-student union created in the 1950s. At the same time the government expanded access to schooling, founded a workers' university, and increased technical education, providing opportunities that many working-class families valued. The mixing of expanded access with political indoctrination exemplifies the movement's dual character.

Sports and leisure were also politicized. The government supported mass sports, and Eva's Foundation organized national children's and youth championships. Argentina hosted the first Pan American Games in 1951, an event Peronist propaganda presented as national achievement. Vacation colonies and workers' hotels provided leisure to people who previously had little access to it. Public celebrations, notably May Day and 17 October, became annual rituals in which large crowds gathered in the Plaza de Mayo, listened to speeches from the Casa Rosada, and renewed their bond with the leader. The balcony, the plaza, and the cheering crowd formed the central iconography of the movement.

Interpreting these rituals requires judgment. Supporters understood them as moments of belonging and recognition, in which workers who had been excluded from public life occupied the nation's central space. Many participants joined with enthusiasm, and oral histories confirm their emotional significance. Opponents saw state-orchestrated spectacle, and in the 1950s attendance at some events was encouraged or required in workplaces and public agencies. Both descriptions contain truth: popular enthusiasm was real, and the state organized and amplified it. Historians avoid reducing the culture either to manipulation or to spontaneous expression.

Historical Vocabulary
Justicialismo

Justicialismo (from justicia social, "social justice") was Perón's name for the doctrine of his movement, which he described as a "Third Position" between capitalism and communism, emphasizing social justice, economic independence, and political sovereignty. Peronists called their party the Justicialist Party.

The term is a doctrinal label, and scholars treat it as a flexible political philosophy shaped by Perón's speeches and improvisations rather than as a systematic ideology. In the 1970s Peronists of very different orientations all claimed it, which shows how broad its meaning was.

Section 77.15

Political Opposition, Press Restrictions, Universities, and the Limits of Peronist Democracy

Peronism won elections, and it also constrained those who opposed it. The Radical Civic Union remained the largest opposition party, competing in elections under conditions increasingly tilted toward the government. Socialists, conservatives, Progressive Democrats, and Communists operated with varying degrees of freedom and harassment. Radical leaders such as Ricardo Balbín faced prosecution under a 1949 law on desacato, which criminalized disrespect toward public officials. Balbín was imprisoned in 1950 for remarks made in a speech. Opposition deputies were stripped of their seats or arrested on occasion, and party offices were sometimes raided, while the opposition continued to run candidates and to publish at least some criticism.

The press was the most visible target. The government expanded its holdings of newspapers and radio through allies and purchases, and withheld newsprint, an imported commodity, from critics. The most prominent case was La Prensa, the country's most distinguished independent daily, owned by the Gainza Paz family. After a labor dispute with its newspaper vendors in early 1951, the paper was closed and subsequently expropriated by Congress, and in 1951 it was handed to the CGT, which operated it as a Peronist paper. Its owner went into exile. Defenders of the government pointed to the labor conflict and to the paper's historic elite associations, while critics, including international press organizations, regarded the expropriation as a clear suppression of an independent voice.

Universities were another contested arena. The government intervened in universities after 1946, and professors who opposed the regime were dismissed or resigned in large numbers. Student organizations, which had been mostly anti-Peronist, were harassed. The government expanded access through free tuition and the creation of a workers' university but also demanded political loyalty. The outcome was damaging to the university's reputation for autonomy and drove many leading academics into exile or private institutions, a loss that critics of Peronism have emphasized and that its defenders have often minimized.

The judiciary was affected as well. The 1947 impeachment of most of the Supreme Court and the subsequent appointment of friendly justices set the pattern for a judiciary that rarely checked the executive. Opponents alleged mistreatment of detainees, including torture of some political prisoners in police custody, and accusations of such abuses were investigated by opposition legislators and later by post-1955 commissions, whose own motivations were political. Historians are cautious about the scale of such abuses, which was smaller than in later Argentine dictatorships but not negligible.

Assessing Peronist democracy requires keeping several truths in view. Perón won two presidential elections, in 1946 and 1951, in which opposition candidates ran and received substantial vote shares. In April 1954 the Peronist ticket also won a separate vice-presidential election, along with legislative contests; this was not a third presidential election won by Perón. He also used state power to disadvantage opponents, restrict independent media, and politicize institutions. Neither fact cancels the other. Some interpretations stress the first and describe Peronism as a democratization of Argentine society; others stress the second and describe an authoritarian populism. The historical debate below presents these views.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Peronism Expand Democracy While Restricting Pluralism?

The question: How should historians characterize a government that gave unprecedented social and political inclusion to workers and women, won competitive elections, and also restricted opposition, the press, and institutional checks?

Interpretation A — Peronism democratized Argentina: Scholars such as Daniel James emphasize that Peronism gave previously marginal groups recognition, rights, and political voice. Unions gained bargaining power, women gained the vote, and the working class became a central actor. On this view, the opposition's complaints were often shaped by contempt for the new social groups, and restrictions on opponents must be set against a broader extension of participation.
Interpretation B — Peronism was an authoritarian populism: Scholars influenced by Gino Germani and others stress that a charismatic leader concentrated power, subordinated unions to the state, and limited dissent. On this view, social benefits were granted from above in exchange for loyalty, and the dismantling of checks on the executive undermined the foundations of liberal democracy.
Interpretation C — A hybrid or ambiguous regime: Many recent historians, including Mariano Plotkin and Mark Healey, treat Peronism as combining inclusion and control, in which the same institutions both empowered and restricted. They ask how people at different levels experienced the regime, and they resist choosing one label.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on election results, union records, legislation, newspapers, oral histories, police and court records, and the accounts of opponents and supporters. The same evidence can support different weights, since election results show real majorities while press and court records show restrictions.

Why it matters: The question shapes how Peronism's legacy is read in Argentina, where later movements invoked it for very different ends, and how Latin American populism is understood more broadly. It also bears on whether electoral majorities legitimize restrictions on minorities.

Section 77.16

The Third Position, Argentine Nationalism, and Peronism in International Affairs

Perón presented Argentina's foreign policy as a "Third Position" between the capitalist West and the communist East, a stance intended to give the country independence in a world dividing into blocs. The phrase gained currency from 1947 and was elaborated in speeches and later in a doctrine promoted to other countries. It combined economic nationalism, a claim to political sovereignty, and a rhetoric of social justice. Its appeal rested on the memory of Argentine neutrality and on the sense, widespread in the postwar world, that new states might refuse automatic alignment.

The reality was more complicated than the rhetoric. Argentina remained firmly anticommunist, repressed the Communist Party, and signed the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance (Rio Treaty) in 1947. Relations with the United States were tense after the Braden episode, and Washington restricted exports to Argentina and excluded it from some Marshall Plan purchases. Argentina, hit by the loss of European markets and the blocked convertibility of sterling in 1947, at times sought dollars from the United States and at other times bargained with Europe and the Soviet bloc. By the early 1950s, as economic problems worsened, Perón sought closer economic cooperation with the United States, welcoming foreign investment, and in 1955 his government signed an oil exploration agreement with a U.S. company that provoked nationalist opposition within his own movement.

Regional diplomacy was an arena of ambition. Perón courted neighbors, proposing economic cooperation with Chile, Paraguay, Bolivia, and Brazil in efforts sometimes described as an attempt to build an Argentine-led bloc. Agreements with Chile's President Carlos Ibáñez in 1953 and with Paraguay's government aimed at closer trade. Neighbors often suspected Argentine hegemonic intentions, and Argentine support for friendly governments and exiles raised concern. The Peronist government also provided asylum to individuals fleeing Europe after the war, including some Nazi collaborators and war criminals, a policy documented by later research and investigated by an Argentine commission, and one that remains a serious stain on the regime's record.

The Third Position thus functioned partly as a domestic and regional political language. It helped Perón claim national dignity and mobilize nationalist sentiment. Historians of Argentine foreign relations separate this language from the actual pattern of diplomatic and economic bargaining, in which Argentina acted pragmatically, shifting between the United States, Europe, and its neighbors according to need. The gap between rhetoric and practice is a theme that recurs throughout the Peronist years.

Division V — Economic Crisis, Conflict, and Overthrow · Section 77.17

Inflation, Agricultural Difficulties, Foreign-Exchange Shortages, and the Limits of Peronist Economic Policy

The favorable conditions of 1946–1948 did not last. The recovery of European agriculture reduced demand for Argentine exports, and prices fell. The IAPI's pricing system had discouraged farmers from planting, and agricultural output stagnated. In 1947 sterling was declared convertible briefly and then suspended, trapping Argentina's reserves in Britain. The country had spent heavily on nationalizations, social programs, and imports of capital goods, and its reserves dwindled. Industrial growth required machinery, fuel, and raw materials that had to be imported with the foreign exchange that agriculture earned, and this basic tension strained the model.

By 1949 inflation had accelerated, reaching roughly 30 percent a year, and the government faced a balance-of-payments crisis. Severe droughts in 1951 and 1952 devastated harvests, and in 1952 the country had to import wheat, a humiliating reversal for a leading exporter. Real wages, which had risen strongly, stagnated or fell, and workers pressed for increases through strikes, which the government sometimes repressed, as in the railway dispute. The government responded with adjustments, but the constraints were severe.

In 1952 the government, led economically by Alfredo Gómez Morales, adopted an austerity plan that froze wages and prices for two years, restricted credit, and encouraged agricultural production by raising prices paid to farmers. The Second Five-Year Plan, announced in 1953, shifted emphasis toward agriculture, heavy industry, and foreign investment, and the government invited foreign capital in petroleum and automobiles. The adjustments reflected a recognition that the early model had reached limits, and they involved compromises with the nationalist and redistributive rhetoric of the movement.

The policy shifts worked in important ways. Inflation fell sharply in 1953, the harvest recovered, and the economy returned to growth in 1953 and 1954, with industrial output rising and living standards recovering for many households. This recovery is essential to a balanced account. The Peronist economy did not simply collapse; it experienced a boom, a crisis, and a recovery, and by 1954–1955 economic conditions were no longer the principal cause of the government's difficulties. The political conflicts that brought it down arose from other sources, discussed in the following sections.

Section 77.18

The Election of 1951, Perón's Second Presidency, and the Intensification of Political Conflict

The election of 11 November 1951 returned Perón to the presidency with about 62 to 64 percent of the vote, against roughly 31 to 32 percent for the Radical candidate Ricardo Balbín, with whom the opposition ran an energetic if constrained campaign. Women voted for the first time in a national presidential election, and their votes contributed substantially to Perón's margin. The available sex-disaggregated returns record approximately 3,816,654 women voting, with 2,441,558 voting for the Peronist ticket—about 64 percent of women who voted, not 64 percent of all Peronist voters. Women's turnout was about 90 percent of the female electorate, although published compilations vary modestly in their counting conventions. These figures make the electoral impact of the Women's Peronist Party measurable, while not proving that every Peronist voter joined it. The scale of the victory reflected real support and the advantages of incumbency, including control of media and restrictions on opponents. A failed military uprising on 28 September 1951, led by General Benjamín Menéndez, had shown the existence of military opposition, and Perón's response included a declaration of a state of internal war and arrests.

Perón began his second term in June 1952, weeks before Eva's death. Her loss changed the movement. She had provided an emotional and organizational link between the leader and the base, and her Foundation and Women's Party had to be reorganized under other hands. The Foundation continued under new leadership for a few years but without her energy, and the Women's Party weakened. The CGT, now led by José Espejo and then others, became more prominent as the main institutional link with workers, and relations between union leaders and the president became more formal and sometimes more strained.

Conflicts intensified in 1953. On 15 April 1953, during a CGT rally in the Plaza de Mayo, bombs exploded, killing several people and wounding many others. Peronist crowds responded that night by burning the Jockey Club, the headquarters of the Socialist Party, and the Radical Party committee, while the police did not intervene. The incident marked a moment when violence became an accepted part of the political conflict, and it encouraged opponents who saw the government as tolerating mob attacks, as well as supporters who saw it as threatened by terrorist plots. Perón's speeches grew more confrontational.

The government's relationship with its institutional supporters also shifted. Business groups, which had initially benefited from protection, were concerned about labor power and about the state's growing role. The armed forces were divided: many officers remained loyal, but others resented political indoctrination, the elevation of union leaders, and the role of Eva's cult, while the navy in particular was strongly anti-Peronist. The rank and file of the labor movement remained overwhelmingly supportive, and the government's strength in the working class contrasted with growing alienation among the middle classes, the Church, and sections of the military. Those alignments would determine the crisis of 1955.

Section 77.19

The Church–State Conflict, the Bombing of Plaza de Mayo, and the Crisis of 1955

The Catholic Church had been an ally of the 1943 military government and had welcomed Perón's early policies, including religious instruction in public schools, which became law in 1947. Catholic social doctrine had overlap with Peronist emphasis on social justice and family. By the early 1950s the relationship was deteriorating. The Church resented the movement's cult of Perón and Eva, which it saw as a rival to Catholic devotion, as well as Peronist control of youth and welfare institutions, and the creation of Peronist organizations that competed with Catholic ones. Catholic activists began organizing in opposition, and the Church became a haven for anti-Peronist sentiment.

In late 1954 the conflict became open. The government legalized divorce, repealed compulsory religious instruction, ended some church privileges, and legalized licensed brothels, measures that the Church condemned. Perón attacked clergy he accused of conspiring against him. On 11 June 1955, the feast of Corpus Christi, a large Catholic procession through Buenos Aires turned into an anti-government demonstration, and the government accused participants of burning the national flag, a claim disputed by Catholics. Two Church officials were expelled from the country shortly afterward. Meanwhile, military conspiracies, especially in the navy and parts of the army, were advancing.

On 16 June 1955, naval aircraft attacked the Plaza de Mayo and the Casa Rosada in an attempted coup. The rebels hoped to kill Perón or force his fall, and they bombed the area around the presidential palace in the middle of the day, when the plaza was crowded with office workers and passersby. Some planes carried the slogan "Cristo Vence," Christ Conquers, linking the rebellion to Catholic anti-Peronism. Perón was moved to a safe location, and loyal army units defeated the rebels, many of whom fled to Uruguay. Argentina’s National Archive of Memory records at least 309 fatalities during the bombardment and related fighting and an undetermined number of wounded, with many of the dead being civilians, including people caught in public transportation. The figure is a documented minimum rather than a fully closed casualty count. The assault was among the gravest attacks on civilians in mid-twentieth-century Buenos Aires. Earlier episodes of mass violence, including the Semana Trágica of 1919 and the repression in Patagonia in 1920–1921, produced higher estimated death tolls.

That evening, Peronist crowds, some reportedly encouraged by officials, burned a number of Catholic churches in the capital, including historic buildings, and damaged ecclesiastical property. The government did not prevent the attacks promptly. The sequence is essential: the bombing was a military and naval attack on civilians and the government, and the church burnings were a retaliation by government supporters against an institution they associated with the rebels, with lasting damage to Catholic-Peronist relations. These actions were committed by different groups with different responsibilities and should not be equated, and each is documented.

Perón responded first with conciliation, offering negotiations with the opposition and ending the state of emergency, and then, at the end of August, with a speech that offered his resignation to the CGT and then, after a mass rally demanded he remain, delivered a bellicose address on 31 August calling on supporters to respond violently to opponents. Many historians see this speech as a sign of the dangerous escalation of rhetoric, which pushed hesitant officers toward rebellion. The country was now deeply divided between supporters who feared the loss of their gains and opponents who saw a regime that had resorted to violence and intimidation.

Section 77.20

The September 1955 Coup, Perón's Exile, and the Enduring Legacy of Peronism

On 16 September 1955 a military rebellion began in Córdoba, led by General Eduardo Lonardi, with support from garrisons elsewhere and from the navy, which blockaded the River Plate and threatened to bombard oil facilities. Naval units under Admiral Isaac Rojas controlled strategic installations, and fighting occurred in several places. Much of the army remained loyal at first, but its commanders were reluctant to carry out a prolonged civil war, and Perón, who had armed Peronist civilians in some accounts but declined to unleash them, preferred negotiation. On 19 September Perón sent a message through Army Minister Franklin Lucero suggesting that his own withdrawal might facilitate negotiations and avoid a wider civil war. Senior officers interpreted the ambiguous message as a resignation and formed a military junta, but Perón disputed that interpretation; historians therefore distinguish the military’s treatment of the message from an uncontested, formally submitted constitutional resignation. As the loyalist command disintegrated and negotiations proceeded, Perón sought refuge aboard a Paraguayan naval vessel. He then left Argentina, beginning an exile of seventeen years that took him to Paraguay, Panama, Venezuela, the Dominican Republic, and finally Spain.

The rebels called their regime the Revolución Libertadora, the Liberating Revolution. Lonardi took the presidency on 23 September with a conciliatory approach, promising "neither victors nor vanquished," but he was removed in November by General Pedro Aramburu and a harder-line faction. The new government dissolved the Peronist Party, intervened in the CGT and in unions, removed Peronist officials, imprisoned leaders, and in 1956 banned the use of Peronist names, symbols, and images, including the mention of Perón and Eva. The 1949 Constitution was annulled. Eva's body was seized and hidden, and public monuments were destroyed.

The repression escalated. In June 1956, a failed Peronist military uprising led by General Juan José Valle was suppressed, and the government executed Valle and a number of other officers, and killed civilians at José León Suárez, an event later documented by the journalist Rodolfo Walsh. These actions created martyrs for the Peronist movement. Peronism was banned from electoral politics for most of the next eighteen years, though its supporters adapted: union leaders defended workers' interests, Peronist currents voted blank or supported other candidates under Perón's direction from exile, and a culture of "resistance" developed in workplaces and neighborhoods.

The assessment of the Peronist decade must hold several things together. Peronism delivered substantial social gains, brought unions and women into national politics, and created a sense of dignity among millions of workers. It nationalized key sectors, built extensive social institutions, and set expectations for Argentine states for generations. It also concentrated power, curtailed opposition and press freedoms, politicized institutions, and generated deep polarization that left a legacy of mutual hostility. The government's defeat resulted from a coalition of military, Church, and middle-class opponents who also resorted to violence, and the repression that followed did not eliminate the movement but deepened its loyalties.

The fall of the government therefore did not end Peronism as an organized political and social force. Its base in the unions, its networks in working-class neighborhoods, and its memory of social achievement survived proscription. Perón returned to Argentina in November 1972 and was elected president in 1973, a development belonging to a later history, and he died in office in July 1974. The movement continued to be a central actor in Argentine politics, claimed by quite different currents, and its history shaped the Argentine experience of democracy, dictatorship, and social conflict for decades to come. The question with which this chapter began, how a government that delivered social reform and won elections could fall to a coup, has no single answer, but it must be answered with attention to both Peronism's achievements and its restrictions on pluralism.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Argentina Before and After the First Peronist Era

What Changed

  • Unions became legally recognized national institutions with real bargaining power
  • Workers gained paid leave, pensions, and legal protections
  • Women gained the vote and entered Congress
  • The state took ownership of railways and utilities and expanded health and welfare
  • Politics became organized around Peronism and anti-Peronism

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on agricultural exports and imported capital goods
  • Military intervention in politics
  • Regional inequality between Buenos Aires and the interior
  • Gendered assumptions about work and family
  • Concentrated landholding in the pampas

Who experienced change most directly: Industrial workers, urban women, and recent migrants. Greater continuity: Large landowners in many respects, rural workers in remote areas, and Indigenous communities in the north. Why the pattern matters: The first Peronist era transformed the terms of Argentine politics and social citizenship while leaving the structural economic constraints that later governments, civilian and military, confronted in turn.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Peronism was a historically distinctive movement that combined the expansion of social and political participation with the concentration of governing authority. It was neither simply a democratic social revolution nor simply an authoritarian project, and understanding it requires holding both dimensions in view across the years from 1930 to 1955.

The conservative restoration after the 1930 coup left Argentina with a political order sustained by fraud and military goodwill, while industrial growth and internal migration created a large urban working class that existing parties and unions only partly represented. The gap between society and politics opened the way for new forms of mobilization, though it did not make Peronism inevitable.

The 1943 coup brought Colonel Juan Perón into government, and he used the Secretariat of Labor and Welfare to build an alliance with union leaders. Labor legislation, wage settlements, pensions, and legal protections gave workers concrete benefits, while the legal recognition of favored unions gave the state leverage over the movement. Argentine unions already had their own traditions and leaders, who chose to cooperate with Perón for reasons of their own.

The mobilization of 17 October 1945 was the founding moment. Workers, union activists, and military negotiators combined to secure Perón's return after his removal, and the event became the movement's central ritual. Eva Duarte, whom Perón married days later, was a participant in his life but not the organizer of the day, a distinction the chapter has insisted upon.

Perón won the 1946 election with a majority, reorganized the movement into a single party, and governed with the support of labor. The IAPI, the Five-Year Plan, the nationalization of railways and utilities, and an expanded health and housing program formed the economic and social core of the first government. These policies delivered gains for urban workers but created tensions with agricultural exporters and strained public finances once the postwar export boom ended.

Eva Perón became a political leader through informal channels, the Eva Perón Foundation, the campaign for women's suffrage, and the Women's Peronist Party. Law 13.010, passed in 1947, and the first national vote of women in 1951 extended citizenship, building on decades of feminist work. Her Foundation delivered substantial concrete aid and also built patronage relationships and escaped normal accountability. Her illness and death in July 1952 transformed the movement's leadership and symbolism.

The Constitution of 1949 wrote workers' rights, family protections, and state economic control into the national charter while allowing presidential reelection. Peronist political culture used radio, schools, cinema, sports, and mass rituals to build belonging and loyalty, and these mixed real popular participation with state orchestration. At the same time, the government restricted opposition leaders, the press, universities, and the courts, so that electoral majorities coexisted with a narrowing of pluralism.

The economy moved from expansion to crisis and then recovery. Inflation, drought, and foreign-exchange shortages struck in 1949–1952, and the government adopted austerity and shifted toward agriculture and foreign investment. By 1953–1954 growth had resumed. The government's fall therefore cannot be explained by economic collapse alone.

Political conflict deepened as Church, military, and middle-class opposition grew and as violence entered politics. The bombs of April 1953, the Church–State rupture of 1954–1955, the naval bombing of the Plaza de Mayo on 16 June 1955, and the burning of churches that followed represented actions by different actors with different responsibilities. The September 1955 rebellion ended Perón's government.

The Revolución Libertadora banned Peronist symbols, intervened in unions, and repressed Peronist uprisings, but proscription did not eliminate the movement. Peronism survived in unions, neighborhoods, and memory and returned to power in 1973. Students should take away both the substance of Peronist achievements and restrictions and a method: evaluating a movement that was both popular and coercive requires attention to evidence from supporters, opponents, and those whose voices are least recorded.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown how a mass movement can arise when a political system loses touch with a changing society. Peronism grew from the gap between Argentina's industrial, urban working class and a political order that excluded it, and it succeeded because it offered organization, material benefits, and recognition that workers experienced as real. That experience explains a loyalty that outlasted the government that created it.

The chapter also shows that inclusion and restriction can advance together. The same state that extended pensions, vacations, and the vote narrowed the space for opposition, subordinated unions and institutions, and polarized the country between supporters and enemies. Understanding Peronism means resisting both the legend of a flawless social revolution and the caricature of simple manipulation, and weighing the evidence from supporters, opponents, and those whose experiences are poorly recorded.

Eva Perón's career connects several of the volume's themes: the political incorporation of women, the politics of welfare, and the power of symbol and personality in mass politics. Her contested memory shows how historical figures are remade by later movements. Peronism also belongs to a wider mid-century Latin American pattern of developmental and populist states, alongside Vargas's Brazil (Chapter 76) and Bolivia's revolution of 1952 (Chapter 78), each with its own relation to labor, nationalism, and the military.

The next chapter turns to Bolivia, where a revolution of miners and peasants in 1952 overthrew an oligarchic order and nationalized the country's tin mines. Students should carry forward the questions raised here: who organizes popular mobilization, how states incorporate and control new constituencies, and how a movement survives the loss of the government that gave it institutional form.
