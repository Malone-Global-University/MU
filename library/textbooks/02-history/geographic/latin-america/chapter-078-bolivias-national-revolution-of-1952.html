Bolivia’s National Revolution of 1952

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: When the Army Lost La Paz

On the morning of 9 April 1952 a conspiracy led by civilian politicians and a handful of police and army officers began a coup in Bolivia. Three days later the Bolivian army had surrendered in La Paz, and the conspiracy had become something its planners did not fully control: a social revolution carried by armed workers, miners, and neighborhood fighters.

The fighting centered on La Paz, the seat of government, a city set in a canyon below the high plateau known as the Altiplano. Some of the decisive reinforcements arrived from outside the city. Miners from nearby districts and from Oruro, accustomed to dynamite and to organized union discipline, entered the contest, and the army, which had expected to put down a political plot, found itself fighting a popular insurrection. By 11 April the military command had collapsed. Víctor Paz Estenssoro, the leader of the Movimiento Nacionalista Revolucionario (MNR), who had been in exile in Argentina, returned within days to take the presidency. What followed over the next twelve years, from 1952 to 1964, was one of the most far-reaching programs of social change in twentieth-century Latin America.

This chapter asks a question that the opening scene makes unavoidable: when a revolutionary movement reaches power through organized workers and excluded rural communities, who subsequently controls the state, and who decides the revolution’s direction? In Bolivia the MNR formed the government, but it neither created nor commanded every force that made the revolution possible. Miners had been building unions and political programs for decades. Aymara and Quechua communities had pressed claims about land and justice since at least the early twentieth century, and Indigenous leaders had convened a national congress in 1945. The government that took office in 1952 therefore ruled in the presence of powerful, armed, and politically experienced allies who had their own objectives.

The revolutionary government did a great deal. It extended the vote to Bolivians who had been excluded by literacy and property requirements, including the Indigenous majority and women. It created a state mining corporation and nationalized the great tin enterprises that had dominated the economy and the politics of the country. It issued an agrarian reform decree that ended the hacienda labor regime and legalized the redistribution of estates. It expanded schooling and attempted to draw rural Bolivians into a single national citizenship. Yet it did not produce a stable revolutionary democracy. Inflation, dependence on tin and on United States assistance, conflict among the MNR’s leaders, tension between the government and organized labor, and the rebuilding of the army together fractured the coalition. On 4 November 1964 a military coup, led by officers whose institution the revolution had once nearly destroyed, removed Paz Estenssoro from power.

The chapter keeps five interconnected questions in view. Who made the revolution? Who gained political power? How were land and mineral wealth redistributed? How did state-building interact with Indigenous rights? And why did the revolutionary coalition fracture? Answering them requires moving among several kinds of history: the political history of parties and presidents, the labor history of mining unions and the Central Obrera Boliviana (COB), the rural history of campesino unions and land occupations, the international history of tin markets and Cold War diplomacy, and the social history of households, schools, markets, and mining camps.

The evidence is correspondingly varied, and so are its biases. Government decrees are precise about legal dates and say little about enforcement. Union documents record demands and ideology but often reflect the views of leaders. Diplomatic reports, especially those of the United States, are rich in detail about elite politics and tend to see events through Cold War categories. Memoirs and party histories were frequently written to defend or condemn the MNR. Land titles and rural court records capture campesino action, but only when communities appeared before the state. Scholars such as Laura Gotkowitz and Carmen Soliz have used such records to show that rural people were shaping reform before and after the decrees were signed, and the chapter follows that approach.

The chapter sits within Part XVII, which examines mass politics and developmental states after the Great Depression. It follows the Argentine case of Juan and Eva Perón in Chapter 77, and it precedes the Guatemalan reforms and the 1954 coup in Chapter 79. Bolivia differs from both. The revolution here was an armed insurrection followed by the destruction of the old army, not a government elected on a reform platform, and it was driven in large part by an Indigenous and mining population that had not previously held the vote. Brief comparison with Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, and Guatemala appears where it clarifies the Bolivian case, but the chapter is meant to stand as a complete Bolivian history of the period 1932–1964.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Trace how the Chaco War, military socialism, and the founding of the MNR produced a revolutionary nationalism that developed over time.
  3. Analyze Indigenous and mining mobilization before 1952, including the Catavi massacre, the 1945 Indigenous Congress, and the Pulacayo Thesis.
  4. Explain why the MNR’s plurality victory in 1951 did not lead to a constitutional transfer of power, and how this crisis led to insurrection.
  5. Assess the April 1952 uprising as a broad social revolution that transformed an originally political conspiracy.
  6. Distinguish the formal government, the COB, and armed grassroots organizations, and evaluate the concept of dual power.
  7. Evaluate universal suffrage, nationalization, and agrarian reform by separating legal decrees from enforcement and lived consequences.
  8. Compare the geography of land reform in the Altiplano, the Cochabamba valleys, and the eastern lowlands.
  9. Interpret the relationship between Bolivia, the United States, and international finance without treating either as simply passive or all-powerful.
  10. Explain why the revolutionary coalition fractured and how the rebuilding of the army prepared the coup of 4 November 1964.
  11. Contextualize competing interpretations of the revolution as social transformation, democratic opening, developmental project, or centralized control.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XVII’s broader argument about mass politics and developmental states in Latin America.

Historical Context

Bolivia in the early twentieth century was a landlocked country whose geography divided it. The western highlands, the Altiplano and the valleys of the eastern cordillera, held most of the population and nearly all of the political and mineral wealth. The eastern lowlands, including the Chaco plains and the department of Santa Cruz, were sparsely settled and weakly connected to the Andean core. Silver mining at Potosí had defined the colonial economy, as Chapter 42 described, and tin replaced silver as the engine of the national economy from the late nineteenth century. The independence-era republic, treated in Chapters 55 and 60, had left unresolved a division between a Spanish-speaking political class and a population that was mostly Aymara, Quechua, and speakers of other Indigenous languages.

Republican Bolivia’s political system was narrow. Voting was restricted by literacy and property requirements, and the franchise belonged to a small minority of adult men. Governments alternated between civilian oligarchic parties, including Liberals and Republicans, and military regimes, but their social base was similar: the large mining entrepreneurs, urban professionals, and landowners. The Indigenous rural majority lived largely under the hacienda system or in free communities whose lands had been under pressure since the liberal land policies of the late nineteenth century. The wars of that period, including the Federal War of 1898–1899 in which Aymara leaders such as Pablo Zárate Willka participated, had shown that Indigenous communities could organize politically and militarily, though they did not win lasting rights.

The Great Depression, discussed in Chapter 75, struck a country whose exports depended on a single mineral. Tin prices collapsed after 1929 and the state’s revenues fell, and the government’s fiscal position deteriorated just as border tensions with Paraguay over the Chaco Boreal intensified. Chapters 76 and 77 show that Brazil and Argentina responded to the same global shock with industrialization and mass politics. Bolivia had far less industry and a far narrower domestic market, which shaped what its reformers could attempt. Its crisis took the form of a disastrous war and then a long search for a new national project.

The reform projects of the 1930s and 1940s, in Bolivia as elsewhere, borrowed from many sources. Some nationalists admired Mexico’s revolution and its constitution of 1917, treated in Chapter 73. Others looked to European corporatist and fascist models, and some of the early MNR’s rhetoric, as well as its ties to a wartime military lodge, alarmed the United States during the Second World War. Labor radicals drew on Marxism and Trotskyism. Indigenous leaders and their lawyers and allies drew on republican law, on the language of rights, and on much older traditions of communal landholding and of petition to the state. This chapter treats those streams as distinct, converging only gradually and imperfectly in 1952.

Division I — Bolivia’s Old Order and the Origins of Revolution · Section 78.1

The Tin Oligarchy, Hacienda Society, and the Exclusion of Bolivia’s Majority

Before 1952, Bolivia’s economy rested on tin, and tin rested on a handful of owners. Three enterprises, associated with Simón I. Patiño, Mauricio Hochschild, and Carlos Víctor Aramayo, controlled the great majority of the country’s tin output. Patiño, who had risen from modest origins in Cochabamba to build a fortune in the Llallagua-Catavi district, became one of the richest men in the world and lived largely abroad, directing his holdings through a company registered outside Bolivia. Hochschild’s firm was headquartered in international mining and trading circles, and Aramayo’s family belonged more directly to the Bolivian elite. Because these firms processed and sold much of their ore abroad, a large share of the value created by Bolivian labor was realized outside Bolivia.

The relationship among mine owners, politicians, and the state shaped national politics. Tin exports were the principal source of foreign exchange, and taxes on mining supplied a significant portion of government revenue, but the mining houses were able to resist heavy taxation, and many politicians, lawyers, and newspapers depended on their patronage. Historians use the term oligarchy for the arrangement, though its members did not always agree with one another and governments sometimes tried to extract more revenue. Critics, including nationalist writers such as Augusto Céspedes and Carlos Montenegro, described the system as a form of economic colonialism in which the state served the “superstate” of the mine owners. The label “superstate” was a polemic, and later scholars have shown that governments retained some autonomy, but the phrase captured a widespread perception that mining wealth did not translate into national development.

The mines themselves were harsh workplaces. Miners labored at altitudes above 4,000 meters, often in dangerous, poorly ventilated tunnels, and silicosis shortened lives. Camps such as Catavi and Siglo XX contained company stores, company-controlled housing, and company police. At the same time, the camps concentrated thousands of workers in close quarters, which fostered union organization and a shared political language. Many miners were of Quechua or Aymara origin or had family ties to rural communities, and they moved between mine and countryside, which linked the labor movement to the rural world.

That rural world was dominated by the hacienda. In the highlands and valleys, large estates held much of the best farmland, and the people who worked them were tied by obligations. A colono was a tenant who received the use of a small plot in exchange for labor on the landlord’s fields and for personal services in the landlord’s household, a set of obligations that Bolivians called pongueaje and related terms. The 1950 census indicates that landholding was highly concentrated, with a small proportion of owners holding the overwhelming majority of the land, and that most agricultural land was cultivated at low productivity. Free Indigenous communities, many organized around the ayllu, held land communally or semicommunally but had been under pressure since the late nineteenth century, when liberal governments encouraged the sale of communal lands and the growth of estates.

It would be a mistake to treat the rural population as uniform. Aymara-speaking communities of the Altiplano around Lake Titicaca and of the Oruro and Potosí highlands had different histories from Quechua-speaking communities of the Cochabamba valleys and northern Potosí. Some communities retained autonomous authorities, such as mallkus and kurakas, and defended communal titles through litigation. Others lived on haciendas, and valley communities had a more commercial agriculture and a more mixed population of smallholders and tenants. These differences shaped how people experienced and responded to reform.

Political exclusion reinforced economic hierarchy. Bolivia’s constitutions restricted voting to literate men who met property or income requirements, which excluded the Indigenous majority and all women from national elections. In the early 1950s, the electorate numbered perhaps one hundred thousand or somewhat more people in a country of roughly three million. Spanish was the language of law, schooling, and government, while most rural Bolivians spoke Aymara or Quechua at home. The racial vocabulary of the period distinguished “Indians” from “whites” and “mestizos,” though, as with the casta categories in Chapter 45, those labels reflected status, language, dress, occupation, and local reputation as well as ancestry.

Historical Vocabulary
Pongueaje

Pongueaje (from pongo, a Quechua-derived term for a household servant) was the obligation of hacienda tenants to provide unpaid personal service in the landlord’s house, in the city or on the estate, usually in rotation by family. It belonged to a wider set of labor obligations that colonos owed in exchange for access to a plot.

The term matters because it shows how the hacienda was a system of labor and status as well as land. Abolishing pongueaje, as the Villarroel government attempted in 1945 and as the 1953 agrarian reform did more durably, attacked the personal domination of landlords as well as the pattern of landholding. Students should avoid reading it as simply “rent”: it governed daily life, family labor, and dignity.

Section 78.2

The Chaco War, Military Socialism, and the Crisis of the Oligarchic Republic

The Chaco War of 1932 to 1935 was the central trauma of twentieth-century Bolivian history before 1952. Bolivia and Paraguay both claimed the Chaco Boreal, a vast, thinly populated lowland between them, which had been disputed for decades and which was rumored, with little proof, to hold petroleum. Border clashes escalated into open war in 1932. Bolivia had the larger population, the larger army, and foreign-trained commanders, yet it fought far from its highland bases, across difficult terrain, and with long supply lines. Paraguay, nearer its own railheads and rivers, gradually gained the initiative. The war ended in a truce in 1935, and a later settlement awarded Paraguay most of the disputed territory, though Bolivia retained a portion of the Chaco and access to the area’s oil prospects.

The war was costly. Tens of thousands of Bolivians died, many from disease and thirst as well as combat, and many more were wounded or taken prisoner. The army had drawn conscripts from the highlands and valleys, among them Aymara and Quechua men who had never left their communities and who now served beside officers who commanded in Spanish and often treated them with contempt. Men from different regions and classes shared trenches, hunger, and fear. Historians have debated how far the war created a common national consciousness, and René Arze Aguirre’s study of rural participation shows that communities experienced it in diverse ways, but veterans emerged with a sense that the old governing class had led them badly and owed them something.

The political consequences were large. The defeat discredited the traditional parties and the high command, and a new generation of intellectuals, officers, and labor leaders began to search for alternatives. Writers such as Céspedes and Montenegro, who had served in the war or covered it, argued that Bolivia had been sacrificed to foreign oil and mining interests. Younger officers, some of whom had been prisoners of war, joined secret lodges and talked of reform. Workers’ organizations, already active in the mines and cities, expanded. Rural communities used the language of service in the war to press claims, and veterans’ associations became channels of political demand.

Two military governments gave this ferment institutional form. Colonel David Toro, who took power in 1936, proclaimed a program of “military socialism,” created a ministry of labor, and in 1937 nationalized the holdings of the United States-owned Standard Oil Company in Bolivia, a notable early instance of a Latin American government seizing a foreign petroleum firm, and one that preceded the Mexican oil expropriation discussed in Chapter 74 by about a year. Toro was overthrown in 1937 and replaced by Germán Busch, a younger officer and war hero who presided over a constituent convention. The Constitution of 1938 recognized social rights, including the social function of property and protections for labor, and Busch’s government issued a labor code. In 1939 a decree required mining companies to deliver their export earnings to the central bank, an attempt to increase state control of foreign exchange. Busch died in August 1939, officially by his own hand, and the conservative restoration that followed rolled back many of these measures.

Historians treat military socialism with caution. Its leaders were improvisers, and their rhetoric exceeded their administrative capacity and often their conviction. Yet it demonstrated that a Bolivian government could challenge a foreign firm and legislate social rights, and it supplied precedents that later reformers cited. Revolutionary nationalism, in other words, developed across two decades. It emerged from defeat in war, intellectual critique, labor organization, and experiments in government, and it did not appear suddenly in April 1952. Comparison with Brazil’s Estado Novo in Chapter 76 and with Argentina’s military governments of the 1940s in Chapter 77 shows that officers in several countries drew similar conclusions from the crises of the 1930s, though Bolivia’s officers lacked the industrial base on which Vargas and Perón could build.

Section 78.3

Indigenous Mobilization, Mining Radicalism, and the Emergence of the MNR

The Movimiento Nacionalista Revolucionario was founded in 1941 by a group of middle-class intellectuals and professionals, among them Víctor Paz Estenssoro, Hernán Siles Zuazo, Augusto Céspedes, Carlos Montenegro, and Walter Guevara Arze. Its early program combined economic nationalism, hostility to the tin houses, and a vague anti-imperialism with a social-reform agenda that stressed national integration rather than class struggle. The party was heterogeneous from the beginning. Some members admired European authoritarian movements, and the early MNR drew suspicion from the United States, which in 1944 withheld recognition from the government that included MNR ministers until the party’s officials were removed. Others moved toward democratic socialism or toward alliances with labor. Because of this diversity, the MNR’s politics in power were never uniform.

The mining unions had meanwhile developed their own history. In December 1942, under the government of Enrique Peñaranda, striking workers and their families at the Catavi camp of the Patiño enterprise, who had presented demands for higher wages, were fired upon by troops on 21 December. The number of dead is disputed. Official statements acknowledged a relatively small number, while union and opposition sources claimed far more, and estimates range from dozens to several hundred. The massacre was a founding memory for the labor movement and a political disaster for the government. The MNR, whose deputies denounced the killing in congress, gained credibility among workers. Among the dead, tradition holds, was María Barzola, a mine worker who carried a Bolivian flag, and her name was later adopted by women’s organizations linked to the MNR, the so-called Barzolas.

In December 1943 a coup by the secret military lodge Razón de Patria (RADEPA), in alliance with the MNR, installed Major Gualberto Villarroel as president. The Villarroel government, which lasted until 1946, was neither simply a labor government nor simply a military one. It encouraged the formation of the Federación Sindical de Trabajadores Mineros de Bolivia (FSTMB) in 1944, with Juan Lechín Oquendo as an early leader, and it sponsored labor legislation. It also used repression against opponents, and its enemies accused it of authoritarian tendencies and of responsibility for the killing of political prisoners in 1944. The regime’s ambiguity is important: it opened space for workers and Indigenous communities and also relied on coercion.

The most notable of these openings was the National Indigenous Congress, held in La Paz in May 1945. Delegates from Aymara, Quechua, and other rural communities, many of them colonos from haciendas, traveled to the capital to present demands, among them an end to unpaid labor service, access to schools, and respect for rights. In response, the government issued decrees abolishing pongueaje and related obligations, although landlords resisted and enforcement was weak. Laura Gotkowitz’s study of the period shows that Indigenous leaders did not merely respond to an invitation from the state. They had been building networks through legal petitions, community alliances, and organizing in the valleys and the Altiplano since the 1920s, and they used the congress to place their claims before a national audience. Rural mobilization continued after the congress. In Cochabamba the Ucureña union, formed in 1936, was among the earliest campesino unions, and in 1947 a large rebellion in the province of Ayopaya challenged hacienda domination before being suppressed.

At the same time, the miners were moving toward a more radical position. At a congress held at the mine of Pulacayo in November 1946, the FSTMB adopted a program, now known as the Pulacayo Thesis, that was strongly influenced by the Trotskyist Partido Obrero Revolucionario (POR) and its principal writer Guillermo Lora. The thesis described the miners as the vanguard of the Bolivian proletariat, rejected collaboration with the “bourgeois” state, and called for workers’ control and for armed organization. Its language was far more radical than the MNR’s program. It is worth noting how many currents ran in parallel: the Trotskyist labor radicalism of the Pulacayo Thesis, the nationalism of the MNR, and the land-and-justice movements of Indigenous communities shared opponents but not always objectives, and the relationships among them were often tense.

Section 78.4

The Fall of Villarroel, the Election of 1951, and the Road to Insurrection

On 21 July 1946 a crowd in La Paz, angry about economic hardship and the government’s repression, attacked the presidential palace. Villarroel was seized and killed, and his body was hung from a lamppost in the Plaza Murillo. The episode left a mark on Bolivian political memory and on the MNR, whose leaders fled, were arrested, or went underground. For the next six years a coalition of traditional parties, known as the Sexenio, governed through a series of civilian presidents and persecuted the MNR and the labor movement.

The Sexenio was a period of political repression and of continued opposition. MNR leaders, including Paz Estenssoro, spent much of the period in exile, in Argentina and elsewhere, but the party’s networks survived. The government used force against strikes, including violent suppression of mining strikes in 1949, and the MNR responded by trying to seize power. In 1949 the party led a rebellion, sometimes called the civil war of 1949, which achieved control of several cities for a short time before the army prevailed. The failure taught MNR leaders that a revolt based on party cadres and sympathetic officers was not enough, and it increased their interest in mobilizing the miners and the urban population as a mass force.

The alliance between the MNR and organized miners was therefore the product of experience, not ideology. Lechín and the FSTMB had political and material reasons to cooperate with the MNR, whose leaders could provide political protection and national visibility, while the party needed the miners’ organization and fighting capacity. At the same time, the miners did not become mere followers. Their unions kept independent leadership and a distinct program, and their willingness to cooperate was conditional.

The presidential election of May 1951 was the decisive test. The MNR nominated Paz Estenssoro, still abroad, for president and Siles Zuazo for vice president. The restricted franchise was small, but its size must not be confused with the number of ballots cast: 204,649 people were registered, and 126,123 valid presidential votes were recorded. Paz Estenssoro received 54,129 votes (42.92% of the valid presidential vote), a plurality ahead of the traditional parties rather than an absolute majority. These are retrospective electoral returns preserved in Bolivian historical compilations; they are not estimates of the entire adult population eligible under universal suffrage. Under the constitution, no candidate having won an absolute majority, congress would select the president from among the leading candidates. Observers widely believed that congress, in which the MNR was weak, would not choose the MNR candidate, but the matter never reached that point.

On 16 May 1951 President Mamerto Urriolagoitía, who had presided over the election, announced that he was transferring power to a military junta headed by General Hugo Ballivián. The episode, known as the Mamertazo, was not a coup in the conventional sense: it was an act by an incumbent civilian president who annulled the electoral process by handing authority to the military. The justification given was that the election had been affected by a Communist threat, a claim that served as pretext. The outcome was to block the constitutional succession that the plurality vote had initiated. For many Bolivians the event confirmed that the oligarchic order would not accept an electoral defeat, which made the insurrection of 1952 seem more necessary to the MNR and its allies.

The Ballivián junta governed for about a year. Its repression and its economic policies, which suffered from a decline in tin prices after the Korean War boom, weakened it. Meanwhile, MNR leaders, notably Siles Zuazo, who was in Bolivia, organized a conspiracy with the support of officers, police, and labor leaders. The conspirators approached General Antonio Seleme, the interior minister and a figure close to the Carabineros, who was prepared to cooperate. In early 1952 the plot was well advanced, though its plans were uncertain and its leaders did not agree on tactics. Rising popular mobilization and division within the military created conditions that no one fully controlled.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From the Chaco War and the 1951 Crisis to the April 1952 Uprising

Long-term causes

Concentrated tin wealth and hacienda landholding; a restricted franchise; the Chaco War defeat and the discrediting of the old governing class; decades of Indigenous petitioning and mining unionization.

Short-term causes

The repression of the Sexenio; the failed MNR revolt of 1949; the MNR plurality of 1951 and the Mamertazo; the weakening of the junta by economic difficulties and military division.

Event or transformation

A conspiracy of MNR leaders and officers in April 1952 became a mass insurrection that destroyed the army’s resistance.

Immediate effects

The collapse of military authority in La Paz; the return of Paz Estenssoro; the formation of the COB; the emergence of armed workers’ and campesino militias.

Long-term consequences

Universal suffrage, mine nationalization, and agrarian reform; a new political role for organized labor and rural communities; later conflict over who controlled the state.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains April 1952. The war, the oligarchy’s refusal to accept the 1951 result, and autonomous mobilization from below each mattered, and the outcome depended on the decisions of many actors.
Division II — The April Revolution and the Reconstruction of Political Power · Section 78.5

The Insurrection of April 9–11, 1952: Workers, Miners, and the Defeat of the Army

The uprising began on 9 April 1952. Siles Zuazo and the conspirators launched their action in La Paz, aided by General Seleme and by units of the Carabineros who had been persuaded to join, and they distributed weapons to MNR militants and sympathizers. The plan appeared to rest on a quick seizure of key points in the capital, but within hours it encountered strong resistance from army units loyal to the junta. The government’s forces held positions around the city and used artillery and rifles, and for a time the outcome was in doubt.

The decisive change came from the participation of armed civilians and miners. Workers in La Paz, including factory employees, railway workers, and members of neighborhood groups, took up arms, and fighters from the poorer districts and the hillsides fought house to house. Miners from the nearby districts, notably from the region around La Paz and from Oruro, entered the fighting, equipped with rifles and with the dynamite they used in the mines. In Oruro, where fighting also took place, the combination of armed workers and a decisive military collapse gave insurgents control of the city. In the Altiplano and the mining districts, the news of the fighting led to local seizures of authority.

The fighting lasted three days and was costly. Casualty figures vary, and they are usually given as in the hundreds of dead and a larger number of wounded. By 11 April the army’s command had lost the capacity to continue, and military units surrendered or dissolved. General Ballivián left power, and the junta collapsed. Seleme, who had wavered during the fighting, sought refuge abroad, and the revolutionary leadership took control of the government. Paz Estenssoro, returning from exile through neighboring countries, arrived in La Paz a few days later and was sworn in as president on 16 April.

The meaning of these events has been debated. An older, party-centered account presented the revolution as a successful MNR coup that had mobilized popular forces. More recent scholarship, including the work of Dunkerley and Gotkowitz, emphasizes that the plan of the leaders was only part of the story: it began as a conventional military-political conspiracy, and its success depended on the unplanned mass participation of workers, miners, and residents who were fighting for objectives that included, but extended beyond, the restoration of the MNR. The uprising therefore changed character. What began as an attempt to place a political party in office became a social revolution that destroyed the institution on which the old order had depended, and left armed civilians in positions of power.

Armed popular participation had immediate consequences. After the surrender, civilians held much of the weaponry that the army had possessed, and the government faced the problem of how to treat the thousands of armed workers. Revolutionary militias, whose members considered themselves the real victors, patrolled cities and mining districts. The MNR leadership could claim authority through control of the presidency and of the party apparatus, but it could not simply disarm the people who had won the fighting. This situation shaped everything that followed.

Section 78.6

Paz Estenssoro, the MNR, the COB, and the Struggle over Revolutionary Authority

Víctor Paz Estenssoro was an economist and a skilled politician, cautious by temperament and committed to a program of nationalist reform rather than to socialist revolution. Hernán Siles Zuazo, who had led the uprising, was more popular with the party’s militants. Juan Lechín Oquendo, a charismatic miners’ leader, commanded the loyalty of organized labor. Together, these three men formed a leadership triangle whose members cooperated and competed over the next twelve years. Paz held the presidency from 1952 to 1956, and his government faced the task of reconstructing a state whose old army and administrative apparatus had been broken.

On 17 April 1952 delegates of unions from La Paz and other regions met and founded the Central Obrera Boliviana. The COB claimed to represent all organized workers, and it was dominated by the miners’ federation and its leader. Lechín became its executive secretary. The COB’s creation was not simply a product of the government: workers organized it themselves in the days after the fighting, and it was a vehicle for labor’s independent demands. It soon acquired a distinct political position, supporting the MNR government in many respects while keeping its own program and its own leadership.

The relationship between the COB and the government took the form that Bolivians called co-gobierno, or joint government. Labor representatives entered the cabinet, with Lechín taking the ministry of mines and petroleum, and other labor leaders holding positions in the ministries of labor and of peasant affairs. The arrangement gave organized labor a voice in policy, but it was never clearly defined, and the participants understood it differently. For MNR leaders such as Paz, co-gobierno was a means to bring the unions into a controllable alliance. For many labor leaders and rank-and-file workers, it was a recognition that workers had earned a share of state power.

The armed militias gave labor power beyond its formal positions. Workers’ militias had formed during the fighting and continued afterward, and miners’ militias controlled the mining districts. The pre-revolutionary army had lost much of its strength, and the government depended on militias as well as party forces for security. The COB and the miners’ unions, in turn, controlled stores of weapons and the capacity to mobilize thousands of people. The result was a situation that the Bolivian Trotskyist writer Guillermo Lora and, later, the Bolivian scholar René Zavaleta Mercado described as dual power, in which formal authority belonged to the government but practical coercive power was shared with armed popular organizations. The term comes from Lenin’s analysis of the Russian revolution, and its application to Bolivia has been debated, since the COB did not claim to replace the government and often supported it.

Students should distinguish three levels. The formal government comprised the presidency, ministries, and party structures of the MNR. Union leadership, including that of the COB and the FSTMB, operated through congresses and executive committees. Armed grassroots organizations, including militias and local committees, responded to local leaders who did not always follow the orders of either. Disputes over authority took place among all three levels. Some labor leaders advocated greater workers’ control and a more radical program, while MNR leaders favored a state-directed national development in which labor would be a participant but not a director. The tension would persist through the entire period.

Historical Vocabulary
Co-gobierno and Dual Power

Co-gobierno (“joint government”) was the Bolivian term for the participation of COB and union leaders in the MNR cabinet after April 1952. Dual power is an analytic concept, borrowed from Marxist theory, describing a situation in which two centers of authority, a formal government and an armed popular organization, coexist.

The terms are useful but not interchangeable. Co-gobierno describes a specific political arrangement, while dual power describes a structural situation. Students should avoid assuming that the labor movement sought to overthrow the government: much of the time it sought to push the government toward its own goals. Some historians argue that “dual power” overstates the degree to which labor’s organizations were a coherent alternative authority.

Section 78.7

Universal Suffrage, Political Citizenship, and the Transformation of Bolivia’s Electorate

On 21 July 1952 the government issued Supreme Decree No. 3128 establishing universal suffrage. The date coincided with the sixth anniversary of Villarroel's death, a connection MNR commentators did not overlook. The decree removed the literacy, property, income, and sex-based qualifications that had restricted the national electorate, admitting women and the Indigenous majority. Its age rules were specific: unmarried Bolivians aged 21 or older and married Bolivians aged 18 or older could vote, subject to exclusions enumerated by the contemporary law. This was among the revolution's most consequential acts because it redefined political membership in a country where most people had never voted; universal suffrage did not mean that every adult of every age was immediately eligible.

The scale of the change can be measured by comparing the same electoral categories. In 1951, 204,649 voters were registered and 126,123 valid presidential votes were recorded. In 1956, 1,119,047 voters were registered and 955,349 ballots were cast, according to a historical election series citing the National Electoral Court and national statistical records. Thus the registered electorate grew about 5.5-fold—not eightfold. The increase in ballots cast was larger, but it should not be presented as a directly comparable ratio between 1951 valid votes and 1956 all ballots. Electoral publications differ slightly in their candidate-vote totals, so those figures must be tied to the edition used. The government and party carried out registration through rural and urban committees. Women, who had been permitted to vote in limited municipal elections in the late 1940s, now voted nationally. Indigenous men and women, many of whom spoke little Spanish, were registered, often by local leaders, and registration became a form of political education as well as an administrative act.

The first presidential election under the new franchise took place in 1956. Hernán Siles Zuazo, the MNR’s candidate, won a large majority of the votes cast, well over three-quarters, against the opposition candidate of the Falange Socialista Boliviana, a right-wing party. The result was not a surprise, since the MNR controlled the machinery of registration and was the party associated with the franchise. It confirmed that the MNR had become the dominant party in a mass electorate, and it initiated a period in which elections served to legitimate the governing party more than to choose among competitors.

Historians separate the legal inclusion represented by the decree from the question of political autonomy. Citizenship in law did not mean that new voters acted independently. Many rural voters were organized through campesino unions that were closely tied to the MNR, and local union leaders often distributed ballots and registered members. Government-aligned organizations supplied transport, instruction, and sometimes pressure. At the same time, rural people used the vote and the unions to press their own demands, and they sometimes backed local leaders against the party’s choices. The result was a form of mobilized citizenship that expanded participation without guaranteeing freedom of choice. Opposition parties, particularly on the right, faced harassment, and the Falange attempted insurrections during the 1950s.

Language and literacy remained obstacles. Ballots used symbols and colors as well as names, a practice that allowed people who could not read to vote, but the ability to read official documents and to deal with the administration in Spanish remained unequal. The revolution thus changed the formal basis of citizenship far more quickly than it changed the conditions in which people exercised it. The comparison with Argentina, where the 1947 law passed under Perón and Eva Perón (Chapter 77) extended the vote to women, shows that Bolivia’s change was distinctive in joining the enfranchisement of women with that of an Indigenous and largely non-literate majority.

Section 78.8

The Destruction of the Old Military Order and the Building of a Revolutionary State

The army that had ruled Bolivia in alliance with the oligarchy was effectively defeated in April 1952, and the revolutionary government set about reducing and reorganizing it. Many senior officers were removed or retired, military budgets were sharply cut, and the officer academy was closed for a time. The measures did not abolish the armed forces altogether, and some units remained in being, but the institution lost its political weight and a large part of its personnel. Militias of workers and campesinos took over many of the tasks of public security, particularly in mining districts and in rural areas.

Parallel to the dismantling of the old army, the MNR expanded its own authority through the state. The party appointed its members to ministries, to the administration of departments, and to the management of state enterprises. It created party organizations in neighborhoods and in the countryside, and it relied on its alliance with unions to staff local government and to mobilize support. The Ministry of Peasant Affairs and the system of rural unions were instruments by which the party reached communities that the old state had barely touched. As the state grew, employment in public agencies became a source of patronage, and party membership became important for access to jobs and favors.

Scholars disagree about what these changes created. One interpretation emphasizes broad popular participation: for the first time, workers and peasants had direct access to power through unions, militias, and local committees. A second stresses party domination, in which the MNR used patronage and control of organizations to monopolize political life and to limit opposition. A third points to competing centers of authority, since the COB, the militias, and the party were never completely subordinate to the presidency. The evidence supports elements of all three. Different regions and moments illustrated different patterns, with the mining districts showing strong union autonomy, many rural districts showing tight party-union integration, and the cities showing a mixture.

The revolutionary state also faced a problem that every government confronts: the need for a monopoly of force. A government that depended on militias for security could not easily discipline them, and the militias’ loyalties lay with local leaders and unions. As the economy deteriorated and political conflict increased, MNR leaders, especially those who favored order and development, began to regard the armed popular organizations as a danger. Paz Estenssoro and Siles Zuazo, in different ways and at different times, tried to rebuild a professional military and to bring militias under control. The decision, which seemed prudent from the viewpoint of a government concerned with stability, would eventually have unforeseen consequences, as Sections 78.19 and 78.20 show.

Division III — Nationalization, Agrarian Reform, and Social Revolution · Section 78.9

The Nationalization of the Tin Mines and the Creation of COMIBOL

The revolution’s assault on the economic power of the tin houses took two separate legal steps. On 2 October 1952, Supreme Decree No. 3196 created the Corporación Minera de Bolivia (COMIBOL) as a state corporation to administer the nationalized mines. On 31 October 1952, Supreme Decree No. 3223 nationalized the properties of the three great enterprises, those of Patiño, Hochschild, and Aramayo. The two acts are related but distinct, and students should not conflate them: the first created the institution, and the second transferred the properties. According to most accounts, the nationalization decree was signed in a public ceremony at the mining district of Catavi, a symbolic setting given its association with the massacre of 1942.

The decision was the product of pressure from several directions. The miners’ unions had demanded nationalization, often with workers’ control, for years, and the MNR’s program had long attacked the tin houses. At the same time, the government was cautious. Paz Estenssoro and his advisers feared the consequences of a rupture with the United States and with the international tin market, and they wished to limit the scope of nationalization to the three largest firms, leaving medium and small mines, which produced a significant share of the output, in private hands. The decree thus reflected a compromise between radical demands and the government’s concern for economic stability.

The nationalized enterprises were among the largest in the country. Between them they accounted for most of Bolivian tin output, and the state now owned the sector that provided the bulk of exports and a large portion of government revenue. The decree promised compensation to the former owners, to be determined through negotiation and to be paid in part from future earnings. The owners contested the terms, and settlements were reached over the following years through negotiation, with the Patiño interests the most reluctant. The matter became part of Bolivia’s relationship with the United States and with the international tin trade.

The ambitions of nationalization were large, and its limitations were severe. The state inherited mines that had been worked for decades with limited reinvestment. The richest veins in many districts had been exploited, ore grades were declining, and equipment was aging. Technical and administrative staff, many of whom were foreign or associated with the former owners, left or were replaced, and COMIBOL lacked an experienced management cadre. International tin prices fell after the Korean War boom, and Bolivia sold tin largely as raw concentrate, which was smelted abroad, with limited domestic processing. COMIBOL’s revenues failed to produce the surplus that revolutionaries had hoped would finance development, and in some years the corporation operated at a loss. Nationalization changed who owned the mines but did not alter Bolivia’s dependence on mineral exports.

Historians therefore assess nationalization differently depending on their criteria. As a political act it was transformative: it destroyed a group of private owners who had shaped national politics for half a century and shifted control of the economy’s core sector to the state. As an economic act it was constrained by market conditions, aging infrastructure, and administrative limits. It also complicated labor relations, because the state was now the employer, and a conflict between workers and the state could no longer be understood as a conflict between workers and foreign capital. That change gave the story of the mines a new set of contradictions, examined in the next section.

Section 78.10

Miners, Workers’ Control, and the Contradictions of State-Owned Industry

The miners were political protagonists of the revolution, and nationalization placed them in a new relationship with their employer. The FSTMB, under Juan Lechín, was the strongest union in the country and the core of the COB. Its leaders sat in the government, and its members controlled militias and neighborhood organizations in the mining camps. The union’s influence in the new state enterprise was therefore extensive, and it used that influence to protect wages, jobs, and working conditions.

Workers’ control, control obrero, was one of the most important demands. The idea, which had appeared in the Pulacayo Thesis, meant that workers’ representatives would participate in managing the enterprise. After nationalization, unions obtained rights to place delegates in the administration of COMIBOL, with a power to veto dismissals and to review management decisions. In practice the arrangement varied. At some mines, union delegates had real power over hiring and discipline. At others, they acted mainly as watchdogs, and technical and financial decisions remained in the hands of managers appointed by the government. Historians generally describe workers’ control as limited but influential, since it constrained management far more than it directed it.

The results were contradictory. Workers gained a degree of job security that they had never possessed, along with improved pay and benefits, including access to better housing, schools, and clinics administered by the enterprise. The state abolished the company stores’ monopoly and replaced them with subsidized stores, the pulperías, which supplied basic goods at prices below market levels. These benefits mattered greatly in communities that had lived under company control. At the same time, employment at COMIBOL expanded beyond what the mines’ productivity could justify, as the government and unions sought to protect jobs and absorb returning workers. Productivity declined, costs rose, and the corporation’s finances deteriorated.

The tension at the heart of the system was between two legitimate sets of goals. Workers wanted wages, employment, and a voice in the enterprise. Government officials, including many in the MNR and its technical advisers, wanted investment, financial stability, and a centralized management that could modernize the mines. The conflict could not be resolved by appeal to a shared interest, since both sides had reasonable claims. It was complicated by the fact that mine revenues were also a primary source of government finance and of foreign exchange, so that disputes within COMIBOL reverberated throughout the economy.

The mining camps were communities as well as workplaces. In places such as Siglo XX, Catavi, and Llallagua, families lived in housing owned by the enterprise, children attended schools it operated, and wives and daughters worked in the camps and sold goods in the markets. Women organized in their own associations, among them the housewives’ committees that emerged in Siglo XX in the early 1960s, and they played an important role in strikes and protests by supplying food, organizing support, and confronting authorities. The testimony of Domitila Barrios de Chungara, whose memoir was published in the 1970s, remains an important source for understanding how mining families experienced the period, though it reflects her particular perspective as a union activist’s wife and later organizer. The social world of the mines made unions a form of community as well as a bargaining agency.

Section 78.11

The Agrarian Reform of August 2, 1953, and the End of the Hacienda Order

Rural mobilization, more than any decree, transformed the Bolivian countryside. In the months after April 1952, campesinos in many regions began to form unions, to refuse labor obligations to landlords, and to occupy land. In the Cochabamba valleys, where union organization was oldest, the campesino federation based at Ucureña took the lead, and its leaders, among them José Rojas, mobilized thousands of members. On the Altiplano, in the Yungas, and in other regions, communities invoked the revolution to claim estates, and landlords fled or sought government protection. The government faced a rural movement that it had not created and could not easily stop.

Carmen Soliz, in her study of agrarian reform, shows that the reform was shaped by this action from below. Campesinos pressed claims through unions, wrote petitions, and acted in the fields, and officials responded by drafting rules that legalized and regulated what was already underway. The government appointed an agrarian reform commission, led by Ñuflo Chávez Ortiz, that studied the problem and drafted a decree. The MNR leadership was divided, since some leaders feared that rapid redistribution would harm production and others supported radical change. In the end the government chose to issue a decree that recognized a change that had already, in large part, taken place.

The agrarian reform decree-law, Decree-Law No. 3464, was signed on 2 August 1953 in a ceremony at Ucureña, in the Cochabamba region. The choice of location acknowledged the place of the valley movement in the history of the reform. The decree abolished unpaid personal service and the labor obligations of colonos, declared that land belonged to those who worked it, and provided for the expropriation of large estates that were not worked directly by their owners or that were held in excess of defined limits. It distinguished among types of property, including the large estate or latifundio, the medium property, and the small holding, and it protected the latter two when they met specified conditions of productivity and direct cultivation.

The decree provided for compensation to expropriated landowners in the form of long-term government bonds, calculated on the basis of declared tax values, which were often low, and the bonds lost much of their worth through inflation. It created institutions to administer the reform: a National Agrarian Reform Service, agrarian judges, and a system of agrarian courts to resolve claims. Landowners could appeal, and the system gave local officials considerable discretion. The decree also recognized the rights of Indigenous communities to land, and it created the possibility of restoring lands that had been lost, though that part of the law was applied unevenly.

The abolition of the old labor regime was the most immediate effect. After 1953 landlords could no longer demand unpaid service, and former colonos became smallholders with legal rights to the plots they cultivated. The end of the hacienda order did not mean the end of rural inequality. Reform proceeded slowly, as the agrarian courts were underfunded and overloaded, and the issuance of titles took years. Historians distinguish carefully among legal decrees, rural occupations, actual redistribution, and subsequent landholding patterns, since each is a separate question, and the answers differ by region.

Section 78.12

Indigenous Communities, Campesino Unions, and the Uneven Geography of Land Reform

The lived consequences of agrarian reform differed widely across Bolivia. In the Cochabamba valleys, where haciendas had been numerous and commercial agriculture was well developed, the reform broke up many estates and created a large class of smallholders. Former colonos became owners of plots, and the union structure became a powerful local institution. In the Altiplano, where much land was already held by free communities and where haciendas were concentrated in specific zones, the reform restored or confirmed communal claims in many places and ended the hacienda system in others. The result was a patchwork that varied by district and by community.

Campesino unions became the principal institutions of rural political life. Each community typically formed a sindicato, and these were linked in provincial and departmental federations and, eventually, in a national confederation. The union acquired functions that overlapped with those of older authorities. It allocated land, mediated disputes, organized communal work, and represented the community before the state and the party. In many Aymara and Quechua communities, union leadership was grafted onto existing authority structures, so that traditional office-holders became union officials, or the union adopted rotating offices that resembled older practices. Elsewhere, the union superseded older authority, and generational and political divisions emerged.

The reform generated disputes over the form of land tenure. The government’s language and legal framework emphasized individual smallholders and productive family farms. Many communities, however, valued communal property and collective management, and in some places the reform produced conflicts between those who wanted individual titles and those who wished to protect communal arrangements. Registration of land was slow and often contested, and disputes over boundaries and over local authority continued for decades. Ordinary people used the agrarian courts, sometimes successfully, to press claims against neighbors, former landlords, or other communities, as Soliz documents.

The relationship between citizenship and identity was complex. The government used the term campesino, peasant, to describe rural people, in place of indio, a word that had acquired strong connotations of subordination. The change signaled a desire to include rural people as equal citizens and to emphasize class rather than race. Many Aymara and Quechua people accepted the new designation, since it carried the promise of citizenship, but it also tended to downplay Indigenous identities, languages, and political traditions. Scholars such as Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui have argued that the revolution’s language of campesino citizenship masked the continuation of Indigenous claims and of ethnic distinctions, which would resurface in later decades in movements for Indigenous rights.

The eastern lowlands followed a different course. In the department of Santa Cruz and the Beni, the hacienda system of the Andes was largely absent, and land was held in large estates that used labor in different ways. The reform had less effect on the lowland estates, and in the following years the government encouraged the development of commercial agriculture there, including sugar and cotton, through credit and road building. Large landholders in the lowlands benefited from state support, and the pattern of concentrated holdings persisted and in some respects expanded, which set the stage for later conflicts over land. The revolution therefore broke up the highland hacienda but contributed, through its development policies, to new patterns of concentration in the east, an irony that Section 78.15 returns to.

Historical Vocabulary
Campesino

The Spanish word campesino, “person of the countryside” and usually translated “peasant,” became after 1952 the official designation for rural Bolivians, replacing indio in government usage. The change reflected a political project of class-based citizenship.

The term matters because it both opened and narrowed possibilities. It offered recognition and a language for claiming rights, and it was widely adopted by rural people themselves. At the same time it obscured the distinct histories of Aymara and Quechua communities and could be used to promote assimilation. Students should avoid assuming that “campesino” and “Indigenous” were opposed or identical categories: people used them in different ways, depending on context.

Division IV — Nation-Building, Citizenship, and Bolivia in the Cold War · Section 78.13

Education Reform, Literacy, Rural Schools, and National Integration

Before the revolution, schooling in Bolivia was sharply unequal. Urban schools served a minority, rural schools were few and poorly supplied, and hacienda owners often opposed the education of their tenants. A national census of 1950 recorded that roughly two-thirds of the population could not read, with illiteracy highest among rural, Indigenous, and female Bolivians. Instruction was in Spanish, a language that many rural children did not speak at home, and curricula assumed a national culture in which Indigenous languages and knowledge had little place.

The revolution inherited, and drew on, an earlier experiment. In 1931 the teacher Elizardo Pérez and the Aymara leader Avelino Siñani founded the school at Warisata, on the Altiplano, which combined instruction with community labor, agriculture, and local governance and was administered in cooperation with Aymara authorities. The Warisata model spread to other rural núcleos before it was curtailed in the 1940s by landowners and conservative governments. It demonstrated that Indigenous communities could build schools on their own terms, and its legacy was claimed by later reformers, though the revolutionary government’s policies differed in important ways from the community-centered ideal.

After 1952 the government expanded rural education quickly. It built schools, trained teachers, and created adult literacy programs, often in cooperation with campesino unions that built classrooms and requested teachers. The state assumed responsibility for rural schooling that landlords had once blocked, and former hacienda lands were sometimes used for school sites. Teachers became important figures in rural communities, as intermediaries between the state, the party, and the community, and teachers’ unions became a significant political force.

The Bolivian Education Code, enacted by Decree-Law No. 3937 on 20 January 1955, gave the reform a legal framework. The code defined education as a national function, called for universal and free primary instruction, and distinguished between urban and rural education systems. It aimed to widen opportunity and to build a shared national citizenship, linking schooling to the integration of rural people into economic and political life. The code reflected the aspirations of the revolution: to treat education as a means of ending exclusion and of creating citizens for a mass electorate.

It also raised questions that scholars continue to debate. The code treated Spanish as the principal language of instruction, with Indigenous languages used, at most, as a bridge to learning it. Critics argue that this approach promoted cultural assimilation and undervalued Aymara and Quechua knowledge and identity. Defenders note that Spanish was the language of law, markets, and politics, and that rural parents often wanted their children to learn it. The historian’s task is to recognize both points: schools expanded access and opened opportunities, and they also carried assumptions about national culture that limited the place of Indigenous languages.

Expanded access should be distinguished from universal or culturally equitable education. Enrollment grew substantially during the 1950s and 1960s, and literacy rates improved, but rural schools remained poorer than urban ones, many children left after a few years, and girls attended less than boys. Teachers often lacked training and materials, and in many communities schooling remained incomplete. The revolution did not erase educational inequality, though it changed the terms on which Bolivians could claim schooling as a right.

Women, Families, Market Workers, and Everyday Life during the Revolution

Women took part in the revolution in many roles, though they appear less often in official records than men. Women fought, carried messages, and supplied food in April 1952. They took part in strikes and demonstrations, organized neighborhood committees, and supported unions and campesino organizations. The MNR created a women’s organization, the Barzolas, named after María Barzola, which mobilized women in support of the party in La Paz and elsewhere, and was known for its militancy and its sometimes aggressive tactics against opponents. The organization illustrates how women entered politics under the party’s banner, while also showing how party leaders sought to channel their participation.

Indigenous women in rural areas played central roles in community life and in the new unions. They worked in the fields, managed households, and sold produce in markets, and they participated in meetings and mobilizations, though positions of union leadership were held mostly by men. The cholas, urban Aymara and Quechua women in distinctive dress who dominated the markets of La Paz and other cities, were prominent traders and household heads. Their commercial networks linked the countryside to the cities, and they were a visible presence in political demonstrations, though colonial-era and republican prejudices against them persisted. Market women formed associations and unions that defended their economic interests and, increasingly, influenced local politics.

In the mining camps, women’s work sustained the economy. Wives and daughters of miners cooked, ran small businesses, sold goods, and in some cases worked at surface tasks such as sorting ore, the palliris. They managed family budgets in conditions of low wages and inflation. During strikes and political crises, women organized support, including collective kitchens, and they sometimes took part in direct action, as at Catavi in 1942. Their organizations, such as the housewives’ committees of Siglo XX, formed in 1961, demonstrated that women had their own political agenda, which included prices, health, and the safety of their families.

The revolution changed some aspects of family life. For many families, the end of hacienda obligations, access to land, and wages in state industry raised incomes and improved status. Rural families gained access to schools and, in some districts, to clinics, and miners’ families benefited from subsidized stores and company or state housing and health care. Women gained the vote in national elections and a visible place in public life. Yet inequalities persisted. Legal and cultural norms continued to give men authority in marriage and property, women’s illiteracy rates remained higher, and women were underrepresented in party and union leadership. Agrarian reform tended to register land in the names of male household heads, which could weaken women’s claims, an issue that scholars of rural gender relations have emphasized.

The central point is the difference between the revolution’s language of universal citizenship and the uneven changes in women’s lives. Women’s participation expanded sharply, and the revolution created new possibilities for them. At the same time, those possibilities were shaped by class, ethnicity, region, and family position. A miner’s wife in Siglo XX, a chola trader in La Paz, an Aymara woman in an Altiplano community, and a middle-class party activist experienced the revolution in very different ways, and it is important not to treat “women” as a single group.

Section 78.15

Roads, Regional Development, Urbanization, and the Transformation of Bolivia’s Economic Geography

The revolutionary government inherited a fragmented country. The Altiplano, with La Paz, Oruro, and the mining districts, held most of the population and political power. The central valleys, centered on Cochabamba, were Bolivia’s granary. The eastern lowlands, centered on Santa Cruz, were isolated by geography and had been tied to Brazil and Argentina more closely than to the Andes. The revolution’s planners believed that national integration required new transportation and a shift of economic emphasis toward agriculture and petroleum, and they hoped to diversify an economy dependent on tin.

Road building was central to this effort, but the projects crossed several administrations. Contracts for the Cochabamba–Santa Cruz highway predated the revolution; construction began in 1944 under Villarroel, after earlier planning and contracting. Subsequent governments obtained financing and continued the works. Paz Estenssoro officially inaugurated the road in September 1954, while paving and complementary works continued; the final official acceptance occurred in 1957 under Siles Zuazo. Substantial foreign assistance supported the road program, which connected the lowlands more directly to Andean markets. The Corumbá–Santa Cruz railway toward Brazil was inaugurated in January 1955 after earlier bilateral agreements and construction; inauguration should not be mistaken for completion of every associated work. These connections changed the lowlands’ economy. Sugar, rice, and cotton production expanded with credit and state encouragement, and Santa Cruz grew into a major commercial center. Migration from the highlands and valleys to the east increased, alongside settlement by agricultural communities arriving from abroad.

Petroleum was part of the story. The government adopted the Petroleum Code under Decree-Law No. 4210 of 26 October 1955 (with implementing regulation No. 4298 of 24 January 1956). Developed with outside legal advice, the code opened the sector to foreign investment, and Gulf Oil subsequently obtained a major concession. Oil development, centered in the east, promised revenue independent of tin, and it also created a regional dispute as Santa Cruz leaders demanded a larger share of royalties from resources extracted in their department. Those disputes anticipated regional tensions that grew in later decades, in part because of the revolution's own development priorities.

The revolution thus began to alter the economic geography of Bolivia. Power remained concentrated in La Paz and the western highlands, but the east gained importance, and the new road and rail links began to create a national market. Urbanization continued as people moved to La Paz, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz, and the cities grew with migrants from the countryside, many of them Indigenous. The state’s development priorities, including infrastructure, credit, and agricultural support, favored commercial agriculture in the lowlands and some valley regions, and the highland communities that had gained land through reform received comparatively little investment.

Historians assess the achievements and the costs together. The integration of the east into the national economy was a significant accomplishment that diversified exports and reduced dependence on tin. At the same time, the expansion of commercial agriculture in the lowlands tended to benefit large landholders and well-connected investors, while highland smallholders remained poor. Regional inequality persisted, and in some respects it deepened, as the lowlands prospered and the highlands struggled. Compared with Brazil’s Vargas era in Chapter 76, where industrialization concentrated in the southeast, Bolivia’s development was modest in scale, but it likewise created new regional disparities.

Section 78.16

The United States, Tin Markets, Foreign Assistance, and Cold War Diplomacy

The revolutionary government faced an immediate problem: its main export was tin, and its main customer was the United States. American firms and the American government had bought Bolivian tin during the Second World War and the Korean War, and the United States held strategic stockpiles. The nationalization of the mines raised concerns in Washington about compensation, about Communist influence, and about the security of supply. The MNR government, for its part, needed to keep selling tin, to obtain food and credit, and to avoid a rupture that could damage the economy.

The relationship evolved through negotiation. The United States recognized the Paz Estenssoro government in 1952, and in 1953 the two countries concluded agreements on tin sales and on compensation for the former owners. Under the Eisenhower administration, whose emissaries, including Milton Eisenhower, visited Bolivia in 1953, the United States decided that supporting the MNR was preferable to isolating it, and it began a program of economic assistance that grew through the decade. The aid included food shipments, which addressed shortages but also affected domestic agriculture, technical assistance, and development loans. Bolivia became one of the largest per capita recipients of American assistance in Latin America, and the aid financed much of the government’s budget.

American motives combined strategic and economic concerns. Policymakers worried about Communist influence, particularly through the labor movement and the Trotskyist currents of the miners’ unions, and they hoped to strengthen the moderate wing of the MNR. They also had an interest in keeping tin flowing and in encouraging foreign investment, including in petroleum. The contrast with Guatemala, examined in Chapter 79, is instructive, since the United States overthrew the Guatemalan government in 1954 but accepted and supported the Bolivian revolution. Historians explain the difference by pointing to the MNR’s willingness to negotiate on compensation, its anticommunist declarations, Bolivia’s weak strategic position, and the absence of a United States corporation at the center of the conflict.

James Siekmeier’s research stresses that Bolivian leaders were not passive recipients. Paz Estenssoro and his ministers bargained skillfully, threatening to turn to other sources, invoking the danger of radicalization, and emphasizing their anticommunism to extract assistance. They obtained resources and political room to carry out reforms that the United States might otherwise have opposed. At the same time, aid created dependence and conditions. American officials pressed for fiscal austerity, for reduced labor influence, for the rebuilding of the army, and for an investment-friendly petroleum policy. The aid relationship thus shaped Bolivian choices without simply determining them.

The dependence on tin markets and external assistance became a source of tension within the revolutionary coalition. Labor leaders and left-wing party members resented American influence and the conditions attached to aid, which they saw as a means of restraining the revolution. Moderate and technocratic leaders regarded the assistance as essential. Disagreements over how to deal with the United States, over whether to accept stabilization programs, and over the rebuilding of the military would deepen as the decade went on, and they help explain the fragmentation described in the next division.

Division V — Economic Stabilization, Revolutionary Fragmentation, and the Coup of 1964 · Section 78.17

Inflation, the 1956 Election, and the Siles Zuazo Stabilization Program

By the mid-1950s the revolution’s economic difficulties were severe. Inflation accelerated, driven by government deficits, by the costs of subsidies and expanded employment, by declining mining output, and by the issuance of money to cover shortfalls. The exchange rate became chaotic, with multiple rates and a large gap between official and market values. Food supplies were disrupted by land reform and by transport problems, and shortages and a black market appeared. Real wages fell, which eroded the gains workers had made. By 1956 annual inflation was running at rates that Bolivians experienced as a crisis.

Hernán Siles Zuazo, elected in 1956 under universal suffrage, took office in the midst of this crisis. His government, under pressure from the United States and from international financial institutions, adopted a stabilization program in December 1956. The program was drafted with the help of an American adviser, George Jackson Eder, and was supported by United States financial assistance and by the International Monetary Fund. Its core elements included unification of the exchange rate, removal of many price controls and subsidies, restraint of government spending and of the money supply, and limits on wage increases.

The program succeeded in its immediate monetary aim. Inflation fell sharply in the following years, the exchange rate stabilized, and goods reappeared in markets. Eder himself later wrote an extensive account that presented the program as a success. Yet the social and political costs were large. Wage restraint and the ending of subsidies reduced workers’ living standards, and the government’s effort to limit the labor union’s influence led to open conflict. The COB resisted, and strikes and protests occurred. Siles used political skill, and also the rebuilt elements of the military and loyal militias, to bring the situation under control. The result was a stabilized currency and a weakened labor movement.

Historians distinguish between the monetary success and the political price. The stabilization program restored a degree of economic order, and it prevented further hyperinflation, but it did so by shifting the burden onto workers, and it increased dependence on American aid and on the preferences of the international financial institutions. It marked an important turn in the revolution’s history: the government, facing severe constraints, adopted a policy that its labor allies saw as a retreat from revolutionary commitments. Comparison with the later stabilization programs of the 1980s, discussed in Chapter 93, shows that the dilemma of balancing price stability against social protection would recur in Bolivia and throughout the region.

Paz Estenssoro’s Return, COMIBOL Reorganization, and the Limits of Revolutionary Development

Víctor Paz Estenssoro returned to the presidency in 1960, having served as ambassador in London during Siles’s term. His second administration sought to consolidate the institutional achievements of the revolution and to promote development through state planning. In 1961 a new constitution was promulgated that restored a framework of revolutionary principles, including social rights and state ownership of natural resources, and, importantly for later events, permitted the consecutive reelection of the president. Juan Lechín, the labor leader, served as vice president, an arrangement that represented the continuing alliance between the party and organized labor, though one increasingly under strain.

Mining remained the key problem. COMIBOL’s output had declined and its costs were high, and the state corporation was a drain on the budget. Paz and his advisers concluded that the enterprise needed restructuring, and in the early 1960s the government negotiated a program, known as the Triangular Plan, with the United States, West Germany, and the Inter-American Development Bank. The plan provided financing to modernize equipment, to improve productivity, and to rationalize the corporation’s operations, and it included conditions that called for reducing the labor force and limiting the influence of unions in management.

The plan intensified the conflict with the miners. Workers regarded retrenchment as a threat to their jobs and to the gains of the revolution, and they saw the limits on workers’ control as an attack on one of its principal achievements. Disputes over labor costs, productivity, equipment, and management brought repeated strikes and confrontations. For the government, the miners’ demands seemed to prevent the reform that the enterprise needed. For the miners, the government’s measures seemed to be abandoning the revolution’s promises to workers. Neither side was simply wrong, which is why the dispute proved so difficult to settle.

The problem reflected a deeper tension in the revolutionary project. An expanding developmental state, aiming at efficiency, planning, and growth, required a degree of discipline and centralization. The original expectations of worker participation, which had been central to the miners’ vision of the revolution, pointed in a different direction. As the state became more bureaucratic and as its leaders came to emphasize production and investment, the space for workers’ control narrowed. Scholars such as James Malloy, writing in 1970, argued that the revolution had become “uncompleted,” unable to reconcile mass participation with economic development, though the interpretation has been debated. By the early 1960s, the miners and the government were increasingly at odds.

Section 78.19

The Fragmentation of the MNR, the Rebuilding of the Army, and the Crisis of Revolutionary Power

The MNR had always contained diverse tendencies, and by the early 1960s these had hardened into factions. Paz Estenssoro represented a technocratic, state-directed development, Lechín spoke for labor and the left of the party, Siles Zuazo led a faction that had its own base and ambitions, and other leaders, including Walter Guevara Arze, who left to form a dissident party, had different views. Personal rivalry intensified the divisions. As the party’s common enemy, the old oligarchy, receded, the factions fought over control of the state and its patronage, and over the direction of policy.

Meanwhile the armed forces were being rebuilt. Beginning in the mid-1950s, and with greater intensity under Paz in the early 1960s, the government reopened military academies, increased budgets, and accepted United States military assistance and training, which many officers valued. The rationale was understandable. The government wanted a professional force to maintain order, to counterbalance the armed militias that it no longer fully controlled, and to reduce dependence on partisan forces. Officers who had been junior in 1952 rose in the ranks, and a new generation, trained in part in the United States and in Bolivian academies, took shape. The reconstructed military was loyal to the government, at least in public, but it had interests and a corporate identity of its own.

While the army grew, the unions’ influence declined. Stabilization had weakened the COB, the retrenchment of COMIBOL and the military’s growing role limited the militias, and the rift between Paz and Lechín deepened. The campesino organizations, which had been aligned with the MNR, increasingly looked for patrons who could protect their land gains, and some local leaders developed ties with officers. The result was a political landscape in which the army’s weight grew, the labor movement’s fell, and the party’s unity weakened, a combination that made the government more dependent on the military for its survival.

The presidential election of 1964 brought the contradictions to a head. The 1961 constitution allowed Paz to seek another term, and he did so, over the objections of rivals within the MNR and of the opposition. Lechín, who had hoped to be the party’s candidate, broke with Paz and formed a separate political group, and Siles and others also opposed the president’s reelection. To strengthen his position and to counterbalance Lechín, Paz chose General René Barrientos, the commander of the air force, as his running mate. The ticket won the election held in May 1964, in circumstances disputed by opponents who denounced the process and in which several opposition groups abstained or were weakened.

The central irony of the period is apparent in hindsight. The institutional reconstruction that the government intended to stabilize the revolution created a military force capable of overthrowing the governing party. Paz and his advisers had reason to believe that a professional army, tied to the United States and dependent on the government for resources, would be loyal. Instead, the officers, who disliked the party’s factionalism and its reliance on civilian militias, and who had their own ambitions, saw the political crisis as an opportunity. The vice-presidential choice of an ambitious general placed a potential rival at the center of power.

Section 78.20

The Military Coup of November 1964 and the Historical Legacy of the National Revolution

In the months after the election, political and social unrest increased. Miners protested against government policy, students and opposition groups mobilized, and the party’s internal divisions deepened. Barrientos, who had a political following of his own, particularly in Cochabamba and among some campesino leaders, quarreled with Paz. General Alfredo Ovando Candia, the army’s commander in chief, also became involved in the conspiracy. The sequence of events leading up to the coup is documented in United States diplomatic reporting and in Bolivian accounts, though details differ.

On 3 and 4 November 1964 the military moved. Pro-Barrientos units helped precipitate the rebellion, and the army high command withdrew its support from Paz Estenssoro, who left Bolivia. The military junta that replaced the MNR was initially the subject of a leadership struggle: Ovando announced on 4 November that he would head it, while Barrientos, the sitting vice president and a principal rebel leader, asserted his own claim. Contemporary United States diplomatic reports described this uncertainty and recorded that Ovando allowed Barrientos to assume the junta's leadership later that day, while retaining command of the armed forces. They did not become formal co-presidents in November 1964: the arrangement that made Ovando co-president with Barrientos was instituted on 27 May 1965, beyond the chapter's principal endpoint. The coup thus overthrew a constitutional president through forces within the military institution his revolution had helped reconstruct, including his own vice president.

The end of the revolutionary government should be distinguished from the survival of the revolution’s major changes. The Barrientos government did not restore the hacienda or the tin barons. Land redistribution remained in place, and the new landholders, the campesino unions, and the memory of the reform became bases of political support. Barrientos cultivated the campesinos through what became known as the pacto militar-campesino, an alliance between the military and the rural unions, and he used that alliance to counter the miners. Universal suffrage remained, though elections were suspended or manipulated under military rule. COMIBOL continued as the state mining corporation, and the nationalization of the mines was never reversed. The military regime was harshly repressive toward the miners, with violent episodes in the mining districts in the following years, which show how sharply the relationship between the state and labor had been reversed.

Historians have offered different interpretations of the revolution. One views it as a profound social transformation that destroyed the oligarchy, ended the hacienda, and brought workers and Indigenous communities into national politics. A second sees it as an incomplete democratic opening, in which expanded citizenship coexisted with party domination and the limits of competition. A third treats it as a nationalist developmental project, comparable to those of Vargas and Perón, that sought to build a state-led economy under conditions of dependence. A fourth emphasizes the constraints that shaped the outcome: class conflict, the exclusion and incorporation of Indigenous people on unequal terms, economic dependence on tin and foreign assistance, and the power of the military. These interpretations overlap, and most scholars draw on several.

The closing irony is hard to miss. A movement that came to power by defeating the army, and that armed workers and campesinos, ultimately lost power to a reconstructed military. Yet the changes it had made proved more durable than the government. The Bolivia of 1964 differed profoundly from the Bolivia of 1951: the old tin oligarchy no longer exercised its former direct economic power, many rural households had obtained land and the vote, and the state was a major actor in the economy. The revolution’s legacy would shape the military regimes of the following decades, the labor movement’s long resistance, and the Indigenous movements of the late twentieth century. Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Revolution as Experienced by Six Groups

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom.

MNR leaders: Many saw the revolution as a nationalist modernization that would end oligarchic rule and build a state-directed economy. They were divided over how much power to share with labor and over how far to depend on the United States.
Miners: They had fought and organized for decades and regarded themselves as co-authors of the revolution. They sought wages, job security, and a voice in the mines, and they resisted measures that seemed to reverse those gains.
Indigenous campesinos: Aymara and Quechua communities saw the end of hacienda obligations and the recovery of land as the revolution’s central meaning. They used unions and courts to defend their claims, and many valued the new citizenship while retaining Indigenous identities and practices.
Women: They fought, organized, traded, and voted, and they gained a public presence. Their experiences varied by class, region, and ethnicity, and many legal and cultural inequalities remained.
Landowners: Hacendados lost estates and labor obligations, and many resisted, appealed to the agrarian courts, or left for the cities. Some, particularly in the lowlands, kept their holdings and gained from state support.
Military officers: Those of the old army saw their institution defeated and cut back. A later generation, trained in a rebuilt force, came to regard the party’s factionalism and its militias as threats, and it acted in 1964.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was the National Revolution a Democratizing Social Revolution or a New System of Centralized Political Control?

The question: Did the revolution of 1952 expand popular power and citizenship, or did it replace the oligarchy with a party-state that controlled the people it had mobilized?

Interpretation A — A democratizing social revolution: Scholars such as Dunkerley, Gotkowitz, and Soliz, in different ways, stress the scale of social change: the end of the hacienda system, universal suffrage, nationalization, and the entry of workers and Indigenous communities into national politics. They emphasize that popular actors shaped the revolution and used its institutions for their own ends.
Interpretation B — A new system of centralized control: Other scholars, including Malloy in his study of the “uncompleted” revolution and critics on the left, stress the MNR’s use of patronage, party-controlled unions, and the state apparatus to manage popular participation, and note the limits placed on opposition and on workers’ control. On this view the revolution built a corporatist state that channeled mass mobilization into forms the party could direct.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on decrees, party and union records, diplomatic reports, rural court records, and electoral data. The first relies on evidence of autonomous action, such as land occupations and union demands. The second relies on evidence of party patronage and on the use of union and party structures to mobilize votes.

Why it matters: The question affects how the revolution’s legacy is understood, including how historians interpret later Indigenous movements and the military regimes. Most recent scholarship avoids a simple choice: popular agency and party control coexisted, in different proportions across regions and over time.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Bolivia before 1952 and after 1964

What Changed

  • The tin houses lost their mines, and the state became the principal owner of the mining sector
  • The hacienda labor regime ended, and many rural families held land
  • The electorate expanded from a small literate minority to nearly all adults, including women and the Indigenous majority
  • Unions and campesino organizations became permanent political actors
  • Rural schooling and migration to the eastern lowlands expanded

What Stayed the Same

  • The economy remained dependent on mineral exports and on foreign markets and finance
  • Rural poverty and regional inequality persisted
  • Spanish remained the language of the state and schooling, and Indigenous people faced discrimination
  • The military remained a decisive political institution

Who experienced change most directly: Former colonos and hacienda communities, women who gained the vote, and mining families whose employer became the state. Greater continuity: Highland smallholders with little access to credit, and the people whose participation was limited by language and poverty. Why the pattern matters: The revolution transformed property and political membership far more than it transformed the structure of the economy, and it shows how a social revolution can survive the fall of the government that carried it out.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Bolivia’s National Revolution of 1952 was a social revolution that exceeded the party that led it. The MNR formed the government and directed many of the reforms, but miners, labor unions, Indigenous communities, and rural activists, many of whom had been organizing for decades, made the revolution possible and shaped its outcome. The central problem, who controls the state when a movement comes to power through armed workers and excluded rural communities, structured the entire period from 1952 to 1964.

Pre-revolutionary Bolivia was marked by concentrated mining wealth, a hacienda system that bound Indigenous tenants through labor obligations such as pongueaje, and an electoral system that excluded the majority. The Chaco War of 1932 to 1935 exposed the weakness of the old order, mobilized a generation of veterans, and led to experiments in military socialism under Toro and Busch. Revolutionary nationalism developed over two decades, and it did not appear suddenly in 1952.

The MNR, founded in 1941, was politically diverse and gained credibility through its denunciation of the Catavi massacre of 1942 and its role in the Villarroel government. Meanwhile Indigenous leaders convened the National Indigenous Congress of 1945, and the miners adopted the radical Pulacayo Thesis in 1946. These were distinct currents, nationalism, labor radicalism, and land-and-justice movements, that shared opponents but not always objectives.

After Villarroel’s overthrow in 1946, the traditional parties ruled through repression. In 1951 the MNR won a plurality in an election in which the electorate was small, and the outgoing president transferred power to a military junta, an act known as the Mamertazo. The blocking of the constitutional route made insurrection more likely, and in April 1952 a conspiracy of MNR leaders and officers began an uprising that became a mass insurrection when armed workers and miners defeated the army.

The decree of 21 July 1952 established universal suffrage and enlarged political citizenship: registered voters increased from 204,649 in 1951 to 1,119,047 in 1956, about 5.5 times as many. The two figures refer to registrations, not to votes cast, and legal inclusion did not guarantee political autonomy.

The revolution then transformed property. COMIBOL was created on 2 October 1952, and the major mines were nationalized on 31 October, ending the power of the tin houses but not Bolivia’s dependence on mineral exports. Workers’ control and state ownership produced real gains and also contradictions between labor’s demands and the state’s developmental goals. The agrarian reform decree of 2 August 1953 ended the hacienda labor regime and legalized redistribution, but it followed and was shaped by rural mobilization, and its effects varied across the Altiplano, the valleys, and the lowlands.

Nation-building extended to schools, family life, and economic geography. The Education Code of 1955 expanded access while raising questions about language and assimilation. Women participated widely and gained the vote, though inequalities persisted. Road building and lowland development integrated the east and created new regional disparities. Relations with the United States combined dependence and bargaining, and aid shaped but did not determine Bolivian choices.

From 1956, inflation and the stabilization program strained the alliance with labor, and the Triangular Plan and the COMIBOL reorganization sharpened conflict with the miners. The MNR fragmented among Paz, Lechín, Siles, and others, while the government rebuilt the army that the revolution had defeated. The disputed election of 1964 and the choice of General Barrientos as Paz’s running mate placed a potential rival at the center of power.

On 4 November 1964, Barrientos and Ovando overthrew Paz Estenssoro. The coup ended MNR rule but not the revolution’s legacy: land redistribution, suffrage, state ownership of the mines, and new forms of rural and labor organization survived. Historians interpret the revolution as a profound social transformation, an incomplete democratic opening, a nationalist developmental project, and a process constrained by class conflict, Indigenous exclusion, dependence, and military power, and most draw on several of these views together.

Chapter Reflection

Bolivia’s revolution matters because it shows a social revolution carried out through armed workers and rural communities, and not through a reform government elected on a platform. It destroyed an oligarchy, ended a labor regime that had bound much of the rural population, extended the vote to an Indigenous and largely non-literate majority, and placed the country’s principal industry under state ownership. Compared with the reform governments of Vargas and Perón in Chapters 76 and 77, it altered property relations and political membership more radically, though it commanded far fewer economic resources.

The chapter also shows how a revolutionary state can undermine itself. The MNR weakened the old army, but it rebuilt a military to secure order, and it depended on foreign assistance that carried conditions. Factional conflict and the strain between labor and development weakened the coalition from within. When the army moved in 1964, it did so as a force the government itself had restored, which is a reminder that the institutions a movement builds to consolidate power may later be used against it.

The legacies extended well beyond 1964. Landholding changes, universal suffrage, and public ownership of mining persisted under military rule but remained contested. Campesino unions and miners’ organizations survived as powerful actors, while Indigenous identities that the official language of campesino citizenship had partly subordinated became increasingly prominent in later political movements. Chapter 96, on Mexico's Zapatistas, offers a comparative perspective on Indigenous resurgence; Chapter 108 provides the closer Bolivian continuation through Evo Morales and the Pink Tide.

Chapter 79 turns to Guatemala, where an agrarian reform and a democratic government confronted United States power in 1954 with a very different result. Students should carry forward the contrast: Bolivia’s revolution survived its initial encounter with the Cold War through negotiation and an armed popular base, while Guatemala’s did not. Together, the two cases show that the paths of mid-twentieth-century reform in Latin America depended on domestic coalitions as much as on foreign pressure.
