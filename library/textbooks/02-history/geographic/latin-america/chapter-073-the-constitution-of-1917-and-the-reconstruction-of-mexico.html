The Constitution of 1917 and the Reconstruction of Mexico

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Constitution in a Country Still at War

On 5 February 1917 a constitution was promulgated in Querétaro that promised land to villages, protections to workers, schools free of clerical control, and national ownership of the subsoil. Outside the assembly hall, the country that was supposed to live under it remained divided, damaged, and only partly governed. Could a constitution born from civil war establish a stable political order while fulfilling the competing promises of the Revolution?

The date was chosen, or at least it fell, at a moment of strange coincidence. On the same day that the text was promulgated, the last units of the United States Punitive Expedition were completing their withdrawal from northern Mexico, ending a military presence that had begun in 1916 in pursuit of Francisco Villa. The new document asserted national sovereignty over land, water, and resources at the very moment when Mexican sovereignty had been tested by foreign troops, by foreign investors, and by a world war whose diplomacy was about to reach Mexico City. The Constitution was written by victors, but the victory was incomplete and the world watching it was not neutral.

Chapter 72 explained how the Revolution destroyed the Porfirian order, brought Francisco Madero to power, and then fragmented after Victoriano Huerta's coup into a war among rival revolutionary armies. By 1915 the forces led by Venustiano Carranza, whose strongest field commander was Álvaro Obregón, had defeated the Division of the North and driven the Zapatistas back to their villages. That chapter ended with military outcomes. This chapter asks what the winners did with them. It follows the attempt to turn military predominance into legal authority, the writing of the Constitution of 1917, its social provisions on land, labor, education, and religion, and the prolonged, violent, often improvised effort between 1917 and 1934 to build institutions that could make those provisions mean something.

The chapter's central argument is that the Constitution articulated an ambitious settlement but could not itself enforce it. Constitutional principles moved faster than governments. Villages petitioned for land and met landowners, surveyors, and judges. Unions invoked the new labor guarantees in a country where employers, state governors, and local courts interpreted them differently. Oil companies and foreign governments challenged the claim that the subsoil belonged to the nation. Catholic organizations challenged the legal status of the church. Generals who had won the war were reluctant to accept civilian limits. Presidents and presidents-elect between 1917 and 1934 repeatedly left office, or were prevented from taking it, through violence, rebellion, or the informal power of a figure behind the presidency. Stability, when it came, depended as much on military authority, centralization, patronage, and negotiated alliances as on constitutional procedure.

The chapter's scope therefore runs from the aftermath of the Constitutionalist victories of 1915 to the inauguration of Lázaro Cárdenas on 1 December 1934. It is organized in five divisions of four sections each. The first follows Carranza from military victory to the Constituent Congress. The second examines the social constitution and its major articles in their original 1917 form, which differ in important ways from the heavily amended text in force today. The third examines Carranza's presidency, where implementation first met resistance. The fourth traces the fall of Carranza and the Obregón presidency, including the great educational and cultural experiment of the early 1920s. The fifth follows Plutarco Elías Calles, the Cristero War, the assassination of Obregón, the founding of the Partido Nacional Revolucionario, and the period called the Maximato.

Evidence in this chapter is unusually rich and unusually partisan. The Diario de los Debates of the Constituent Congress records the arguments of delegates, but it was published after the fact and its editing is itself a subject of study. Decrees and presidential messages record intention more reliably than result. Diplomatic correspondence, especially that preserved in United States and British archives, is detailed on oil and investment but reflects the interests of foreign governments. Petitions from villages and unions, parish and church records, school reports, newspaper coverage, photographs, murals, and later memoirs by participants on every side all survive, and each source has a purpose. The chapter's primary source windows rely on original 1917 texts, not on the amended modern Constitution, and they model how historians separate what a document declared from what a government accomplished.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It does not retell Celaya, the Convention of Aguascalientes, or the campaigns against Huerta, which belong to Chapter 72. It stops at Cárdenas's accession. His agrarian redistribution, the oil expropriation of 1938, the transformation of the party into the Partido de la Revolución Mexicana in 1938 and later the Partido Revolucionario Institucional in 1946 belong to Chapter 74. Students should read this chapter as the bridge between revolutionary warfare and the institutional system that Chapter 74 will examine, and as a reminder that the second was not the inevitable outcome of the first.

Historical Context

Chapters 71 and 72 described the regime of Porfirio Díaz, with its concentration of land, foreign capital, railways, and political repression, and the revolutionary upheaval that followed its collapse in 1911. By 1914 the revolution had split along lines that were regional, social, and political. Emiliano Zapata's movement in Morelos demanded the return of village lands taken by sugar estates. Francisco Villa's Division of the North drew on northern ranchers, miners, railway workers, and former estate hands. Carranza, a former Porfirian senator and governor of Coahuila, led a movement that called itself Constitutionalist because its origin was the claim that Huerta's coup had broken the legal order of the Constitution of 1857. That claim gave Carranza legal language and a base among northern politicians, professionals, and military officers, many of them from Sonora, Coahuila, and the Gulf coast.

Carranza's Plan of Guadalupe of March 1913 had been a narrow document, calling for the restoration of constitutional order and not for social reform. As the war against Huerta turned into a war among revolutionaries, Carranza found that a purely legalistic program could not compete for popular loyalty with the agrarian demands of the Zapatistas or the social ambitions of the Convention of Aguascalientes. In December 1914 he added provisions promising reforms to the Plan, and on 6 January 1915 he issued a decree on agrarian restitution, drafted largely by the lawyer Luis Cabrera. Those measures, together with a February 1915 agreement with the Casa del Obrero Mundial that produced the "Red Battalions" of urban workers, were tools for winning a war. They were also the first signs that a Constitutionalist government would need to incorporate social demands in order to govern.

The government of the country in 1915–1916 was a patchwork. The railways, the currency, the courts, and the tax system had been damaged or fragmented. Competing paper money issued by different factions had destroyed confidence in the currency. Agricultural production in several regions had fallen, and food shortages, epidemics of typhus and influenza, and rural insecurity afflicted cities and villages alike. Federal authority over states and localities depended on the personal loyalty of generals who often governed the regions they had conquered. Mexico's population, estimated at roughly fifteen million before the Revolution, declined in the decade of violence, through deaths in combat and from disease, famine, and emigration. Estimates of excess mortality vary widely and should be treated as uncertain.

Mexico's constitutional inheritance was the Constitution of 1857, the charter of the Liberal Reform. It had established a federal republic, guaranteed individual rights, curtailed the corporate privileges of the Catholic Church, and encouraged the individualization of property, which in practice facilitated the loss of village lands. Its liberal vocabulary of equal citizens and private ownership had been used by the Porfirian regime to justify a political order that many Mexicans experienced as dictatorship. The revolutionaries therefore faced a problem of legitimacy. They claimed to restore the Constitution of 1857 yet many of them believed it needed to be changed, because its formal liberalism had not protected workers, villages, or the nation's resources.

Two international contexts shaped the period. The first was the United States, whose forces had occupied Veracruz in 1914 and entered northern Mexico in 1916, and whose companies held large stakes in mining, railways, land, and oil. The second was the First World War, which made Mexico a subject of German, British, and American strategic interest and which raised the value of Mexican petroleum. Britain's Royal Navy was converting to oil, and Mexican production grew rapidly. Foreign governments therefore regarded Mexican constitutional language on the subsoil as a threat to their nationals' property, while Mexican leaders regarded it as an assertion of national sovereignty. Chapter 74 will return to the consequences, and this chapter treats the early stages.

Section 73.1

Carranza's Constitutionalism, the Defeat of Rival Factions, and the Problem of National Authority

By the end of 1915 the Constitutionalists had won the decisive battles against the Division of the North, and the United States had extended de facto recognition to Carranza's government in October. The Convention that had met at Aguascalientes and later in Mexico City had dissolved as a national authority, and Villa's army had been reduced to guerrilla warfare in Chihuahua. The Zapatistas held much of Morelos but lacked the means to threaten the capital. Contemporaries and later historians have therefore usually described 1915 as the moment when the Constitutionalists became the dominant faction. That description is correct, but it needs qualification. Predominance did not mean control.

The Constitutionalist movement was a coalition. Its leaders included lawyers and administrators around Carranza, such as Luis Cabrera and Félix Palavicini, and a group of military and political figures from Sonora, among them Obregón, Calles, and De la Huerta, whose political experience had been shaped in a northwestern state with a prosperous agricultural economy, mining interests, and a tradition of conflict with the Yaqui. It also included regional commanders, such as Pablo González in the northeast, who held authority over their own armies and territories, and it included reform-minded governors who had adopted radical measures in their states. Salvador Alvarado in Yucatán, for example, had issued labor and agrarian decrees and supported a campaign of feminist congresses in 1916. Carranza's legalism and caution therefore coexisted with state-level experiments he did not control.

The weakness of central authority was visible in every domain. Federal finances depended on customs revenue, on forced loans, and on seizures of property, and the paper currency issued by various factions had lost value. Railways, the arteries of the Porfirian economy, had been wrecked by retreating armies, and rolling stock had been captured and redistributed. Inflation drove up prices of food and housing in cities, producing strikes in Mexico City in 1916. In the countryside, military governors ruled regions as virtual proconsuls, and demobilization was difficult because a peasant or workingman who had joined an army had few places to go back to. The Constitutionalist army, which numbered in the tens of thousands, was expensive to maintain and dangerous to disband.

Carranza's decree of 12 December 1914, which added to the Plan of Guadalupe, and his agrarian decree of 6 January 1915 had committed the movement to reform. The January decree declared null the alienations of village lands made in violation of the Ley Lerdo of 1856 and later laws, created national and local agrarian commissions, and provided for the restitution of lands to villages that could prove they had been despoiled and for grants to villages that needed land. It is best understood as a document with two purposes. It answered the Zapatista challenge by offering a legal route to land. It also kept control of the process in the hands of Constitutionalist authorities, who decided which villages qualified. Chapter 72 treated the military context. For this chapter, the decree is a foundation, and the next sections show how it was translated into constitutional language.

Military predominance did not automatically establish legitimacy because Carranza's own justification for the movement was constitutional. The movement had begun by denying that Huerta's government was legal. To claim legitimacy, the Constitutionalists would eventually need to demonstrate that they had restored a legal order, and the Constitution of 1857, whose language they invoked, was both a symbol and an obstacle. Carranza wished to preserve the continuity of the Liberal constitutional tradition while strengthening the executive, which he believed the 1857 charter had left too weak against Congress. Many of his followers wished to go further. Pressure for social reform came from workers' organizations, from agrarian advocates among his own generals, and from the practical need to win the allegiance of rural people who had joined Zapata or Villa. A purely political document would not satisfy them. The convening of a constituent assembly was Carranza's means of regularizing his authority, and he intended to control it.

Section 73.2

The Call for a Constituent Congress and the Politics of Representation, 1916

In September 1916 Carranza issued a decree reforming the Plan of Guadalupe and calling for elections to a Constituent Congress. The timing followed several developments. The immediate military threat from Villa had receded, although Villa's raid on Columbus, New Mexico, in March 1916 and the American Punitive Expedition that followed had shown the vulnerability of the northern border. Industrial strikes in Mexico City in the summer of 1916 had alarmed Carranza, who suppressed them harshly and dissolved the Casa del Obrero Mundial's wartime alliance with the movement. At the same time, pressure had grown for a political settlement that would give the movement a recognizable legal foundation. The call combined these pressures: it offered a constitutional process while defining it narrowly.

The decree provided that delegates would be elected on 22 October 1916, in proportion to population, with one deputy for each seventy thousand inhabitants. The Congress would meet in the city of Querétaro, chosen partly for its security and partly as a symbolic site associated with earlier constitutional events, including the 1867 surrender of Emperor Maximilian. It would open on 1 December, when Carranza would present a draft, and it would have two months to complete its work. These details mattered. The short timetable made it difficult for the Congress to start from scratch, and the election was conducted under conditions of Constitutionalist military occupation.

The most consequential feature of the call was its definition of who could be a delegate. People who had served governments or factions that opposed the Constitutionalist cause were ineligible. This excluded Huerta supporters, as might be expected, but it also excluded the adherents of the Convention of Aguascalientes, which meant Villistas and Zapatistas. The assembly that wrote the nation's constitution therefore contained no delegates from the two movements that had most insistently demanded land reform. Revolutionary representation was defined as representation of the Constitutionalist wing. Historians commonly note the irony that the document credited with embodying the agrarian revolution was written in the absence of its principal agrarian armies.

The election itself was imperfect. Large parts of the country were not under effective government control. Morelos and areas of Chihuahua and other states remained contested, so some districts could not hold elections or had delegates chosen under unusual circumstances. Turnout appears to have been low and the process was administered by military and civilian officials loyal to Carranza. Women could not vote or be candidates, and participation appears to have been concentrated in towns and cities. The delegates who emerged were overwhelmingly men of the middle class, including lawyers, teachers, engineers, journalists, and military officers, and many of them were relatively young. A large number had served in the Constitutionalist army, and the Congress had a significant military bloc.

The call thus embodied a tension that ran through the whole constitutional process. It offered a legal and relatively open forum in which delegates would argue and vote, and the delegates did in fact take that freedom seriously. It also limited the forum so that Carranza's movement retained control over what could be considered constitutional. Students should hold both facts in mind. The Congress was not a rubber stamp, and it was not a representative body of the Mexican nation as a whole.

Historical Vocabulary
Constituyente, social constitutionalism, individual guarantees, and constitutional reform

A constituyente is an assembly called to write or fundamentally revise a constitution, as distinct from an ordinary legislature. Mexico's Congress of 1916–1917 is often called the Congreso Constituyente, although Carranza's decree formally framed its task as reforming the Constitution of 1857. That framing was part of the political design, because it allowed the Constitutionalists to claim continuity while writing a new text.

Individual guarantees (garantías individuales) are the rights of persons against government, such as liberty, due process, and property, which the Constitution of 1857 had enumerated and the 1917 text retained as its first chapter. Social constitutionalism is a modern term for the practice of writing duties of the state toward social groups, such as workers or villages, into a constitution. Mexico's charter is often cited as an early example, although historians debate how far the label describes the original text and how far it was applied later. Constitutional reform is the formal process of amendment, which the Mexican Constitution has undergone many times, so the text students read today is not the text of 1917.

Carranza's September 1916 Decree Calling a Constituent Congress

Creator: Venustiano Carranza, First Chief of the Constitutionalist Army in charge of the Executive Power · Date and place: September 1916 (commonly dated 14 September), issued from Mexico City · Type: An executive decree amending the Plan of Guadalupe · Edition: Published in the Diario Oficial de la Federación; reproduced in the INEHRM collection on the Constituent Congress and the Constitution of 1917

Documentary edition / catalog: 14 September 1916 decree — INEHRM edition · Readable edited transcription.

Context: Carranza issued the decree as military leader of a movement that held power without a constitutional mandate. It reflects his decision to regularize the government through an assembly he could shape.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): The decree changes the earlier plan so that, once the Constitutionalists control the country, the First Chief will call elections to a Congress charged with considering reforms to the Constitution of 1857. It fixes the election date, the proportion of deputies to population, and the meeting place and opening date. It excludes from eligibility those who served governments or factions opposed to the Constitutionalist cause. The First Chief will present a draft of reforms, and the Congress is to complete its work within a short period.

Analysis: The decree reveals how Carranza understood legitimacy: as a continuation of the 1857 constitutional order under his leadership, not as a new beginning. It also shows the exclusion of rival revolutionaries from representation. Its limits are equally important. It states procedure, not outcomes, and does not tell us how elections were actually conducted in contested districts, how many people voted, or what ordinary Mexicans outside the Constitutionalist zone thought of the process. The voices of Villistas, Zapatistas, women, and most rural communities are absent.

  1. What procedures does the decree establish, and who does it exclude?
  2. Why might Carranza have presented the work as reform of the 1857 Constitution?
  3. What can historians learn from the decree about Carranza's conception of legitimate representation?
  4. What can historians not establish from the decree alone about how the election actually took place?
Section 73.3

The Querétaro Congress: Carranza's Draft, Radical Delegates, and Constitutional Debate

The Congress opened on 1 December 1916 in the Teatro Iturbide, a nineteenth-century theater in Querétaro, with Carranza in attendance. The delegates numbered roughly two hundred, and they met in an atmosphere of intense political discussion. Carranza presented his proposed reforms to the Constitution of 1857 in a message in which he criticized the earlier charter for having been too formalistic and for having weakened the executive. The draft kept the federal republican structure, preserved the rights enumerated in 1857, and proposed a number of changes. It strengthened the presidency, abolished the vice presidency, provided for a free municipality, restricted the use of the amparo, and included limited provisions on labor and land. It did not contain the extensive social programs that the final text would include.

The assembly was not divided into formal parties, but contemporaries and historians have identified two broad tendencies. One was a group often called the renovators or moderates, associated with Carranza's closest supporters, including Palavicini and Macías, who favored a constitution close to Carranza's draft with a strong executive. The other was a group often called the radicals, Jacobins, or the left, which included Múgica, Jara, Luis G. Monzón, Esteban Baca Calderón, and others, who believed that the Revolution required constitutional commitments on land, labor, and religion. The labels were not fixed. Delegates shifted positions depending on the question, and several figures associated with the moderate group supported social provisions when the debates reached them. The decisive fact was that the radical tendency was able to build majorities.

Why did that happen? Several explanations are generally given. Many delegates had direct experience of the war and of rural and industrial conflict, and they felt that a constitution that ignored land and labor would repeat the failures of 1857. The Congress was also an arena in which the Sonoran generals' ambitions and Obregón's influence mattered, and Obregón was sympathetic to labor and agrarian measures. Carranza himself lacked the means to impose his draft by force within the Congress, and he was reluctant to break with the delegates openly. Finally, the delegates were working under time pressure and in a context in which Zapata and Villa still claimed to speak for the agrarian revolution, which gave added urgency to the argument that the Constitutionalists had to offer something concrete.

The Congress worked through committees. The first committee, chaired by Múgica, examined constitutional provisions such as those concerning education and religion, and it reported that Carranza's draft was insufficient. The debates were open and lively. On 26–28 December the delegates debated Article 5, which concerned freedom of work and contracts, and in that discussion Jara and Héctor Victoria of Yucatán argued that labor protections belonged in the constitution itself and not merely in ordinary legislation. Macías, who had helped prepare Carranza's labor proposals, supported the principle, and a special group, with Pastor Rouaix prominent, took charge of developing a separate section of the Constitution on labor and social welfare. Rouaix also coordinated the group that drafted what became Article 27.

The debates on education and religion were among the sharpest. Carranza's draft would have retained secular instruction in official schools. The committee proposed stricter language that would also affect private primary schools and religious ministers, and delegates argued about whether the state should regulate or merely refrain from supporting religion. Some delegates cited the historical record of the Catholic Church's alliance with conservative regimes, including support for Huerta, as justification. Others, including some who were not hostile to religion, argued that the new provisions threatened freedom of conscience. The final text sided largely with the committee.

The Congress is therefore best understood as a forum in which Carranza's initial constitutional liberalism was amended by a majority that wished to embed the Revolution's social purposes in the nation's fundamental law. The product was a document that went beyond the First Chief's draft and later complicated his government. That outcome was not planned by any single person. It emerged from debate among delegates of varied backgrounds under the pressure of events.

Section 73.4

The Constitution of 1917: Republican Government, Constitutional Authority, and Revolutionary Legitimacy

The Congress completed its work on 31 January 1917, when delegates approved and signed the text. The Constitution was promulgated on 5 February 1917, and it generally entered into force on 1 May 1917, the day Carranza took office as constitutional president, subject to transitional provisions on elections. These dates matter and should not be collapsed. Approval was the act of the Congress, promulgation was the executive's formal publication, and entry into force depended on the establishment of the constitutional authorities the text itself created. For three months, between publication and May, the country lived in a transitional condition, with Carranza continuing to exercise authority as First Chief.

The new Constitution kept much of the structure of 1857. It declared Mexico a representative, democratic, federal republic composed of states free and sovereign in their internal affairs. It affirmed popular sovereignty and the division of powers among executive, legislative, and judicial branches. It retained the individual guarantees of the first title and the writ of amparo, the Mexican procedure by which individuals could seek court protection against official acts, which had been one of the characteristic institutions of nineteenth-century Mexican constitutionalism. Congress remained bicameral, and the judiciary was headed by a Supreme Court of eleven justices.

Compared with 1857, the Constitution strengthened the executive in several ways. The president could issue regulations, had a veto, and, under Article 29, could with the agreement of the cabinet and Congress or its permanent commission suspend guarantees in cases of emergency. Article 33 authorized the executive to expel foreigners deemed undesirable without a trial. Carranza's own desire for a stronger executive was thus largely fulfilled, although the Congress also preserved legislative powers and declined some of his proposals on the Court and on the amparo. The original Article 83 set the presidential term at four years and prohibited reelection absolutely, a provision rooted in the experience of Díaz's thirty-year rule and reaffirmed Madero's slogan of effective suffrage and no reelection.

The distinctive feature of the document was the incorporation of social obligations into constitutional law. Article 27 declared national ownership of land and waters and of subsoil resources, and provided for agrarian restitution and distribution. Article 123 set labor guarantees. Article 3 required secular education in official schools and regulated private primary instruction, and Articles 24 and 130 regulated worship and the legal position of churches and ministers. Article 115 established the free municipality. These provisions represented the Revolution's attempt to correct what its leaders understood as the failures of Porfirian liberalism. The next division examines them in detail.

Historians distinguish the legal establishment of rights from their enforcement, and the distinction is fundamental to this chapter. A constitution created obligations and legal bases for claims, but its provisions needed enabling laws, administrative bodies, courts willing to interpret them, and political leaders willing to apply them. Many of the Constitution's social articles required later legislation that took years or decades to produce. A federal labor law was not enacted until 1931. Agrarian legislation was issued piecemeal. Land redistribution depended on commissions, governors, and local power. The text was a program as much as a rule of law.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: A Social Revolution Written into Law, or a Legitimation of Military Victors?

The question: Did the Constitution of 1917 represent a genuine social revolution, or was it principally an effort by the victorious Constitutionalist leadership to legitimize its authority?

Interpretation A — A genuine social revolution in law: Some historians emphasize that the Constitution broke with nineteenth-century liberal assumptions by giving the state responsibility for social conditions and by declaring national ownership over land and subsoil. On this view, the radical delegates responded to genuine popular demands and to the experience of the war, and the text provided a legal basis for later reforms, from land redistribution to labor law and, eventually, the oil expropriation of 1938. They argue that the Constitution mattered because it changed what governments could legitimately claim to do.
Interpretation B — A strategy of legitimation and control: Other historians emphasize that the Congress excluded the armies most committed to agrarian reform, that Carranza and his successors used constitutional language selectively, and that social provisions were often invoked to attract support without being enforced. On this view, the Constitution served to legitimize a new elite and to channel popular demands through state-controlled institutions, and its promises helped demobilize rural and labor movements by offering legal routes that governments administered.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the Congress's debates, on the text, and on the record of implementation under Carranza, Obregón, and Calles. Interpretation A relies heavily on the content and long-term influence of the articles and on the pressure from popular movements. Interpretation B relies on the composition of the Congress, on enforcement records, and on the way successive governments used constitutional claims. Most scholars now hold positions that overlap the two, emphasizing that the Constitution was both a response to popular pressure and an instrument of state-building.

Why it matters: The question affects how the postrevolutionary state is understood. If the Constitution was principally a social revolution in law, later failures look like betrayals of an achieved settlement. If it was principally legitimation, the later system looks like a consistent extension of the logic of 1917. A more balanced view treats the document as a contested compromise whose meaning was determined by later struggle.

Section 73.5

Article 27: Land, Natural Resources, National Ownership, and Agrarian Rights

Article 27 is the longest and most consequential of the social provisions. Its first principle was that ownership of lands and waters within the national territory belonged originally to the nation, which had the right to transmit that ownership to individuals and thereby create private property. This formula reversed the liberal assumption that property was a natural right prior to the state. In the constitutional text, private property existed because the nation had conveyed it, and the nation retained the right to impose on it whatever limits public interest required. The article allowed expropriation only for public utility and with indemnification, so it did not abolish private property. It made property subject to social purposes.

The article then listed purposes that the nation could pursue through such regulation. These included dividing large landed estates, developing small property, creating new rural population centers with lands and waters, and protecting agriculture. It also declared that villages, hamlets, and communities that lacked lands or waters, or that had been despoiled of them, were entitled to restitution or to grants taken from adjoining properties. It declared revocable the alienations of village lands made in violation of the law of 25 June 1856 and later measures, which carried forward the restitution logic of Carranza's decree of 6 January 1915. Students should note that the text distinguished between restitution of lands the community could show it had lost and grants of lands it needed, which could be taken from neighboring estates. That distinction became central to every petition.

The second major principle concerned natural resources. The nation held direct ownership of minerals and of substances forming deposits distinct from the components of the soil, expressly including petroleum and hydrocarbons, and this ownership was inalienable and imprescriptible. Private parties could exploit these resources only under concessions granted by the federal government. The language marked a break from the Porfirian mining and petroleum legislation, which had treated subsoil resources as appurtenant to surface property. It created a legal basis for later conflict with foreign companies that held land and had begun drilling before 1917. Importantly, the article established a principle that Cárdenas's expropriation of 1938 would later invoke, but the article itself did not nationalize any company or take any oil property in 1917. That later episode belongs to Chapter 74.

Article 27 also addressed the rights of foreigners and of churches. Only Mexicans by birth or naturalization and Mexican companies could acquire ownership of lands, waters, and concessions, though foreigners could acquire such rights if they agreed to be treated as Mexicans regarding that property and not to invoke the protection of their governments, on pain of forfeiture. This Calvo-style clause, named for the Argentine jurist Carlos Calvo, was central to the diplomatic conflicts discussed in Section 73.12. The article also provided that religious institutions could not acquire, hold, or administer real property, and that church buildings were the property of the nation. That provision revived the logic of the Liberal Reform, and it fed the religious conflict examined in Section 73.18.

Students should keep several concepts distinct. National ownership meant the nation's original title. Private property meant property conveyed by the nation under the Constitution and subject to modalities. Restitution meant returning lands a community could prove it had lost. Expropriation meant taking land for public utility with indemnification. Distribution meant the actual granting of land to villages or individuals through administrative procedures. The Constitution established the first four as legal powers. The fifth required commissions, surveys, decisions by governors, and the cooperation or acquiescence of landowners, and it advanced slowly and unevenly. The article's language also reflected compromise. Zapatista demands, set out in the Plan de Ayala, had called for restitution and for the expropriation of portions of large estates, and some of the Constitutionalist delegates, especially those influenced by Rouaix's group, sought to absorb those ideas. Carranza, by contrast, regarded restitution mainly as a corrective for specific past injustices and was wary of a general redistribution.

Historical Vocabulary
Ejido, agrarian restitution, national domain, and municipio libre

The ejido was historically a common land held by a village, and in postrevolutionary law it became a form of land grant to a community, with use rights for its members and legal restrictions on sale. Its legal characteristics changed over time, and the ejido of 1920 is not identical to the ejido of the 1930s or to the form that existed after 1992 reforms. Agrarian restitution was the return to a village of lands it could prove it had lost, in contrast to dotación, a grant of land to a community that lacked enough.

The national domain (dominio de la nación) refers to the nation's direct ownership of subsoil resources and certain waters under Article 27. The municipio libre, or free municipality, was a revolutionary slogan and constitutional principle under Article 115 that sought to give local governments elected leadership and independent finances, in contrast to the Porfirian system of political prefects appointed from above. In practice, municipal freedom was limited by state governors and by fiscal dependence.

Section 73.6

Article 123: Labor Rights, Working Conditions, Unions, and Social Protection

Article 123 formed its own title of the Constitution, devoted to labor and social welfare. It emerged from the debate over Article 5 and from the Congress's decision that labor protections should be fundamental law. Mexico's industrial workforce was small in proportion to the whole population, but it was concentrated in textile mills, mines, railways, petroleum fields, and port cities, and it had a history of organizing and of conflict, from the strikes at Cananea in 1906 and Río Blanco in 1907 to the wartime activity of the Casa del Obrero Mundial. Delegates cited those experiences when they argued that labor questions could not be left to employers' discretion.

The article set a maximum working day of eight hours and a shorter limit for night work. It prohibited the employment of children under twelve and limited the working day of those between twelve and sixteen to six hours. It required a day of rest after six days of work. It forbade unhealthful or dangerous industrial work for women and for minors at night, provided that pregnant women should not perform heavy physical labor in the months before childbirth and should receive a period of rest after it, and guaranteed nursing mothers breaks during the workday. These provisions reflected prevailing ideas about women's labor, which combined protection with assumptions about women's roles, and they should be read in that light. They offered real safeguards, and they also reinforced the view of women workers as a special category.

Wage provisions included a minimum wage intended to meet the normal needs of a worker as head of a household, equal pay for equal work regardless of sex or nationality, payment in legal currency rather than in vouchers or tokens, and premium pay for overtime. The article provided for workers' participation in profits, for housing, schools, and medical services in large enterprises, and for employer liability for industrial accidents and occupational diseases. It provided for indemnity for unjust dismissal. It also encouraged the establishment of popular insurance institutions, an early statement of social-insurance ideas whose realization came slowly.

The article recognized workers' and employers' rights to organize unions and associations, and the workers' right to strike and employers' right to lockouts under defined conditions. Disputes were to be submitted to boards of conciliation and arbitration made up of equal numbers of worker and employer representatives with a government representative. This design gave the state a central role as mediator. It meant that labor rights would be shaped by the composition and attitudes of boards and by the political relationship between unions and governments. The 1917 text also placed responsibility for labor legislation with the Congress and the state legislatures, so enforcement varied across the country until a 1929 amendment federalized lawmaking and the Federal Labor Law of 1931 provided a national code.

Employers, workers, regional authorities, and judges could interpret the guarantees differently for predictable reasons. Many employers regarded the provisions as burdensome and used delay, legal challenge, and local influence to avoid them. Workers in organized industries could use the article to press claims, especially when they had political allies, whereas agricultural workers, domestic servants, and workers in small shops had little leverage. State governments differed. Veracruz and Yucatán, for example, had labor-friendly administrations that issued laws before and after 1917, whereas other states had weaker enforcement. The article therefore did not establish uniform conditions. It established claims that unions and governments could use, and it created an arena in which the labor movement and the state would negotiate their relationship through the 1920s.

Section 73.7

Articles 3, 24, and 130: Public Education, Religious Liberty, and Church–State Relations

The provisions on education and religion drew on the nineteenth-century Liberal Reform, whose laws in the 1850s and 1860s had disentangled the state from church institutions, nationalized much church property, and established civil marriage. Many revolutionaries regarded the Catholic Church as an ally of the Porfirian order and, after 1913, of Huerta, though the church was not politically uniform. Catholic social movements had also developed in the early twentieth century, including Catholic labor organizations and political groups that sought a reformist role. The Constituent Congress's treatment of religion therefore reflected both anticlerical conviction among many delegates and the Liberal tradition, and it provoked conflict that lasted for decades.

The original Article 3 stated that instruction was free, but that instruction given in official establishments of education would be secular, as would primary instruction, both elementary and higher, given in private establishments. It prohibited religious corporations and ministers of any denomination from establishing or directing primary schools. Private primary schools could be established only under official supervision, and primary instruction in official institutions would be free. Carranza's draft had been narrower, and the committee's text, supported by the radical majority, extended the secular requirement to private schools. Students should not conflate this text with the later socialist education amendment of 1934, which belongs to Chapter 74, or with the modern educational rights language added in subsequent decades.

Article 24 guaranteed freedom of religious belief and the freedom to practice ceremonies in temples or at home, provided they did not constitute an offense under law. It prohibited Congress from enacting laws establishing or prohibiting any religion. It provided that public acts of worship would ordinarily take place in temples and that those held outside would be subject to supervision by the authorities. The article thus protected belief and private and temple worship, while allowing state regulation of public religious life.

Article 130 was the most restrictive. It gave federal authorities power to intervene in matters of religious worship and external discipline, declared marriage a civil contract, and provided that the law did not recognize legal personality in religious institutions called churches. Ministers of worship were treated as persons practicing a profession and were subject to the laws. State legislatures could determine the maximum number of ministers in each locality, and only Mexicans by birth could exercise the ministry. Ministers could not criticize the fundamental laws, the authorities, or the government in public or private meetings, in acts of worship, or in religious publications. They could not vote, be elected, or associate for political purposes. Religious periodicals could not comment on national political affairs, and political parties could not carry names that indicated a religious affiliation. Together with the property restrictions in Article 27, these provisions placed the church in a legally precarious position.

Historians stress that these provisions were not an attack on belief as such and that many Mexicans who supported land or labor reform were devout Catholics. Many revolutionary soldiers and leaders were Catholic. The provisions targeted institutional power, education, property, and political activity. Yet the language gave governments a legal basis to restrict the church sharply whenever they chose to enforce it. Between 1917 and 1926 the provisions were enforced unevenly, with some governors applying them zealously and some federal administrations tolerating considerable church activity. The decisive escalation under Calles came after a change in federal policy and political circumstances, and it must be distinguished from the text itself. Section 73.18 traces that shift.

Section 73.8

Federalism, the Free Municipality, Presidential Authority, and the Limits of Political Citizenship

The political articles built the structure of government. Article 39 declared that national sovereignty resides essentially and originally in the people and that all public power derives from them. Article 40 declared the people's will to constitute a representative, democratic, federal republic. Article 49 divided supreme power among the legislative, executive, and judicial branches. These articles carried forward the formal language of 1857, but Mexican politics in the following years would show how often practice diverged from them.

Federalism was affirmed in principle, with states free and sovereign in their internal regime, yet the Constitution also gave the federal government powers that could limit them. The Senate could declare the disappearance of constitutional powers in a state under certain circumstances, and the president commanded the army and the federal intervention that could accompany a political dispute. Carranza and his successors were repeatedly able to influence state politics. Governors, in turn, remained strong regional figures, often with their own militias and clients. The distribution of power between center and states was thus continuously contested rather than settled.

Article 115 established the free municipality as the basis of the territorial and political organization of the states. Each municipality would be governed by an ayuntamiento elected by direct popular vote, with no intermediate authority between it and the state government, and with the administration of its own finances. This replaced the political prefects who had stood between governors and towns under Díaz and who had been hated symbols of centralized control. In principle, the free municipality offered villages and towns a means of local self-government. In practice, municipalities lacked resources, state legislatures set their taxes, and governors and military commanders intervened regularly. Even so, municipal office became an important arena of local politics, including for villages pursuing land petitions, because municipal authorities could certify claims and represent communities.

The presidency was strong and was bounded by the principle of nonreelection. The original Article 83 provided for a four-year term and forbade reelection absolutely. This reflected the revolutionary rejection of Díaz's personal rule and the lesson that Madero's slogan had expressed. Yet a strong presidency without a mechanism for orderly succession would produce repeated crises. Nonreelection removed the means by which a president might remain in office legally, but it did not provide a method for choosing a successor, so succession remained subject to rivalry among generals, to manipulation by incumbents, and to rebellion. The amendments of 1927 and 1928 that allowed Obregón's return, discussed in Section 73.19, show how quickly the principle came under pressure.

The Constitution proclaimed popular sovereignty while limiting political citizenship. Article 34 defined citizens as Mexicans who had reached eighteen years of age, if married, or twenty-one, if not, and who had an honest means of living. The wording did not state sex explicitly, but the Congress and the election laws applied citizenship to men. Women could not vote or hold national office. Hermila Galindo, a writer, teacher, and collaborator of Carranza who had edited the journal La Mujer Moderna and participated in the feminist congresses of Yucatán in 1916, sent the Congress a petition arguing that women should be recognized as citizens with political rights. She also sought election as a deputy in 1917 in a Mexico City district and, according to accounts, received votes, but she was not seated. The Congress rejected women's national suffrage, and the committee's reasoning reflected assumptions that women were not yet prepared for political participation or that their role was domestic.

Women were nonetheless political actors in these years. They served as teachers, labor organizers, journalists, and members of feminist associations, and in some states women obtained local political rights in the 1920s. Yucatán recognized women's municipal and state electoral participation in the early 1920s under Governor Felipe Carrillo Puerto, and Elvia Carrillo Puerto, his sister, was elected to the state legislature, though the gains were reversed after his assassination in 1924. San Luis Potosí and Chiapas also granted limited rights for a time. National recognition came later, with the municipal vote in 1947 and the right to vote and run for national office by constitutional reform in 1953, first exercised in the federal elections of 1955. The contrast between revolutionary claims about popular sovereignty and the actual boundaries of citizenship, which excluded women, is part of the Constitution's history.

Division III — Carranza's Constitutional Government and the Limits of Reconstruction

Section 73.9

Carranza's Constitutional Presidency and the Reconstruction of Federal Government, 1917–1920

Elections for president and Congress were held in March 1917 under the new constitutional rules, and Carranza won by an overwhelming margin against weak or nominal opposition. He took office on 1 May 1917, the date the Constitution came into general force. The vice presidency, abolished in the new text, no longer existed, and the Congress and the judiciary were constituted under the new framework. The Supreme Court began functioning shortly afterward, with justices chosen through the indirect electoral procedure the Constitution prescribed. After years of rule by decree, Mexico formally had a constitutional government.

The appearance of normality masked fragile foundations. The new government faced severe fiscal problems. Revenue was low, the military budget consumed a large part of what there was, and the discredited paper money of the revolutionary years had to be replaced. In 1916 and 1917 the government moved back toward metallic currency, and the decision stabilized prices to some extent, though supply of coin and the condition of the banking system remained serious problems. Railways, mining, and agriculture were slowly recovering, and many railway lines remained under military control. Reconstruction of federal finance depended heavily on customs duties and on taxes on exports, including petroleum, which meant that the government's solvency was tied to the very foreign-controlled industries that Article 27 challenged.

Carranza's political style shaped the presidency. He was cautious, legalistic, nationalist, and mistrustful of his generals and of the Sonoran group around Obregón. Obregón, who had been his Secretary of War, left the cabinet in 1917 and returned to Sonora, where he cultivated chickpeas and political alliances. Carranza relied instead on civilian advisers and on commanders he considered loyal, and he tried to reduce the autonomy of some regional military leaders by rotating assignments. Governors elected under the new constitutional order were often former revolutionary officers, and many were allies of the Sonoran faction, which meant that the president's formal powers coexisted with substantial regional autonomy. In several states, such as Veracruz and Yucatán, governors adopted agrarian and labor policies that were more radical than the president preferred.

Constitutional forms coexisted with emergency practices. Press freedom was formally protected, but critical newspapers could be harassed. Strikes were treated as threats to order. The army remained large and active, and commanders in conflict zones governed with considerable discretion. The government used the powers of the executive to expel foreigners under Article 33, and it relied on provisions allowing the suspension of guarantees when it considered the situation grave. Historians generally agree that the Carranza government wished to establish civilian constitutional authority but lacked the resources and the political base to do so without relying on military force and patronage.

The relationship between the federal government and the states illustrated the difficulty. Governors and commanders who controlled regions could not be dismissed without risk, and some used federal funds and troops for their own purposes. The government's treatment of properties seized by revolutionary authorities, many of which it returned to former owners to restore production and reassure investors, disappointed agrarian reformers who had hoped for redistribution. In the countryside, the army was dedicated to defeating Zapatistas, Villistas, and other armed groups, and in the cities the government depended on the support of organized workers only intermittently. Carranza's constitutional presidency thus showed that a constitution could establish civilian institutions but that the political resources to make them effective had to come from elsewhere.

Section 73.10

Agrarian Reform in Practice: Land Petitions, Restitution, and Rural Resistance

The January 1915 decree and Article 27 created a procedure that unfolded across many local settings. A village or community seeking land presented a petition, typically to the governor, accompanied by evidence of its need or of past dispossession. Local agrarian commissions examined the case, surveyors measured nearby estates, and the governor could grant provisional possession. The National Agrarian Commission then reviewed the file and recommended a definitive resolution, which the president issued. Each step was an opportunity for delay and for intervention by landowners, politicians, or intermediaries, and communities needed lawyers, surveyors, or political patrons to navigate it.

Communities faced specific obstacles. Many lacked the colonial titles or other documents that would prove dispossession, either because they had been lost, destroyed, or never existed. Surveys required technical personnel who were scarce. Landowners hired lawyers, sought amparo protection, and used political connections to obstruct. Estate owners also manipulated the legal categories, arguing that a community was a mere collection of individuals and not a legitimate village with standing to petition. Boundaries and titles were uncertain after decades of Porfirian land surveys and sales. Petitioners who persisted risked retaliation, including violence from estate guards and local authorities. Meanwhile, definitive grants were rare, and provisional possession could be revoked.

Carranza's own attitude limited the process. He regarded restitution as a way of correcting specific injustices, not as a general redistribution of property, and he feared the economic and political consequences of mass land seizure. Many of the intervened estates were returned to their former owners after 1917. Most tallies place the land definitively granted during his presidency at well under half a million hectares, though historians' figures differ depending on whether provisional and definitive grants are counted. By comparison with the scale of rural demand, the result was small. Yet the procedure had effects beyond its numerical output. It created a legal channel through which villages could claim land, it generated archives of petitions that historians still use, and it gave rural communities a vocabulary and an institutional path for pressing demands.

Regional variation was marked. In Morelos, the Zapatista heartland, the war had devastated the sugar estates and the villages. Federal campaigns under Pablo González in 1916–1917 caused widespread destruction, and census figures suggest the state's population fell sharply, from roughly 180,000 in 1910 to roughly 100,000 by 1921, though wartime counts are uncertain. Villages there pursued restitution in an environment where the Zapatistas had in practice already redistributed land in areas they controlled, and the federal government regarded the movement as an enemy. In the northern agricultural districts of Chihuahua and Coahuila, the large estates of families such as the Terrazas had been confiscated by revolutionary authorities, but their legal future was uncertain, and many were later returned or compensated. In Yucatán, the henequen economy remained dominated by planters, and reforms under Alvarado and later Carrillo Puerto addressed the conditions of workers and the henequen market before land distribution. In Veracruz, Governor Adalberto Tejeda later became associated with an active agrarian program. In Sonora and Sinaloa, commercial agriculture and Yaqui land claims followed their own course. Indigenous communities in Oaxaca, Chiapas, and the Huasteca and Sierra regions pursued claims in the language of community titles.

Rural resistance also took armed and unarmed forms. Villagers petitioned, litigated, occupied land, and sometimes joined armed bands. Landowners formed white guards, and in some regions local caciques allied with federal officers to suppress agrarian activists. The politics of agrarismo, the movement that sought land reform, therefore developed from below as well as from above, and it overlapped with the labor and regional politics of the 1920s. For Carranza, agrarian reform was a tool to be used cautiously. For many villages, it was a right they intended to claim.

Section 73.11

Organized Labor, Employers, and the Politics of Article 123

Wartime inflation shaped labor politics. The collapse of the revolutionary paper money meant that workers paid in depreciated currency saw their real wages vanish, and in 1916 workers in Mexico City demanded payment in metallic currency. Strikes in May and again in the summer of 1916 paralyzed services in the capital. The Casa del Obrero Mundial, an anarcho-syndicalist center that in 1915 had made an agreement with the Constitutionalists and provided the Red Battalions, led the movement. Carranza responded with force. He had strike leaders arrested and invoked an 1862 law that carried the death penalty for disturbing public order, and the Casa was shut down. The episode occurred while the Constituent Congress was being organized, and it left a legacy of distrust between the president and organized labor, even as the Congress wrote a sweeping labor article.

Labor organization continued under constitutional government. Strikes occurred in textiles, mining, railways, the petroleum fields, and port cities, and unions used the new constitutional language to support their claims. Employers resisted. In Veracruz and Yucatán, state governments passed labor laws that gave unions greater protection. In 1918, a congress held in Saltillo, Coahuila, with the sponsorship of Governor Gustavo Espinosa Mireles, founded the Confederación Regional Obrera Mexicana, the CROM. Its leading figure was Luis N. Morones, a former electrician and union organizer with a talent for political bargaining. The CROM was reformist and pragmatic, in contrast to the anarcho-syndicalist tradition of the Casa, and it was willing to ally with governments to win benefits for its members.

The relationship between the CROM and the political leadership developed gradually. Carranza distrusted organized labor, and the CROM began to look for allies among his rivals. In 1919, Morones and Obregón reached an understanding, widely referred to as a pact, under which the CROM's political arm, the Partido Laborista Mexicano, supported Obregón's candidacy in return for expected support for labor's interests. This alliance would become a pillar of the Sonoran governments of the 1920s. It brought the CROM access to government, to labor boards, and, under Calles, to posts including Morones's tenure as Secretary of Industry, Commerce, and Labor. It also made the CROM a tool of governments to a degree that critics, including rival unions, bitterly condemned.

Rival currents persisted. The anarcho-syndicalist tradition survived in the Confederación General de Trabajadores, founded in 1921, which rejected collaboration with the state and was repressed, especially after strikes in the early 1920s. Railway workers, miners, and oil workers had their own organizations and conflicts. In cities, labor conflict extended to everyday life, and in 1922 a tenants' movement in Veracruz led by Herón Proal organized rent strikes involving thousands of residents and had to be suppressed with troops. Women workers, especially in textile factories, cigarette factories, and the garment trades, joined unions and strikes, though male leaders dominated union leadership.

The uneven enforcement of Article 123 followed from the structure of labor relations. The article gave states responsibility for labor law, so conditions varied by region. Boards of conciliation and arbitration were staffed by officials whose behavior depended on political relationships. Employers could appeal to courts, and in the early years the courts debated whether the boards' awards were binding. Large industries and organized workers had leverage, while agricultural laborers, domestic workers, and artisans in small shops were largely outside effective protection. The constitutional guarantee was thus a resource unions could use if they had political allies, not a self-executing standard.

Historical Vocabulary
CROM, collective bargaining, secular education, and Ley Calles

The CROM (Confederación Regional Obrera Mexicana) was the national labor confederation founded in 1918 that became the most influential union organization of the 1920s through its alliance with Obregón and Calles. Collective bargaining is negotiation between organized workers and employers over wages and conditions, which Article 123's recognition of unions and the right to strike made legally possible, though governments and boards often shaped outcomes.

Secular education (educación laica) means public instruction free of religious doctrine, a principle of the Liberal Reform that was written into Article 3. It should not be equated with hostility to religion, though Catholic critics often saw it that way. The Ley Calles was the common name for the 1926 reform of the federal penal code, issued under President Calles, that specified penalties for violating the constitution's religious provisions and sparked the Cristero conflict. The term refers to the 1926 enforcement measure, not to the Constitution itself.

Section 73.12

National Resources, Foreign Investment, Mexican Neutrality, and the First World War

Petroleum was the most valuable and politically sensitive resource in Mexico. The industry had grown rapidly after 1901, centered on the Tampico–Tuxpan region of the Gulf coast, particularly the zone known as the Golden Lane. Foreign companies, including the British-owned Mexican Eagle company associated with Weetman Pearson, the American Huasteca company associated with Edward Doheny, and affiliates of Standard Oil, dominated production. Output rose from a few million barrels in 1910 to a peak of about 193 million barrels in 1921, when Mexico was among the world's leading producers. Oil provided export revenue, customs duties, and royalties, and it was strategically valuable to the navies and industries of the Allied powers during the First World War.

Article 27's assertion that the nation owned petroleum and other subsoil resources directly challenged the legal basis on which the companies operated. Many had acquired land, or leased it, before 1917 under Porfirian legislation that treated subsoil rights as belonging to surface owners. Foreign governments argued for the doctrine of acquired rights, holding that rights obtained under earlier law could not be nullified retroactively. Mexican officials answered that the nation's ownership was original and that concessions were required. The dispute raised questions of retroactivity, the legal status of foreign property, and the Calvo clause, which required foreigners to forgo diplomatic protection for property in Mexico.

Carranza's government took several distinct steps. A decree of 19 February 1918 introduced petroleum-related taxes; measures of 31 July established a deadline for the filing of declarations or manifestaciones concerning oil holdings, while an 8 August measure regulated the denunciation of petroleum claims on unappropriated land. The separate decree dated 12 August 1918 protected specified recognized claims—lands already explored or worked, or acquired after geological survey—from denunciation even where the July declaration had not been filed by the deadline. For such claims it provided for special exploitation contracts with the Department of Industry, Commerce and Labor pending legislation implementing Article 27, while imposing charges on undeclared claims. These measures were not a single general registration decree, and the terms and legal consequences of each must be distinguished. The companies refused to comply fully, the United States and British governments protested, and Carranza's government, which needed revenue and wished to avoid a confrontation with Washington, enforced the decrees inconsistently. In September 1918, Carranza also articulated what became known as the Carranza Doctrine, asserting the legal equality of states, nonintervention, and the principle that foreigners should not enjoy privileges beyond those of Mexicans. The doctrine was a statement of principle meant to resist diplomatic pressure and became part of Mexican foreign policy tradition.

The First World War shaped these disputes and Mexico's international position. Mexico remained neutral, and its neutrality was a deliberate policy of a government that wished to keep its distance from both sides, that distrusted the United States after the 1914 occupation of Veracruz and the 1916 Punitive Expedition, and that had no capacity for overseas war. The government's relations with Germany were cordial, and some Mexican officers admired German military efficiency, while the Allied and American governments pressed Mexico to restrict exports of oil to the Central Powers and to curb German influence.

The Zimmermann Telegram of January 1917 must be understood as a document of German policy. German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann instructed his minister in Mexico City to propose, in the event that the United States entered the war, an alliance in which Germany would offer financial support and the prospect of Mexico recovering lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona, and to suggest that Mexico invite Japan to join. British naval intelligence intercepted and decoded the message and passed it to the United States in February, and it was published in early March 1917, contributing to American entry into the war in April. Germany proposed an alliance, and Mexico did not accept it. Carranza's government had the proposal examined by military and other advisers, who concluded that Mexico lacked the means to wage war against the United States and that the proposal offered little. Mexico remained neutral. It is incorrect to describe the telegram as evidence of a Mexican plan to invade the United States. It is correct to say that it exposed Mexico's strategic vulnerability and the way great-power rivalry could treat Mexican territory and sovereignty as bargaining chips.

These episodes show how sovereignty and dependence constrained policy. Mexico's claim to resource ownership was a central expression of revolutionary nationalism, yet the government depended on oil revenue and foreign capital, and it faced powerful governments that could withhold recognition, loans, or arms. The United States had recognized Carranza's government, de facto in October 1915 and fully in 1917, and relations stayed tense. Mexico's leaders therefore combined assertive principles with practical flexibility, a pattern that would recur under Obregón and Calles.

Division IV — Revolutionary Succession and National Reconstruction

Section 73.13

Continued Revolutionary Warfare, Zapatista Resistance, and the Assassination of Zapata

The promulgation of the Constitution did not end the war. Armed groups of varied size and purpose operated across the country through 1920 and beyond. Villa's forces, reduced after 1915, remained a guerrilla presence in Chihuahua and Durango and occasionally struck towns and railways. Felipe Ángeles, the former federal officer who had joined Villa, returned from exile in the United States in 1918, was captured, and was executed in Chihuahua in November 1919 after a court-martial that drew protest. Other rebels included followers of Félix Díaz, nephew of Porfirio, in Veracruz and Oaxaca, and regional bands that mixed political objectives with banditry. In the Huasteca oil region, the commander Manuel Peláez controlled territory with the tolerance and, according to contemporary evidence, financial support of oil companies, which gave him a role in the diplomatic disputes of Section 73.12.

Morelos remained the principal theater of agrarian war. Federal campaigns in 1916 and 1917 under Pablo González had retaken towns and sugar estates, and troops had destroyed crops and villages. The Zapatistas, led by Emiliano Zapata with commanders such as Genovevo de la O, withdrew to the mountains and continued to fight. They also continued to administer land and local affairs where they could, redistributing estates in practice and rebuilding village institutions, and in 1918 Zapata and his circle sought allies beyond Mexico through manifestos addressed to other revolutionaries and to Latin American audiences. The population of Morelos suffered from hunger, epidemic disease, forced relocation, and the destruction of livelihoods. Women supported the movement as soldiers, couriers, cooks, and organizers, and many suffered violence from federal troops.

The assassination of Zapata followed a plan that combined military operations with deception. In early 1919, Pablo González, the federal commander in Morelos, sought a way to remove the Zapatista leader. Colonel Jesús Guajardo, a federal officer, engaged in an exchange with Zapata in which he appeared willing to defect with his troops. To persuade Zapata of his sincerity, Guajardo attacked and executed a Zapatista officer who had been suspected of disloyalty. On 10 April 1919 Zapata went to meet Guajardo at the Hacienda de Chinameca, and as he entered, soldiers in Guajardo's escort shot him. The documentary record on the details derives from participants, from official reports, and from later accounts, and it varies in particulars, but the central facts of the deception and killing are well established. Guajardo was promoted and rewarded, and González, who had planned the operation, was widely understood to have acted with the knowledge of the Carranza government, though how far Carranza himself directed it has been debated.

The death of Zapata removed the movement's central leader, but it did not resolve the social movement he represented. The Zapatistas continued under new leadership, in particular Gildardo Magaña, a former Zapatista secretary and negotiator who reorganized the movement, and they did not forget the killing. Villagers' demands for land restitution continued, and the Zapatistas' attention shifted to alliance with Carranza's rivals. The subsequent adhesion of Zapatista leaders to the Sonoran rebellion of 1920, discussed in the next section, brought them into the new governing coalition and helped place Zapatistas in government positions in Morelos. Historians therefore distinguish between the elimination of a revolutionary leader and the survival of Zapatismo, which continued as a political tradition, as a source of legitimacy for later governments, and as a language of claims.

Demobilization posed a related problem. A very large number of men had spent years in armies and had no secure livelihoods to return to, and the government could neither pay them nor safely dismiss them. Officers, many of whom had risen from modest origins, saw the army as a career and as a route to land and office. Villa's followers and the Zapatistas had no assurance of pensions or of land. The government's strategy would develop under Obregón through settlements, grants, and reorganization, but through the end of the Carranza presidency, the continued presence of armed groups and of generals with their own followings underscored that the Revolution's military phase had not yet ended.

Section 73.14

The Presidential Succession Crisis, the Plan of Agua Prieta, and Carranza's Fall, 1920

The Constitution barred reelection, so the end of Carranza's four-year term in 1920 required a successor, and the question of who it would be dominated politics from 1919. Obregón, who had remained out of government since 1917, announced his candidacy in June 1919 and began a national campaign supported by the Partido Liberal Constitucionalista, by the CROM through its Laborista party, by agrarian leaders, and by military officers who saw in him the most plausible leader. Carranza, who distrusted Obregón and the Sonoran group, declined to support him. He preferred a civilian successor and promoted Ignacio Bonillas, an engineer who had served as Mexican ambassador in Washington and who had little political base. Critics called Bonillas an imposed candidate and mocked his obscurity.

The conflict was sharpened by tension between Carranza's government and the state of Sonora, then governed by Adolfo de la Huerta. A dispute over the use of the waters of the Sonora River, in which the federal government asserted authority, combined with Carranza's decision to send federal troops into the state, was regarded by Sonoran leaders as an attack on state sovereignty and on the principle of the free state in the Constitution. In April 1920, the federal government summoned Obregón to Mexico City as a witness in an investigation of a rebellion in which he was allegedly implicated. Obregón, who regarded the summons as a trap, escaped from the capital and traveled to Guerrero and then to Sonora, where he joined the opposition. The episode convinced many officers that Carranza intended to prevent a fair election.

On 23 April 1920, Sonoran leaders proclaimed the Plan of Agua Prieta. The plan declared that Carranza had violated popular sovereignty by imposing a candidate and had violated the sovereignty of the states by intervening in Sonora, and that he had therefore lost his claim to the presidency. It named De la Huerta supreme chief of a liberating army and provided for an interim government and new elections. Plutarco Elías Calles, who had resigned from Carranza's cabinet, was a key figure, and Obregón joined. The movement was a revolt framed in constitutional language, which echoed Carranza's own Plan of Guadalupe of 1913 in its claim to defend legality against an usurper.

Support for Carranza dissolved quickly. Generals in many states adhered to the plan, including commanders who had been his supporters, and the Zapatistas joined as well. Few federal forces were willing to fight. On 7 May, Carranza left Mexico City by train with officials, soldiers, and a large quantity of government funds and documents, intending to establish the government at Veracruz. The train was attacked and had to be abandoned, and the party continued on horseback through the mountains of Puebla. On 21 May 1920 at Tlaxcalantongo, in the Sierra Norte de Puebla, Carranza was killed in a night attack by forces led by a local commander, Rodolfo Herrero, whose forces had offered the party shelter. Herrero claimed the president died in the fighting, and a court-martial acquitted him, but historians generally regard the killing as an assassination and debate who, if anyone, ordered it. On 1 June 1920, Congress named De la Huerta interim president.

The episode demonstrated that presidential succession remained the weak point of the revolutionary state. Nonreelection prevented a president from perpetuating his rule, but without a recognized mechanism for choosing a successor, rivalry among leaders was resolved through military force. The principle of a civilian presidency bound by law collided with the practical power of generals. The fall of Carranza also showed how much of his power rested on the loyalty of regional commanders, and how quickly constitutional authority could fail without it. Elections in September 1920 confirmed Obregón as president, and the peaceful inauguration on 1 December 1920 ended a decade of nearly continuous turmoil at the national level, even though it had been achieved through a rebellion.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Succession Disputes, Regional Military Alliances, and the Fall of Carranza

Long-term causes

The absence of a mechanism for orderly presidential succession after the end of Díaz's rule; the autonomy of regional commanders; Carranza's distrust of the Sonoran leaders; and the gap between constitutional promises and government performance.

Short-term causes

Carranza's refusal to support Obregón and his sponsorship of Bonillas; the dispute with Sonora over federal authority and water; the summons of Obregón to Mexico City in April 1920; and doubts about electoral fairness.

Event or transformation

The Plan of Agua Prieta, proclaimed on 23 April 1920, and the collapse of Carranza's support, leading to his flight and death at Tlaxcalantongo on 21 May.

Immediate effects

De la Huerta's interim presidency from 1 June; adhesion of generals and Zapatistas to the new coalition; the settlement with Villa; the election and inauguration of Obregón.

Long-term consequences

The Sonoran dynasty governed from 1920 to 1934 through a combination of military authority, labor and agrarian alliances, and negotiation; the precedent of armed pronouncement remained available in later succession disputes.

Historical complexity

Carranza's fall cannot be reduced to personal ambition or to one blunder. It involved structural weaknesses of the revolutionary state, regional politics, and contingent decisions, and responsibility for his death remains debated.

Section 73.15

Álvaro Obregón's Presidency, Military Settlement, and Agrarian Reconstruction, 1920–1924

Adolfo de la Huerta's brief interim government, from June to November 1920, completed several settlements. The most important was with Francisco Villa, who in July 1920 agreed to lay down arms in return for a hacienda at Canutillo, Durango, where he would live with a small escort and with settled former followers. The arrangement removed the most famous guerrilla from the field and turned some of his men into farmers. Obregón was inaugurated on 1 December 1920. His government began with considerable assets: a military victory that was largely uncontested, the support of the CROM and agrarian leaders, the adhesion of Zapatistas, and substantial prestige as the most successful revolutionary general. It also faced a depleted treasury, a restive army, and an unrecognized status in Washington.

Obregón was pragmatic. He sought to reduce the autonomy of military commanders by reorganizing military zones, rotating generals, reducing the size of the army, and offering retirements and land grants, and the army's size was roughly halved over the early 1920s. These measures were partial. Many generals retained local power and economic interests, and the Secretary of War's office came to depend on managing their loyalty. Obregón's government also used patronage, offering offices, contracts, and land to supporters, and it relied on the alliance with the CROM and on agrarian leagues whose militias could be mobilized.

Agrarian policy proceeded through legislation and local conflict. The Ley de Ejidos of December 1920 and the Reglamento Agrario of April 1922 specified procedures for petitions and set limits on the size of properties exempt from expropriation. Obregón did not favor a sweeping redistribution, and his government regarded the ejido partly as a means of pacification and partly as a transitional institution, but his administration granted more land than Carranza's, with commonly cited tallies on the order of a million hectares, though the figures vary by source and method. Zapatistas in Morelos benefited, and the state's villages obtained land and the return of local institutions. Agrarian leagues in Veracruz, Michoacán, Yucatán, and elsewhere grew, and agrarian leaders became significant political actors.

Foreign relations were a central concern because the United States withheld formal recognition of Obregón's government until it received assurances on petroleum and on claims. In 1922, Mexico and an international committee of bankers negotiated the De la Huerta–Lamont agreement on the foreign debt. The decisive negotiations came in the Bucareli conferences of May to August 1923, held in Mexico City between American and Mexican representatives. The outcome consisted of understandings, not a formal treaty. They included claims commissions for damages suffered by foreigners during the Revolution, and compensation arrangements for foreign-owned agrarian lands expropriated beyond a specified size. They also included assurances, consistent with decisions by the Mexican Supreme Court, that Article 27 would not be applied retroactively to oil lands where companies had performed positive acts before 1 May 1917. The United States restored diplomatic recognition at the end of August 1923. Critics in Mexico attacked the agreements as concessions of sovereignty, and supporters regarded them as a pragmatic price for stability.

The agreement also affected domestic politics. Obregón's decision to support Calles as his successor angered De la Huerta, who had hoped to be the candidate, and in December 1923 De la Huerta joined a rebellion that began in Veracruz and spread to Jalisco and other states. A large share of the army, including many generals, joined the rebels. Obregón and Calles responded by mobilizing loyal troops, armed agrarian and labor militias, and by obtaining the ability to buy weapons from the United States. The revolt was defeated by early 1924, at a high cost. Many rebel officers were executed, and in Yucatán, Governor Felipe Carrillo Puerto was captured and executed by rebel-aligned forces in January 1924. In July 1923, Francisco Villa had been assassinated in Parral, Chihuahua, in an ambush whose organizers were never conclusively established and for which Obregón's and Calles's circle have been suspected by many historians, though proof is lacking.

The Obregón presidency thus illustrated the distinction between political reconstruction and the disappearance of armed opposition. It ended with a peaceful transfer of power, the first since the Revolution began, but it did so after a major rebellion and the use of political killings. The government consolidated through a combination of negotiation with foreign governments, concession to organized labor and agrarian groups, reorganization of the army, and force.

Section 73.16

Education, Cultural Nationalism, Public Institutions, and the Rebuilding of Everyday Life

Education was the most visible area of public investment in the early 1920s. The Secretaría de Educación Pública, SEP, was created by decree on 3 October 1921, after a constitutional reform authorized the federal government to act in education throughout the country. Its foundation should not be confused with the adoption of Article 3 in 1917. Obregón appointed José Vasconcelos, a philosopher and former Convention-era official who was rector of the National University, as the first secretary. Vasconcelos took charge of an ambitious program that treated education as a national mission, and the education budget grew substantially during his tenure.

The program included the establishment of rural schools, literacy campaigns, the founding of libraries, and the publication of inexpensive editions of classic works. Vasconcelos sent teachers into the countryside as agents of national integration, and the "cultural missions" that began in 1923 sent teams of teachers and specialists into rural communities to train local teachers and to introduce agriculture, crafts, hygiene, and music. Rural teachers, often young and sometimes women, worked in difficult conditions with low pay and limited supplies, and they became intermediaries between villages and the state. Women educators played important roles, including the kindergarten pioneer Rosaura Zapata, the feminist educator Elena Torres, and the Chilean poet Gabriela Mistral, whom Vasconcelos invited to advise on rural education.

Cultural policy was linked to education. Vasconcelos commissioned murals for public buildings, notably the Escuela Nacional Preparatoria and the new SEP building. Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros painted walls with scenes that mixed revolutionary history, labor, Indigenous heritage, and social criticism, and in 1923 they and others formed a union of painters and sculptors that issued a manifesto in favor of public art. The murals gave visual form to a revolutionary nationalism in which the mestizo and Indigenous past were presented as foundations of the nation. Anthropologist Manuel Gamio promoted the study of Indigenous communities and archaeological sites as part of nation-building, and Vasconcelos's essay on the "cosmic race," published in 1925, expressed an optimistic view of mixture as Mexico's contribution to the world.

Education and cultural nationalism served integration, but they also carried assumptions that marginalized Indigenous cultures. The policy of teaching Spanish and of making Indigenous children into citizens of a national culture, known as castellanización or incorporation, treated Indigenous languages and customs as obstacles to modernization. The Department of Indigenous Culture and later institutions such as the Casa del Estudiante Indígena in Mexico City, founded in 1926, brought Indigenous students into schools while presenting their cultures as raw material for a mestizo nation. Celebrations of the Indigenous past coexisted with pressure on living Indigenous communities to assimilate. Local resistance, selective adoption, and community use of schools to support their own claims were also part of the story.

Public institutions grew in other areas, including public health campaigns and the reconstruction of railways and roads, and the government's relationship with ordinary people changed as it began to supply services as well as demands. The expansion was limited by money, by regional resistance, and by the opposition of the Catholic Church, which regarded the schools as threats. Education also became a flashpoint. Some rural teachers were attacked by landowners or by communities that opposed state schooling, and the conflicts intensified during the religious controversy of 1926–1929 and again in the 1930s.

Everyday Life and Social History

Teachers, Petitioners, Workers' Families, and Women Activists in Postwar Mexico

For a rural teacher in the early 1920s, the revolutionary state could mean a one-room school with few books, a salary that arrived late or not at all, and dependence on the goodwill of a village that might support the school or distrust it. Teachers organized literacy classes for adults as well as children, taught agriculture and hygiene, and sometimes helped villagers prepare land petitions. Some were threatened by landowners or local bosses. For women teachers, the work offered a rare public role and exposed them to danger and to criticism.

For a village seeking land, the process of petitioning involved collecting titles, electing a committee, traveling to the state capital, paying for surveys and legal help, and waiting for years. Petitioners knew that a favorable provisional ruling might be reversed, that estate guards might harass them, and that their cause might become entangled in factional politics. Many drew on community memory, on colonial documents kept in the village chest, and on alliances with agrarian leagues.

Workers' families lived with the aftermath of inflation, shortage, and epidemic. In cities, housing was scarce and expensive, and the Veracruz tenants' strike of 1922 showed how daily costs could produce mass mobilization. Union membership gave some families access to benefits, mutual aid, and labor board procedures, while those outside organized industry relied on kin networks and informal work. Women worked in factories, workshops, markets, and households, and they joined unions, wrote for labor papers, and led strikes. Women activists also organized feminist congresses and associations, pressed for civil-law reform and for the vote, and taught.

The surviving record of these lives is uneven. Petitions, school reports, union newspapers, photographs, and oral histories preserve some experiences, while others appear only in the reports of officials, in the complaints of employers, or in the images made by artists with their own purposes. The silences are themselves evidence of whose voices the archive records.

Section 73.17

The Calles Presidency: Economic Institutions, National Resources, and Administrative Consolidation, 1924–1928

Plutarco Elías Calles won the July 1924 election and took office on 1 December 1924, in the first transfer of the presidency from one revolutionary leader to another that did not follow the overthrow or death of the incumbent. Born in Guaymas, Sonora, Calles had been a schoolteacher and local official before he became a general and governor of Sonora, and he served Obregón as Secretary of the Interior. His presidency is often characterized as more systematically institution-building than Obregón's. He had the support of the CROM, whose leader Morones became Secretary of Industry, Commerce, and Labor, and of agrarian groups, and he governed with a cabinet that included economists and administrators such as Alberto Pani at Finance.

Financial reconstruction was a priority. The government introduced an income tax in the mid-1920s, reorganized fiscal administration, and sought to stabilize the currency and the public debt. The Banco de México, inaugurated on 1 September 1925, was designed as a central bank with the exclusive legal authority to issue banknotes; the earlier system of private issuing banks had been dismantled during the revolutionary years. The new institution did not immediately possess the independence, reserves, or monetary instruments of a mature central bank. Private commercial banks continued other financial operations, while public confidence in the bank's notes, their circulation, and its practical influence over credit remained limited. Legal monopoly over issuance and effective nationwide circulation were different achievements, and wider acceptance of Banco de México notes developed over subsequent years. The bank also served the government, and its institutional significance unfolded gradually. Other institutions followed, including a national agricultural credit bank founded in 1926, which was intended to provide loans to farmers and ejidos, though its resources were limited and its loans often went to better-off producers.

Infrastructure and administration expanded. The National Highway Commission, created in 1925, began a road program that linked Mexico City to other regions, and the National Irrigation Commission, established in 1926, began irrigation works, especially in the north. The state also grew in education, in health, and in the army, where Secretary of War Joaquín Amaro introduced professional training and discipline and reorganized military administration. These changes strengthened federal capacity, and they gave the government new instruments of control.

Agrarian policy under Calles was ambivalent. His administration distributed more land than Obregón's, with commonly cited tallies of roughly three million hectares, though sources vary. Yet Calles favored small private farming and irrigation-based commercial agriculture in the northern manner. A 1925 law on the parceling of ejido land encouraged the division of communal ejidos into individual plots, which advocates presented as a path toward productivity and critics saw as a step toward weakening communal tenure. In 1930, after leaving office, Calles publicly argued that the ejido program had not produced the prosperity promised, a statement that signaled a retreat from agrarian reform in his circle and that Cárdenas later reversed.

Petroleum policy was the most explosive foreign question. In December 1925, the government issued a petroleum law that required companies to exchange titles held before 1917 for fifty-year confirmatory concessions, which companies and their governments regarded as a violation of the Bucareli understandings and of acquired rights. Tensions with the United States rose sharply in 1926 and 1927, with the American ambassador and Secretary of State Frank Kellogg accusing Mexico of hostility to American property and, in some statements, of Bolshevik tendencies, and with a war scare in late 1926 and early 1927. The crisis eased after the appointment of Dwight Morrow as ambassador in 1927. Morrow cultivated Calles and encouraged compromise. In November 1927 the Mexican Supreme Court ruled that key provisions of the law were unconstitutional as applied to rights acquired through positive acts before 1917, and in January 1928 Congress amended the law to provide for confirmatory concessions without a time limit. Oil companies thus retained their operations, while the principle of national ownership remained in law. The episode showed that the revolutionary state could assert sovereignty but could not afford to expropriate. That would change in the 1930s, as Chapter 74 explains.

Students should distinguish the creation of institutions from their immediate effectiveness. The Bank of Mexico, the credit bank, and the irrigation and highway commissions were foundations of the postrevolutionary state, but their early work was limited, and their reach depended on the political arrangements that allowed them to operate. The Calles government's state-building also relied on an alliance with the CROM and on increasing control over labor, agrarian leagues, and the army. It was an administrative consolidation that coexisted with censorship, patronage, and the repression of opponents.

Section 73.18

The Cristero War, Religious Conflict, and the Limits of Church–State Accommodation, 1926–1929

The religious conflict emerged from the enforcement of provisions written in 1917 but largely applied unevenly until the mid-1920s. During Obregón's presidency the church had continued to operate schools, hold public ceremonies, and organize lay associations, and some governors had enforced the anticlerical articles while others had not. Catholic social organizations had grown, including the Asociación Católica de la Juventud Mexicana and, in 1925, the Liga Nacional Defensora de la Libertad Religiosa, which organized lay Catholics to press for changes in the constitutional provisions. The Calles government, which was committed to a state monopoly over education and to curbing church political influence, tolerated a schismatic national church launched in 1925 with CROM backing, and it reacted to a statement by Archbishop José Mora y del Río in early 1926 that the church would not accept the anticlerical articles.

In June 1926, Calles promulgated a reform of the federal penal code, popularly called the Ley Calles, which specified penalties for violating the constitution's religious provisions. It required the registration of priests with the civil authorities, restricted religious education and public religious activity, and prescribed fines and imprisonment. It took effect on 31 July 1926. The Catholic hierarchy responded by suspending public worship in churches across the country from 1 August, and the Liga organized an economic boycott. The suspension of Mass and the sacraments was a profound disruption in a country where religious practice was central to community life, and it intensified anger in many regions.

Armed resistance began in late 1926 and became a significant rebellion in 1927. The insurgents were called Cristeros from their rallying cry of Viva Cristo Rey, "Long live Christ the King." The conflict was concentrated in west-central Mexico, in Jalisco, Colima, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Zacatecas, Nayarit, and neighboring areas, a region of smallholders, rancheros, and rural communities with strong parish institutions, and it also reached other parts of the country. Participants included peasants, ranchers, artisans, and some former soldiers, supported by village networks and by the clergy, though the bishops themselves were divided over armed resistance. A group of lay organizers supplied political direction, and in 1927 the Liga engaged General Enrique Gorostieta, a former federal officer, to lead the insurgent army. Estimates of the number of insurgents at the peak vary and are commonly given in the tens of thousands.

Women played major roles. Women's Catholic organizations, notably the Brigadas Femeninas de Santa Juana de Arco, smuggled money, ammunition, and information, maintained supply networks, and nursed the wounded, and some women were arrested and killed. Rural communities provided food and shelter at great risk. On the other side, federal troops carried out severe measures, including the forced relocation of rural populations in parts of Jalisco and the destruction of villages and crops. The government armed agraristas, recipients or petitioners for land, who fought against the Cristeros, and agrarian reform and religious conflict thus became intertwined in some regions, although many villagers did not conform to a simple pattern. Not all Catholics supported the rebellion, and some supported the government. Not all government supporters were anticlerical. In some localities, both sides included peasants. The conflict also involved personal and local rivalries that predated the religious issue.

Violence extended to cities. In November 1927, after an attempted bombing of Obregón's car by Catholic militants, the Jesuit priest Miguel Pro, who had been serving clandestinely, was executed along with others without a fair trial, and the event, photographed and widely publicized, became a powerful symbol for Catholics. The assassination of Obregón in 1928 by a Catholic activist, discussed below, deepened the government's hostility. The war's total death toll is estimated variously from roughly sixty thousand to one hundred thousand, including combatants and civilians, and the figures should be treated as uncertain.

The conflict ended through negotiation. The new interim president, Emilio Portes Gil, along with Ambassador Morrow, who facilitated contacts, arranged talks with church leaders, notably Archbishop Leopoldo Ruiz y Flores and Bishop Pascual Díaz. The resulting arrangements of June 1929 allowed the resumption of public worship, with priests registering and the government promising not to enforce the laws in certain ways, including in religious instruction inside churches and in the right of petition. The constitutional provisions were not amended. The Vatican approved the settlement, but the Liga and many Cristero leaders regarded it as a betrayal. After the agreement, hundreds of former Cristeros were killed in reprisals, and local restrictions on the number of priests continued in several states. Tensions persisted into the 1930s, and a smaller second wave of rebellion occurred. The settlement therefore ended the war without resolving the underlying question of church–state relations, which remained legally unsettled until constitutional reforms in 1992.

Section 73.19

Obregón's Reelection, Assassination, the PNR, and the Politics of the Maximato, 1928–1934

As the end of Calles's term approached, Obregón decided to return to the presidency. The original Constitution had banned reelection absolutely, so supporters amended Article 83 in January 1927 to permit nonconsecutive reelection and, in January 1928, to extend the presidential term from four to six years. Opposition to the amendments among military figures led to a revolt in the fall of 1927 by Generals Francisco Serrano and Arnulfo Gómez. Serrano and several associates were seized and killed at Huitzilac, Morelos, on 3 October 1927, and Gómez was captured and executed in November. Obregón won the election of July 1928 with little organized opposition.

On 17 July 1928, at a restaurant called La Bombilla in San Ángel, near Mexico City, Obregón was shot and killed by José de León Toral, a young Catholic activist. Toral was tried and executed, and Concepción Acevedo de la Llata, a religious figure who was accused of influencing him, was sentenced to prison. The assassination occurred at the height of the Cristero War and in a political atmosphere in which opponents suspected Calles and Morones, whom Obregón's supporters blamed, though evidence for their involvement was not established. The death of the president-elect created the most serious succession crisis of the postrevolutionary period. Obregón had been the one leader with enough prestige to command the loyalty of the generals, and his death left a vacuum.

Calles responded with a political initiative. In his final annual message to Congress, delivered on 1 September 1928, he announced that he would not seek to remain in power and called for a transition from a politics dominated by individual leaders, which he called a regime of caudillos, to one governed by institutions and laws. Congress named Emilio Portes Gil, a lawyer and former governor of Tamaulipas, as interim president in September, and he took office on 1 December 1928. In the same period Calles and a group of political and military figures organized a committee to form a national party, whose organizing manifesto was issued on 1 December 1928. The Partido Nacional Revolucionario was founded in March 1929 at a convention in Querétaro.

The PNR joined many regional parties, political clubs, and caudillo networks into a single organization that nominated candidates and coordinated succession. It did not eliminate competition or coercion. In March 1929, even as the party was being founded, General José Gonzalo Escobar led a military rebellion in Sonora and other states, and the federal army, with Calles as Secretary of War and with commanders such as Lázaro Cárdenas, defeated it within weeks. The PNR's candidate in the November 1929 election was Pascual Ortiz Rubio, a former ambassador, who faced José Vasconcelos, the former Secretary of Education, running an opposition campaign that drew students, professionals, and urban supporters. The official results gave Ortiz Rubio an overwhelming margin, while Vasconcelos and his supporters alleged fraud and violence. The PNR of 1929 was a precursor to the later party system, but it was not the PRM of 1938, which restructured the party around corporate sectors, or the PRI of 1946, whose name and organization came later.

The period from 1928 to 1934 is called the Maximato after Calles's informal title of Jefe Máximo de la Revolución, the supreme chief. Calles remained the dominant political figure, working through the party, the army, his network of allies, and the cabinet, even though he held no formal office for much of the time. The three presidents of the period differed. Portes Gil, who governed from 1928 to early 1930, was energetic, supported agrarian reform in some states, signed the religious settlement of 1929, and at times asserted independence. Pascual Ortiz Rubio, inaugurated on 5 February 1930, survived a shooting on the day of his inauguration, struggled to assert authority, and resigned in September 1932. Abelardo L. Rodríguez, a Sonoran general and businessman, finished the term in November 1934, with a pragmatic style, and his government established the first minimum-wage commissions. Historians therefore describe the presidents as constrained rather than powerless, since they made decisions and sometimes clashed with Calles, though the Jefe Máximo's influence was great.

Other developments of the early 1930s included the 1931 Federal Labor Law, which made Article 123 operational on a national basis after a 1929 amendment federalized labor legislation, and the decline of the CROM's dominance after Obregón's death. Mexican foreign policy also changed, and in 1930 the Estrada Doctrine declared that Mexico would not pass judgment on the legitimacy of foreign governments. These years also saw growing criticism of Calles from within the Revolutionary coalition, leading to the political realignment that brought Cárdenas to power.

Historical Vocabulary
Caudillismo, PNR, Maximato, and revolutionary institutionalization

Caudillismo is political rule by personal leaders whose authority rests on followings, often military, rather than on formal institutions. Historians use the term for much of nineteenth-century Latin America and apply it to postrevolutionary Mexico with care, since Obregón and Calles both used personal networks while building institutions. The PNR (Partido Nacional Revolucionario) was the governing party founded in 1929 to organize revolutionary elites and manage succession. The Maximato is the name for the years 1928–1934, when Calles, the Jefe Máximo, exercised influence behind the presidency.

Revolutionary institutionalization refers to the process by which leaders replaced personal rule and armed rivalry with party, bureaucratic, and legal structures. It did not mean democratization. The institutions of the 1920s and 1930s often centralized power and incorporated groups selectively, as Chapter 74 will show in the later PRM and PRI.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Díaz's Personal Rule to the Presidential Succession Systems of 1917–1934

What Changed

  • A constitution banning reelection replaced a regime that had perpetuated one man in office for decades, and the original Article 83 was a direct response to Díaz
  • Rulers came from the middle and provincial classes and from the revolutionary army, not from the Porfirian elite
  • Government assumed social responsibilities in education, labor, and agrarian matters, and organized groups gained a recognized place in politics
  • By 1929 a national party began to organize succession, replacing improvised rebellions with a managed process

What Stayed the Same

  • Political authority remained highly personal and concentrated, first in Carranza, then Obregón, then Calles
  • Succession was decided by negotiation among elites, sometimes by force, and not by competitive elections
  • The army and patronage networks remained central to stability, and elections often lacked real competition
  • Censorship, repression, and unequal political participation continued

Who experienced change most directly: Organized workers, petitioning villages, rural teachers, and military officers experienced new opportunities and new forms of control. Greater continuity: Women excluded from national suffrage, many rural communities facing local bosses, and Indigenous people subjected to assimilation policies saw continuity in exclusion. Why the pattern matters: The system that emerged avoided the instability of the 1910s through centralization, and that trade-off between order and open competition shaped Mexican politics for decades.

Section 73.20

The Revolutionary State by 1934: Achievements, Contradictions, and the Transition to Cárdenas

By 1934, the revolutionary state had developed institutions that did not exist in 1917. There was a central bank, federal ministries of education and agriculture with provincial networks, a national highway and irrigation program, a labor law, a professionalized army more firmly under presidential direction, and a national party that organized the political elite. A series of presidents had completed or nearly completed their terms, and the open military revolts of the early 1920s had given way to more managed succession. For a country that had seen the presidency changed by coup, assassination, and rebellion in the preceding decades, these were significant changes.

The achievements need to be measured against the promises. Agrarian reform had distributed land, with commonly cited tallies of several million hectares by 1934, but this was a small share of what the Constitution's language had implied and left the hacienda system largely intact in many regions. Labor law had been enacted and organized workers had gained recognition, but the labor movement was fragmented and subordinated to governments, and many workers remained outside protection. Public schools had spread, and rural teachers worked in thousands of communities, but literacy remained low, and schooling was limited in much of the countryside. National ownership of resources was established in law, but oil companies continued to operate under negotiated terms.

The Great Depression exposed the limits of the system. Mexico's exports of minerals and oil fell sharply after 1929, and the value of exports dropped by roughly half in the early 1930s. Unemployment rose, wages fell, and government revenue declined. Hundreds of thousands of Mexican workers and their families, including some who were United States citizens, were repatriated or left the United States during the 1930s, adding pressure on rural communities. The government abandoned gold coinage in 1931. The crisis undermined confidence in the existing governing coalition and in the cautious economic policies associated with Calles, and it led some political leaders to seek a broader popular base.

The costs of reconstruction were also evident. The postrevolutionary state used force against rebels, strikers, and opponents. Political killings were frequent, and electoral processes, including the 1929 election, were manipulated. Censorship and patronage were established practices. Women remained excluded from national suffrage, and Indigenous communities were treated as subjects of assimilation. The Cristero War left tens of thousands dead and a legacy of mistrust between the Catholic population and the state. The gap between constitutional promises and actual results was one of the main themes of political criticism and of later reform movements.

Within the governing group, dissatisfaction with the Maximato grew. Some leaders, including Lázaro Cárdenas, a general from Michoacán who had served as governor and as president of the PNR, believed the Revolution needed renewed social content. The party's convention at Querétaro in December 1933 adopted a Six-Year Plan, which offered a program of agrarian, labor, and educational measures, and nominated Cárdenas for the presidency. He won the election of 1934 and took office on 1 December 1934. His government's policies, including the major agrarian redistribution, the expropriation of the oil companies in 1938, the reorganization of the party into the PRM, and the later development of the PRI system, belong to Chapter 74.

Returning to the opening question, the evidence suggests a mixed answer. The Constitution of 1917 did provide a durable legal framework and a vocabulary of social rights that later governments and movements used. The governments from Carranza to Rodríguez did build institutions that reduced armed conflict and extended federal authority. Yet stability rested heavily on centralized power, on negotiated alliances with selected groups, and on the Sonoran leaders' control of the army and the party. Whether the postrevolutionary state was best understood as a constitutional order or as a new hierarchy that selectively incorporated revolutionary demands is a question on which historians continue to disagree, and the answer differs by region and by social group.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Constitution of 1917 was not the conclusion of the Mexican Revolution but the beginning of a difficult, contested process of turning revolutionary demands into government, law, and social policy. It followed that process from Carranza's victories of 1915 to the inauguration of Lázaro Cárdenas in 1934, and it treated the constitutional text, the governments that administered it, and the societies that tested it as parts of a single story.

By 1915 the Constitutionalists had defeated their principal rivals in the field, but military predominance did not confer legitimacy. Carranza's movement had been founded on the claim of restoring legality, so it needed a constitutional settlement. It also needed to answer demands for land and labor reform through measures such as the agrarian decree of January 1915. The call for a Constituent Congress in September 1916 was an attempt to regularize authority on terms the First Chief could shape, and its exclusion of Villistas and Zapatistas meant the assembly represented the Constitutionalist wing only.

The Congress at Querétaro, which opened on 1 December 1916, was nonetheless a genuine forum of debate. A majority of reform-minded delegates, including Múgica, Jara, and the drafting group around Rouaix, pushed the document beyond Carranza's draft. The text approved on 31 January 1917, promulgated on 5 February, and generally in force from 1 May retained the federal republic, the guarantees of 1857, and the amparo, strengthened the executive, and added substantial social provisions.

The social articles established the document's distinctive character. Article 27 declared the nation's original ownership of land and waters, provided for restitution and distribution to villages, and asserted national ownership of the subsoil. Article 123 enumerated labor guarantees and created conciliation and arbitration boards. Articles 3, 24, and 130 combined secular education with regulation of worship and the legal position of the church. Article 115 created the free municipality, and Article 83 prohibited reelection. The Constitution also left women outside national citizenship, despite Hermila Galindo's petition.

Carranza's presidency, from 1 May 1917, showed the distance between text and practice. The government faced fiscal weakness, regional commanders, and continued rebellion. Agrarian reform under Carranza was limited, and many intervened estates were returned. Labor, after the repression of the 1916 strikes, found new organization in the CROM, founded in 1918, which allied with Carranza's rivals. In foreign affairs, Article 27 and the petroleum decrees produced conflict with the United States and Britain, while Mexico stayed neutral in the First World War and rejected the proposal in the Zimmermann Telegram.

Armed conflict and succession politics undermined Carranza. The assassination of Zapata in April 1919 removed a leader but not Zapatismo. Carranza's attempt to impose Bonillas as successor, his conflict with Sonora, and the Plan of Agua Prieta of April 1920 led to his flight and killing at Tlaxcalantongo. The succession crisis showed that nonreelection, without a mechanism for choosing successors, left the presidency vulnerable to rebellion.

The Obregón presidency from 1920 to 1924 began reconstruction through settlements with Villa and the Zapatistas, partial reduction of the army, alliances with labor and agrarian groups, and the Bucareli understandings that brought United States recognition. It also faced the De la Huerta rebellion and was accompanied by political killings. Its most visible cultural achievement was the educational and artistic program of Vasconcelos, with the SEP created in October 1921, rural schools, cultural missions, and the muralists. That program promoted national integration and also pressed assimilation on Indigenous communities.

Calles governed from 1924 to 1928 with a more systematic institutional program, including the Banco de México, credit and irrigation agencies, highways, and military professionalization, while navigating a dispute with the United States over petroleum. His enforcement of the constitutional religious provisions through the 1926 Ley Calles contributed to the Cristero War of 1926–1929, a major rebellion in west-central Mexico that ended in a negotiated settlement that did not resolve the underlying constitutional questions.

Obregón's reelection under amended rules and his assassination in July 1928 created a crisis that Calles addressed by calling for institutional politics and by helping to found the PNR in March 1929. The Maximato, with Portes Gil, Ortiz Rubio, and Rodríguez as presidents and Calles as Jefe Máximo, was a period of managed succession, a labor code, and growing strain under the Great Depression. The PNR was a precursor of the later PRM and PRI, not their equivalent.

By 1934 the revolutionary state had institutions, a legal vocabulary of social rights, and a more reliable method of succession. It also relied on centralization, patronage, military authority, and selective incorporation of social groups, and it left many promises unfulfilled. The chapter leaves the question of whether it was a constitutional order or a new hierarchy to historians' continuing debate, and it hands the story to Chapter 74, in which Cárdenas would give the revolutionary promises a new, more radical life.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Constituyente
An assembly called to write or revise a constitution; the Congress of Querétaro, 1916–1917.
Plan of Guadalupe
Carranza's March 1913 proclamation against Huerta, later amended in 1914 and 1916 to add reform commitments and the call for a Congress.
Constitutionalists
The faction led by Carranza that claimed to restore the legal order of the Constitution of 1857.
Individual guarantees
Rights of persons against government, such as liberty and due process, enumerated in the Constitution's first title.
Amparo
A Mexican legal procedure allowing individuals to seek court protection against unconstitutional acts of authority.
Social constitutionalism
The practice of writing duties of the state toward social groups, such as workers and villages, into constitutional text.
Article 27
The 1917 provision declaring original national ownership of land, waters, and subsoil resources and authorizing restitution and distribution of land.
National domain
The nation's direct ownership of subsoil resources, including petroleum, under Article 27.
Restitution and dotación
Return of lands a village could prove it had lost, and grants of land to communities lacking sufficient land.
Ejido
A form of community land holding and land grant in postrevolutionary law, with restrictions on sale.
Expropriation
Taking of private property for public utility with indemnification, as authorized by Article 27.
Acquired rights
The doctrine, urged by foreign governments, that rights obtained under earlier law could not be nullified retroactively.
Calvo clause
A provision requiring foreigners acquiring property to renounce diplomatic protection by their governments.
Article 123
The 1917 labor and social welfare title, setting the eight-hour day, minimum wage, union and strike rights, and arbitration boards.
Boards of conciliation and arbitration
Tripartite bodies with worker, employer, and government representatives established to settle labor disputes.
CROM
The Confederación Regional Obrera Mexicana, founded in 1918 under Luis N. Morones, allied with Obregón and Calles.
Casa del Obrero Mundial
An anarcho-syndicalist labor center that allied with the Constitutionalists in 1915 and was suppressed in 1916.
Municipio libre
The free municipality of Article 115, governed by an elected ayuntamiento without intermediate political authority between it and the state.
No reelection
The principle, in the original Article 83, that a president could never be reelected, later amended in 1927–1928.
Secular education
Public instruction free of religious doctrine, required by Article 3, extended in 1917 to private primary, elementary, and higher teaching.
Ley Calles
The 1926 penal code reform specifying penalties for violating the constitution's religious provisions.
Cristeros
Participants in the armed rebellion of 1926–1929 against enforcement of anticlerical laws, named for the cry Viva Cristo Rey.
Plan
A Mexican political tradition of proclaiming a government illegitimate and setting out an alternative order, as in the Plan of Agua Prieta.
Zimmermann Telegram
A January 1917 German proposal of an alliance with Mexico in the event of United States entry into the war, which Mexico did not accept.
Carranza Doctrine
A 1918 statement of sovereign equality, nonintervention, and no special privileges for foreigners.
Bucareli understandings
The 1923 agreements between Mexico and the United States on claims, land compensation, and oil rights that preceded United States recognition of Obregón.
Secretaría de Educación Pública
The federal ministry of education created on 3 October 1921, first led by José Vasconcelos.
Cultural missions
Teams of teachers and specialists sent from 1923 to train rural teachers and assist communities.
Caudillismo
Rule by personal leaders whose authority rests on followings, often military, more than on institutions.
Maximato and Jefe Máximo
The period 1928–1934 in which Calles, the supreme chief, exercised influence behind the presidency.
PNR
The Partido Nacional Revolucionario, founded in 1929; renamed PRM in 1938 and PRI in 1946, with changes in structure.

Chapter Reflection

The chapter began with a question about whether a constitution born from civil war could establish a stable political order while fulfilling the Revolution's competing promises. The record from 1917 to 1934 suggests that it could establish a framework and a language, and that the order it helped sustain was real but costly. The Constitution gave villages, workers, teachers, and nationalists a legal vocabulary with which to press claims, and later governments found that they could neither ignore it nor fully fulfill it.

The chapter also reveals the relationship between law and power. Provisions on land, labor, resources, and religion changed what governments could legitimately attempt, yet each depended on administrators, courts, armies, and alliances. Where those were strong, as for organized labor allied with the Sonoran leaders, rights could be used. Where they were weak or hostile, as for many petitioning villages and for the church, the same text could be delayed, bargained away, or turned into an instrument of conflict. Succession crises and the formation of the PNR show how stability was constructed through centralization.

The chapter connects different regions and histories: the agrarian war of Morelos, the industrial and port cities, the commercial agriculture of the north, the oil fields of the Gulf, the Catholic communities of the west-central highlands, and the diplomacy of Washington, Berlin, and London. It also connects to the longer history of Indigenous and rural communities, whose land claims, language, and schooling were reshaped by a state that both invoked their heritage and sought to integrate them, and to the history of women, who were active in nearly every arena while barred from national suffrage.

Chapter 74 takes up the next stage. Lázaro Cárdenas inherited the institutions, the party, and the unfulfilled promises described here. His redistribution of land, his alliance with organized labor and peasants, the oil expropriation of 1938, and the reorganization of the party would give new force to the Constitution's social articles and would also deepen the centralized system that later generations would call the PRI regime. Students should carry forward the lesson that the Revolution's meaning was made after the fighting, through law, administration, and renewed conflict.
