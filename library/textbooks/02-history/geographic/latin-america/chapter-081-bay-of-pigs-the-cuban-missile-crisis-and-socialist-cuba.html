Chapter Opening: An Island at the Center of the Cold War

A government that had held power for less than four years found itself, in October 1962, at the center of the most dangerous confrontation of the Cold War, with its own territory as the stage and its own leaders largely absent from the bargaining that decided the outcome.

On Saturday, 27 October 1962, a U-2 reconnaissance aircraft flown by Major Rudolf Anderson Jr. of the United States Air Force was shot down over eastern Cuba by a Soviet-operated surface-to-air missile. Anderson was killed. Cuban armed forces had been mobilized since the previous Monday, Cuban anti-aircraft gunners had been ordered to fire on low-flying American aircraft, and tens of thousands of Soviet personnel were on the island with weapons that included medium-range nuclear missiles. In Washington, officials who had no complete picture of what was deployed in Cuba debated whether to bomb and invade. In Moscow, a leadership that had placed the missiles in secret was weighing how to retreat without humiliation. In Havana, Fidel Castro and his colleagues were preparing for an invasion they believed could come at any hour. The day is conventionally remembered as the moment when the world came closest to nuclear war.

The question this chapter asks is how that situation came about. In January 1959 a coalition of Cubans of very different political backgrounds had overthrown Fulgencio Batista, the story told in Chapter 80. The coalition promised national sovereignty, the restoration of the Constitution of 1940, honest government, and social justice. Within four years the island had a centralized socialist state allied with the Soviet Union, a population divided by the largest emigration in its history, a trade embargo imposed by its closest neighbor, and a leadership that had survived a CIA-organized invasion and a nuclear crisis. Within eleven years it had a single Communist Party, a state sector that had absorbed almost the whole economy, and a national harvest campaign whose failure exposed the limits of the system it was meant to vindicate.

The chapter argues that this transformation cannot be explained by a single cause. American hostility was real and consequential: the United States cut Cuba's sugar quota, imposed economic restrictions, sponsored an invasion, and ran a sabotage and propaganda program. But Cuban leaders also made choices that were their own. They postponed elections, closed independent newspapers, centralized control of the unions, expanded the security apparatus, and aligned the revolution with Marxism-Leninism, in some cases before and in some cases independently of American pressure. Historians dispute how much weight each factor deserves, and a Historical Debate box in this chapter sets out that disagreement. What this chapter resists is the assumption that the institutions of 1970 were fully determined in January 1959, or that Washington and Havana were the only actors whose decisions mattered.

The chapter also insists that social achievement and political restriction be examined independently. The Cuban government carried out agrarian reform, a national literacy campaign, a rapid expansion of schooling and rural medical care, rent reductions, the dismantling of formal racial segregation, and the incorporation of women and the poor into public life on a scale unmatched in Cuba's republican history. It also eliminated competitive elections, controlled the press, imprisoned opponents, forced into military-run labor camps people it judged socially or ideologically nonconforming, and drove hundreds of thousands of Cubans into exile. Neither list cancels the other. Students are asked to hold both in view and to ask who experienced the revolution as liberation, who as dispossession, and who as both at once.

Evidence comes from sources that often contradict one another. U.S. declassified records, published in the Foreign Relations of the United States series and collected by the National Security Archive, document American planning and decision-making in unusual detail. Soviet records became partly available after 1991 and underlie major studies of Soviet strategy. Cuban sources include the laws and decrees of the revolutionary government, the speeches of Fidel Castro, official statistics, educational reports, and testimony from literacy volunteers and others, but Cuban state archives remain largely closed to independent researchers, and published testimony was often shaped by revolutionary or exile politics. Memoirs by participants on every side must be read as acts of self-presentation. The apparatus of this chapter, including nine Primary Source Windows, is designed to teach this kind of critical reading.

The chapter begins where Chapter 80 ended, with the victory of January 1959, and does not retell the Moncada attack, the Granma expedition, or the guerrilla war. It follows the revolutionary government through the reforms of 1959 and 1960, the invasion at the Bay of Pigs in April 1961, the construction of a socialist society, the missile crisis of October 1962, and the consolidation of the state through the Communist Party, the Revolutionary Offensive of 1968, and the failed ten-million-ton sugar harvest of 1970. Che Guevara's guerrilla campaigns and the wider Latin American insurgencies they inspired are treated in Chapter 82, and Colombia's parallel conflict in Chapter 83. Here the focus is on the island itself and on how its revolution became a state.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the transfer of authority from the provisional civilian government of January 1959 to a government dominated by Fidel Castro and the Rebel Army, distinguishing formal offices from real power.
  2. Explain the provisions and consequences of the Agrarian Reform Law of 17 May 1959, and distinguish it from the second reform of October 1963.
  3. Analyze how domestic political decisions, as well as nationalization and foreign policy, narrowed political pluralism in 1959–1960.
  4. Describe the sequence of nationalizations, trade restrictions, and diplomatic steps that ended in the rupture of relations on 3 January 1961.
  5. Distinguish the Bay of Pigs invasion (a CIA-organized operation by Cuban exiles) from Operation Mongoose, and assess the reasons for the invasion's failure.
  6. Evaluate the significance of Castro's socialist declaration of 16 April 1961 and distinguish ideological declaration from institution-building.
  7. Assess the achievements and limits of the literacy campaign, the mass organizations, and health, housing, and cultural policy, including their effects on women, Afro-Cubans, and religious communities.
  8. Explain the missile crisis as a confrontation among three governments, giving Cuban and Soviet motives independent treatment.
  9. Distinguish the public and secret elements of the October 1962 settlement and identify what remained unresolved after 28 October.
  10. Analyze the creation of the Communist Party of Cuba, the UMAP camps, and the boundaries of revolutionary citizenship.
  11. Interpret Cuba's revolutionary internationalism as an independent policy that was also constrained by Soviet priorities.
  12. Evaluate competing judgments of whether Cuba by 1970 had achieved the independence and social justice promised in 1959.

Historical Context

Cuba became formally independent in 1902 after the war of 1895–1898 and a three-year U.S. military occupation. The Platt Amendment, written into the Cuban Constitution under U.S. pressure, gave Washington a right to intervene in Cuban affairs and secured the naval station at Guantánamo Bay. The amendment was abrogated in 1934, but the lease of Guantánamo remained, and the habit of U.S. involvement in Cuban politics persisted. Chapter 67 traced the end of Spanish rule; here the important point is that Cuban nationalism of the twentieth century was shaped by a persistent sense that sovereignty had been incomplete from the start.

The republican economy rested on sugar. Sugar accounted for the great majority of Cuba's exports, and the United States was the dominant buyer, purchasing sugar at a preferential quota price and selling manufactured goods to Cuba in return. American companies owned a large share of the sugar mills, most of the telephone and electric utilities, and substantial cattle and mining properties. Sugar was a seasonal crop, so many rural workers were employed only for the harvest, the zafra, and endured months of unemployment, the tiempo muerto. Large estates held much of the best land while many cultivators were tenants, sharecroppers, or squatters without title.

Cuba was in some respects relatively well off by Latin American standards, with a large urban middle class, high rates of literacy and doctors per capita in Havana and other cities, and a lively press. But the averages concealed deep inequalities between city and countryside, between Havana and the eastern provinces, and between white and Black Cubans. Schooling and medical care were thin in much of the rural interior, and Afro-Cubans faced discrimination in employment and in many private clubs and facilities. The Constitution of 1940 had promised social rights and democratic institutions, and many of its provisions had never been fully implemented, which gave the promise of its restoration lasting political force.

Batista's coup of 10 March 1952 ended that constitutional order, and Chapter 80 showed how a broad opposition, including the July 26 Movement, the Directorio Revolucionario, urban clandestine networks, and other groups, forced him from power on 1 January 1959. The groups did not share a program. Liberals wanted the 1940 Constitution and elections, nationalists wanted economic sovereignty, and some leaders and the Cuban Communists of the Partido Socialista Popular (PSP), who had joined the anti-Batista struggle late, had Marxist convictions. The Cold War context mattered from the start. The United States had recently overthrown Guatemala's reformist government (Chapter 79) and viewed leftist nationalism in the Caribbean with suspicion, while the Soviet Union, under Nikita Khrushchev, was looking for opportunities in the decolonizing world. Both facts shaped how each side read the other's intentions after January 1959.

Section 81.1

The Revolutionary Government, Political Legitimacy, and the Struggle for Authority in 1959

When Batista fled Havana in the early hours of 1 January 1959, no single institution held undisputed authority. The revolutionary coalition installed a civilian government: Manuel Urrutia Lleó, a judge who had defended the rebels in court, became provisional president, and José Miró Cardona, a prominent lawyer and head of the Havana bar association, became prime minister. Both were respected figures from the liberal opposition, and the cabinet included moderates who expected a return to the 1940 Constitution and to elections. Washington recognized the new government within days.

Formal offices, however, did not match real power. The Rebel Army, commanded in the field by Fidel Castro and including Raúl Castro, Che Guevara, and Camilo Cienfuegos, controlled the countryside and, after the rebel columns entered Havana, the capital's garrisons. Castro had enormous popular prestige, a talent for mass oratory, and a position as commander in chief that no civilian minister could match. He used television and public rallies to speak directly to the Cuban people, and in doing so he often overruled or bypassed the cabinet. The Fundamental Law of February 1959 adapted the 1940 Constitution so that the cabinet could legislate, which concentrated lawmaking power in a body where Castro soon sat as prime minister, after Miró Cardona resigned in mid-February and Castro took the post on 16 February 1959.

The conflicts that followed show how authority shifted. Within the government, moderates worried about the prosecution of Batista's officials, about the growing role of PSP members, and about the pace of reform. In July 1959 a dispute over the pace of events and over Urrutia's public remarks against communism led Castro to resign as prime minister, a move that prompted large demonstrations in his favor. Urrutia resigned on 17–18 July, and Osvaldo Dorticós Torrado, a lawyer sympathetic to the revolution's radical wing, became president. The episode demonstrated that the presidency now served at the pleasure of the armed revolutionary leadership, and Castro returned as prime minister, having shown that no cabinet could govern without him.

One of the most contested aspects of the period was revolutionary justice. In the first months of 1959, tribunals tried former Batista officials, police, and informers for torture and killings. Many Cubans, remembering the brutality of the dictatorship, strongly supported punishment, and public trials attracted large crowds. The most prominent, that of Colonel Jesús Sosa Blanco in January 1959, was staged in a Havana sports stadium before a large audience. Trials were conducted with limited procedural safeguards by courts of revolutionary officers, appeals were restricted, and the law was applied retroactively. Estimates of those executed in 1959 vary widely, from several hundred upward, depending on source and definition. U.S. press and officials objected sharply, Castro defended the trials as justice and criticized foreign interference, and the controversy reinforced both American suspicion and Cuban sensitivity to outside criticism.

The promise of elections became progressively less certain. In early 1959, Castro and other leaders spoke of elections within roughly eighteen months, a timeline that was then extended, and by 1960 Castro and his supporters argued that the revolution had its own mass support and no longer needed a conventional campaign. This was not a sudden decision but a series of postponements in which the leadership concluded that political parties tied to the old order would hinder the revolution. Historians such as Samuel Farber stress the role of this choice in the creation of a one-party state, while others, such as Louis A. Pérez Jr., emphasize the pressures of confrontation. The immediate result was that by the end of 1959 the revolutionary government, though still formally provisional, rested on the authority of Castro and the Rebel Army rather than on any constitutional procedure.

Section 81.2

The Agrarian Reform Law of May 17, 1959, and the Transformation of the Countryside

The first great social measure of the revolutionary government was the Agrarian Reform Law, signed by Castro on 17 May 1959 in the Sierra Maestra. Land reform had been a demand of Cuban reformers for decades, was promised in the program of the July 26 Movement, and was popular among rural workers. The law responded to a countryside in which large sugar estates and cattle ranches covered much of the land while tenants, sharecroppers, and seasonal laborers lived with little security.

The law set a general limit on landholding of thirty caballerías (a Cuban measure of roughly 13.4 hectares, so about 400 hectares), with allowances of up to one hundred caballerías for especially productive sugar, rice, or cattle enterprises. Land above the limit was subject to expropriation, with compensation in twenty-year government bonds at a modest interest rate, based on the value declared by owners for tax purposes. Foreign ownership of agricultural land was restricted, which struck directly at American-owned sugar estates and ranches. Tenants, sharecroppers, and other cultivators were entitled to titles to a "vital minimum" of land, set at two caballerías (about 27 hectares), and the law forbade the subdivision of the largest efficient units into small parcels where this would reduce production.

The law also created the Instituto Nacional de Reforma Agraria (INRA), which became one of the most powerful institutions of the revolutionary state. Led by Castro as president, with Antonio Núñez Jiménez as executive director, INRA administered expropriations, organized cooperatives and state farms, directed agricultural credit, and soon took on housing, road construction, education, and industrial planning in rural areas. It was staffed in part by Rebel Army officers, and its regional delegates became the visible face of the revolution in the countryside. In effect, INRA made agrarian reform an instrument of state building as much as of redistribution.

The consequences must be distinguished carefully. Many tenants and small farmers did receive titles, and the reform broadened rural access to land, credit, and public services. But much of the expropriated land was not divided among individual cultivators. Large sugar properties, in particular, were converted into cooperatives and then into state-administered farms, where workers received wages rather than land. By 1960 the state controlled a large share of Cuba's agricultural land, and estimates for the proportion run to roughly two-fifths of farmland. This was land retained under public administration, not land transferred to those who worked it, and the choice to keep the sugar estates intact reflected both economic reasoning about economies of scale and the leadership's preference for state control. Rural workers gained steady employment, better wages, and new schools and clinics, while smallholders found their autonomy bounded by state purchasing and supply.

Landowners reacted differently. Cattle ranchers in Camagüey and large sugar owners often opposed the reform and many left the island, while some middle farmers who kept their land under the limit were initially reassured. Foreign-owned properties, notably those of large U.S. corporations, were the most exposed. The United States government sent a formal note in June 1959 protesting that the compensation terms were inadequate and that payment was not prompt, effective, or adequate by the standard Washington asserted. Cuban officials replied that compensation based on tax assessments was fair, since owners had long declared low values to avoid taxes, and that the law was an exercise of sovereignty. The reform therefore became the first major point of conflict between the revolution and U.S. economic interests, though the U.S. response in 1959 remained diplomatic. The second agrarian reform, of October 1963, is a separate law and belongs to Section 81.17.

The Agrarian Reform Law of 17 May 1959

Creator: Cuban Council of Ministers under Fidel Castro · Date: 17 May 1959 · Type: Statute · Publication: Gaceta Oficial de la República de Cuba; translations are held in U.S. diplomatic files and collections on Cuban law

Documentary locator: Reproduced 1959 Agrarian Reform Law (official gazette in historical study). These links identify published records or research collections, not a claim of facsimile collation by MGU.

Context: The law was signed in the Sierra Maestra and issued with a ceremony intended to link it to the rebellion. It was the revolution's first major structural reform and the first law to threaten foreign-owned property on a large scale.

Editorial paraphrase of the law's central provisions (not a quotation): The law limits private landholdings to about thirty caballerías, with higher limits for especially productive estates; provides for expropriation of land above the limit; sets compensation in long-term bonds based on declared tax value; restricts agricultural land ownership to Cubans and Cuban companies; grants cultivators titles to a minimum plot; and creates INRA to carry out the reform.

Analysis: The law reveals the leadership's intentions through what it chose to regulate: ownership limits, compensation, and a powerful state agency. It does not show what happened in practice, since implementation involved decisions about which land would be divided and which converted to state farms. It also cannot show how tenants, workers, and owners experienced the process. Its compensation terms, written in the language of sovereignty, were interpreted in Washington as confiscation, and reading it alongside the U.S. note of June 1959 shows how the same text could be judged by different standards.

  3. What can historians learn from the compensation provisions about the government's priorities?
  4. Why can the law alone not tell historians how much land reached individual cultivators?
Section 81.3

Revolutionary Justice, Political Opposition, the Press, and the Consolidation of Power

Between 1959 and 1960 Cuba's political space narrowed. The change was not produced by a single decree. It came through a series of conflicts in which the leadership defined its opponents broadly and responded to them with the instruments of the state. Because the narrowing began before the major nationalizations and before the Bay of Pigs, it cannot be explained only as a reaction to American hostility, though hostility later reinforced it.

Opponents were not a single group. Former Batista supporters and officials, many of whom fled the island in 1959, were the first targets of the revolutionary courts. A second group consisted of liberal and democratic critics who had opposed Batista and now expected elections and a free press. A third consisted of anticommunist revolutionaries, former rebels and members of the anti-Batista underground who objected to the growing influence of the PSP and to the leadership's drift toward the left. A fourth consisted of clandestine opponents who, from 1960, took up armed resistance, some of them with American assistance. Treating them as one category erases the political history of the revolution itself, since many opponents had fought against Batista.

The case of Huber Matos illustrates conflict within the leadership. Matos, a rebel commander and military chief of Camagüey Province who had fought in the Sierra Maestra, wrote a letter of resignation in October 1959 expressing concern about communist influence. Castro treated it as an act of disloyalty. Matos was arrested on 21 October, and Camilo Cienfuegos, sent to Camagüey to carry out the arrest, disappeared on a flight back to Havana a week later, a loss that deprived the revolution of one of its most popular figures. Matos was tried for treason and sentenced to twenty years, which he served in full. The episode occurred in a tense atmosphere: a former air force chief who had defected, Pedro Luis Díaz Lanz, had flown over Havana from Florida that month and dropped leaflets, and anti-aircraft fire in response killed and wounded civilians. The sentence of Matos sent a signal that criticism from within the revolutionary ranks would not be tolerated.

Control over institutions of public opinion followed. Independent newspapers came under pressure, partly from printers' unions and revolutionary activists who inserted "coletillas," rebuttals printed at the end of articles that the government disliked. By 1960 the major independent papers, including the venerable Diario de la Marina, had been closed, seized, or forced into exile, and radio and television moved under state-aligned control. The labor movement was also contested: at a congress of the Confederación de Trabajadores de Cuba in November 1959, leaders of the July 26 Movement who resisted communist influence were displaced, and the confederation came under leadership aligned with the government. Independent political parties dwindled as the government claimed to represent the people directly.

Relations with the Catholic Church deteriorated gradually. The Church was influential in education, and many bishops and laypeople who had opposed Batista grew alarmed at the leftward turn. A pastoral letter in August 1960 expressed concern about communism, and the government accused the clergy of siding with counterrevolution. The conflict peaked in 1961, discussed in Section 81.11. Historians must acknowledge that the revolutionary government faced real threats, including sabotage, armed bands, and a hostile superpower. But not all dissent was conspiracy, and the leadership's willingness to classify liberal and revolutionary critics as enemies is part of the historical record. By the end of 1960 the government's authority rested on mobilization, the security services, and Castro's personal leadership more than on institutional restraint.

Section 81.4

Nationalization, Soviet Trade, and the Breakdown of Cuban–U.S. Relations

The relationship between Havana and Washington deteriorated through 1960 in a sequence of actions and reactions that each side justified by the other's conduct. The key disagreements concerned property and compensation, oil, sugar, and the intentions of the Cuban leadership. The sequence is best understood as a spiral in which economic sovereignty, disputed expropriation, security fears, and ideological confrontation reinforced one another.

In February 1960 Anastas Mikoyan, a senior Soviet leader, visited Havana and signed a trade agreement under which the Soviet Union would buy sugar over several years and extend credit, including Soviet oil. For Cuba, the agreement offered a market and a supplier outside American control. For Washington, it signified Soviet entry into the Caribbean. Cuba's three foreign-owned oil refineries, owned by Texaco, Shell, and Esso (Standard Oil of New Jersey), were pressed by the Cuban government to process Soviet crude. The companies refused, with support from the U.S. government, and in late June 1960 the Cuban government seized the refineries.

The United States responded in July by cutting the remaining Cuban sugar quota for 1960, removing a large part of Cuba's preferential market. The Cuban government replied in early August with a law authorizing the nationalization of the principal U.S.-owned companies, including the telephone and electric utilities and the sugar mills owned by American interests. In October 1960 it nationalized the banks and several hundred of the largest Cuban-owned enterprises, and an Urban Reform Law allowed tenants to become owners of their dwellings through payments. The Cuban leadership presented these measures as the recovery of the national economy, while many Cuban owners viewed them as confiscation. The United States banned most exports to Cuba in October, excepting food and medicine, as a partial embargo.

A distinction is essential. The restrictions of 1960 were partial. The comprehensive U.S. embargo, covering nearly all trade, was proclaimed by President Kennedy in February 1962 and is treated in Section 81.8. On 3 January 1961, days before Kennedy's inauguration, the Eisenhower administration announced the severing of diplomatic relations, after Castro demanded a drastic reduction in the staff of the U.S. embassy in Havana. Both governments then lacked a regular channel of communication, and the Swiss embassy later represented U.S. interests in Havana.

Historians weigh the causes differently. American officials argued that Cuba had expropriated American property without adequate compensation and aligned itself with the Soviet bloc. Cuban leaders argued that the United States had sought to strangle the revolution economically and that the sugar quota cut was an act of aggression. Both claims contain truth, and the sequence suggests mutual escalation. Some scholars argue that the Cuban leadership intended radical change and was prepared to accept confrontation, while others argue that U.S. pressure left it few alternatives. Documents on both sides indicate that by mid-1960 Washington was already considering how to remove Castro, which is the subject of the next division.

Historical Vocabulary
Nationalization and Expropriation

Expropriation is the taking of private property by a state, usually under a law claiming public purpose. Nationalization is the transfer of whole enterprises or sectors to state ownership. In Cuba in 1959–1960, expropriation applied to land under the Agrarian Reform Law, and nationalization applied to refineries, utilities, banks, and industrial firms.

International law of the period held that states could expropriate property but disputed the standard of compensation. The United States asserted a standard of prompt, adequate, and effective payment, while Cuba paid in long-term bonds or, for U.S. firms in 1960, offered a mechanism tied to future sugar sales that Washington rejected. The terms matter because they show that the dispute was about legal principle as well as power. Students should avoid treating the word "confiscation" or "recovery" as a neutral description.

Section 81.5

Cuban Exiles, Domestic Resistance, and the Planning of a U.S.-Backed Invasion

Opposition to the revolutionary government grew inside Cuba and among those who left. Emigration began immediately after January 1959 with Batista's officials and associates, then widened through 1960 and 1961 to include business owners, professionals, landowners, and middle-class Cubans alarmed by nationalization, the closing of the press, and the drift toward Marxism. Many early exiles settled in Miami. In December 1960 the Catholic Welfare Bureau and U.S. officials began what became known as Operation Peter Pan, in which roughly fourteen thousand unaccompanied Cuban children were sent to the United States over the next two years, a movement driven by rumors that the state would remove children from parental authority.

The exile community was politically diverse. It included former Batista supporters, constitutional democrats like Miró Cardona, who left Cuba and became president of the Cuban Revolutionary Council formed in 1961, disaffected veterans of the rebellion such as Manuel Artime, businessmen, clergy, and student leaders. Their aims differed: some wished to restore the old order, others to complete a revolution they believed had been betrayed. This diversity complicated American planning, since Washington wanted a political front that was neither pro-Batista nor too far to the left.

Inside Cuba, resistance took several forms. From 1960 armed groups, some of whom had supplies dropped by American aircraft, operated in the Escambray mountains of central Cuba and other rural areas, where they were eventually pursued by militia and army units in a prolonged campaign that the government called the struggle against bandits and that lasted into the mid-1960s. Clandestine urban networks planned sabotage, including fires and bombings, and an underground press circulated. The difficulty was coordination: dispersed groups, infiltrated by state security and watched by neighborhood committees, could not easily synchronize with a foreign-based operation, and the American planners' assumption of a unified internal opposition was optimistic.

On 17 March 1960, President Eisenhower approved a CIA program of covert action against the Castro government. The plan envisioned propaganda, the formation of a Cuban opposition front, a paramilitary force trained outside Cuba, and a clandestine network inside. Under Richard Bissell, the CIA's deputy director for plans, the paramilitary force evolved into Brigade 2506, recruited from exiles and trained in Guatemala and elsewhere, which grew to roughly fourteen hundred men. Its members included former soldiers, students, professionals, and some Afro-Cuban volunteers. The invasion was meant to seize a beachhead where a provisional government could be installed and request recognition and assistance. A central assumption, repeated in U.S. planning documents, was that the landing would spark a popular uprising and army defections.

Kennedy inherited the plan in January 1961. He accepted the concept but insisted on limiting visible U.S. involvement, which led to a change of landing site from near the town of Trinidad, with better prospects for escape to the Escambray, to the Bay of Pigs on the Zapata Peninsula, remote and swampy, with an airstrip. Air strikes were to destroy Castro's small air force beforehand, using Cuban exile pilots flying aircraft painted with Cuban markings. The invasion was therefore a U.S.-organized covert intervention carried out by Cuban exiles, rather than a conventional invasion by the U.S. Army. The government in Havana, however, had warning: news of the training had appeared in the American and Guatemalan press, and Cuban intelligence had penetrated exile circles.

Section 81.6

The Bay of Pigs Invasion, April 17–19, 1961, and the Defeat of Brigade 2506

The operation began on 15 April 1961, when B-26 bombers flown by exile pilots from bases in Nicaragua struck airfields at Havana, San Antonio de los Baños, and Santiago de Cuba. The planes carried Cuban Air Force markings, and a cover story claimed they were piloted by defecting Cuban airmen. The strikes destroyed some Cuban aircraft but left a handful of combat-capable planes, including T-33 jet trainers and Sea Fury fighters, which would prove decisive. The cover story was quickly exposed at the United Nations.

On the night of 16–17 April, Brigade 2506 landed on the southern coast of Cuba, in the Zapata Peninsula, at Playa Girón and Playa Larga. Inland, paratroopers dropped by air on the morning of 17 April and seized the road center of San Blas; this was not a separate amphibious landing. The area is flat, swampy coastal land, crossed by few roads, with a narrow causeway at each approach, and an airstrip at Girón. The brigade's objective was to secure the beachhead and airstrip, hold it against counterattack, and await a political reaction. The geography cut both ways: it made the landing hard to reach quickly by land, but it also trapped the invaders, with the Escambray mountains, intended as a refuge, more than eighty miles away.

The landing went wrong quickly. Coral reefs fouled some boats, and Cuban militia detected the landing early. Kennedy, concerned to preserve deniability, had canceled a second wave of air strikes on the airfields the evening before. On 17 April the Cuban pilots flying the surviving aircraft sank two of the brigade's transport ships, the Houston and the Río Escondido, which carried ammunition, fuel, and communications equipment, and drove away the others. The invaders were left without much of their supplies and under constant air attack. Cuban ground forces, including militia battalions, army units, and police, moved toward the area along the causeways, supported by artillery and tanks, with Castro personally directing operations from a command post at a sugar mill near the front.

Fighting lasted three days. The brigade fought hard, and in the first hours some Cuban militia units suffered heavy casualties. But the invaders ran short of ammunition, and the expected uprising did not occur. Instead, Cuban authorities arrested large numbers of suspected opponents in the days around the invasion, with estimates reaching into the tens of thousands, which eliminated potential internal support. On 18 and 19 April the brigade's position collapsed. A final U.S. decision to allow unmarked Navy aircraft to fly cover came too late, and four American civilian pilots employed by the CIA, flying from Nicaragua, were killed. By 19 April the invasion was defeated. Roughly a hundred or more members of the brigade died and approximately twelve hundred were captured, figures that vary by source, and Cuban losses are commonly reported at over a hundred and fifty killed, though counts differ. The prisoners were held until December 1962, when Cuba released them in return for food and medicine from American donors valued at tens of millions of dollars.

Analysts distinguish several kinds of failure. Intelligence failure lay in assuming that Castro's support was weak and that Cuban forces would defect. Planning failure lay in the changes to the landing site and the dependence on air strikes whose success was uncertain. Operational failure involved the ships, communications, and ammunition. And political failure lay in Kennedy's effort to mount a large operation while concealing American authorship, a contradiction that left the force without the support it needed. Later internal CIA reviews, including the Kirkpatrick report, criticized the agency's handling. For Cuba, the victory was a vindication of the revolution's military mobilization and of Castro's leadership. For the United States, it was a public humiliation, and for exiles, a bitter betrayal. It also fixed Cuban leaders' conviction that the United States would try again.

Section 81.7

The Socialist Declaration, Revolutionary Victory, and the Reorganization of Cuban Politics

On 16 April 1961, the day after the air attacks on Cuban airfields, Castro spoke at a funeral in Havana for the victims, who numbered about seven. In the speech he declared that the revolution was a socialist revolution. The date matters. The declaration came one day before the landings at the Bay of Pigs, and Castro, who expected invasion, framed the coming defense as a defense of a socialist revolution by workers and peasants. It must not be portrayed as a decision first made after the invasion failed, although the defeat of the landing strengthened its political effects.

The declaration was the culmination of a process rather than a bolt from the blue. By 1961 the nationalizations had put most of the economy in state hands, the government had signed trade and credit agreements with the Soviet bloc, and PSP members held positions in the administration and the unions. Why Castro made the announcement at that moment is debated. Some scholars argue that he sought to rally the militias with an ideological appeal and to give the revolution an identity distinct from liberal reformism. Others suggest that he wanted to bind the Soviet Union more firmly to Cuba's defense. Both motives could have operated together, and the sources do not allow a definitive ranking.

The victory at Playa Girón, as Cubans named it, gave the revolution a founding myth. The defeat of a force organized by the world's strongest power strengthened national pride and Castro's authority, and Cubans who had been uncertain about the leadership's direction found their loyalty reinforced by the sense that the nation was under attack. The invasion also justified a security crackdown, including mass arrests and tighter surveillance. After the landing, the legitimacy of the government increasingly rested on mobilization and on defense of the revolution, rather than on competitive elections that had been postponed indefinitely.

Institutional change followed. In 1961 the three organizations that had made the revolution, the July 26 Movement, the Directorio Revolucionario, and the PSP, were merged into the Organizaciones Revolucionarias Integradas (ORI), directed in practice by veteran PSP organizers such as Aníbal Escalante. Escalante's efforts to build the ORI around the old communist cadres provoked a reaction. In March 1962 Castro denounced "sectarianism" and removed Escalante from his post, asserting the primacy of the leaders of the guerrilla movement over the old party apparatus. The organization was reconstituted in 1962 as the United Party of the Socialist Revolution (PURSC), and in 1965 as the Communist Party of Cuba, discussed in Section 81.18.

On 1 December 1961, in a long televised speech, Castro stated that he was a Marxist-Leninist and would remain one for life. The statement clarified the leadership's identity but should be distinguished from the institutional process that followed. A declaration is not a party, a constitution, or a system of administration. Cuba in 1961 did not yet have a ruling party, a codified legal system, or a settled economic structure, and these came over the following fifteen years, culminating in the Constitution of 1976. The 1961 declarations opened a period of experimentation and improvisation in which the shape of the socialist state remained unsettled.

Section 81.8

Operation Mongoose, the U.S. Embargo, Cuban Exile Politics, and Hemispheric Isolation

The Kennedy administration did not abandon its goal of removing Castro after the invasion failed. In November 1961 the president authorized a new covert program, Operation Mongoose, under the general direction of Brigadier General Edward Lansdale, a counterinsurgency specialist, with Attorney General Robert Kennedy closely involved. Mongoose was separate from the Bay of Pigs. It followed it, was designed under Kennedy rather than Eisenhower, and sought to foster a revolt inside Cuba through intelligence collection, propaganda, economic warfare, and sabotage rather than through a single invasion.

Declassified planning documents show that the program's aims were ambitious. Lansdale's proposals of early 1962 envisioned phases of preparation leading to an "open revolt" and the overthrow of the regime, with a target of October 1962 in some versions. Activities included infiltration of agents, sabotage of industrial and transportation targets, and attacks on the sugar harvest. Historians distinguish carefully between documented authorized activities, which included raids, sabotage attempts, and propaganda, and proposals that were never approved or carried out. The Pentagon's proposals of March 1962, known as Operation Northwoods, which contemplated staging incidents to justify intervention, were rejected by civilian leaders. Separately, the CIA had made attempts to arrange Castro's assassination, documented by the Senate's Church Committee in 1975, although the relationship of those plots to Mongoose is disputed.

Economic pressure intensified. On 3 February 1962, Kennedy proclaimed a comprehensive embargo on trade with Cuba, effective 7 February, expanding the partial restrictions of 1960. The embargo cut Cuba off from its traditional supplier of machinery and spare parts, a serious problem for an economy whose factories and vehicles were of American manufacture, and forced a reorientation toward the Soviet bloc. Cuban officials described it as economic warfare, while American officials defended it as a response to Cuba's alignment with the Soviet Union and its expropriation of U.S. property. Its economic effects, compared with the effects of domestic policy decisions, are debated, and the embargo became a permanent feature of Cuban politics, treated in Cuba as an explanation of shortages and in the United States as a tool of pressure.

Diplomatically, Cuba was increasingly isolated in the hemisphere. At a meeting of the Organization of American States in Punta del Este, Uruguay, in January 1962, a majority of members voted to exclude Cuba's government from participation in the inter-American system on the grounds that Marxism-Leninism was incompatible with it. The vote was fourteen to one, with six abstentions, among them Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Bolivia, Ecuador, and Mexico, which indicates that Latin American governments were far from uniformly aligned with Washington. Castro answered with the Second Declaration of Havana, calling for revolution in Latin America. In 1964 the OAS adopted sanctions, and all member states except Mexico severed relations.

Emigration continued. Between 1959 and the mid-1960s several hundred thousand Cubans left, most of them for the United States, in an exodus that changed the island's social structure. Motives differed: some left because they lost property, some because they objected to the end of political pluralism, some because they feared religious or educational changes, some because they were separated from family, some because they saw no economic future. It would be wrong to assume that all emigrants held identical views, and wrong to assume that all who stayed supported the government. The prospect of further invasion, combined with infiltration, sabotage, and the threat of Mongoose, shaped Cuban security policy. It led the government to build up its armed forces and militias and to seek a firmer military relationship with the Soviet Union, which opened the path to the deployment described in Division IV.

Section 81.9

The Literacy Campaign of 1961 and the Transformation of Education

The government declared 1961 the "Year of Education" and launched a national literacy campaign that became one of the revolution's best-known achievements. Before 1959 Cuba had a relatively high literacy rate for Latin America, but the national average concealed sharp differences. The census of 1953 recorded illiteracy of roughly a quarter of the population aged ten and over, and the proportion was considerably higher in rural areas, especially in the eastern provinces, where many children lacked access to a school at all. Rural teachers were poorly paid, and school attendance in the countryside was irregular.

The campaign was organized around volunteers. Roughly a hundred thousand young people, many of them secondary-school students aged ten to nineteen, were enrolled as brigadistas in the Conrado Benítez Brigades, named after a young Afro-Cuban volunteer teacher killed in January 1961 by counterrevolutionary guerrillas in the Escambray. Other brigades mobilized workers and adult volunteers, and trained teachers supervised the effort. Schools closed in the spring so that students could go to the countryside, live with rural families, work alongside them during the day, and teach in the evening by the light of a small lantern issued to each volunteer. Materials included a primer, with lessons built on revolutionary themes such as land reform and the cooperative, and a manual for the teachers. In all, official figures counted several hundred thousand volunteers, including tens of thousands of professional teachers, and over seven hundred thousand people taught to read and write.

The social history of the campaign is as important as its statistics. Young women made up a large share of the brigadistas, and for many it was their first extended time away from home. Some families were reluctant to allow daughters to go, fearing for their safety and honor and disliking the change in authority, and the government and the Federation of Cuban Women worked to reassure parents. Rural families hosted urban teenagers and shared their houses and meals, and the encounter between city and countryside, between white middle-class students and often poorer and darker rural households, reshaped the participants' views of their own country. Some brigadistas were killed in attacks by armed opponents, and the campaign was carried out under threat of invasion, which gave it a defensive and heroic character.

On 22 December 1961, Castro announced in Havana that Cuba had been declared a territory free of illiteracy, with a reported illiteracy rate of roughly four percent. Historians treat these claims with a distinction between official declarations and independently measured outcomes. International observers, including UNESCO and visiting scholars such as Jonathan Kozol, who wrote an appreciative account in 1978, found that the campaign substantially reduced illiteracy, and later censuses confirmed very high literacy rates. But the 1961 figures were produced by the government, definitions of literacy were minimal, and follow-up programs were needed so that newly literate adults did not lapse. The government launched follow-up courses and later a drive to bring all adults to the sixth-grade level.

The campaign was both an educational initiative and a political one. It was framed as the revolution's gift to the poor and as a national project in which the whole society participated, and its primers conveyed political messages. Education's subsequent role in the system included rapid expansion of schooling, scholarships, technical training, rural boarding schools, and, from 1961, the nationalization of private and religious schools, which placed all instruction under state control. Education served both civic inclusion and political formation: the school became a place where children were taught revolutionary values, and families found the state increasingly involved in the upbringing of the young. The campaign should be seen as a major episode of social history in which hundreds of thousands of ordinary Cubans participated, rather than as a statistic demonstrating governmental success.

Section 81.10

Women, Mass Organizations, Race, and the Expansion of Revolutionary Citizenship

The revolutionary state built its relationship with the population through mass organizations that mobilized people by sector and neighborhood. The Federación de Mujeres Cubanas (FMC), the Federation of Cuban Women, was founded in August 1960 under the leadership of Vilma Espín, a veteran of the urban underground and the Sierra Maestra who later married Raúl Castro. It combined earlier women's groups into a single organization attached to the revolution. Its early initiatives included schools for domestic servants and for peasant girls, such as the Ana Betancourt school, which brought thousands of young rural women to Havana for training in sewing and other skills, and it organized women for the literacy campaign, militia service, and volunteer work. The FMC also promoted the opening of child-care centers, the círculos infantiles, which allowed more mothers to work.

Women's opportunities expanded in education, paid employment, and public life. Domestic servants, a large group in prerevolutionary Havana, were retrained, and women entered fields from teaching and medicine to agriculture and administration. But the FMC operated within the state and the party's priorities rather than as an independent movement, and assumptions about gender remained. Child care and housework were still seen as women's work, the FMC's early courses often reinforced traditional domestic skills, and women were underrepresented in the senior leadership. The "double burden" of paid work and household labor became a lasting feature of women's experience. The result was real expansion of opportunity, constrained by the persistence of older gender expectations.

The Comités de Defensa de la Revolución (CDRs), Committees for the Defense of the Revolution, were established in late September 1960 in a speech by Castro, in response to bomb attacks and sabotage. Organized by city block and rural neighborhood, the committees began as a vigilance system and expanded into a network that carried out vaccination drives, blood donation, cleaning campaigns, ration and housing administration, and political meetings. For many Cubans they were a framework of neighborhood solidarity and a means of access to services. For others they were a mechanism of surveillance: committee members kept records of residents' activities and attitudes, and a negative report could affect employment and housing. The CDRs thus illustrate the dual character of the mass organizations, which combined participation with discipline.

The revolution's treatment of race requires careful attention. In 1959 Castro spoke against racial discrimination, and the government moved to open beaches, parks, hotels, and workplaces that had been segregated in practice, though not by law. Afro-Cubans, who disproportionately belonged to the poor, benefited from the land reform, rent reductions, education, and health programs. At the same time, the government in the early 1960s declared racial discrimination eliminated and closed Black social societies and clubs, treating independent race-based organization as divisive. Scholars such as Alejandro de la Fuente have shown that Afro-Cubans gained substantially in education, employment, and health but that prejudice survived in attitudes, hiring, and representation in leadership, and that the official claim that the race problem had been solved limited open discussion. Afro-Cuban military commanders such as Juan Almeida held high rank, yet top leadership remained predominantly white.

Trade unions, youth organizations, and peasant associations completed the structure. The Asociación Nacional de Agricultores Pequeños (ANAP), founded in 1961, represented small farmers, the Union of Young Communists organized youth, and the Cuban Workers' Confederation (CTC) became an instrument of state labor policy. In each case the organization channeled participation and also limited independent action. The tension at the center of revolutionary citizenship was that Cubans who had been excluded from public life, rural people, the poor, Afro-Cubans, and women, gained new access to schools, clinics, workplaces, and political activity, while independent associations and opposition were shut out. Participation expanded within the framework of the state, and the framework itself was not open to challenge.

Exiles: Their motives ranged from loss of property to rejection of one-party rule to family separation, and they were not uniformly conservative.

Section 81.11

Health Care, Housing, Religion, and Cultural Transformation

Health care was reorganized under the Ministry of Public Health. Before 1959 medical services were concentrated in Havana and other cities, with private clinics and mutual-aid societies serving those who could pay and little available in much of the countryside. The government created a rural medical service in 1960, sending newly graduated doctors to underserved areas, built rural hospitals and clinics, and launched vaccination campaigns that targeted diseases such as polio and, later, measles and diphtheria. Medical education was expanded and made free. The effort took place in difficult conditions, since a large share of Cuba's doctors, perhaps half, emigrated in the early years, leaving shortages that the government met by accelerating training. Over time the health system produced marked reductions in infant mortality and increases in life expectancy, though the figures come largely from Cuban government statistics, which international bodies have broadly but not uncritically accepted.

Housing was another focus. A rent reduction of March 1959 cut rents by roughly a third to a half, and the Urban Reform Law of October 1960 allowed tenants to become owners of their dwellings through payments over a period of years while ending most private renting, and it limited the property landlords could own. The measure benefited tenants and shifted the housing market under state control. It also affected owners who had lived on rental income, and the state's involvement in housing allocation gave it a new instrument of influence. Construction did not keep pace with need, and shortages and overcrowding persisted, since resources went to other priorities.

The relationship between the government and the Catholic Church deteriorated from mid-1960. The Church, which ran private schools attended by many middle-class and some poorer children, grew alarmed by the leftward movement and some clergy and laity were associated with the opposition, including those who took part in conspiracies. In June 1961 the government nationalized all private schools, including Catholic and Protestant ones, and in September 1961 it expelled well over a hundred priests, including the auxiliary bishop of Havana, Eduardo Boza Masvidal, who were put on a ship to Spain. Religious practice was not banned, and church buildings stayed open, but believers were discouraged from advancement in the party and some public careers, and religious institutions lost their public role. Afro-Cuban and syncretic religions such as Santería continued to be practiced privately, regarded by some officials as backward.

The revolution promised wider access to culture. It founded the Cuban Film Institute (ICAIC) in March 1959, the Casa de las Américas in April 1959 under Haydée Santamaría, a national publishing house, a national ballet and theater companies, and book fairs and mobile cinemas that carried films and performances to the countryside. Ordinary Cubans gained access to books, films, and cultural events on a scale unknown before. But debate arose over artistic freedom. In May 1961 the censorship of PM, a short documentary about nightlife in Havana, led to a controversy about the limits of expression and the closure of the literary supplement associated with the film's makers.

Castro addressed the controversy in his "Words to the Intellectuals" of 30 June 1961, delivered at the National Library after meetings with writers and artists. He asserted that creators might work in any style within the revolution but that nothing should be permitted against it. The formulation, widely cited, left open who would decide what counted as being within the revolution, and historians read it both as a promise of broad tolerance for socialist and nonsocialist art and as the foundation for later restrictions. The transformation extended beyond the economy: it reached into family life, schools, hospitals, religious institutions, and cultural expression, and in each domain the state became the principal organizer of social life.

Section 81.12

Central Planning, Food Rationing, Labor, and the Everyday Socialist Economy

After the nationalizations, the government faced the task of running an economy it had taken over. A Central Planning Board, the Junta Central de Planificación (JUCEPLAN), was created in 1960 under Regino Boti, and ministries were established to direct production. Che Guevara became president of the National Bank in November 1959, headed the industrial department of INRA in 1960, and in February 1961 became Minister of Industries. He championed rapid industrialization and a model in which enterprises were managed as units of a single state budget without market-style profit accounting, a system he called budgetary finance. Others, drawing on Soviet practice, favored economic calculation and material incentives. This disagreement, sometimes called the Great Debate of 1963–1965, was about how a socialist economy should be organized.

Early planning emphasized industrialization and diversification away from sugar, on the assumption that dependence on a single crop was the root of underdevelopment. The effort ran into difficulties: shortages of capital, spare parts, and trained managers, the loss of technicians who emigrated, and the cutoff of American suppliers for factories built with U.S. machinery. Agricultural output fell, partly because sugar acreage was reduced and farmland converted to other uses. By 1963 the government shifted course, returning to sugar as the engine of development and planning to expand output, using revenue from sugar exports to finance industrialization later.

In March 1962 the government introduced food rationing. The libreta de abastecimiento, a ration book, set monthly allocations of staple foods and later other goods at low fixed prices. Rationing reflected shortages, caused by several factors: the loss of imports under the embargo, the disruption of production during nationalization and agrarian transformation, increased consumption by lower-income Cubans whose wages and employment had risen, and the diversion of resources to the military. Historians dispute the weight of these causes, and Cuban officials emphasized the embargo while critics emphasized domestic policy. Whatever the balance, rationing ensured that basic food reached the poor and became a permanent feature of Cuban life, along with price controls and public distribution.

Workers' experience changed. The state became the employer of most workers, wages were set centrally, and unemployment, especially seasonal unemployment in the sugar zones, fell sharply. Unions lost autonomy, and their role shifted to mobilizing production. The leadership debated incentives. Guevara argued for moral incentives such as honor, social recognition, and volunteer work, which would create a new socialist consciousness, while others defended material incentives such as wage differentials. Volunteer labor on weekends, including sugarcane cutting, became part of the culture of mobilization. In practice, the revolution used both, and the relative weight shifted over time, with the late 1960s marked by a strong turn to moral incentives.

Dependence on the Soviet Union grew. The Soviets purchased sugar, supplied oil, machinery, and credit, and eventually became Cuba's principal trading partner, and Cuba's trade deficit was financed by Soviet credit. The leadership tried to preserve autonomy, and relations were not always smooth: Cuban leaders disagreed with Moscow over economic strategy, revolutionary policy, and, after 1962, over how to deal with the United States. Dependence on a single patron replaced dependence on a single market, a trade-off that students should weigh carefully.

Historical Vocabulary
Libreta de abastecimiento

The libreta, or ration book, was introduced in March 1962 to distribute staple foods and other basic goods at fixed low prices to households registered with local stores. Each household's entitlement was recorded in the book and varied by family size.

The libreta mattered because it guaranteed basic provisioning for the poor while also marking the shift to a system in which the state controlled distribution. It is often cited as evidence of the embargo's effects, and equally as evidence of domestic economic decisions. Students should avoid treating it as simply one or the other.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Workers, Volunteers, and Divided Families

Rural households. For a sugar worker's family in the eastern provinces, the reforms brought steady employment, new schools and clinics, and in some cases a house. Households that once endured months of unemployment between harvests now had work in state farms and cooperatives. Smallholding families gained titles but learned to depend on state agencies for credit, seed, and sale of their crops, and some resented bureaucratic control.

Urban workers. Factory and service workers saw wages rise relative to prices and rents fall, but found that goods became scarcer, queues longer, and unions less independent. Weekends increasingly included volunteer work and meetings of the neighborhood committee.

Literacy volunteers. Teenage brigadistas lived for months in remote households, an encounter that changed both volunteers and hosts and that many remembered as formative. Some went home proud, and some, as later memoirs show, felt used or, after leaving the island, disillusioned.

Families divided by emigration. Departure was often a family decision made under strain, with some members leaving and others remaining, and with long separations, since after the missile crisis direct flights were suspended and travel became difficult. Parents who sent children alone through Operation Peter Pan sometimes waited years to reunite. Those who stayed faced suspicion and loss of jobs or housing if relatives were identified as having left. The silences in the surviving sources about these private experiences are themselves evidence of whose lives the official record preserved.

Section 81.13

Cuban Security, Soviet Strategy, and the Secret Deployment of Nuclear Missiles

The decision to place Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba was made by two governments for different reasons. For Cuba, the central motive was defense. After the Bay of Pigs, Operation Mongoose, the exclusion from the OAS, and the embargo, Cuban leaders had concluded that a larger American attack was likely. They believed Cuba needed a deterrent that Washington would respect. Castro has said, and Soviet and Cuban documents support, that Cuba accepted the missiles to strengthen its defense and to show solidarity with the socialist camp. Cuban officials did not regard the weapons as a Soviet imposition.

Soviet motives were more complex. Nikita Khrushchev, who had boasted publicly of Soviet missile power, knew that the Soviet Union lagged far behind the United States in strategic weapons, with the United States holding a large advantage in intercontinental missiles and bombers. The United States had also placed Jupiter medium-range missiles in Turkey and Italy, which could strike Soviet territory within minutes. Khrushchev reportedly conceived the idea in the spring of 1962, partly as a way to redress this imbalance quickly and cheaply, partly to defend Cuba, and partly to gain leverage in negotiations over Berlin. Historians such as Aleksandr Fursenko and Timothy Naftali, drawing on Soviet records, stress that Khrushchev saw the deployment both as protection for an ally and as a strategic gamble. It was not simply an act of aggression, and it was not simply an act of altruism.

Negotiations were conducted in the summer. A Soviet delegation visited Havana in late May, Raúl Castro traveled to Moscow in July, and Che Guevara went in August to settle details, and an agreement was reached to deploy missiles and troops. The operation, code-named Anadyr, was carried out in secrecy, with Soviet troops told they were being sent to a cold climate, and ships carrying personnel and equipment sailed with their cargoes hidden. Soviet planners eventually landed some forty thousand troops, which was far more than American intelligence estimated at the time, along with surface-to-air missiles, coastal-defense cruise missiles, tanks, fighter aircraft, and the equipment for medium-range ballistic missiles of the R-12 type that could reach much of the eastern United States.

A crucial distinction concerns types of nuclear weapons. The strategic missiles, the R-12s, were what American reconnaissance found and what the crisis publicly concerned. In addition, the Soviets sent tactical nuclear weapons, short-range systems for use against an invasion force, including rockets and warheads for coastal missiles. This was not known to the United States at the time and became clear only after the Cold War, when Soviet participants disclosed it at conferences in the early 1990s. Authority to use them was a matter of unresolved command arrangements and later dispute. Knowledge of these weapons is retrospective: American leaders in 1962 did not have a full picture of what was on the island, and the danger was greater than they realized.

The crisis was therefore a three-country confrontation. Cuba was not a mere stage on which Washington and Moscow played. Its leadership had its own reasons, its own forces, and its own fear, and it later insisted on its sovereignty over the weapons' presence and the terms of their removal. At the same time, the Soviets controlled the missiles and made the decisions about them, and Cuban leaders had limited ability to shape the outcome. Both facts, Cuban agency and Cuban dependence, belong in the account.

Section 81.14

The Discovery of the Missiles, ExComm, and Kennedy's Naval Quarantine

On 14 October 1962, a U-2 flown by Major Richard Heyser photographed sites near San Cristóbal in western Cuba. Analysts at the National Photographic Interpretation Center examined the film on 15 October and concluded that it showed medium-range ballistic missile equipment. On the morning of 16 October, National Security Adviser McGeorge Bundy informed President Kennedy. The administration had been warned by critics, notably Senator Kenneth Keating, that missiles might be in Cuba, and Kennedy had issued public warnings in September against offensive weapons there. The Soviets had assured him privately that they would not place such weapons in Cuba, so the discovery appeared as deception.

Kennedy convened a group of advisers known as the Executive Committee of the National Security Council, or ExComm, which met repeatedly over the following days. Members included Robert Kennedy, Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, Secretary of State Dean Rusk, Bundy, General Maxwell Taylor, Treasury Secretary Douglas Dillon, Under Secretary George Ball, and Theodore Sorensen. Kennedy secretly recorded some meetings, and the transcripts, along with the National Archives and State Department records, preserve the deliberations. Participants debated several options: a surgical air strike on the missile sites, a broader air attack, an invasion, diplomatic negotiation, and a naval blockade. Military leaders favored air strikes and invasion, and some advisers warned that an attack would kill Soviet personnel and risk wider war. McNamara and others argued for a more limited response.

The leadership settled on a naval measure and on 22 October Kennedy announced it in a televised address. He disclosed the presence of the missiles, demanded their removal, declared that any missile launched from Cuba against a country in the hemisphere would be treated as an attack by the Soviet Union on the United States, and announced a "quarantine" on shipment of offensive weapons to Cuba. The quarantine took effect on 24 October. On 23 October the Organization of American States voted unanimously to support the action, which gave it a regional legal basis. Cuban forces had been mobilized and placed at full alert on 23 October, and Castro addressed the nation to denounce the American actions and affirm Cuba's right to defend itself.

Soviet ships approaching the quarantine line stopped or turned back on 24 October, in what Secretary of State Rusk reportedly described as a moment when the two sides had stared at one another. At the United Nations on 25 October, U.S. Ambassador Adlai Stevenson displayed reconnaissance photographs. Meanwhile, work on the missile sites continued, and the United States increased low-level reconnaissance flights. Cuban anti-aircraft units, on alert, began firing at them. The deployments, the mobilization, and the chain of command among Soviet, Cuban, and American forces created a situation in which mistakes could escalate. The public speech, aimed at rallying domestic and international support, concealed the confidential debate, which showed much more uncertainty about the risks.

Historical Vocabulary
Quarantine versus Blockade

A blockade, under international law, is an act of war in which one state prevents ships of all nations from entering the ports of another. The Kennedy administration chose the term quarantine for its measure, which was limited to offensive weapons and justified by the OAS vote, in order to avoid the legal implications of a formal blockade.

The distinction was partly legal and partly diplomatic, intended to reduce the appearance of an act of war and to build regional support. Students should recognize that, in practice, the measure involved warships stopping vessels at sea, and that the choice of word was itself a strategic decision.

Section 81.15

Black Saturday, October 27, and the Secret Negotiations That Averted Nuclear War

The most dangerous phase came between 26 and 28 October. On the evening of 26 October the White House received a long, personal letter from Khrushchev, which proposed that the Soviet Union would remove its missiles if the United States pledged not to invade Cuba. The tone was emotional, and the message offered a way out. The next morning a second message, broadcast publicly by Moscow radio, raised the price: it demanded that the United States also remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey. The two letters put Washington in a quandary, since the public demand seemed harder to meet than the private offer.

Cuban leaders were meanwhile preparing for attack. Castro, convinced that an American invasion was imminent, sent a letter to Khrushchev on 26 October, which urged firmness, and which Soviet readers took to suggest that Moscow should consider a nuclear first strike if Cuba were invaded. Its meaning is debated. Castro later said he had intended to indicate that an American invasion would be answered with force, and not to advocate a preemptive strike, but Khrushchev, reading it, concluded that Castro was inviting catastrophe. The episode shows that the Cuban leadership was prepared to risk extreme escalation, and that Moscow's perception of Havana's recklessness influenced its decision to settle.

On 27 October, Major Rudolf Anderson's U-2 was shot down over Cuba by a surface-to-air missile fired by a Soviet unit, killing the pilot. The shootdown, which occurred against standing orders to avoid such actions without Moscow's approval, reflects the danger of unauthorized escalation and uncertain command arrangements, and Soviet and American records indicate that local commanders took the decision. Kennedy's advisers debated whether to retaliate. The president decided to wait. Elsewhere, near the quarantine line, American destroyers dropped practice depth charges to force a Soviet submarine, B-59, to surface. Later accounts, by participants and historians such as Serhii Plokhy, report that the submarine's officers debated using a nuclear-armed torpedo, and that the flotilla chief of staff, Vasily Arkhipov, helped prevent it. Details of this episode are reconstructed from later testimony and should be treated with caution.

That evening, Robert Kennedy met privately with Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin. He conveyed that the United States was prepared to give a no-invasion assurance if the missiles were withdrawn. He also stated, as a private understanding that could not be made public, that the American Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be removed within a few months, although the president would not accept a public trade. On 28 October, Khrushchev announced on Moscow radio that the Soviet Union would dismantle and return the missiles. The settlement had two components: a public one, in which the United States pledged not to invade Cuba, and a secret one, in which the Jupiters were removed from Turkey by the following spring. Keeping the second secret allowed Kennedy to appear to have won without concession, and its disclosure much later changed the understanding of how the crisis ended.

October 28 did not resolve everything. The agreement was reached in principle. It was followed by negotiations over verification, over the removal of Soviet IL-28 bomber aircraft that the United States regarded as offensive, and over the terms of the American pledge. And there remained the gap in knowledge: American leaders did not know that tactical nuclear weapons were on the island, and the Cuban government had not been consulted at all. The historical account must therefore separate what participants knew in October 1962 from what historians learned later, and must recognize that the outcome depended on a combination of leaders' prudence, restraint by some subordinates, and luck.

Section 81.16

Castro's Five Points, Cuba's Exclusion, and the Diplomatic Aftermath

Cuban leaders learned of Khrushchev's decision from the radio, and the circumstances caused deep resentment. The settlement was reached between Washington and Moscow without consulting Havana, which regarded the weapons' presence and withdrawal as matters touching Cuban sovereignty. Castro reportedly reacted with anger and felt humiliated that his government was treated as a bargaining chip. The episode revealed the limits of Cuba's autonomy within the Soviet alliance.

On 28 October Castro issued a statement of five demands as the conditions for a real settlement. They were five separately articulated demands: (1) an end to the American economic blockade and commercial and economic pressure; (2) an end to subversive activities, including the landing of agents and organization of mercenary invasions; (3) an end to attacks on Cuba originating from bases in the United States and Puerto Rico; (4) an end to violations of Cuban airspace and territorial waters by American aircraft and warships; and (5) the withdrawal of the United States from its Guantánamo naval base and the return of that territory to Cuba. These points were Cuba's statement of the conditions for peace and a declaration that its sovereignty was not negotiable by others.

Castro also refused to accept inspection of Cuban territory by the United Nations, which he regarded as an infringement on sovereignty. This created an impasse, since the United States wanted verification that the missiles had been removed. Anastas Mikoyan was sent to Havana and spent several weeks in November persuading the Cuban leadership. The compromise was that Soviet ships carrying the missiles would be inspected at sea by American naval vessels, with Soviet cooperation. The dispute over IL-28 bombers was resolved in November when Khrushchev agreed to withdraw them, and on 20 November, Kennedy announced the end of the naval quarantine.

The American non-invasion pledge was important but limited. It was given in the correspondence between Kennedy and Khrushchev and was conditioned on verified removal of the missiles and a guarantee against future introduction of offensive weapons. It was never embodied in a formal treaty, because the conditions of verification were not completed to the American satisfaction, owing to Cuban refusal of on-site inspection. As a result the pledge existed as an informal understanding, and its force was uncertain. Cuba's leaders remained fearful that the United States could resume efforts to overthrow them, and these efforts, including covert operations and attempts on Castro's life, did not stop.

The consequences for the three governments differed. For the United States and the Soviet Union, the crisis led to new mechanisms of communication, including a direct "hotline" agreed in June 1963, and to the Limited Test Ban Treaty of August 1963, which marked a thaw in superpower relations. For Cuba, the outcome was ambivalent. The island was more secure from invasion in practice, since Washington's pledge, however informal, constrained direct attack. But it was more dependent on Soviet protection, with a Soviet leadership whose reliability had been called into question. Castro visited the Soviet Union in April and May 1963, was received with honors, and the alliance was repaired, though tension remained. Did Cuba become more secure, or more dependent, or both? The evidence suggests both.

Cause and Effect: From the Reforms of 1959 to the Missile Crisis of 1962

Long-term causes

Cuban dependence on sugar and U.S. markets, the legacy of the Platt Amendment and unfulfilled nationalism, rural inequality, and Cold War rivalry in the hemisphere.

Short-term causes

The agrarian reform and nationalizations, the U.S. sugar quota cut and embargo, Cuban-Soviet trade, the Bay of Pigs, Operation Mongoose, and Khrushchev's strategic calculations.

Event or transformation

Secret Soviet deployment of nuclear missiles in Cuba and the American quarantine of October 1962.

Immediate effects

Withdrawal of missiles and bombers, a secret removal of U.S. missiles from Turkey, a non-invasion understanding, and Cuban anger at exclusion.

Long-term consequences

A superpower hotline and test-ban treaty, an enduring embargo, Cuban military dependence on the Soviet Union, and a durable myth of resistance.

Historical complexity

No single actor caused the crisis. Cuban choices, American covert action, and Soviet gambles interacted, and none was inevitable.

Section 81.17

The Second Agrarian Reform, Agricultural Production, and the Challenges of Socialist Development

The countryside experienced a further transformation in October 1963, when the government issued a second Agrarian Reform Law. The first law of 1959 had set a limit of about thirty caballerías. The second reduced the permitted private holding to five caballerías, roughly sixty-seven hectares. It was a distinct stage and a separate law, and it must not be confused with the measure of 1959.

The reasons were political and economic. Many farmers with holdings between sixty-seven and four hundred hectares, who had been protected in 1959, were regarded by the government as a source of political opposition and as employers of wage labor, and some were suspected of supporting armed bands in the Escambray. The government wanted to bring the remaining medium-sized farms under state planning and to consolidate control over production and marketing. The law transferred the land of larger farmers to the state sector, and the state's share of agricultural land rose to roughly two-thirds or more.

The consequences for rural households varied. Farmers with holdings above the limit lost land, though they received compensation, usually through lifetime payments or other arrangements, and often became employees of the state farms they had once owned. Small farmers below the limit kept their land but were organized through ANAP and tied to state purchasing. Cooperatives, which had been part of the 1959 scheme, were gradually reduced in favor of state enterprises. Managers appointed by the state replaced owners, and agricultural policy was directed from Havana.

Production suffered. The transformation of ownership and labor relations coincided with other problems. Hurricane Flora in October 1963 devastated eastern Cuba and killed over a thousand people. Shortages of machinery, fertilizer, and spare parts, inexperienced managers, and the migration of rural workers to cities and to the army all reduced output. Sugar harvests, which had been about 6.8 million tons in 1961, fell to roughly 3.8 million tons in 1963. The earlier policy of diversifying away from sugar was reversed, and the government decided to concentrate on sugar and use the earnings to buy industrial goods.

This decision made Soviet trade even more central. In January 1964 Cuba and the Soviet Union signed a long-term agreement under which the Soviets would purchase increasing quantities of Cuban sugar at a guaranteed price, rising toward several million tons a year by the end of the decade. The agreement gave Cuba a stable market and subsidized prices, while committing it to a sugar-based economy. Problems of distribution of machinery, labor, and investment persisted, since planners struggled to allocate scarce resources. The second agrarian reform thus completed the transfer of most agricultural land to the state, with mixed effects on production and a deepening of Cuba's economic dependence on a single crop and a single patron.

Section 81.18

The Communist Party, Political Centralization, Dissent, and the Boundaries of Revolutionary Citizenship

In October 1965 the revolutionary organizations were consolidated into the Partido Comunista de Cuba (PCC), the Communist Party of Cuba, with a Central Committee announced by Castro. The name was new, but the party was the successor of the ORI and PURSC, built by the leadership of the guerrilla movement. At the same time, the newspapers Revolución and Hoy were merged into Granma, the party's official organ. The institutions of the state, the party, the armed forces, and the mass organizations were closely interlinked, and Fidel and Raúl Castro stood at the head of the party and the armed forces. The 1965 consolidation is distinct from the 1976 Constitution, which gave the state its formal institutional structure, and neither should be projected back to 1959.

There were no competitive multiparty elections. The party was defined as the leading force of society, and participation took place through the party, the mass organizations, and, from the late 1960s, local assemblies. The government sought loyalty through education, workplace organization, censorship, and security institutions. Neighborhood committees monitored residents. The Ministry of the Interior and the state security services monitored opposition, and tens of thousands of Cubans were imprisoned for political offenses in the early 1960s, a figure that is difficult to determine precisely because estimates differ widely. Dissent was treated as counterrevolution.

The Unidades Militares de Ayuda a la Producción (UMAP), Military Units to Aid Production, operated from 1965 to 1968. They were established in connection with the military draft, which was introduced in 1963, and were located mainly in Camagüey Province. The units were forced-labor camps, in which men conscripted into military service were assigned to agricultural work, including sugarcane cutting, under harsh conditions of long hours, poor food, and military discipline. They targeted people considered socially or ideologically nonconforming: gay men, Jehovah's Witnesses and other religious believers, including Catholic and Protestant seminarians, young people regarded as idle or deviant, and others who were judged unfit for regular military service but were not considered to be trusted to remain at home.

The institution is documented in specialist scholarship, such as the work of Ian Lumsden and of Abel Sierra Madero, and in testimony from survivors, and the existence of the camps and their basis in forced labor is not in dispute. The numbers are. Estimates of those who passed through the camps range from roughly twenty-five thousand to thirty-five thousand or more, and estimates of deaths and suicides are contested and rest on survivor testimony and limited records. These figures require attribution. Following domestic and international criticism, including from some Cuban intellectuals and from the Union of Young Communists, the government dissolved the units in 1968. The UMAP, like the executions and imprisonments of the early 1960s, cannot be omitted from an account that also credits the revolution's social achievements, since it shows how the boundaries of revolutionary citizenship were drawn.

Those boundaries were defined by loyalty and conformity, and by gender and sexuality norms. Homosexuality was treated as incompatible with the revolutionary "new man," and gay men were dismissed from teaching and cultural positions and were subject to harassment. Religious believers were limited in their careers. At the same time, citizens who conformed gained benefits in education, health, and employment. The result was a system in which social rights were extensive and civil and political rights were narrow, and in which the line between inclusion and exclusion was drawn by the state.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Domestic Choices, U.S. Hostility, or Interaction?

The question: Was Cuba's socialist transformation principally shaped by the revolutionary leadership's own choices, by U.S. hostility, or by the interaction between the two?

Interpretation A — Domestic choice as the main driver: Scholars such as Samuel Farber emphasize that the leadership made decisions, including postponing elections, controlling the press and unions, and centralizing authority, that were not forced on it. On this view, American hostility was real but did not determine that the revolution would become a one-party state, and some critical steps preceded major U.S. measures.
Interpretation B — U.S. hostility as the main driver: Other historians, including Louis A. Pérez Jr. in parts of his work and many Cuban official accounts, emphasize that the quota cut, embargo, covert operations, and invasion pushed a nationalist reform movement toward radicalism, centralization, and alliance with the Soviet Union, because it needed defense and markets.
Interpretation C — Interaction: Many scholars hold that the two processes reinforced each other: leadership choices raised American suspicion, American measures justified more radical responses, and the Soviet alignment emerged from the spiral.

Evidence: Each view relies on interpretation of the timing of events, on U.S. declassified records, on Soviet archives, and on Cuban speeches and laws. Cuban internal deliberations remain largely inaccessible, which limits conclusions about the leadership's intentions in 1959.

Why it matters: The question affects how responsibility is assigned for the narrowing of political freedom and for the confrontation with the United States, and whether Cuba's trajectory is seen as contingent or as predetermined. Most recent scholarship rejects both a purely domestic and a purely external explanation.

Section 81.19

Che Guevara, Revolutionary Internationalism, and Cuba's Place in the Third World

Cuba's leaders believed that the revolution should be defended by spreading it. Even before the missile crisis, Havana supported insurgents in Latin America, and in 1963 the government sent military advisers and soldiers to help Algeria, newly independent, in a border conflict with Morocco. In Venezuela a large arms cache discovered in November 1963 was traced to Cuba. The aim was to weaken the United States' hold on the hemisphere, to support the revolutionary left, and to create allies. Chapter 82 examines the guerrilla movements in detail, and this section concentrates on the island's role.

Che Guevara became the symbol of this policy. Born in Argentina, he had joined Castro in Mexico and risen to the top of the revolutionary government, but he was increasingly dissatisfied with Soviet-style economics and with Moscow's emphasis on peaceful coexistence. In 1965 he disappeared from public life. In October of that year Castro read a farewell letter in which Guevara renounced his positions and Cuban citizenship to fight elsewhere. He led a Cuban contingent to the Congo in 1965 to support rebels, an expedition that failed, in part because of disorganization and the rebels' weakness, and he withdrew in November. In 1966 he went to Bolivia to organize a guerrilla force.

The Bolivian campaign failed. The guerrillas found little support among peasants, who were suspicious of foreigners and in a region including Guaraní-speaking communities and Spanish speakers; the guerrillas had prepared in Quechua, which was more appropriate to other parts of Bolivia, and the Bolivian Communist Party, led by Mario Monje, declined to back an insurgency directed by a foreigner. Bolivian army units, advised by U.S. personnel including a Cuban-American CIA officer, tracked the band. Guevara was captured on 8 October 1967 and executed the following day at La Higuera. His death, and his image in a famous photograph, made him a global icon of revolution, a figure later reproduced on posters and T-shirts in contexts remote from his politics. The construction of his image, by Cuban propaganda, by admirers, and by commercial reproduction, is part of the historical record and should be kept distinct from his actual career.

Havana also became a center of Third World politics. In January 1966 it hosted the Tricontinental Conference, which brought together delegations from Asia, Africa, and Latin America, and created the Organization of Solidarity with the Peoples of Africa, Asia and Latin America. In July–August 1967 it hosted the first conference of the Latin American Solidarity Organization (OLAS), which endorsed armed struggle as the main path in the region. Delegates from established Communist parties criticized this line, and Moscow disliked it.

The tension with the Soviet Union was real. Cuba criticized Soviet advocacy of peaceful coexistence and pressed for armed struggle, while Moscow wanted stable relations with the United States and with Latin American governments. Soviet pressure grew in 1967–1968 as Cuba's economy depended on Soviet oil, and in January 1968 the government put on trial a group of old communists, the "microfaction" led by Aníbal Escalante, accused of conspiring with Soviet officials. In August 1968, after publicly criticizing aspects of Soviet policy, Castro gave a qualified endorsement of the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia, a sign of Cuba's constraints. Cuban internationalism was an independent policy, pursued with Cuban resources and ideas, and it was also limited by dependence. It would be mistaken to portray it simply as an extension of Moscow's objectives, and equally mistaken to ignore Soviet leverage. Latin American governments, with U.S. support, responded with counterinsurgency, and the failure of rural guerrillas in the late 1960s led many revolutionaries to reconsider.

Section 81.20

The Revolutionary Offensive of 1968, the Ten-Million-Ton Harvest, and the Legacy of Socialist Cuba

In March 1968 Castro announced the Revolutionary Offensive, a campaign that nationalized the remaining small private businesses, including bars, restaurants, repair shops, street vendors, and small producers, tens of thousands of enterprises. He criticized these small owners as a source of speculation and unearned profit amid shortages and held that the revolution should not tolerate them. The measure virtually eliminated private economic activity outside small farming. It also removed informal sources of goods and services on which many urban households had relied, which worsened shortages and lengthened queues.

The offensive was accompanied by a turn toward moral incentives, mass mobilization, and economic centralization. Volunteer work, in which students, office workers, and soldiers cut cane or built public works, was promoted as the path to a communist consciousness, and wage differentials were reduced. Consumer goods were deprioritized in favor of investment. Planning institutions were weakened, as decisions were increasingly made by the leadership personally, and statistical and administrative practice deteriorated. Workers' absenteeism increased in the following years, a sign of fatigue, and the experiment drew on a cult of will rather than on economic calculation.

The ten-million-ton sugar harvest was the clearest example of the system. First advanced by Castro as early as 1963, the goal of harvesting ten million metric tons of sugar in 1970 became an overriding national campaign as preparation intensified during 1969. Its execution relied on mobilizing hundreds of thousands of volunteers and workers, extending the harvest period, and redirecting resources from other sectors. The goal had a political meaning: reaching it would show that socialism could surpass capitalism in development and that Cuba could finance its own growth, and it would reduce dependence on Soviet aid. Much of the economy was subordinated to the harvest, with factories, transport, and administration pulling workers and fuel into the cane fields.

The goal was not met. The official final figure was about 8.5 million tons, the largest harvest in Cuban history but well short of the target. The shortfall was consequential, since the effort had required concentrating labor, investment, and administration on a single production target, and other sectors suffered: non-sugar agriculture, industry, and transport slumped in 1970 and after, and the economy faced shortages and a growing trade deficit. On 26 July 1970 Castro publicly acknowledged the failure, took responsibility, and, as widely reported, offered to step down if the people wished, a gesture that was met with the crowd's affirmation. The episode led the government in the early 1970s toward institutionalization, closer integration with the Soviet economy, including entry into the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance in 1972, and the reintroduction of material incentives and planning. These later developments are noted only as consequences.

Evaluation requires weighing achievements against costs. By 1970 Cuba had virtually eliminated illiteracy, built a national system of schools and rural clinics, redistributed income, reduced unemployment, and opened public life to groups that had been excluded, with consequences that international observers recognized. It also had food rationing and shortages, no competitive elections, controlled media, political imprisonment, forced-labor camps in the recent past, an exile population of several hundred thousand, and an economy dependent on Soviet subsidy and on sugar. Whether the revolution had achieved the independence and social justice promised in 1959 depends on which promises and which Cubans one considers. The country had escaped U.S. domination and had survived invasion, but had replaced one dependence with another, and political renewal had been replaced by centralized control. The evidence permits students to weigh these judgments without imposing a single verdict.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: The Promises of 1959 and the Cuba of 1970

What Changed

  • A provisional civilian government promising elections became a one-party state under Castro and the Communist Party
  • Land, banks, industry, and nearly all commerce passed from private owners, including foreign companies, to the state
  • Literacy, schooling, rural health care, and access to public life expanded sharply
  • The U.S. market and suppliers were replaced by the Soviet bloc, and several hundred thousand Cubans emigrated
  • Independent press, unions, parties, schools, and religious institutions lost their autonomy

What Stayed the Same

  • The economy remained dependent on sugar and on a single external patron
  • Nationalism and the demand for sovereignty remained central to political legitimacy
  • Personal leadership by Fidel Castro remained decisive
  • Women's domestic burdens and racial prejudice persisted in new forms

Who experienced change most directly: Landowners, business owners, clergy, and urban professionals lost the most, while rural workers, the poor, and young people gained access to education and services. Greater continuity: Sugar workers continued to cut cane, and the patterns of economic dependence persisted. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a revolution can achieve major social redistribution while reproducing structural dependence and narrowing political freedom, and that these outcomes must be assessed separately.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the victorious revolutionary coalition of January 1959 became a centralized socialist state, how its relations with the United States deteriorated into armed intervention and nuclear crisis, and how those developments transformed Cuban society. Its central argument is that neither domestic revolutionary choices nor U.S. hostility is sufficient on its own to explain the outcome, and that the institutions of 1970 were not fully determined in 1959.

In 1959 the provisional civilian government of Urrutia and Miró Cardona held formal office while the Rebel Army and Fidel Castro held real power. Castro became prime minister in February, Urrutia was replaced by Dorticós in July, revolutionary tribunals prosecuted Batista's officials with limited safeguards, and the promised elections were repeatedly postponed. The Agrarian Reform Law of 17 May 1959 limited landholdings, expropriated large estates, and created INRA, but much of the land passed to state farms rather than to individual cultivators.

Political space narrowed through 1959 and 1960. The imprisonment of Huber Matos, the closure of independent newspapers, the capture of the labor confederation, and the deterioration of relations with the Church were political decisions that preceded the major nationalizations and the Bay of Pigs. Opponents were diverse, ranging from Batista supporters to liberal democrats and disillusioned revolutionaries, and treating them as a single category obscures the revolution's own political history, while real threats from armed opposition and foreign hostility also existed.

Relations with Washington collapsed in 1960 through a spiral: Soviet trade and the refinery dispute, the U.S. sugar quota cut, Cuban nationalization of American and Cuban enterprises, and a partial U.S. embargo, ending with the rupture of diplomatic relations on 3 January 1961. The comprehensive embargo of February 1962 was a separate and later measure.

The Bay of Pigs invasion of 17–19 April 1961 was a CIA-organized covert operation carried out by Brigade 2506, a force of Cuban exiles, begun under Eisenhower and executed under Kennedy. It failed because of faulty assumptions about an uprising, a changed and unfavorable landing site, the loss of supply ships, and inadequate air support. Castro's declaration on 16 April that the revolution was socialist preceded the landing, and the victory at Playa Girón consolidated his authority. Operation Mongoose, authorized afterward, was a separate and larger covert campaign, and the embargo and the OAS exclusion of Cuba deepened its isolation.

The revolution built a new social order through mass organizations and public programs. The literacy campaign of 1961 mobilized roughly a hundred thousand young volunteers and substantially reduced illiteracy, the Federation of Cuban Women and the Committees for the Defense of the Revolution incorporated women and neighborhoods, and health, housing, and cultural policy expanded access. These measures widened citizenship for rural people, the poor, Afro-Cubans, and women, while eliminating independent associations, nationalizing church schools, expelling priests, and subjecting culture to the limits of "within the revolution." Social achievement and political restriction have to be assessed independently.

The missile crisis of October 1962 was a confrontation among three governments. Cuba accepted Soviet missiles to defend itself against a feared invasion, and Khrushchev sought to redress the strategic balance and protect an ally. After the U-2 discovery of 14 October, ExComm chose the naval quarantine, and the crisis reached its peak on 27 October with the shootdown of Anderson's aircraft and the secret meeting of Robert Kennedy and Dobrynin. The settlement had a public non-invasion pledge and a secret understanding about the Jupiter missiles in Turkey. Tactical nuclear weapons on the island were unknown to Washington. Castro, excluded from the bargain, issued his Five Points and resisted inspection, and the resolution left Cuba more secure from direct attack but more dependent on Soviet protection.

From 1963 the state consolidated. The second Agrarian Reform Law of October 1963 reduced private holdings to about sixty-seven hectares. The Communist Party of Cuba was formally constituted in 1965, with no competitive elections, and the UMAP camps of 1965–1968 subjected conscripted men judged nonconforming to forced labor. Che Guevara's departure, failed campaigns in the Congo and Bolivia, and the Tricontinental and OLAS conferences expressed an independent but constrained revolutionary internationalism.

The Revolutionary Offensive of 1968 eliminated small private business, and the campaign for a ten-million-ton sugar harvest in 1970 concentrated labor, investment, and authority on a single target. The harvest reached about 8.5 million tons, a record that nonetheless fell short and disrupted other sectors. By 1970 Cuba had achieved major gains in literacy, health, and social inclusion, and had survived intervention, but at the cost of political competition, civil liberties, a large emigration, and a dependence on the Soviet Union that echoed in new form the dependence it had sought to escape.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a national revolution became an international crisis and then a durable state. In the years from 1959 to 1970, Cuba's leaders redefined sovereignty, redistributed land and opportunity, and built institutions that mobilized the population, while the United States and the Soviet Union treated the island as a stake in a global rivalry. The result was neither the democratic renewal that many of the revolution's early supporters expected nor the simple instrument of Moscow that its opponents described.

The chapter also shows why historians insist on separating questions that political argument often combines. Whether the revolution reduced illiteracy and extended health care is a different question from whether it permitted dissent, and whether Washington acted aggressively is a different question from whether Havana made independent choices. The sources, produced by governments with strong interests, require critical reading, and the silences of ordinary Cubans, exiles, and prisoners are part of what must be reconstructed.

Cuba's experience shaped the hemisphere. It inspired insurgent movements and provoked counterinsurgency across Latin America, intensified U.S. concern about the region, and became a reference point for supporters and opponents of revolution alike. Chapter 82 turns to Che Guevara and the spread of revolutionary movements, and Chapter 83 to Colombia's armed conflict, both of which cannot be understood without the Cuban example. The students who carry this chapter forward should remember that Cuba's revolution was at once a social transformation, a Cold War flashpoint, and a state-building project, and that its legacy remained contested long after 1970.
