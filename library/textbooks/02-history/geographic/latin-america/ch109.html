Chapter Opening: A Country That Lost Its Currency, Its Output, and Millions of Its People

On the night of 3 January 2026 the man who had governed Venezuela for almost thirteen years was seized in his own capital by foreign soldiers and flown to a New York courtroom. Yet the government he had led did not disappear. The vice president who took his place had served him for years, the institutions that had backed him largely remained in place, and the country she inherited had already lost most of its economy, much of its oil output, and, by international estimates, close to a quarter of its population to emigration.

This chapter explains how that situation came about. Chapters 105 through 107 showed how petroleum shaped Venezuelan democracy, how Hugo Chávez used oil revenue to build the Bolivarian project, and how that project centralized control over PDVSA, the social missions, and oil diplomacy. Chapter 108 placed Chávez within the wider Pink Tide. This chapter begins where those accounts end: with Chávez's death on 5 March 2013 and the succession of Nicolás Maduro, and it follows the Venezuelan state through two economic collapses, one institutional collapse, one demographic upheaval, and, in 2026, a violent external decapitation of its leadership whose consequences are still unresolved.

The central argument is that Venezuela's crisis had several interacting causes and cannot be reduced to one. Petroleum dependence made the economy and the state vulnerable to price swings. Domestic policy, including exchange controls, price controls, borrowing, and monetary financing of deficits, deepened the vulnerability. The physical deterioration of the oil industry turned a price shock into a lasting loss of national income. Political conflict emptied elections and legislatures of their capacity to resolve disputes. International sanctions, which grew from targeted measures in 2015 to sweeping financial and petroleum restrictions in 2017 and 2019, added pressure to an economy that was already contracting. Together these developments produced hyperinflation, the collapse of public services, and the largest displacement in the modern history of the Americas.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I covers the succession and the first signs of economic erosion, 2013–2016. Division II examines the breakdown of constitutional government, from the opposition's 2015 legislative victory to the dissolution of the parallel interim government in 2023. Division III is devoted to economic collapse, hyperinflation, and everyday life. Division IV treats the exodus as social history, not merely as a statistic. Division V covers the disputed 2024 election, the events of 3 January 2026, and the interim government that followed, and it ends on 16 November 2026 with the transition still unfinished.

Evidence requires particular care in this chapter. For much of the period the central bank stopped publishing reliable inflation and output series, so historians rely on International Monetary Fund estimates, independent economists, university surveys such as the Encuesta Nacional de Condiciones de Vida (ENCOVI), and humanitarian monitoring. Election results exist at several levels: official declarations, polling-station tally sheets, and independent observer analyses. Human-rights findings come from United Nations bodies, national organizations such as Foro Penal and Provea, and the International Criminal Court. Government and opposition sources each have strong interests. The chapter therefore keeps asking who produced a figure, by what method, and for what purpose.

Two distinctions run through every section. The first separates allegations, indictments, official findings, court rulings, and independently verified facts. Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores have been charged in the United States but have not been tried, and the chapter says only what the public record supports. The second separates events that are settled from events still unfolding. Everything up to the end of 2025 can be narrated as history. The events of 2026 are recorded as they stood on 16 November 2026, and the chapter explicitly identifies what remained undecided.

Finally, Venezuela's experience should not be read as a story of a single leader or party. Millions of Venezuelans supported, opposed, endured, or fled the governments described here, and their motives changed over time. The chapter also keeps the United States in its proper place. Washington's sanctions, indictments, and military action were consequential, but the central actors in this history are Venezuelan: voters, soldiers, officials, workers, mothers who crossed borders on foot, and neighbors who stayed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Distinguish official election declarations, opposition allegations, and independent findings in the elections of 2013, 2018, and 2024.
  3. Trace the sequence by which the opposition's 2015 legislative majority lost effective power, including the recall dispute and the 2017 Constituent Assembly.
  4. Analyze the relationship between the 2014 oil-price collapse and the longer decline of PDVSA's physical production.
  5. Explain why Venezuela experienced hyperinflation and distinguish inflation, hyperinflation, redenomination, and dollarization.

Historical Context

By 2013 Venezuela was the clearest example in Latin America of a rentier petroleum state. Chapter 105 traced how oil revenue financed the Punto Fijo democracy and how the collapse of oil prices and the austerity of the late 1980s led to the Caracazo of 1989. Chapter 106 followed Chávez from his 1992 coup attempt to his 1998 election and the 1999 Constitution, which expanded presidential authority, created a Supreme Tribunal of Justice and a National Electoral Council, and provided for a presidential recall referendum at the midpoint of a term. Chapter 107 showed how, after the 2002–2003 confrontation, Chávez took control of PDVSA, dismissed thousands of managers and technicians, and used oil revenue for the social missions and for oil diplomacy through Petrocaribe and ALBA.

The commodity boom of the 2000s made that model look sustainable. High prices allowed rapid growth in imports and public spending, a large reduction in measured poverty, and the expansion of subsidized food, health, and education programs. They also allowed the government to keep the bolívar overvalued under the exchange controls introduced in 2003, to keep domestic prices of many goods and of gasoline very low, and to borrow heavily, including from China in oil-backed loans. Non-oil exports shrank, the country imported a growing share of what it consumed, and the state's ability to pay for imports depended on a single commodity. These were the inherited vulnerabilities that Maduro faced.

Chávez's government had also changed the relationship between state institutions. After the opposition boycott of the 2005 legislative elections, the National Assembly was dominated by the ruling coalition. The Supreme Tribunal was enlarged in 2004, and the armed forces were given a larger role in administration, food distribution, and state enterprises. The ruling party, the Partido Socialista Unido de Venezuela (PSUV), was founded in 2007. By 2012 the practical power of Chávez, rather than the formal separation of powers, held these institutions together, and his illness, announced in 2011, raised the question of what would hold them together without him.

The wider region mattered too. The end of the commodity boom around 2013–2014 weakened governments across South America, as Chapter 108 explained, and the political center of gravity shifted. Venezuela's regional allies lost resources, and its neighbors, especially Colombia, would become the first destination of its emigrants. At the same time the United States, which had imported Venezuelan crude for a century, was becoming less dependent on imported oil because of domestic shale production, which reduced the cost to Washington of confronting Caracas.

Students should also keep in mind that this chapter's subject is unfinished in a way most of the book's subjects are not. Earlier chapters could rely on archives, completed trials, and decades of scholarship. This chapter relies on contemporary reporting, international organizations, court filings, and survey data, and it records the status of unresolved matters on a specific date.

Section 109.1

The Death of Hugo Chávez and the Succession of Nicolás Maduro, 2013

Hugo Chávez died in Caracas on 5 March 2013, after nearly two years of treatment for cancer, much of it in Cuba. His illness had already forced a succession crisis. In December 2012, before leaving for a final operation in Havana, he told Venezuelans that if he could not continue, they should vote for his vice president, Nicolás Maduro. The endorsement was unusual in a political culture that had long been organized around Chávez himself, and it carried enormous weight among his supporters. It also made Maduro the designated heir of a movement whose unity depended on the person who had founded it.

Maduro came from the movement's organizational rather than military side. Born in Caracas in 1962, he worked as a Caracas Metro bus driver and became a union activist. He supported Chávez after the 1992 coup attempt, was elected to the National Assembly in 2000, and served as its president from 2005 to 2006. Chávez then made him foreign minister, a post he held for six years while Venezuela built the diplomatic network of ALBA and Petrocaribe described in Chapter 107. In October 2012, after Chávez won his last election, Maduro became vice president. He was therefore well known within the ruling coalition and had close relations with Cuban officials, but he lacked Chávez's military credentials, his rhetorical command, and his personal following among poor Venezuelans.

The constitutional mechanics of succession were disputed from the start. Under Article 233 of the 1999 Constitution, if the president's permanent absence occurs before inauguration or within the first four years of a term, a new election must be held within thirty days. Chávez had been reelected in October 2012 but could not be sworn in on 10 January 2013 because he was in Cuba. The Supreme Tribunal ruled that the inauguration was a formality that could be postponed and that the government's administration continued, with Maduro as vice president in charge. Opposition lawyers argued that the vacancy had begun to matter under the constitution and that the National Assembly president, Diosdado Cabello, should have assumed the interim presidency. After Chávez died, the Tribunal ruled that Maduro, as vice president, would serve as acting president and could be a candidate, and the National Electoral Council set the election for 14 April.

Government supporters saw this as a lawful continuation of an elected administration, and the opposition saw an executive-aligned court deciding a contested constitutional question in the ruling party's favor. Both positions have a textual basis, which is part of why the episode matters: it showed an institution that had already begun to read constitutional ambiguity consistently in the ruling coalition's favor, a pattern that would recur.

The comparison between Chávez and Maduro helps explain what followed. Chávez had governed as a leader whose popularity let him arbitrate among military officers, party barons, regional governors, and Cuban advisers. Maduro governed as the first among several powerful figures who each controlled resources. Cabello commanded networks inside the party and the security services, Rafael Ramírez had long run PDVSA and the oil ministry, and a large officer corps held ministries, state enterprises, and control over food and currency. Maduro's authority came from Chávez's endorsement and from his ability to keep this coalition satisfied. As the oil income that had financed that satisfaction declined, the task became harder.

Section 109.2

The April 2013 Presidential Election: A Narrow Victory and a Disputed Mandate

The election of 14 April 2013 pitted Maduro against Henrique Capriles Radonski, the governor of Miranda state who had lost to Chávez by about eleven points six months earlier. Turnout was high, about four fifths of registered voters. The National Electoral Council reported that Maduro received approximately 50.61 percent of the vote and Capriles approximately 49.12 percent, a margin of roughly 1.5 percentage points, or a little over 200,000 votes out of some fifteen million cast. A campaign that had begun with Chávez's popularity at its back had ended with the ruling party barely holding the presidency.

Electoral percentages require a named denominator. The widely cited final CNE tabulation gave Maduro 50.61 percent and Capriles 49.12 percent of valid votes, whereas the first CNE bulletin, issued before the final tally, reported approximately 50.66 and 49.07 percent. These are differently timed official returns, not independent rival estimates. The first bulletin is preserved at the CNE press record; the CIDOB election dossier records the final comparison. Students should not silently substitute an election-night bulletin for the final tally.

Capriles refused to accept the result and demanded a full recount. The electoral authority had already audited a sample of the electronic voting machines' paper receipts, as it did after every election, and after the dispute it agreed to extend the audit to the remainder of the machines. The opposition argued that this audit compared receipts only with machine totals and did not examine the voter registry, voter-identification records, or the possibility of irregularities before ballots were cast. The Supreme Tribunal later dismissed the opposition's legal challenge. Foreign observers divided as well: the Union of South American Nations and most Latin American governments accepted the result, while the United States called for a recount.

The post-election period involved violence. Protesters took to the streets in several cities, and government-aligned groups and security forces were involved in confrontations that left several people dead and many injured. The government blamed opposition-organized violence, while the opposition blamed security forces and armed civilian groups known as colectivos. The accounts cannot be reconciled from the available record, and students should treat claims about responsibility as claims.

It is useful to separate what is established from what is alleged. It is established that the electoral authority declared a narrow Maduro victory, that the opposition contested it, that the authority conducted an extended audit of machine receipts, and that the courts upheld the result. It is alleged by the opposition that irregularities, such as the use of public resources and problems with voter assistance and identification, affected the outcome. No independent institution conducted a full audit of the voter registry, so the claim that the election was stolen has not been shown, and the claim that it was fully verified has not been shown either. The result was, at minimum, an unresolved dispute from the first day of the Maduro presidency.

The political consequences were large. A president who had won by an officially reported margin of roughly 224,000 votes needed to consolidate. Maduro increasingly relied on the armed forces and the party apparatus, and he adopted a style of rule that treated opposition criticism as part of an external "economic war." Opposition leaders, for their part, concluded that the electoral route faced structural obstacles, and the disagreement over whether to continue to use it would divide them for the next decade.

Creator: Consejo Nacional Electoral (CNE) and Tribunal Supremo de Justicia · Date: 15 April 2013 (results); later 2013 (court ruling) · Type: Official electoral bulletin and judicial decision

Context: The CNE announced results within hours of the polls closing and Maduro was proclaimed president-elect. Capriles filed a challenge before the Supreme Tribunal's Electoral Chamber, arguing that irregularities warranted annulment. The Tribunal dismissed the challenge in August 2013.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: The electoral authority's bulletin reported that Maduro received 50.61 percent and Capriles 49.12 percent of valid votes, and announced the audit of remaining machine receipts. The Tribunal's ruling rejected the opposition's request to annul the election.

Analysis: The bulletin establishes the official count and the authority's proclamation. The ruling shows how the dispute was resolved within Venezuela's institutional channels. Read together, they show that the procedure was followed formally, which is why the government treated the matter as closed.

Limitations: Both documents come from institutions whose independence was itself contested. The bulletin does not show the data needed to test the opposition's claims, such as the voter-registry audit, and the Tribunal's ruling does not constitute an independent verification of the result.

Section 109.3

Maduro, the PSUV, the Armed Forces, and the First Opposition Protests, 2013–2014

To understand how Maduro held power, it helps to separate formal offices from informal centers of authority. Formally, the constitution gave the president wide powers, including the ability to rule by decree under enabling laws, which the Assembly granted him in November 2013. Informally, authority was spread among several networks. The PSUV provided mass organization, control of most governorships and mayoralties, and a pipeline of candidates. Diosdado Cabello, who presided over the Assembly until 2016 and then became a party vice president and interior minister, linked the party to parts of the security services. The armed forces, whose officers held ministries, state enterprises, and control over ports, food distribution, and later mining, formed a third network. In October 2014 General Vladimir Padrino López became defense minister, a post he would hold for more than a decade. Cuban advisers and intelligence officers formed a fourth, less visible, influence.

Maduro maintained authority by distributing access to these resources and by invoking Chávez's memory. He also adopted the language of external conspiracy: he spoke of an "economic war" waged by business owners and foreign powers, a framing that allowed the government to explain shortages as sabotage and to justify controls and prosecutions. Critics argued that the explanation concealed the effects of the government's own policies. Supporters argued that real efforts to destabilize the government existed and that the framing described them. Both statements can be partly true, and the chapter returns to the question in Division III.

The first sustained challenge came in early 2014. Protests began in February in the western border state of Táchira, after a student was assaulted and over concerns about crime and shortages, and they spread to Mérida, Caracas, and other cities. In January, opposition leaders Leopoldo López, María Corina Machado, and Antonio Ledezma had launched a campaign called La Salida ("the exit"), which called for pressure to end Maduro's government before the end of his term. Henrique Capriles and much of the parliamentary opposition rejected the strategy, arguing that it invited repression and that elections were the proper way to change a government. The opposition was therefore divided between those who emphasized street mobilization and those who emphasized elections and negotiation.

The protests produced deaths, injuries, and arrests. On 12 February 2014, a large march in Caracas ended with shootings in which at least three people died, among them a student, Bassil Da Costa. The government blamed opposition groups and said some protesters had built barricades and attacked public property, while human-rights organizations documented the use of force against demonstrators, detentions, and abuse in custody, and attacks by armed civilian groups. The government's own tally of deaths associated with the unrest reached 43. López surrendered to authorities on 18 February and was later sentenced to nearly fourteen years in prison. Machado lost her seat in the Assembly in March, and opposition mayors were removed and jailed.

Historians therefore analyze three things separately. Peaceful protest is a protected exercise of political rights. Advocacy of constitutional change, including calls for a president to leave office, is political speech that can be assessed on its own terms. Unlawful violence, whether by protesters, security forces, or armed civilian groups, is a matter for investigation and, where evidence permits, prosecution. Government rhetoric tended to merge the three, and some opposition rhetoric did too. The 2014 episode set a pattern in which a political demand, a security response, and a dispute over legitimate and illegitimate force would recur in 2017.

Venezuela's opposition had faced this choice before. Section 109.4

The Oil-Price Collapse of 2014–2016 and the Weakening of the Petroleum State

The price of Brent crude, which had exceeded 100 dollars a barrel for most of the preceding four years, fell sharply from mid-2014 and was near 30 dollars in January 2016. Venezuela's export basket, composed of heavier and more sour crudes, fell lower still. Oil accounted for roughly 95 percent of Venezuela's export earnings, and oil revenue paid for most imports and a large part of public spending. The fall therefore reduced the supply of dollars with which the economy bought food, medicine, machinery, and inputs for its remaining industry. By most estimates imports fell by more than two thirds between 2012 and 2016.

The shock exposed vulnerabilities that policy had created. Since 2003 the government had set an official exchange rate that valued the bolívar far above its market price and rationed access to dollars through a state agency. Those who received dollars at the official rate could import cheaply or resell the dollars at a large profit, and the gap between the official and parallel rates became a source of corruption and capital flight. Over the next years the government introduced a series of overlapping exchange systems, including SICAD and SIMADI, without eliminating the multiple-rate structure. The Law of Fair Prices of 2014 and a broader price-control system fixed prices of staples below production costs, which discouraged domestic production, encouraged smuggling across the Colombian border, and produced the queues and shortages that became an emblem of the crisis.

PDVSA's condition made matters worse. Since the dismissal of thousands of employees in 2003, the company had lost technical expertise. It had also been used as a fiscal instrument, paying for social programs, subsidized fuel, and loans to allies, which left less for investment and maintenance. PDVSA's debt grew, and its output began falling even when prices were high. Studies by the International Monetary Fund identify both domestic economic mismanagement and falling oil production as major contributors to the crisis, which is a reminder that the price collapse arrived in an industry already weakened.

Maduro's government made a consequential choice about how to adjust. It continued to service external bond debt, paying billions of dollars to bondholders during the worst years, and cut imports instead. It also financed deficits by borrowing from the central bank, which lent to the government and to PDVSA by creating bolívars. This monetary financing was the mechanism by which falling oil income became rising inflation. The economy contracted in each year from 2014, inflation moved into triple digits by 2015, and the shortages of staples and medicines became general.

It is important to distinguish the price shock from the domestic decisions and from the later sanctions. Prices fell for reasons outside Venezuela. The decision to protect debt payments, retain the exchange system, and finance deficits with money creation was Venezuelan. The sanctions that would later restrict borrowing and oil sales did not yet exist in their broad form: in 2015 the United States sanctioned individual officials, and the financial restrictions began in 2017. This sequence is the factual basis for the conclusion, developed in Division III, that the economic collapse began before the broadest sanctions and cannot be explained by them alone.

By 2016, therefore, the conditions for a deeper catastrophe were in place: a government with no access to cheap dollars, a central bank financing the state, an exchange system that rewarded arbitrage, price controls that suppressed supply, an oil company in decline, and a political system in which an elected opposition had just won control of the legislature. How that last development unfolded is the subject of Division II.

Historical Vocabulary
Rentier State

A rentier state is one that obtains most of its revenue from the sale of a natural resource or other external rent, not from taxing the domestic economy. Venezuela's oil revenue accrued directly to the government through royalties, taxes, and PDVSA's earnings.

The concept helps explain why the state could spend heavily without relying on taxpayers and why its fortunes followed oil prices. It should not be read as a claim that oil dependence made collapse inevitable. Other oil exporters, including Norway, managed rents differently, and Venezuela's own policy decisions determined how the rent was used.

Division II · 2015–2021Institutional Conflict, Opposition Movements, and the Crisis of Constitutional Government
Section 109.5

The Opposition's 2015 National Assembly Victory and the Breakdown of Institutional Cooperation

The legislative elections of 6 December 2015 took place amid shortages, triple-digit inflation, and the lowest approval ratings of Maduro's presidency. The Mesa de la Unidad Democrática (MUD), which united parties from the center-left to the right, won a decisive victory. It initially received 112 of the Assembly's 167 seats, a two-thirds supermajority, against 55 for the PSUV and its allies. The supermajority mattered because under the constitution it allowed the Assembly to remove Supreme Tribunal justices, convene a constituent assembly, approve certain organic laws, and, in conjunction with other steps, pursue a recall of the president. The government accepted the results on election night, which many observers noted was to its credit, and the vote showed that Venezuelans could still change the composition of an institution through the ballot.

The victory did not translate into authority. Before the new Assembly took office on 5 January 2016, the outgoing Assembly, still controlled by the PSUV, appointed thirteen new Supreme Tribunal justices and twenty-one alternates in a rapid process that opposition lawyers said violated the constitution's procedures. Then the Tribunal's Electoral Chamber accepted challenges to the election of four legislators from Amazonas state, three of them opposition members, and suspended their proclamation. The opposition swore them in anyway, and the Tribunal declared the Assembly in contempt. For the following months it ruled that the Assembly's acts were null, which stripped the supermajority of its practical effect: the mere suspension of three seats took the opposition below the two-thirds threshold.

From 2016 the Tribunal repeatedly overturned or blocked Assembly legislation, including an amnesty law, a law to give property titles to occupants of public housing, and efforts to reform the central bank and the Tribunal itself. The Assembly continued to hold hearings, issue declarations, and challenge decrees, but it could not control the budget, the courts, the electoral authority, or the security services. Maduro governed under successive emergency decrees, a practice that allowed him to bypass the legislature.

The episode shows why the line between electoral victory and political power was so important in Venezuela. In many democracies a legislative majority can shape policy through legislation, budgets, appointments, and oversight. Here the executive, the judiciary, the electoral authority, and the armed forces were aligned, and the Assembly had no independent means of enforcing its decisions. Opposition leaders concluded that elections could expose the government's unpopularity but could not by themselves replace it. Government leaders argued, with some constitutional support, that courts have the authority to settle electoral disputes and that the opposition had acted improperly by seating legislators whose election was under challenge.

The opposition’s supermajority also proved vulnerable to litigation over the certification of members from Amazonas state. Judicial action against those deputies altered the arithmetic of a two-thirds majority, while the tribunal increasingly treated further Assembly activity as invalid. This episode illustrates why the mere numerical outcome of a legislative election does not by itself establish an institution’s effective powers: recognition of members, access to budgetary information, the legal status of statutes, and executive compliance determine whether a legislature can exercise oversight. Opposition victories had political significance even where institutional enforcement failed.

Section 109.6

The Supreme Tribunal, the Failed Recall Process, and the Constitutional Crisis of 2016–2017

The 1999 Constitution, drafted under Chávez, allowed voters to recall any elected official halfway through his term by referendum. Chávez had survived a recall in 2004, and the opposition now invoked the same provision. After the half-way point of Maduro's term was reached in April 2016, the MUD collected signatures for the first stage, which required about one percent of registered voters, and submitted several times that number. The electoral authority, however, slowed the process. It disqualified some signatures, set demanding requirements for verification, and required the second stage, a petition by twenty percent of voters in every state, to take place late in the year. The date mattered. If a recall succeeded before 10 January 2017, which marked the start of the final two years of the term, a new election would be held. If it succeeded after that date, the vice president would complete the term.

In October 2016 several lower criminal courts in states controlled by the ruling party ruled that the first-stage signature collection had been tainted by fraud, and on 20 October the electoral authority suspended the process. The effect was to remove the constitution's principal peaceful mechanism for ending a presidency. A dialogue facilitated by the Vatican and former Latin American presidents began shortly afterward, but it produced no agreement on electoral timing or the release of political prisoners and collapsed in early 2017.

The more consequential confrontation came on 29 and 30 March 2017, when the Tribunal's Constitutional Chamber issued rulings 155 and 156. The first stripped Assembly members of parliamentary immunity on the ground that the Assembly was in contempt, and the second said that the Tribunal itself could exercise the Assembly's legislative powers. The rulings drew condemnation from many governments and from the attorney general, Luisa Ortega Díaz, a longtime chavista who described them as a rupture of constitutional order. Within two days the Tribunal, apparently at the president's request, revised the most controversial passages. Ortega Díaz later broke with the government, fled the country, and was dismissed by the Constituent Assembly.

Venezuelan legal scholars divided along political lines but also on technical ones. Many independent constitutionalists argued that the rulings contradicted the separation of powers, because the constitution grants legislative power to the Assembly alone and does not allow the Tribunal to assume it. Government jurists argued that contempt of court justified extraordinary measures and that the Tribunal is the final interpreter of the constitution. The chapter's point is not to pick between those positions on every detail but to notice what the episode demonstrated: that when courts, electoral authorities, and the executive are aligned against an elected legislature, constitutional checks stop functioning as checks.

The two Constitutional Chamber decisions should be read separately. Decision 155 (28 March 2017) restricted legislative action and invoked emergency powers; Decision 156 (29 March) claimed authority for the Chamber itself, or an institution it designated, to exercise legislative competencies while it considered the Assembly in contempt. The 1 April clarifications withdrew portions of the rulings but did not settle the longer struggle over who could authorize budgets, scrutinize ministries, or approve oil arrangements. A court ruling can appear procedurally regular and nevertheless produce a serious conflict over constitutional separation of powers; distinguishing these propositions is central to understanding the crisis.

Historical Vocabulary
Recall Referendum (Referendo Revocatorio)

The recall referendum is a mechanism in Article 72 of the 1999 Constitution by which voters can ask for a vote on removing an elected official after the half-way point of a term. It requires signature thresholds and is run by the National Electoral Council.

It was a distinctive feature of the Bolivarian constitution, which its authors presented as an expression of participatory democracy. Chávez survived a recall in 2004. Its blocking in 2016 is important not because recalls are guaranteed to succeed but because it removed an agreed procedure for resolving the dispute over Maduro's mandate. Note that the timing rule meant a recall after January 2017 would not have produced a new election.

Section 109.7

The 2017 Protests, Constituent Assembly, and Political Repression

Between April and July 2017 Venezuela experienced the largest and deadliest wave of protests of the Maduro years. They began after the March rulings and were fueled by shortages, inflation, and the cancellation of the recall. Demonstrations occurred nationwide, in poor as well as middle-class districts. The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights reported that at least 120 people died in the context of protests between April and July, and it attributed a substantial share of the deaths to security forces and to armed pro-government groups, while also noting deaths caused by protesters and violence by some demonstrators. Thousands were detained, many were brought before military courts, and rights organizations documented abuses in custody. The government said that violent opposition groups attacked security forces and public property, and it presented the response as a defense of order.

On 1 May 2017 Maduro announced that he would convene a National Constituent Assembly. The 1999 Constitution allows the people, through the president, to convene one, but the 1999 procedure had been preceded by a referendum in which voters approved the idea and its basic terms. In 2017 no such referendum was held. The electoral rules gave disproportionate weight to territorial units and to sectoral representation, which favored the ruling party, and the opposition boycotted the election of 30 July. The government reported high turnout, while the opposition and independent analysts disputed the figure, and the voting-technology company Smartmatic said the turnout figure had been manipulated by at least one million votes. Many governments refused to recognize the body.

The comparison with 1999 is instructive. The 1999 Assembly was approved by referendum, elected in a vote the opposition contested but participated in, and operated in a context of broad public support for change. Its decisions were also then ratified by referendum. The 2017 body lacked the prior authorization, was elected under rules the opposition rejected, and used its supra-constitutional claims to assume the Assembly's legislative powers, to remove the attorney general, and to approve laws, including one against "hate." It continued until December 2020. Government supporters argued that the constituent power is sovereign and that the body ended the violent street confrontations, which in fact subsided after its creation. Critics argued that it institutionalized the nullification of the elected legislature.

The human-rights record of these years is documented in reports of the UN High Commissioner, in the findings of the Independent International Fact-Finding Mission established by the Human Rights Council in 2019, and in the work of national groups such as Foro Penal and Provea. These bodies reported arbitrary detention, torture and ill-treatment, enforced disappearances of short or long duration, and extrajudicial killings, particularly in police and special-forces operations in poor neighborhoods. In its 2020 report the Fact-Finding Mission concluded that there were reasonable grounds to believe that crimes against humanity had been committed, and that officials in the security services bore responsibility. The government rejected these findings as politically motivated.

Students should keep three levels apart: documented violations, which are established by multiple independent investigations and corroborating testimony; allegations of individual criminal responsibility, which require evidence presented in court; and judicial findings, which are decisions by courts after proceedings. The International Criminal Court's Prosecutor opened a formal investigation into the situation in Venezuela, known as Venezuela I, in November 2021, after a preliminary examination that began in 2018. An investigation is not a finding of guilt, and by the cutoff of this chapter no trial of Venezuelan officials in The Hague had begun.

The OHCHR investigation covering 1 April–31 July 2017 reports that Venezuela’s Attorney General’s Office was investigating 124 deaths linked to demonstrations as of 31 July. It separately attributed alleged responsibility to security forces for at least 46 deaths; the broader 124 must not be presented as 124 killings by state agents. OHCHR undertook remote interviews and documentary review after being denied entry, which warrants attention to both the evidentiary methods and the limits of access. Counting everyone killed in the protest context is a different question from determining how a specific person died and who was responsible.

Section 109.8

The 2018 Presidential Election, Juan Guaidó, and Venezuela's Competing Claims to Political Authority

Maduro brought the presidential election forward to 20 May 2018. The major opposition parties boycotted it, arguing that the electoral authority was biased, that leading opposition figures had been barred or jailed, and that conditions were not competitive. Maduro won with about two thirds of the votes cast, in a vote with turnout of less than half of the electorate by the official count and lower by opposition estimates. The Lima Group of Latin American governments, the European Union, and the United States declined to recognize the result.

On 10 January 2019 Maduro was inaugurated for a second term. On 5 January the Assembly had elected a young deputy from Vargas state, Juan Guaidó, as its president, and on 23 January, at a street rally in Caracas, Guaidó declared that he was assuming the interim presidency. He relied on Article 233, which addresses the absence of a president, arguing that Maduro's reelection was illegitimate and that the presidency was therefore vacant. The United States recognized him within minutes, and more than fifty governments, including most of the European Union and the Lima Group members, soon did the same. Mexico, Uruguay, and others, along with Russia, China, Cuba, and Turkey, did not.

Recognition and control were different things. Guaidó was recognized as interim president by dozens of governments, which transferred some Venezuelan state assets abroad to his representatives, but he did not control the ministries, the security forces, the oil company, the budget, or the territory. Maduro retained the loyalty of the armed forces' high command, the Tribunal, the electoral authority, and the Constituent Assembly. Efforts to force a transition, including a delivery of humanitarian aid at the Colombian border in February 2019, an uprising by a small group of soldiers on 30 April 2019, and a poorly planned maritime incursion in May 2020, failed. Negotiations in Oslo and Barbados in 2019 and Mexico City in 2021 did not produce a durable settlement.

Meanwhile, the opposition itself weakened. The 2020 parliamentary election, held on 6 December, was boycotted by the main opposition parties, and the ruling party and its allies won an overwhelming majority of seats in a vote with low turnout. Guaidó's Assembly term, which had ended, was extended by the opposition through a statute, and critics inside and outside the opposition argued that the interim government had lost its institutional base while its control of Venezuelan assets abroad created disputes and, in some cases, corruption allegations. In December 2022 opposition legislators voted to dissolve the interim arrangement, effective at the beginning of 2023, and the United States and others shifted to dealing with Maduro's government on specific issues.

The conflict from 2019 to 2023 should be understood as a contest between competing claims of legitimacy, not as a struggle between two governments exercising equal authority in Venezuela. One claimant governed and controlled the means of coercion but was widely regarded abroad as illegitimate. The other claimed constitutional authority and international recognition but had little capacity to govern. That asymmetry defined both what each could do and the limits of external pressure.

The Guaidó episode also shows that recognition by foreign governments is not the same as effective governing capacity. Some states accepted his interim claim or transferred control of particular external assets, while Maduro’s government continued to administer most ministries, command coercive institutions, and control territory. The opposition divided between seeking international pressure, negotiating electoral conditions, and participating in elections it judged unfair. This divergence matters because outside recognition could sustain a legal or diplomatic claim without transferring domestic administrative authority. It also helps explain why the opposition ultimately ended its interim arrangement before the 2024 electoral challenge: a strategy designed to contest the presidency had not displaced the institutions that determined government policy and coercive power.

Division III · 2014–2025Economic Collapse, Hyperinflation, Shortages, and the Changing Venezuelan Economy
Section 109.9

The Collapse of Petroleum Production, National Income, and Public Finance

The contraction of the Venezuelan economy after 2013 was among the deepest peacetime collapses recorded in the modern world. Estimates of the cumulative fall in gross domestic product between 2013 and the early 2020s differ by source and method, but most place it at well over half and some above seventy percent, a decline comparable to those following wars or systemic breakdowns. The central bank stopped publishing national accounts for several years, so analysts rely on the International Monetary Fund, the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean, and independent economists, and each series has its limitations.

The collapse of oil production was the physical core of the contraction. An International Monetary Fund study reports that Venezuelan crude output averaged about 2.8 million barrels per day during 2008–2013, fell to about 0.9 million in 2019, and reached roughly 0.4 million in mid-2020. The pace of this decline cannot be attributed to price changes, since the fall in output continued after prices partly recovered in 2017 and 2018. It reflected declining investment, deferred maintenance, the loss of skilled personnel, shortages of equipment and diluent for the heavy crude of the Orinoco Belt, theft and corruption, power failures, and limited access to financing and technology.

Public finance followed. Revenue fell with oil exports, and the government covered its deficits by creating money, which fed inflation, and by reducing imports and public investment. It paid external debt for several years, then fell into default on much of its sovereign and PDVSA bond debt in late 2017. Debt restructuring was blocked by sanctions that barred U.S. persons from trading in new bonds and, later, in existing ones. The result was a government that could not borrow, could not export at scale, and relied on printing money.

It is essential to distinguish the oil-price collapse from the decline in physical production. The price fell in 2014–2016, recovered partly, and fell sharply again in 2020 during the pandemic. Output fell in nearly every year between 2013 and 2020 regardless of the price. This is one reason that explanations combining domestic management failures with price shocks and, after 2017, financial restrictions are more persuasive than any single account. The interaction also mattered: low output made price shocks more painful, borrowing restrictions made investment harder, and fiscal desperation led to more money creation.

The statistical distinction becomes visible in annual data. EIA’s crude oil including lease condensate series reports approximately 2.500 million barrels daily for 2013, 1.486 million for 2018, 0.878 million for 2019 and 0.527 million for 2020. These are calendar-year averages, not a measurement of a single month; the mid-2020 trough cited above is still meaningful but cannot replace the annual mean. ECLAC’s 2022 national study retrospectively estimated real-GDP contractions of 19.6 percent in 2018, 28.0 percent in 2019 and 30.0 percent in 2020. The concurrence of sharply falling physical oil output and economy-wide activity strengthens the conclusion that this was a sustained production crisis, although it does not by itself allocate causal responsibility among investment failures, sanctions, mismanagement, and the pandemic.

Export earnings require a third measurement, not merely production times a benchmark price. The exported volume differs from gross extraction because of domestic refining, storage, and logistical losses; the realized price depends on crude quality and discounts, and receipts may be affected by debt repayment, intermediaries, or sanctions-related payment structures. A classroom proxy of production × assumed price can demonstrate sensitivity, but it must be labelled a hypothetical gross-output calculation and never reported as observed export revenue. Venezuela’s declining capacity to convert petroleum resources into foreign exchange made food imports, debt service and machinery purchases increasingly difficult even when world crude prices recovered.

Section 109.10

Hyperinflation, Currency Collapse, and the Destruction of Purchasing Power

Inflation in Venezuela was high for decades before Maduro. After 2013 it passed into a different category. By 2015 it exceeded one hundred percent a year, and by 2016 it was in the hundreds. The central bank stopped publishing price indices in 2015, and the opposition-led National Assembly began publishing its own series, which differed in methodology and sometimes in results from the IMF’s retrospective estimates and other independent series. In late 2017 monthly inflation passed the threshold of fifty percent a month that economists commonly use to define hyperinflation, and it remained above that level for most of the next three years.

The causes were monetary and fiscal. The government ran large deficits, financed them with central-bank credit, and so created money at a rapid pace. The supply of goods fell with the collapse of imports and production, so that more bolívars chased fewer goods. The multiple exchange rates and the gap between official and parallel rates eroded confidence, and Venezuelans, expecting prices to rise, tried to turn bolívars into dollars or goods immediately. That behavior accelerated the depreciation, which raised prices, which led the government to create still more money to pay its bills: a feedback loop typical of hyperinflation. Sanctions that limited borrowing also removed alternatives to money creation, but the loop was already running.

The IMF's historical series identify an annual-average consumer-price inflation rate of approximately 65,374.1 percent in 2018 (the retrospective WEO PCPIPCH series); the National Assembly's series and independent estimates differed. Students should note that the IMF also issued forecasts that were far higher than realized values, so forecasts and outcomes must not be confused. Inflation then fell in stages, to roughly 20,000 percent in 2019 by some estimates, then to the hundreds of percent in the early 2020s, and lower afterward, as the government eased controls, restrained money creation, and dollars circulated more widely. Annual figures from 2019 onward vary considerably by source.

The government responded with redenominations. In August 2018 it removed five zeros from the currency, creating the bolívar soberano, and in October 2021 it removed six, creating the bolívar digital. Together with the earlier removal of three zeros in 2008, the currency's nominal value was cut by a factor of 10 to the 14th power. Redenomination changes the numbers on bills and prices, but it does not change the underlying rate of money creation, so it cannot end inflation. In practice Venezuelans increasingly used U.S. dollars, first informally and then with tacit government acceptance, for larger transactions, rents, and imported goods. By many estimates a majority of transactions were in dollars by 2021, although wages and pensions were still paid mainly in bolívars.

The human consequences depended on who had access to dollars. Workers paid in bolívars and pensioners on fixed state pensions suffered the largest loss. By 2019 the official minimum wage and pension were worth only a few U.S. dollars a month at parallel-market rates, and the government supplemented them with bonuses delivered through the Carnet de la Patria system. Households with relatives abroad, employees of foreign firms, merchants dealing in dollars, and owners of dollar assets were protected to some degree. Those without such access were not. In this way hyperinflation redistributed wealth sharply, and it changed behavior: people stopped saving in bolívars, spent wages the day they were paid, bartered, and built social networks around access to hard currency.

Inflation comparisons require three labels: which price index is used, whether a percentage is calculated from an annual average or an end-of-year observation, and whether the number is a retrospective estimate or a forecast. In the October 2026 WEO-derived annual-average series, inflation rises from 62.2 percent in 2014 to 121.7 percent in 2015, 438.1 percent in 2017 and 65,374.1 percent in 2018; the same series gives 19,906.0 percent in 2019, 2,355.2 percent in 2020, 1,588.5 percent in 2021, and 186.5 percent in 2022. The dramatic numerical decline after 2018 signifies slower price increases, not a return to former price levels. A household that has lost purchasing power does not regain it merely because the inflation rate falls.

Household Purchasing-Power Exercise

A hypothetical illustration (invented numbers, not data). Suppose a teacher is paid 1,000 bolívars on the first of the month, and a kilogram of rice costs 10 bolívars. The wage buys 100 kilograms. If prices double every week, then by the end of the fourth week rice costs 160 bolívars, and the same 1,000 bolívars buys about 6 kilograms. If the teacher spends the wage on the first day, the household obtains most of its value. If the teacher waits for the end of the month, it loses about 94 percent.

Now suppose a cousin abroad sends 20 dollars a month, which the family converts on the first day. The cousin's remittance holds its value much better than the wage does. The exercise shows why inflation is particularly destructive for wages and pensions that adjust slowly or only in periodic increases: income arrives at a fixed time while prices change continuously. Questions: (1) What is the effect on the teacher if the wage rises 50 percent each month while prices double each week? (2) Why would the family value the remittance more than a wage increase? (3) What behaviors would you predict among households?

Historical Vocabulary
Hyperinflation

Hyperinflation is extremely rapid inflation, commonly defined by economists as a rate above 50 percent per month. It is a different phenomenon from inflation in general, from a currency redenomination (removing zeros), and from dollarization (the use of foreign currency in place of a domestic one).

Hyperinflation has occurred in Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in the 2000s, and elsewhere, almost always when governments finance large deficits with money creation under conditions of crisis. In Venezuela it began in late 2017. Its end did not restore purchasing power or monetary stability: prices remained high and unstable, and the economy stayed partially dollarized.

Section 109.11

Food Shortages, Medicine, Public Services, and Everyday Life during the Crisis

Economic statistics become real in households. By 2016 shortages of staples, such as flour, cooking oil, milk, and sugar, had made queues a daily feature of urban life. Price controls and the lack of dollars for imports emptied shelves, while goods reappeared at much higher prices on informal markets run by resellers known as bachaqueros. Medicines were scarce for chronic diseases, cancer, HIV, and diabetes; hospitals lacked basic supplies; and doctors and nurses emigrated in large numbers. Preventable diseases such as measles and diphtheria returned in 2017–2018, and the Pan American Health Organization recorded outbreaks that spread to neighboring countries.

Surveys by Venezuelan universities, the ENCOVI project run by the Universidad Católica Andrés Bello and other institutions, documented the scale. Respondents reported weight loss, skipped meals, and reliance on a narrow diet of starch. In its surveys of the late 2010s and early 2020s ENCOVI found that income poverty above ninety percent and that extreme poverty applied to a majority of households. The United Nations World Food Programme's 2019–2020 assessment found that roughly one in three Venezuelans was food insecure. ENCOVI's methods differ from official statistics: it is an independent household survey with a defined sample, whereas government data were incomplete after 2014. Both have limitations, and students should treat the survey estimates as informed estimates, not exact counts.

In 2016 the government created the Local Supply and Production Committees (CLAP), a system of subsidized boxes of staples delivered to households through local committees tied to the party and the military. The stated aims were to guarantee supply and bypass speculators. For many households in the worst years the boxes were a significant source of food. Critics, including independent journalists and some prosecutors abroad, alleged that the program was used for political control through the Carnet de la Patria, that contracts for imported food involved corruption, and that deliveries favored loyal communities. The program's defenders deny that it was discriminatory. The evidence on both sides is incomplete, and what is clear is that the system placed access to food partly in the hands of the political authorities.

Public services decayed in parallel. Water systems, which depended on electricity and chemicals, failed in many cities. Public transport lost vehicles because of the lack of spare parts. The most dramatic breakdown was the blackout that began on 7 March 2019, when a failure at the Guri hydroelectric complex and its transmission lines left most of the country without power for days, with further outages that month and afterward. The government accused the United States and the opposition of sabotage, including cyberattacks. Independent engineers and utility specialists pointed to years of deferred maintenance, the loss of skilled staff, and vegetation fires on transmission corridors. The verified facts are that the grid failed, that hospitals and water systems stopped functioning, and that maintenance had been neglected. The sabotage claims were not corroborated by independent evidence made public.

The burdens fell unevenly. Women, who headed a large share of poor households, managed food and care under shortages, and pregnant women faced rising risks as prenatal and delivery care deteriorated. Children suffered malnutrition and school absence, as teachers left or could not live on their wages and schools lacked water and food. Older people on state pensions of a few dollars a month could not buy food or medicine unless relatives sent remittances. Indigenous communities, such as the Wayuu in Zulia and the Warao in the Orinoco Delta, were already poor and had little access to health services, and many moved across borders. Public employees, whose salaries had fallen to a few dollars, took second jobs or left. Those with relatives abroad, dollars, or access to the CLAP system fared better than those without.

Everyday Life and Social History

Queues, Pots, and Remittances: Household Strategies during the Crisis

Households developed a repertoire of strategies. Families formed networks to share queue duty, one member taking the early-morning shift at a supermarket while another worked. Neighbors pooled money for water trucks, cooked on firewood or charcoal when gas ran short, and collected rainwater during blackouts. Parents and grandparents skipped meals so children could eat, a pattern recorded in surveys. Teachers and nurses took informal jobs, such as selling snacks or teaching private lessons, and some professionals worked as couriers or drivers.

Many strategies were gendered. Women organized food, water, and medical care, and they often led community kitchens and church-based aid. Remittances, mostly small monthly sums sent by emigrated relatives, sustained millions of households and linked everyday survival to the migration described in Division IV. The silences in the official record, which for years did not acknowledge a humanitarian emergency, make surveys, oral histories, and the reports of church and community organizations important sources.

Section 109.12

Sanctions, Economic Adjustment, Dollarization, and the Uneven Stabilization of the Early 2020s

International sanctions developed in stages, and the stages matter for causation. In March 2015 the United States imposed targeted sanctions on seven Venezuelan officials accused of human-rights abuses and declared a national emergency. Further individual designations followed, including of President Maduro in July 2017. In August 2017 the Trump administration issued an executive order restricting new borrowing and trading in certain Venezuelan government and PDVSA bonds. In January 2019 it designated PDVSA as a blocked entity, which in effect cut off the largest source of its sales to the United States, and in August 2019 it blocked the property of the Venezuelan government. The European Union and Canada imposed targeted sanctions on officials and an arms embargo, but not a general embargo of oil purchases.

The relationship between sanctions and the economy is contested. The petroleum industry was already contracting before the 2019 oil sanctions, as the figures in Section 109.9 show, and the economy was shrinking, inflation was high, and imports had collapsed years earlier. At the same time, the financial restrictions of 2017 limited the government's ability to refinance debt, and the 2019 measures cut exports to the largest market, reduced the supply of diluent and equipment, and raised the cost and risk of dealing with Venezuelan entities. Some studies, notably by the Center for Economic and Policy Research, argued that sanctions caused tens of thousands of deaths; others disputed the methods and the attribution. The United Nations special rapporteur on unilateral coercive measures criticized the sanctions' humanitarian effects after a 2021 visit, while the U.S. government has maintained that sanctions targeted the government and included exemptions for food and medicine. The chapter does not adopt a casualty figure for sanctions.

The 2019 collapse of oil exports and the pandemic of 2020 were followed by a partial and uneven adjustment. From 2019 the government quietly relaxed several controls: it stopped enforcing many price controls, allowed dollars to circulate, reduced the pace of money creation, and let the exchange rate move more freely. Imports of consumer goods rose, supermarkets and "bodegones," shops that sold imported goods in dollars, appeared in Caracas and other cities, and the economy began to grow modestly from 2021 after seven years of contraction. Hyperinflation ended by the usual definition at the end of 2021 or soon after, although annual inflation remained far above regional averages.

This was not a general recovery. Wages and pensions remained a small fraction of the cost of a basic basket of goods. Growth was concentrated in commerce, imported goods, and some urban districts, while the public sector and rural areas remained poor. The new dollar economy widened inequality between those who earned dollars and those who did not. Economic improvement also depended on external factors, including oil export licenses, remittances, and gold and illicit revenues of uncertain size.

Between 2022 and 2024 U.S. policy shifted several times. In November 2022 the Treasury Department issued Chevron a limited license to resume some operations, after negotiations in Mexico between the government and the opposition. After the Barbados Agreement of October 2023, it issued a broader general license (General License 44) allowing transactions with the oil sector for six months. When the government disqualified opposition candidates and electoral conditions deteriorated, the license was allowed to lapse in April 2024, with a wind-down period and some specific licenses. These changes illustrate that sanctions were used as bargaining instruments, and also that Venezuelan oil output responded to them: it recovered modestly after the 2022 and 2023 easing.

Historical Comparison

Historical Comparison: The Cuban Embargo and Venezuelan Sanctions

The U.S. embargo of Cuba (Chapter 81) was comprehensive, covered trade and finance, and lasted more than sixty years, while Soviet and later Venezuelan support partially offset it. The sanctions on Venezuela were narrower in form, aimed at financial transactions, the oil company, and the property of the government, and they left broad channels of trade open, although the effect on a country whose revenue depended on oil exports was large. In both cases the sanctioning power pursued political change and the targeted government did not fall because of sanctions alone; in both, the targeted government blamed foreign hostility for domestic hardship, while critics pointed to domestic policy. The comparison helps students avoid two errors: treating sanctions as irrelevant, and treating them as the sole explanation.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate I: Why Did Venezuela's Economy Collapse?

The question: How much of the collapse resulted from domestic policy, petroleum-industry deterioration, external price shocks, and international sanctions?

Interpretation A — Domestic policy and institutional decay: Many economists argue that exchange and price controls, monetary financing, the politicization of PDVSA, expropriations, and the neglect of maintenance made the economy fragile before 2014 and that these choices turned a price decline into a catastrophe. They note that output was falling during the boom and that other oil exporters adjusted without hyperinflation.
Interpretation B — External pressure as an amplifying cause: Other scholars and officials argue that the price fall and, after 2017, financial restrictions and oil sanctions blocked refinancing, reduced revenue, and made recovery impossible, and that the government's adjustment options narrowed accordingly. The Venezuelan government and its allies emphasize this account and describe sanctions as economic warfare.
Interpretation C — Layered causation: A large body of scholarship combines the two, treating domestic fragility as the precondition, the oil price as the trigger, and sanctions as an accelerant after 2017 and especially 2019. On this view the questions are about timing and weight, not either-or.

Evidence: The timing of output decline (from about 2013), inflation (accelerating in 2015–2017), sanctions (from 2017 and 2019), and the comparison with other exporters. Sanctions-related studies differ in method.

Why it matters: The answer shapes assessments of responsibility, of policy options, and of what recovery requires. A recovery plan that assumes only sanctions caused the collapse differs from one that assumes institutional reform is necessary.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Venezuelan Economic Collapse and Hyperinflation

Long-term causes

Dependence on oil exports for foreign currency and public revenue; the exchange-control system since 2003; weakened non-oil production; PDVSA's politicization and underinvestment.

Short-term causes

The 2014–2016 oil-price collapse; a decision to maintain debt service while cutting imports; monetary financing of deficits; price controls.

Event or transformation

A deep contraction of output and imports, and from late 2017 hyperinflation.

Immediate effects

Shortages, collapse of real wages, deterioration of health and public services, emigration.

Long-term consequences

A partly dollarized economy with deep inequality, a degraded oil industry, and one of the largest migrations in the world.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the outcome. Sanctions added pressure but began after contraction was underway, and the collapse should not be reduced to the policies of one leader or to external pressure.

Section 109.13

From Economic Migration to Mass Displacement: The Scale of the Venezuelan Exodus

Venezuela had been a country of immigration for much of the twentieth century, drawing Europeans, Colombians, and other Latin Americans to its oil-fed cities. Emigration was small until the late 1990s, when professionals and wealthier families began leaving, and it grew during the Chávez years. After 2015 it became a mass movement. The people who left were increasingly ordinary: workers, farmers, nurses, teachers, students, and families with children, and by 2018 many traveled by bus and on foot, carrying little. They left because wages could not buy food, because medicines and medical care were unavailable, because electricity and water failed, because of insecurity, and, for some, because of political persecution or fear of it.

Several categories are used in discussing them, and they are not interchangeable. An economic migrant moves principally for work or income. A refugee in the strict sense is a person with a well-founded fear of persecution on specified grounds under the 1951 Refugee Convention. An asylum-seeker is a person who has applied for protection and awaits a decision. The 1984 Cartagena Declaration, adopted by Latin American states, broadens the refugee definition to include people fleeing generalized violence, massive human-rights violations, or events that seriously disturb public order, and some countries have invoked it for Venezuelans. UNHCR and the International Organization for Migration use the combined term "refugees and migrants" because most Venezuelans have not been formally recognized as refugees, though many need international protection. Many others cross borders daily or seasonally to buy goods, work, or visit relatives and then return.

Measurement is hard. Venezuelans left through official crossings and informal paths known as trochas, moved from one country to another, returned temporarily, or lacked documents. Host governments count different things: permits issued, people registered, asylum applications, or estimated stocks. The regional coordination platform for refugees and migrants from Venezuela (R4V), led by UNHCR and IOM, reconciles these sources and publishes estimates, which are revised.

The scale is nonetheless clear. UNHCR reported nearly 7.9 million Venezuelan refugees and migrants worldwide in its December 2025 update. Regional monitoring estimated approximately 6.98 million in Latin America and the Caribbean as of August 2026. These numbers refer to different geographic populations and reporting dates: the first covers the whole world, including Spain, the United States, and other destinations, and the second is limited to the region, so they should not be read as competing counts of one group.

The exodus was both a consequence of the crisis and a regional transformation in its own right. It changed the demography of Colombia, Peru, Chile, Ecuador, and Brazil, strained local services, shaped national elections, and created transnational family networks. For Venezuela it meant the loss of workers and professionals, a skewed age structure, and a dependence on remittances.

Population counts are not interchangeable across jurisdictions or dates. UNHCR’s December 2025 global summary placed Venezuelan refugees and migrants worldwide at nearly 7.9 million. The 2026 Mixed Migration Centre regional overview, drawing on regional reporting, counted 6.98 million in Latin America and the Caribbean as of August 2026, slightly above its November 2025 regional figure of 6.91 million. The gap between global and regional totals is a geographical distinction, not evidence that roughly one million people returned to Venezuela. Return decisions also depend on employment, security, family ties, housing and legal status in host countries, not solely on a change in the presidency.

Refugee, Migrant, and Temporary Protection

A refugee under international law is a person outside his or her country who has a well-founded fear of persecution on certain grounds and cannot or will not return. A migrant is a broader term for a person who moves across borders, for any reason. Temporary protection is a status granted by a host state to a group, often for a fixed period, that gives rights to stay and sometimes to work without determining individual refugee claims.

Section 109.14

Migration Routes: Colombia, Brazil, the Andes, Central America, and North America

The largest flow ran west into Colombia. The principal crossing was the Simón Bolívar International Bridge between San Antonio del Táchira in Venezuela and Cúcuta in Colombia, in the department of Norte de Santander. Thousands crossed daily in 2018 and 2019, many to shop or work and return, others to stay. Maduro had closed the border in 2015, and in February 2019 the Venezuelan government blocked bridges to stop the entry of humanitarian aid, which led many to use unofficial paths. The border formally reopened to commercial traffic in 2022. Colombia, with a land frontier of over two thousand kilometers and shared Caribbean-coast and Andean communities, absorbed the largest number.

From Colombia, many continued south. Venezuelans traveled through Ecuador toward Peru and Chile, often on foot along the Pan-American highway or by bus, crossing mountain passes in the Andes and, in the north of Chile, the Atacama desert. Peru, Ecuador, and Chile introduced visa requirements in 2019, which pushed many onto irregular routes and into the hands of smugglers. A second route led east and south to Brazil through Pacaraima in the state of Roraima, where the Brazilian army and the UN set up shelters, and a third led by sea to the Caribbean islands of Aruba, Curaçao, and Trinidad and Tobago, a dangerous route that led to deaths at sea and disputes over detention and deportation.

After 2021, as conditions in some South American host countries became harder, because of the pandemic, the loss of informal jobs, and anti-migrant political sentiment, more Venezuelans headed north. The route crossed Colombia to the Darién Gap, an area of roadless rainforest and mountains between Colombia and Panama, then through Central America and Mexico toward the United States. Crossings of the Darién rose to record levels in 2022 and 2023, and Venezuelans were among the largest national groups. The journey exposed people to drowning, disease, robbery, sexual violence, and extortion by armed groups and smugglers. Women and children were especially vulnerable. Some Venezuelans also went to Europe, mainly Spain, by air, where many had family links and language advantages.

Policy changes shifted routes. U.S. humanitarian parole programs and Temporary Protected Status gave some Venezuelans legal avenues in 2021–2023, and in 2023 the U.S. and Mexico agreed on new arrangements that affected entry points. In 2025 the legal channels available in the United States narrowed sharply, and removal flights to Venezuela became a contested element of bilateral relations. These developments, with the fall of Maduro in 2026, changed expectations about return and further movement, but the net effect on flows was not yet clear by November 2026.

Students must distinguish several kinds of movement. Daily commuting or shopping trips across the Colombian border are not permanent migration. Seasonal or circular labor migration is different again. Permanent migration, with the intention to settle, and international asylum claims, based on fear of persecution, are further categories. Humanitarian agencies, churches, and local volunteers, including networks in Cúcuta, Pacaraima, and Tulcán, provided meals, shelter, and information, often with few resources. The role of criminal organizations in smuggling and extortion along these routes is real, but it must not be generalized to migrants as a group; the history of Tren de Aragua belongs to Chapter 112.

The humanitarian geography of movement was not fixed. Entry requirements, visa rules, labor-market demand and border enforcement altered routes over time; a corridor that had been relatively straightforward for families in one year might require a dangerous informal crossing in the next. Exposure to extortion, violence, trafficking and exploitation varied according to legal status, resources, age, disability and whether people traveled with children. Migration records that count arrivals at a border checkpoint describe an event or flow and cannot be added directly to a register of residents as though they counted different persons. A household might cross multiple borders or return temporarily, while remaining one family in population-stock statistics.

Section 109.15

Refugee Protection, Temporary Status, Employment, and Integration in Host Countries

Host governments responded in different ways. Colombia, the largest host, initially tolerated irregular entry and issued special permits, then in February 2021 announced a Temporary Protection Statute that offered registration and a ten-year residence status, with access to health care, education, and work, to Venezuelans who had entered before a cutoff date, with a path for later arrivals to regularize. More than two million people registered, which was a rare example of large-scale regularization by a lower-middle-income country. The policy was supported by international donors and criticized for leaving out those who arrived irregularly after the cutoff.

Brazil adopted a humanitarian reception approach called Operação Acolhida (Operation Welcome) in 2018, managed with the army and international agencies. It provided shelters in Roraima, documentation, and a program of relocation ("interiorização") to other cities in exchange for job offers or family ties. In 2019 Brazil began recognizing many Venezuelans as refugees on a group basis, invoking the Cartagena Declaration, an approach used by few other governments. Peru gave temporary permits at first and later restricted entry; Ecuador and Chile introduced visa requirements and special regularization processes; other states, including Argentina and Uruguay, offered residence under regional agreements.

Integration was uneven. Venezuelans faced obstacles in obtaining work permits and validating professional qualifications, such as medical degrees, and many worked informally as vendors, delivery workers, domestic workers, and construction laborers, often at low pay. Children enrolled in school in large numbers, but language was not usually a barrier, as it was in many other displacement crises, while documentation, overcrowding, and discrimination were. Health systems absorbed new patients with variable support. Xenophobia grew, driven by competition for jobs and housing, by sensational coverage of crimes committed by a few migrants, and by politicians who used migration as an issue. Violent incidents occurred in northern Brazil in 2018 and in northern Chile and Peru in later years.

The distinction between refugee recognition and temporary status matters. A recognized refugee has protection against return, a path to long-term residence, and specific legal rights. Temporary protection or a residence permit gives rights without that guarantee and can be ended. Most host governments chose temporary status because it was administratively faster, gave control over numbers, and avoided the legal commitments of refugee recognition, although it offered less security. The choice shows how governments attempted to balance humanitarian obligations, the capacity of public services, security concerns, and domestic political pressure.

Host-country classifications can themselves change the apparent scale of protection. A person regularized by a temporary protection statute may be counted in a migration stock without having received an individual refugee-status determination; asylum-seeker totals count pending claims rather than recognized refugees. Comparing Colombian temporary-protection registrations with a Peruvian residence category or a Brazilian asylum record therefore risks converting administrative differences into an inaccurate demographic ranking. A useful comparison identifies a common reference date, whether each figure is a stock or a cumulative count, and the agency that recorded it.

Section 109.16

Families, Remittances, Labor, Identity, and the Long-Term Consequences of Venezuelan Displacement

Migration divided families. Parents left children with grandparents, siblings split between countries, and couples separated for years. Some children grew up supported by remittances and video calls, and the care of older relatives in Venezuela fell to people who stayed, mostly women. Researchers describe transnational families whose members maintained economic and emotional ties across borders, often with distinct experiences of crisis, one group coping with scarcity at home and the other with insecurity and discrimination abroad. Educational disruption was significant, since children left schools in Venezuela, where teachers had emigrated and classes were cut, and entered new school systems with different curricula.

Remittances were a lifeline. Estimates of total remittances to Venezuela vary and are hard to verify, because much was sent informally, but analysts have suggested that they reached billions of dollars in the early 2020s and for millions of households were more important than wages. Remittances also helped finance the dollarized economy described in Section 109.12. They increased inequality between households with and without relatives abroad, and they created a dependence on migrants' success in host labor markets.

In host countries Venezuelan migrants worked in services, commerce, construction, transport, health care, and agriculture, and many started small businesses, such as restaurants and bakeries that introduced arepas and other foods to cities across South America. Economic studies for several host countries, including Colombia and Peru, generally found modest effects on local wages and employment overall, with larger effects in informal sectors, and positive effects on aggregate output and fiscal revenue over time when migrants could work legally. Findings vary by country and method. Professionals such as doctors, engineers, and academics often worked in jobs below their qualifications, a loss of human capital for both Venezuela and host countries.

Identity changed too. Venezuelans abroad built associations, wrote about their experience, and developed a transnational identity marked by memory of crisis and, for many, uncertainty about return. Political attitudes among migrants were diverse; they did not all support the opposition, and many were not engaged in politics. Voluntary return depended on income, housing, security, and the availability of services in Venezuela, and in practice it remained limited before 2026. After January 2026, expectations of return rose among some migrants, but political change does not immediately resolve employment, housing, public services, or security. Many had children in school abroad, jobs, and legal status that could be lost if they returned.

Migrants must not be confused with criminal organizations. The overwhelming majority of Venezuelan migrants have no connection with organized crime, and surveys in several host countries found that they were no more likely than natives to commit crimes. A specific transnational criminal group that emerged from a Venezuelan prison and then followed migration routes is the subject of Chapter 112. Treating the migration as a crime story misrepresents it and reproduces the stereotypes that fueled xenophobia.

Section 109.17

The Barbados Agreement, María Corina Machado, and the Opposition's Electoral Strategy, 2023–2024

After the interim government dissolved, the opposition and the government returned to negotiation under pressure from the United States, which wanted a path to an election, and from Latin American governments that wanted to end the crisis. Talks mediated by Norway and hosted in Mexico in 2021–2022 produced a limited social agreement but no electoral deal. In October 2023, in Barbados, representatives of Maduro's government and the Plataforma Unitaria, the opposition coalition, signed an agreement that committed both sides to hold the presidential election in the second half of 2024, to allow national and international observers, to respect each party's right to choose its candidate, and to pursue the lifting of sanctions. On the same day the United States issued General License 44 easing oil-sector sanctions for six months, an explicit linkage between electoral commitments and economic relief.

The opposition then held its own presidential primary on 22 October 2023, organized by an independent commission without the electoral authority's help. María Corina Machado, a former legislator who had been a prominent critic of the government for two decades and who had argued for a firm stance against it, won with an overwhelming majority, receiving roughly nine in ten votes among more than two million cast according to the organizers. The government and the Tribunal had already moved against her. The comptroller general had disqualified her from holding public office for fifteen years in June 2023, and in January 2024 the Tribunal upheld the ban. The government denounced the primary as unlawful and opened investigations of its organizers.

The Barbados deal was implemented only in part. The government did not lift candidate bans, did not allow the European Union's observation mission to deploy after the Union renewed sanctions on officials, imposed restrictions on opposition campaigning and on registering voters abroad, and arrested some campaign workers. The opposition tried to register a replacement candidate, the academic Corina Yoris, but she was unable to register in the very short window available. In March 2024, at the last minute, the coalition registered Edmundo González Urrutia, a retired diplomat and little-known figure, whose name appeared on the ballot under the card of a small party. Machado campaigned across the country on his behalf, and crowds in towns that had not seen large opposition rallies for years showed an unexpectedly strong mobilization.

The distinction between agreement and implementation is important. The Barbados text established principles, and the government interpreted several of them narrowly. Where it controlled the electoral authority, the courts, and the comptroller, it could comply with the letter while limiting the competitiveness of the contest. The election therefore took place under conditions that international observers had criticized in advance: restricted candidacies, limited access to media, a rushed and restricted process for the diaspora, and an electoral authority that was not independent.

Section 109.18

The July 28, 2024 Presidential Election, Disputed Results, and the Crisis of 2025

The election was held on 28 July 2024. After polls closed, the National Electoral Council, headed by Elvis Amoroso, announced in the early hours of 29 July that Maduro had won with about 51 percent against about 44 percent for González, with roughly four fifths of the vote counted, and described the trend as irreversible. It blamed a cyberattack for subsequent delays and did not publish the results by polling station that the electoral law and past practice required. Within days the opposition began publishing images of the voting machines' printed tally sheets (actas), which it said it had collected from witnesses in most polling stations, on a public website. The sheets showed a large lead for González. The Supreme Tribunal's electoral chamber then declared the election result valid on 22 August after reviewing materials presented by the electoral authority, in a process the opposition did not accept.

The most substantial independent assessment came from the Carter Center, which had been invited to observe, along with a United Nations panel of experts. The Center concluded that the election did not meet international standards of electoral integrity and could not be considered democratic. In its final report it analyzed tally sheets representing 81.7 percent of polling stations, which it found authentic by examining security features, and reported that the sheets showed about 67.1 percent for González and about 30.4 percent for Maduro among the votes documented. These are results from the available forms and not certified nationwide totals: the sheets were not a complete set, and an authoritative count requires the electoral authority to release its own disaggregated data. Even so, the analysis showed that the official declaration could not be reconciled with the available documents. The electoral authority's failure to publish detailed results, which was the simplest way to substantiate its announcement, was therefore the decisive fact.

The Carter Center tested whether missing polling-station records could plausibly reverse the lead. In results forms covering 81.7 percent of polling stations, González had 7,156,462 votes and Maduro 3,241,461—a margin of 3,915,001 votes. The remaining stations had 3,576,544 registered voters. Even if every one of those registered voters voted for Maduro and none for González, the documented lead would not be erased: on the Center’s calculation González would still obtain approximately 50.2 percent nationwide against at most 47.8 percent for Maduro. This is a deliberately extreme upper-bound test, not a claim that the missing stations actually voted that way. It establishes the inconsistency of the CNE’s announced victory with the disclosed voting-station evidence, while it does not by itself replace an officially published and certified full set of precinct-level returns.

Protests erupted on 29 and 30 July, in poor as well as middle-class districts that had been strongholds of the ruling party. Statues of Chávez were toppled in some places. The government responded with force and with arrests. Human-rights organizations reported about two dozen deaths and more than two thousand detentions in the following weeks, including minors, and the authorities used terrorism charges against many detainees. Several opposition leaders, journalists, and campaign workers were jailed or went into hiding, and González left for Spain in September under legal threat. International reaction was divided: the United States, the European Parliament, and many Latin American governments rejected the declared result, and some, including the United States, recognized González as the winner. Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico sought a negotiated solution and urged the publication of the polling data, while Russia, China, Cuba, and others congratulated Maduro.

On 10 January 2025 Maduro was inaugurated for a third term. The day before, security officials briefly detained Machado after a rally in Caracas. Throughout 2025 the repression continued, including the arrest of foreigners and political figures. The United Nations' independent Fact-Finding Mission published a report in 2025 on violations in the post-election period, documenting arbitrary detention, enforced disappearance, and other abuses, which it attributed to state security forces and pro-government groups and described as part of a policy to silence dissent. In October 2025 the Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded the Peace Prize to Machado, citing her efforts for democratic rights in Venezuela.

Section 109.19

January 3, 2026: The U.S. Military Operation, Maduro's Capture, and the International-Law Crisis

Relations between Washington and Caracas deteriorated sharply during 2025. The second Trump administration revoked a license that allowed Chevron to operate, restored and expanded sanctions, and in August 2025 raised the reward for information leading to Maduro's arrest to 50 million dollars. It built up a large naval and air presence in the Caribbean, and from September 2025 U.S. forces carried out strikes on boats that officials said were carrying drugs from Venezuela, killing many people in operations whose legal basis was disputed in the United States and abroad. In November 2025 the administration designated a network it called the Cartel de los Soles, which it linked to Maduro and senior officers, as a foreign terrorist organization; Venezuelan officials said no such cartel existed, and analysts have long described the name as a label for corruption inside the armed forces rather than a formal organization. By December the administration had announced measures against sanctioned oil tankers.

On the night of 2–3 January 2026, U.S. forces carried out a military operation in Caracas. They captured Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores and transferred them to the United States. Casualty figures were reported differently, and no single total is adopted here. On 5 January both appeared in the Southern District of New York before Judge Alvin K. Hellerstein and pleaded not guilty. On 8 October 2026 prosecutors unsealed a fifth superseding indictment, S5 11 Cr. 205, adding a torture-conspiracy count to the previously charged narcoterrorism, cocaine-importation and firearms counts. The indictment accuses Maduro and Flores of involvement in a system of repression and the abuse of detainees, including U.S. citizens. Both pleaded not guilty to the revised charges on 15 October. An indictment records prosecutorial allegations; it does not constitute a judicial finding of guilt. The defendants retain the presumption of innocence. The Justice Department announcement links to the indictment itself.

Pre-trial litigation remained unresolved at the 16 November 2026 cutoff. A July scheduling order had set oral argument on immunity and dismissal for 17 November 2026, with trial provisionally scheduled to begin on 1 June 2027. At the 15 October arraignment the court moved oral argument to 17 December 2026 at 11 a.m. and the trial date to 14 June 2027. Judge Hellerstein died on 28 October; the matter was reassigned to Judge Analisa Torres, who maintained the existing deadlines. The operation raised a separate set of questions about international law. The UN Charter prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state, with exceptions for Security Council authorization and for self-defense against an armed attack. On 5 January the Security Council met in emergency session. The Secretary-General expressed concern about compliance with the Charter and emphasized the prohibition on the use of force and the importance of Venezuela's sovereignty. Russia, China, Cuba, and some Latin American governments condemned the action as aggression, while other governments welcomed Maduro's departure but criticized or declined to endorse the method. U.S. officials presented the operation as an effort to bring indicted criminal defendants to justice and as action against narco-terrorism, while also stating wider aims related to the hemisphere and to Venezuelan oil. The legal question, whether a state may use armed force on another state's territory to arrest its leader, is distinct from the question of whether the allegations are true.

The removal of an incumbent did not by itself create a new constitutional order. Maduro's capture removed one man, not the party, the courts, the electoral authority, the security services, or the officials who ran them. The government's most senior figures stayed in office, and the United States chose to work with the successor government rather than with the opposition that had won the 2024 vote according to independent tally-sheet analysis. A democratic order requires that a government depend on free elections, independent courts, and accountable security services, and none of these was created by the arrest of a leader. That is the problem that Section 109.20 addresses.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate II: Can the Illegitimacy of a Government or Allegations against Its Leader Justify Foreign Military Intervention?

The question: Does the fact that many governments regarded Maduro as illegitimate, or that he was indicted for drug trafficking, make the U.S. use of force lawful or justified?

Interpretation A — The operation was unlawful: Most international lawyers argue that the Charter's prohibition on force has only two accepted exceptions, Security Council authorization and self-defense, neither of which clearly applied. They hold that a government's illegitimacy, even if widely recognized, does not remove its state's sovereignty, that law enforcement does not authorize cross-border military raids without consent, and that proposed doctrines such as humanitarian intervention or intervention to restore democracy are not accepted legal exceptions without Council approval.
Interpretation B — The operation was justifiable: Supporters argue that Maduro was not Venezuela's legitimate president after the 2018 and 2024 elections, that the United States and many states did not recognize him, that he faced a pending indictment, that Venezuelan territory was used for drug trafficking that harmed the United States, and that the Council cannot act because of vetoes. Some invoke a broad idea of self-defense against narco-terrorism or the "unwilling or unable" theory.
Interpretation C — Politically defended, legally contested: Some scholars distinguish political and moral arguments from legal ones and conclude that, whatever one thinks of Maduro's rule, the legal case was weak and the precedent troubling, while also noting that state practice and international reactions will shape how the rules evolve.

Evidence: The text of the UN Charter and Council records; the U.S. indictment and official statements; the positions of governments; scholarly commentary; and earlier cases such as Panama 1989.

Why it matters: If the argument from illegitimacy were accepted, any state could justify force against a government it considered illegitimate. If it were rejected, the international system would offer no remedy against rulers who steal elections and abuse their citizens other than diplomacy and sanctions.

Student task: Separate three questions, whether the allegations are proven, whether Maduro was a legitimate ruler, and whether the use of force was lawful, and explain why the answer to one does not settle the others.

Section 109.20

Delcy Rodríguez, the 2026 Interim Government, and Venezuela's Unresolved Future

On 3 January the Supreme Tribunal ordered Vice President Delcy Rodríguez to assume the presidency as acting president, and the armed forces backed the decision. She was sworn in by her brother, Jorge Rodríguez, who presides over the National Assembly. In her first statements she denounced the operation and called Maduro the legitimate president, but in the following weeks she cooperated with Washington on oil and other issues. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said at first that he did not regard the government as legitimate because Venezuela lacked free elections, while President Trump described Rodríguez as cooperative and said the United States would be involved in managing the transition. The Tribunal had designated her tenure as an interim period of ninety days, extendable to 180 by the Assembly. The legal basis on which she continued to hold the office beyond those periods is disputed and is not resolved here from verified legal documents.

The government enacted measures that marked a shift. In late January the Assembly approved, and Rodríguez signed, a reform of the Hydrocarbons Law that opened the oil sector more widely to private and foreign participation and loosened state control. The U.S. Treasury issued general and company-specific licenses to firms including Chevron, and reports indicated that proceeds of Venezuelan oil sales were routed to accounts under U.S. control. Repsol signed an agreement in April to resume operational control of a joint venture, and Chevron expanded its stake in another. Output in 2026 was reported at between 0.8 and 1 million barrels a day, far below the historical peak. The agreements raised questions about constitutional authority, since the constitution requires that the state retain ownership of PDVSA, about the difference between announcements and actual investment, and about who benefited.

On 19 February the Assembly approved an Amnesty Law covering events from 1999 to 2026, including the 2002 coup and the post-2024 protests, and Rodríguez signed it. The government reported that thousands of detainees were released under it, while the human-rights group Foro Penal counted fewer who were political prisoners and said that between about 470 and 670 remained in custody afterward. The government announced in April that the amnesty process would wind down, and organizations such as Provea argued that amnesty should be the foundation of institutional reform, not a time-limited gesture. The authorities also announced the closure of El Helicoide, a prison linked to abuses. Critics noted that the law excluded some categories and did not make it easy for exiled opposition leaders to return.

The 29 January 2026 Hydrocarbons Law reform (Gaceta Oficial No. 6,978 Extraordinary) broadened opportunities for private entities to carry out primary hydrocarbon activities under revised contractual arrangements. Expanded operating rights, revenue-sharing terms, and licensing should not be conflated with a transfer of legal ownership of PDVSA or subsurface resources. Similarly, the 19 February Amnesty Law (Gaceta Oficial No. 6,990 Extraordinary) established categories of covered acts and exclusions; counting government decisions approving relief, court orders changing precautionary conditions, individuals physically released from prison, and independently classified political prisoners produces different totals. The distinction helps students evaluate reform announcements as implementation questions rather than completed institutional transformation.

On 24 June 2026, two earthquakes of reported magnitudes 7.2 and 7.5 struck northern Venezuela, damaging housing, hospitals, transportation, and water and electrical systems. Fatality counts changed sharply as searches progressed. PAHO’s initial situation reports were preliminary, while a 23 September Amnesty International assessment reported that the national government’s 24 August update counted 6,509 deaths. The governor of La Guaira separately gave an estimate of 7,265 deaths. A PAHO September situation report cited 6,301 deaths in an official 24 August figure, illustrating documentary discrepancy even when reports refer to ostensibly the same update. These figures have not been reconciled into a final authoritative total. Missing-person reports are not equivalent to confirmed fatalities: a La Guaira official figure of 1,579 missing contrasts with much larger citizen compilations that contained duplicates and unverified entries. The disaster placed immediate demands on shelter, emergency care and reconstruction while complicating an already uncertain political transition.

By the autumn the central question was elections. Rodríguez told the UN General Assembly on 23 September that elections would be held and that her government had begun dialogue with opposition sectors, but she gave no date. Reuters reported on 26 September that the vote might not occur for a year or more because of planned overhauls of the courts and electoral authority, and that critics said Washington had favored oil and investment deals over democratic concessions. In October the Trump administration was reported to have outlined a road map in which judicial and electoral reform would come first, after which Rodríguez would declare the presidency permanently vacant and start a constitutional timetable for an election. María Corina Machado, who had been sidelined in the first months and whose efforts to return had been blocked, was reported to have returned to Venezuela on 25 October. On 29 October Rodríguez said in a televised address that elections would be held, organized by a renewed electoral authority and with international observers. As of 16 November 2026 no election date had been set.

Opposition demands centered on independent courts and electoral authority, the release of all political prisoners, the return of exiles, civil liberties, and international observation. Government officials said reforms required time. Observers noted that Diosdado Cabello, who controls the security apparatus, remained in office, and that the same party dominated the Assembly and courts. Whether reform would produce credible elections or rebrand continued control remained open, and the chapter does not predict which.

Several matters remained unresolved on 16 November 2026: the date and conditions of elections; the composition and independence of the courts and the electoral authority; the number of political prisoners; the legal basis of the interim authority; the terms and implementation of the oil agreements; the outcome of Maduro's and Flores's criminal case, including the immunity motions; the international-law debate over the operation; the final human toll and reconstruction after the earthquakes; and the decisions of millions of Venezuelans abroad about whether to return. A recovery in the economy would require stable monetary policy, restored oil investment, rebuilt power and water systems, and wages that buy food. Democratic accountability would require independent institutions and credible elections. Return and reconciliation would require security, housing, work, and a way of addressing grievances on all sides.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: The Tribunal's order named Rodríguez acting president for a defined period without setting an election date. The Amnesty Law covered specified events from 1999 to 2026. Briefing papers describe oil agreements and the continued absence of an election date.

Analysis: Together these records show that the transition was managed through existing Venezuelan institutions and U.S. licensing decisions, and they provide dated evidence of what the government enacted. Comparing the laws with independent counts of releases separates announcements from implementation.

Limitations: Official texts and figures come from interested parties. Counts of prisoners released differ by source, briefings were written while events were unfolding, and the published licenses do not reveal the terms of all private agreements.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Venezuela's path from the death of Hugo Chávez in March 2013 to the unresolved transition of November 2026, and it has argued that the outcome had several interacting causes. Petroleum dependence, domestic economic policy, the physical decline of the oil industry, political and institutional conflict, and international sanctions combined to produce hyperinflation, mass displacement, and a prolonged dispute over who had the right to govern.

Maduro's succession rested on Chávez's endorsement, a contested constitutional interpretation by the Supreme Tribunal, and a narrow election victory in April 2013 that the opposition disputed and the courts upheld. He governed through a coalition of party, military, and regional networks, and he faced the first major protests in 2014, in which peaceful demonstration, advocacy of constitutional change, and unlawful violence had to be distinguished.

The oil-price collapse of 2014–2016 exposed the fragility created by exchange controls, price controls, borrowing, and central-bank financing of deficits, in an economy that depended on oil for nearly all of its foreign currency. The government protected debt payments and cut imports, and it financed deficits by creating money. The decline in PDVSA's physical output, from about 2.8 million barrels a day on average in 2008–2013 to about 0.9 million in 2019 and 0.4 million in mid-2020, turned a price shock into a durable loss of income.

The opposition's landslide in the December 2015 legislative elections did not transfer power. The Tribunal blocked the Assembly, the recall referendum was suspended in 2016, and in 2017 the Tribunal's attempt to assume legislative powers and the creation of a Constituent Assembly without a prior referendum removed the elected legislature's authority. Protests in 2017 produced more than a hundred deaths and extensive documentation of abuses by United Nations bodies, and the International Criminal Court opened an investigation in 2021.

The 2018 election, boycotted by the main opposition, led to Juan Guaidó's claim to the interim presidency in 2019 and to recognition by dozens of governments. Recognition did not give him control over institutions or territory, and the contest was one of legitimacy, not of two equal governments. The interim arrangement dissolved at the start of 2023.

Inflation became hyperinflation in late 2017, and the realized annual inflation of about 65,374 percent in 2018 by the IMF's series stood at the peak of a process in which wages, savings, and confidence were destroyed. Redenominations in 2018 and 2021 changed the numbers but not the underlying monetary dynamics. Dollarization offered partial relief to those with access to dollars, and it widened inequality.

Food shortages, the collapse of the health system, the failure of water and electricity services, and the 2019 blackouts reshaped everyday life. The CLAP program supplied food to many households while placing access to it partly under political control. Women, children, older people, Indigenous communities, and public employees were hit hard, and households coped with queues, shared labor, and remittances.

Sanctions developed in stages from targeted measures in 2015 to financial restrictions in 2017 and oil and government-asset sanctions in 2019. The economy was already contracting before the broadest measures, and the sanctions added to the pressure. Historians weigh domestic policy, industry decline, price shocks, and sanctions differently, and the chapter has treated their relative weight as a question of timing and evidence.

The exodus, which UNHCR counted at nearly 7.9 million refugees and migrants worldwide in December 2025, became one of the largest displacements in the modern Americas. People moved through Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Chile, Brazil, the Caribbean, and the Darién Gap, and host states responded with temporary protection, humanitarian reception, and restrictions. Migration divided families, created remittance economies, and reshaped host societies, and it must not be confused with organized crime.

The July 2024 election produced an official declaration for Maduro that the electoral authority could not substantiate with disaggregated data, and independent analysis of the tally sheets, representing 81.7 percent of polling stations, showed a large lead for the opposition candidate among documented votes. Maduro was inaugurated in January 2025 amid continuing repression.

On 3 January 2026 U.S. forces captured Maduro and Flores, who face criminal charges in New York and have not been tried. The operation raised distinct questions of criminal responsibility and of international law. Delcy Rodríguez became acting president, cooperated with Washington on oil and prisoner releases, and promised elections without setting a date. Earthquakes in June added a humanitarian emergency. As of 16 November 2026 the transition was unresolved.

Key Terms

Chavismo
The political movement and ideology associated with Hugo Chávez, which included the PSUV, military officers, and popular organizations; Maduro's supporters are often called chavistas.
Madurismo
A term for the style and coalition of Maduro's government, often used to distinguish it from Chávez's personal leadership.
PSUV
Partido Socialista Unido de Venezuela, the ruling party founded in 2007.
MUD
Mesa de la Unidad Democrática, the opposition coalition that won the 2015 legislative elections.
Plataforma Unitaria
The opposition coalition that negotiated the Barbados Agreement and backed Edmundo González in 2024.
PDVSA
Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A., the state oil company.
Rentier state
A state that draws most revenue from an external resource rent rather than domestic taxation.
Exchange control
A system, in place from 2003, by which the government set exchange rates and rationed dollars.
Law of Fair Prices
The 2014 law and related regulations that fixed maximum prices and profit margins.
Hyperinflation
Extremely rapid inflation, commonly above 50 percent per month.
Redenomination
Removing zeros from a currency's unit; Venezuela did so in 2008, 2018, and 2021.
Dollarization
The use of U.S. dollars in place of or beside the domestic currency.
Bachaquero
A reseller of scarce goods in informal markets during the shortages.
Recall referendum
A vote on removing an elected official from office after the half-way point of a term, provided in the 1999 Constitution.
Constituent Assembly
A body convened to write or rewrite a constitution; the 1999 body was approved by referendum, the 2017 body was not.
Desacato
Contempt; the Tribunal's 2016 label for the Assembly after it seated three disputed legislators.
Article 233
The constitutional provision on the president's absence, invoked by Guaidó and in succession disputes.
Interim (acting) president
A person who exercises the presidency temporarily; this status was claimed by Guaidó in 2019 and held by Rodríguez in 2026.
Legitimacy
The recognized right to rule, which can differ from effective control of institutions.
Acta
The printed tally sheet from an electronic voting machine, central to the 2024 dispute.
CNE
Consejo Nacional Electoral, the electoral authority.
TSJ
Tribunal Supremo de Justicia, the highest court.
Colectivos
Pro-government armed or community groups, some involved in violence against protesters.
La Salida
The 2014 opposition campaign calling for pressure to end Maduro's government.
Carnet de la Patria
An identification card linked to state benefits and records.
CLAP
Local Supply and Production Committees, which distributed subsidized food boxes from 2016.
ENCOVI
Encuesta Nacional de Condiciones de Vida, an independent household survey.
Sanctions
Government measures restricting economic or financial transactions with a country, entity, or individual.
General license
A U.S. Treasury authorization permitting certain transactions otherwise blocked by sanctions.
Foreign Terrorist Organization
A U.S. legal designation, not a universal scholarly category.
Refugee
A person with a well-founded fear of persecution who is outside his or her country.
Cartagena Declaration
A 1984 regional instrument that broadens the refugee definition to include generalized violence and mass human-rights violations.
Temporary protection
A time-limited group status granting residence and rights without individual refugee determination.
Trocha
An informal path across the Colombian-Venezuelan border.
Remittance
Money sent home by migrants, a vital source of household income.
Darién Gap
Roadless jungle between Colombia and Panama on a major northbound migration route.
Operação Acolhida
Brazil's humanitarian reception operation for Venezuelans, begun in 2018.
Amnesty Law
The February 2026 law covering political offenses from 1999 to 2026.
Head-of-state immunity
A doctrine that shields sitting or former rulers from foreign prosecution, contested in Maduro's case.
Narcoterrorism
A U.S. criminal charge combining drug trafficking with support for terrorist organizations, as alleged in the Maduro indictment.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a state built on a single export can lose the capacity to govern, to provide for its people, and to settle its disputes peacefully. The oil that financed the Bolivarian project also made it vulnerable, and decisions about exchange rates, prices, debt, and money determined how deeply a price shock would cut. The story is therefore one of institutions and policy choices, as well as of leaders.

It also shows how the loss of trusted procedures, the recall, the legislature, and the vote, pushed political conflict into the streets, into foreign capitals, and eventually into a military operation. The 2024 election and the events of 2026 are connected by the same problem of legitimacy: who has the right to govern, and who decides. The removal of one leader did not answer that question.

The Venezuelan exodus links this chapter to the rest of Latin America. Millions of people changed the demography of neighboring countries and became part of the migration routes through Central America and Mexico that Chapters 111 and 112 take up, including the criminal organizations that exploited them and the security policies that responded. Chapter 110 turns to social movements, Chapter 111 to Central America, and Chapter 112 to transnational crime, including Tren de Aragua.

Students should carry forward two lessons. The first is methodological: in contemporary history, evidence is partial, interested, and changing, and historians must date their claims and state what remains undecided. The second is substantive: neither economic collapse nor political transition has a single cause or a single author, and the history of Venezuela remained, on 16 November 2026, unfinished.
