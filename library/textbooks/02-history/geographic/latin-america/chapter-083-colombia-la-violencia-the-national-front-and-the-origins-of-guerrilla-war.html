Colombia: La Violencia, the National Front, and the Origins of Guerrilla War

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations
Chapter 83 · Principal coverage 1946–1974

Chapter Opening: A Killing in Bogotá and a Longer Crisis

On the afternoon of 9 April 1948, Jorge Eliécer Gaitán, the most popular opposition figure in Colombian politics, was shot on a busy street in central Bogotá. The killing set off an urban uprising, but the violence that followed across the countryside was not simply a consequence of one shot. It drew on older struggles over municipal power, land, party loyalty, and the meaning of citizenship.

Gaitán was shot shortly after midday on Bogotá's Carrera Séptima. He died within hours, and the man identified as the gunman, Juan Roa Sierra, was killed by an enraged crowd before he could be questioned in any meaningful way. News spread quickly by radio. Crowds attacked the offices of the governing party's newspapers, burned trams and public buildings, and looted shops. Part of the capital's police force sided with the crowd, and the army had to restore order. The city was hosting the Ninth International Conference of American States at that moment, so the disorder unfolded in front of foreign delegations. This chapter begins with that day because its consequences are so visible, but it does not treat the day as the origin of Colombia's mid-century crisis.

The central problem of the chapter is stated simply and answered with difficulty. Colombia's political leaders eventually succeeded in ending the Liberal–Conservative warfare that had devastated parts of the countryside. They did so through a negotiated settlement, the National Front, under which the two traditional parties alternated the presidency and divided public offices for sixteen years. Yet the same period saw the formation of the Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia (FARC), the Ejército de Liberación Nacional (ELN), the Ejército Popular de Liberación (EPL), and, at its end, the Movimiento 19 de Abril (M-19). Why did a settlement that reduced one kind of conflict leave the conditions for another?

The answer offered here has several parts, and the chapter takes care not to collapse them into one. La Violencia was a set of partisan conflicts, state repression, local feuds, and rural self-defense efforts whose intensity varied enormously from one region to another. The National Front was a political bargain among elites that stabilized the presidency and ended the dictatorship of General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, but it limited competition and left rural inequalities largely untouched. The guerrilla organizations that appeared in the 1960s had distinct origins: communist campesino self-defense in the case of the FARC, revolutionary nationalism and Cuban inspiration in the case of the ELN, a Maoist split within the communist movement in the case of the EPL, and a dispute over electoral legitimacy in the case of the M-19.

An interpretive safeguard governs the whole chapter. Colombia's armed conflict did not begin because of the Cuban Revolution of 1959. Cuban example mattered, particularly to the ELN, and the Cold War shaped how Colombian and U.S. officials interpreted rural unrest. But some of the most important rural communities that later formed the FARC had been shaped by the violence of the late 1940s and 1950s, by land disputes decades older, and by the Colombian Communist Party's work among settlers and tenants. Colombia's Truth Commission, in its 2022 final report, traces precisely these continuities between partisan violence, campesino self-defense, and later insurgency, without suggesting that the later conflict was inevitable.

The landmark two-volume study La violencia en Colombia (1962 and 1964) was written by Germán Guzmán Campos, Orlando Fals Borda, and Eduardo Umaña Luna through a university-based academic publishing initiative. Guzmán had served on the official 1958 commission investigating political violence, and his collected field materials helped inform the subsequent book; the book itself was not the government commission's formal report. It remains indispensable and also reflects the political climate in which it was written. Even the basic question of how many people died is disputed, as Section 83.4 explains.

The chapter has twenty sections arranged in five divisions. Division I examines the political polarization that led to the Bogotazo and the descent into rural violence (1946–1953). Division II covers military rule, the attempt at pacification, and the political negotiations that created the National Front (1949–1958). Division III analyzes the National Front's operation, its exclusions, and the failed promise of agrarian reform (1958–1974). Division IV traces the origins of the FARC, ELN, and EPL (principally 1950–1967). Division V treats counterinsurgency, the disputed election of 1970, the social history of conflict, and the settlement's limits, ending in 1974. The chapter follows Chapter 82, which discussed Che Guevara and the continental spread of guerrilla theory; here the emphasis falls on Colombia's own history. Chapter 84 turns to Brazil's military regime.

The chapter's boundaries matter. Colombia's later conflict, including the expansion of guerrilla warfare in the 1980s, the cocaine economy, paramilitary violence, and the peace process that ended with the 2016 accord, belongs to Chapters 97 through 100. This chapter mentions those developments only as consequences to be understood later, and it avoids reading them backward into the 1960s.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Liberal–Conservative rivalry, patronage, local government, and the political reforms of the Liberal Republic prepared the ground for La Violencia.
  2. Distinguish the assassination of Jorge Eliécer Gaitán, the Bogotazo, and the longer national conflict, without asserting an unproven conspiracy.
  3. Analyze the regional variation of partisan violence and the roles of police, irregular partisan groups, and Liberal guerrillas.
  4. Assess the human and social consequences of La Violencia, including displacement, land conflict, and the contested estimates of mortality.
  5. Evaluate why the coup of 13 June 1953 was initially welcomed by many political leaders, and why Rojas Pinilla's pacification was partial.
  6. Trace the negotiations of Benidorm and Sitges, the fall of Rojas Pinilla, and the plebiscite of 1 December 1957, distinguishing women's enfranchisement in 1954 from its first national exercise in 1957.
  7. Interpret the National Front as both a genuine political settlement and a restriction on competition.
  8. Evaluate Law 135 of 1961 and the rise of ANUC as evidence of the gap between legal reform and implementation.
  9. Compare the origins, social bases, and ideologies of the FARC, ELN, EPL, and M-19.
  10. Contextualize U.S. assistance and Colombian counterinsurgency without treating Colombian policy as made in Washington.
  11. Interpret primary sources such as Gaitán's speech, Law 135, the Simacota manifesto, and the 1970 electoral dispute, noting evidentiary limits.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XVIII's argument that Latin American insurgencies had domestic roots as well as Cuban inspiration.

Historical Context

Colombia in the mid-twentieth century was a country of regions. Three Andean cordilleras divide it, and the Magdalena and Cauca valleys, the Caribbean coast, the Pacific lowlands, the eastern plains (Llanos Orientales), and the Amazonian south each developed distinct economies and political cultures. Bogotá sat on a high plateau, relatively isolated from the coast until river steamers, railways, and later roads reduced the distance. The central state was institutionally weak by comparison with, for example, Chile or Mexico. National authority depended on negotiated relations with regional elites, and in many rural districts it was represented chiefly by a mayor, a police detachment, and a parish priest.

Since the mid-nineteenth century, nearly every Colombian family had belonged, by inheritance and local loyalty, to the Liberal or Conservative party. These were not simply ideological organizations. They were networks of patronage that distributed municipal offices, tax collection, police posts, schoolteaching positions, and access to credit. Historically the Conservatives were associated with the Catholic Church, social hierarchy, and centralized authority, and the Liberals with federalism, anticlerical reform, and commercial interests, though both parties included landowners, merchants, and many poor supporters. The War of the Thousand Days (1899–1902) had killed tens of thousands and left a memory that the parties could destroy each other. For much of the early twentieth century the Conservatives held national power until the Liberals returned in 1930, a transition notable for being relatively peaceful.

The coffee economy shaped the countryside. Smallholder coffee production in Antioquia, Caldas, Tolima, and Cundinamarca coexisted with large estates, particularly in Tolima, Cundinamarca, and the Caribbean lowlands, where tenants and sharecroppers worked under conditions that provoked organizing in the 1920s and 1930s. Settlers moving up mountain slopes and into frontier valleys often found that title to the land was claimed by estate owners. Catherine LeGrand's study of frontier expansion shows how these disputes shaped rural politics long before 1948. Legislation in 1936 (Law 200), under President Alfonso López Pumarejo, declared a social function for property and tried to protect cultivators, but landowners resisted and its effects were mixed.

Two further conditions deserve mention. The first is the Colombian Communist Party, founded in 1930, which organized workers and in the 1930s and 1940s built a presence among tenants and settlers in parts of Cundinamarca, Tolima, and elsewhere. It remained small electorally but influential in particular localities. The second is the state's coercive capacity. The police, whose control was being centralized in the 1940s and 1950s, and the army were small and often tied to party politics. A change of government could mean the replacement of a municipality's police with men of the other party, which is one reason that national elections could produce local violence.

Finally, mid-century Colombia was urbanizing. Bogotá, Medellín, and Cali grew, industrial employment expanded in the 1930s and 1940s, and the Depression and Second World War reshaped the economy. Urban workers and a growing middle class created political space for a leader like Gaitán, who appealed over the heads of party notables. The decade between 1936 and 1946 therefore saw the combination of reform ambition, entrenched local power, rural land conflict, and an electorate that was growing and politicizing faster than the parties' institutions could absorb.

Section 83.1

The Liberal Republic, Conservative Restoration, and the Crisis of Colombian Party Politics

The Liberal Party returned to the presidency in 1930 after decades of Conservative government, and the sixteen years that followed are called the Liberal Republic. Its most ambitious phase came under Alfonso López Pumarejo (1934–1938), whose program, the Revolución en Marcha, produced the constitutional reform of 1936, expanded the state's role in education and labor relations, recognized the right to organize, and, through Law 200 of 1936, declared that property had a social function and sought to clarify land titles in favor of cultivators. The reforms reduced the Church's formal role in education and broadened the language of citizenship. They also alarmed landowners, Conservative leaders, and many Catholics, who read them as an attack on property and religion. Richard Stoller's work on this period shows that the Liberal governments were divided between reformers and cautious notables, and the reformist energy faded after 1938.

The reform program shaped rural politics unevenly. Law 200 encouraged some landowners to evict tenants and sharecroppers before they could claim rights, a result opposite to what its drafters intended. Later legislation in 1944 that regulated sharecropping was widely seen by agrarian activists as favoring estate owners. Organized labor grew, with the Confederación de Trabajadores de Colombia (CTC) founded in 1935, and Communist and Liberal activists contested leadership of unions and agrarian leagues. By the mid-1940s the Liberal Republic had delivered some modernizing change and left a sense, among many poorer Colombians, that reform had been promised and then withdrawn.

The partisan system gave local office extraordinary weight. A mayor appointed by the governor, and a governor appointed by the president, controlled municipal police, the registry of voters, local taxes, and the award of jobs. When the national party changed, so did governors, and governors replaced mayors and police chiefs, often down to the lowest ranks. Voters recognized that a change of regime in Bogotá meant a change of authority in their village. The same logic applied to public employment, from rural teachers to tax collectors. In such a system, the transfer of national power could set off a local contest, and the stakes included physical safety. Mary Roldán's study of Antioquia shows how a Conservative governor's administration used police and appointments to remake local power, with consequences that went well beyond ideology.

The election of 1946 turned this system into a crisis. The Liberals, who had held the presidency since 1930, split. Official party leaders backed Gabriel Turbay, a respected party figure, while Jorge Eliécer Gaitán ran as a dissident Liberal with a more radical and popular program. The Conservatives united behind Mariano Ospina Pérez, a coffee-growing engineer from a prominent Antioquian family, and won with a plurality, roughly 566,000 votes against roughly 441,000 for Turbay and 359,000 for Gaitán. Together the two Liberals polled more than Ospina, which is why many Liberals later regarded the result as the product of their own division. Ospina formed a bipartisan government of national union, but the Conservatives now held the governorships and police.

The consequences appeared first in rural areas. As Conservative governors replaced Liberal mayors and as police units were reconstituted with partisans, local Liberal communities faced harassment, dismissal, and violence. Liberal and Conservative leaders in Bogotá, who often had social and family ties, continued to negotiate, but their ability to control behavior in places like the highlands of Boyacá or the coffee hills of Tolima weakened. The partisan conflict was therefore not an isolated reaction to a killing in 1948. By 1947 reports of killings and expulsions in several departments were already reaching the capital, and Gaitán, who had taken over the Liberal Party leadership after the 1947 midterm elections, was denouncing them in public.

Historical Vocabulary
La Violencia

La Violencia ("the Violence") is the name Colombians give to the wave of political and rural violence in the middle of the twentieth century. It is a historians' category as well as a popular one, and it has no agreed beginning or end. Some scholars begin it in 1946 with the transfer of power to the Conservatives, others in 1948 with the Bogotazo, and a recent demographic study by Julio Romero-Prieto and Adolfo Meisel-Roca argues from mortality data that the sharpest rise in violent deaths began in 1949. Endings proposed range from 1953, when General Rojas Pinilla took power, to 1958, when the National Front began, to the mid-1960s, when partisan banditry subsided.

The label is useful but can mislead. It suggests a single national war, whereas the violence took different forms in different places, and it can obscure continuities, such as the survival of armed rural communities that helped form later guerrilla organizations. Students should treat La Violencia as a cluster of related conflicts rather than one event.

Section 83.2

Jorge Eliécer Gaitán, Popular Mobilization, and the Bogotazo of 9 April 1948

Jorge Eliécer Gaitán was born in 1903 into a modest Bogotá family and rose through law, studying in Colombia and then in Rome, where he took a doctorate in criminal law. He became known as an orator and trial lawyer, and he entered politics as a Liberal. His speech in the Congress in 1929 on the 1928 killing of striking banana workers at Ciénaga, in the United Fruit Company zone, gave him national prominence. He served as mayor of Bogotá in 1936–1937 and as minister of education in 1940–1941. W. John Green's study of gaitanismo and Richard Sharpless's biography show that he built a movement that combined legalistic appeals to the Liberal tradition with a more insurgent language of the people against the oligarchy.

His political ideas are best understood as a form of democratic populism. Gaitán distinguished between the "political country," which he identified with the party elites that dominated Congress and the press, and the "national country" of workers, peasants, artisans, and small merchants who were excluded from real influence. He called for social reform, including protection of labor, credit and land for small cultivators, and urban improvements, and he insisted on public morality. His supporters were drawn heavily from Bogotá's working neighborhoods, from other cities, and from the Liberal peasantry in several regions. He was criticized by Liberal notables as a demagogue and by Conservatives and some Communists as a threat or a rival, and his relations with organized labor were complicated. Herbert Braun's study shows how his public image and the experience of urban crowds produced a politics of dignity and anger.

After his defeat in 1946, Gaitán took control of the Liberal Party. The Liberal slate he backed won the congressional elections of March 1947, and he became the party's sole director. As violence in the countryside increased, he used his position to publicize it. On 7 February 1948 he led the March of Silence in Bogotá, a vast procession, estimated at around 100,000 people, who carried black flags and kept silence as a sign of mourning for the victims of violence. Gaitán's speech, the Oración por la Paz, addressed the president directly, asking him to use state authority to stop the persecution of Liberals. The silence of the crowd was part of its meaning. It signaled discipline, grief, and a claim that the Liberal masses were not a mob.

On 9 April 1948, shortly after one o'clock in the afternoon, Gaitán was shot as he left his law office on the Carrera Séptima near the Avenida Jiménez. He died at a clinic within the hour. The man who shot him, Juan Roa Sierra, was beaten to death by bystanders. Within minutes news was broadcast, and crowds gathered at the clinic and in the center. They approached the presidential palace and attacked the Capitol, the Palace of Justice, the archbishop's palace, ministries, churches, hotels, and shops; trams were burned and looting spread. Radio stations were seized, some by Liberal activists who broadcast calls to resist. Part of the capital's police joined the crowd, while the army defended the presidential palace and gradually retook the streets. Fidel Castro, then a student visiting Bogotá, was in the city and witnessed the disorder, a coincidence later invoked in many retellings of the event.

The disorder spread. Revolutionary juntas formed in several towns, including Barrancabermeja, the oil-workers' center, and in parts of Tolima and Santander, and for a few days some local authorities were displaced. Conservative officials and priests were attacked in some places, and Conservatives in turn organized reprisals. In Bogotá, Liberal leaders met with President Ospina Pérez the evening of the killing and agreed to remain in a coalition government, while the military and police reasserted control. The number of dead in the capital is uncertain, with estimates ranging from several hundred to several thousand, and the damage to the city center was large. This was a major urban disturbance but a short one. The much longer conflict that followed took place mostly outside the capital.

The identity and motives of Gaitán's killers remain contested. Roa Sierra's death prevented a trial. Investigators in 1948 and later offered several theories, including that he acted alone, that he was manipulated by political enemies, or that foreign or domestic conspirators were involved. Conservative officials blamed communists, Liberals blamed the government and its allies, and later writers have proposed other conspiracies. No account has been established by evidence that historians widely accept as conclusive, and this chapter does not assert one. What historians can establish is the effect: the Colombian Truth Commission identifies the assassination as a critical moment in the escalation of already developing violence. Students should keep apart three things: the assassination, the Bogotazo, and the national conflict that followed.

Primary Source Window 1

Gaitán's Oración por la Paz, 7 February 1948

Creator: Jorge Eliécer Gaitán · Date and place: 7 February 1948, Plaza de Bolívar, Bogotá · Type: Political oration, delivered to a crowd and addressed to President Ospina Pérez; later widely reprinted

Context: The speech closed the March of Silence, called by Gaitán as director of the Liberal Party to protest killings and persecution of Liberals in the provinces. The crowd, estimated at around 100,000, carried black flags and remained silent.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): Gaitán addresses the president on behalf of an immense, silent multitude, and asks that the government use its authority to end the persecution and killing, insisting that the Liberal crowd seeks peace, not vengeance. He presents the silence of the march as proof of the people's discipline, and he warns that the continuation of violence will bring grief to the country and responsibility to those in power.

Analysis: The oration shows how Gaitán combined a plea to public authority with the mass presence of the crowd, and how he framed Liberals as law-abiding citizens facing partisan persecution. It also shows that violence was already a national political issue more than two months before his death. Because he spoke as a partisan leader, his account of who was responsible reflects Liberal experience and does not provide neutral measurement of the killings.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the editorial paraphrase suggest about how Gaitán addressed the president and the crowd, and why would the silence of the march matter?
  2. Who created the speech, in what capacity, and for what audience and purpose?
  3. What does the oration reveal about political violence before 9 April 1948?
  4. What can historians not establish from the speech alone, such as the number or distribution of victims, or the causes of the violence?

Evidentiary limits: Students should consult a published Spanish text of the speech, since this window offers only a paraphrase and does not reproduce its wording. Different printings may vary, and the speech was a partisan intervention.

Section 83.3

Chulavitas, Pájaros, Liberal Guerrillas, and the Geography of Partisan Violence

After April 1948, rural violence intensified in several regions at once, though its forms varied. Historians distinguish at least four kinds of armed actor, and the distinctions matter because the common phrase "partisan violence" can hide who held weapons and under what authority. The first were state security forces, principally the police, which in many districts had been restaffed with partisans. The second were irregular groups aligned with one party, often operating with the tolerance or support of local officials. The third were Liberal guerrillas, who formed for self-defense or resistance. The fourth were private armed men serving landowners or local bosses, whose interests were not always identical to those of either party.

The word chulavitas refers to police recruits associated with the village of Chulavita in Boyacá, a strongly Conservative highland area from which many were drawn. In popular usage, and in Liberal memory, the term came to denote Conservative-aligned police and irregular auxiliaries who participated in attacks on Liberals. Historians treat it with care. Many police were not chulavitas in the narrow sense, and the label carried political weight. Pájaros ("birds") were Conservative-aligned irregular killers, most famously in the Cauca valley. Tuluá, in Valle del Cauca, became associated with León María Lozano, called "El Cóndor," who organized Conservative armed action with, according to scholarly accounts, the tolerance of local officials. The term was applied loosely elsewhere, and its use in Liberal accounts reflects partisan experience.

Liberal resistance took several forms. In the eastern plains, Liberal guerrillas emerged in 1949 and 1950 and organized with a degree of structure. In parts of Tolima, Santander, and Antioquia, local self-defense bands formed, sometimes under commanders who had been police or peasants. These groups attacked Conservative civilians and officials and were also responsible for killings and destruction. Gonzalo Sánchez and Donny Meertens, in Bandits, Peasants, and Politics, trace how some armed leaders became regional "bandits" with political significance, a theme returned to below. Violence against the Church, including the burning of Conservative and clerical property in some places, was also part of the Liberal radical response in the early years.

The geography of the violence was uneven. It was most intense in the Andean departments where Liberal and Conservative populations were intermingled and municipal control was disputed: Boyacá, Santander and Norte de Santander, Tolima, Caldas, Antioquia, and the Cauca valley. The Llanos Orientales saw a distinct pattern of guerrilla organization. Some regions, including parts of the Caribbean coast and of Nariño, experienced much less. James Henderson's study of Tolima shows how coffee-estate conflict, local boss rivalries, and party identification intertwined, while Roldán's work shows that in Antioquia the Conservative departmental government used police and local allies to impose control. Control of roads, markets, and municipal seats mattered, since armed groups sought to dominate the points where goods, votes, and authority passed.

Why did party identity matter so much? Colombian families inherited Liberal or Conservative loyalty and expressed it in dress, church attendance, trading partners, and marriage. In a village divided between the two, the party that controlled the mayor and police could protect its supporters and harass opponents, so the cost of being on the wrong side could be high. But partisan identity did not explain all violence. Old family feuds, boundary quarrels, debts, and disputes over land or coffee harvests could be recast as partisan conflict, and killers could use party labels as justification. Sánchez and Meertens argue that studying violence requires attention to local social relations and material interests, and not only to the leadership of the parties.

The roles of national and regional actors also differed. Leaders in Bogotá, such as Ospina Pérez, Laureano Gómez, and the Liberal chiefs, issued statements, negotiated, and sometimes encouraged or tolerated armed action. Regional commanders and local bosses controlled the day-to-day conduct of violence and often had autonomy. This divergence would matter later, when peace agreements negotiated by national figures proved difficult to enforce in the districts.

Section 83.4

Rural Communities, Land Conflict, Displacement, and the Human Consequences of La Violencia

Rural society in the affected regions included smallholders, tenants and sharecroppers, day laborers, estate owners, settlers, and merchants. In coffee areas, small and medium producers often lived near large estates. Tenants on those estates worked plots in exchange for labor or a share of the crop, and in the 1930s and 1940s many had joined agrarian leagues. Settlers in frontier areas cleared forest and planted food crops and then confronted claims by large landowners with legal titles. Debt to local merchants and moneylenders was common. These tensions existed before partisan violence and gave it material stakes: a family driven off its plot by "Conservatives" or "Liberals" might be losing land that a neighbor, or an estate owner, had long wished to acquire.

The effects on households were severe. Families fled their farms to towns, to other rural districts, to Bogotá and other cities, and to frontier regions, often leaving crops and animals behind. Abandoned property was frequently occupied, sold at distress prices, or acquired by the persons who had forced the departure. In some places entire hamlets were burned. Fields went unharvested in coffee districts, which affected local markets and wages. Schools closed or lost teachers, and the sick found fewer clinics. Parents sent children to relatives. Return was difficult: even where political tension subsided, the land might be held by others, and old enmities remained.

Women, children, Indigenous people, Afro-Colombians, and noncombatant civilians experienced the conflict in distinct ways. Women were targets of sexual violence and of reprisals aimed at their families, and they also sustained households, managed flight, kept farms going, and sometimes acted as messengers or supporters of armed groups. Children were killed, orphaned, or displaced and lost schooling. Indigenous communities in the Andean departments, such as the Nasa (Páez) of Cauca and Tolima, faced pressure on resguardo land and, in some places, violence. Afro-Colombian communities in the Cauca valley and on the Pacific side lived in regions where partisan violence reached, though their experience is less documented. Most civilians belonged to neither armed group and sought to avoid both, sometimes by paying protection or moving. The sources describe these experiences unevenly: official reports and later compilations emphasize killings and armed leaders, while the testimony of women, children, and ethnic minorities is thinner and has to be reconstructed from oral histories, church records, and judicial files.

Some communities organized their own defense. These organizations might be armed bands, but they could also be village committees that guarded roads, shared information, or organized collective flight. In parts of Tolima and Cundinamarca, communities with a history of agrarian organizing, including Communist-influenced leagues, formed self-defense groups that combined protection with the defense of land claims. Their importance extended beyond the years of greatest violence. Later chapters of this story, including the origins of the FARC, grew out of such communities, though this does not mean that every self-defense group became a guerrilla organization.

The number of deaths is contested. For decades a figure of roughly 200,000, and in popular memory 300,000, was repeated, deriving from early studies and journalism, including the work of Guzmán Campos, Fals Borda, and Umaña Luna, and from later reconstructions such as Paul Oquist's. In 2019 Julio Romero-Prieto and Adolfo Meisel-Roca challenged these figures using census and vital-registration evidence. For 1949–1958, their estimated maximum was 57,737 and their more conservative estimate 39,142; for 1949–1966, their maximum was 113,032 and conservative estimate 80,498. These are method-dependent demographic reconstructions, not certified counts of political deaths. Rural underregistration and the classification of violent deaths remain important questions. This chapter therefore supplies attributed ranges rather than one definitive toll; any comparison must identify the dates, definitions, and method used.

The deeper consequence was a loss of confidence in ordinary institutions. Many rural people experienced the police as partisan, the courts as slow or captured, and the municipality as a source of danger. Some communities learned to rely on armed protection and kin networks. This legacy complicated all later efforts at pacification, because a government that asked communities to disarm was asking them to trust institutions that had failed them.

Section 83.5

Laureano Gómez, Constitutional Crisis, and the Escalation of Political Warfare

The coalition that Ospina Pérez formed after the Bogotazo did not last. Liberal ministers left the cabinet in 1949 as reports of persecution of Liberals accumulated, and the Liberal Party, which still held a majority in Congress, became the center of opposition. On 8 September 1949 a shooting inside the Chamber of Representatives killed a Liberal congressman and wounded others, an episode that shocked even hardened politicians. Ospina, invoking a state of siege, closed Congress on 9 November 1949 and ruled by decree. The presidential election, moved up to late November 1949, took place without Liberal participation: the party's candidate, Darío Echandía, withdrew, citing the lack of guarantees. Laureano Gómez, the leader of the party's most militant wing, was elected unopposed and took office on 7 August 1950.

Gómez was an intellectual, a fierce orator, and an admirer of Spanish Catholic corporatism. His government sought to reorganize the state on authoritarian and Catholic lines, and he proposed a constitutional reform with corporatist features. The Conservative Party itself was divided. Ospina Pérez and his followers, the ospinistas, were more willing to compromise with the Liberals, while the laureanistas and the smaller group led by Gilberto Alzate Avendaño followed different lines. These divisions would matter in 1953 and again in 1956–1957. Gómez became ill in 1951 and left the presidency in the hands of Roberto Urdaneta Arbeláez as acting president, though he remained a powerful figure and at times tried to direct policy.

Partisan repression expanded in these years. The police, with the support of the military in some regions, conducted operations against Liberal districts. Conservative and Liberal communities suffered massacres, expulsions, and property seizures. Press censorship was imposed under the state of siege. In September 1952, after Conservative-aligned mobs attacked the Liberal newspapers El Tiempo and El Espectador and the homes of leading Liberals in Bogotá, and after attacks on church and clerical property in a climate of reciprocal hostility, the conflict displayed how far the rhetoric of partisan war had spread. The Liberal elite was now directly threatened, and some Liberals reconsidered their position.

Armed Liberal resistance grew, and its most developed form appeared in the Llanos Orientales. There, from 1949, Liberal guerrillas organized in Casanare, Meta, Arauca, and neighboring areas. Commanders emerged, including Guadalupe Salcedo, Eliseo Velásquez, and, in other zones, figures such as Dumar Aljure. The Llanos guerrillas established a structure of commands and in 1952 adopted a set of regulations, often called the "Leyes del Llano," covering administration, justice, and discipline in guerrilla territory. The plains were thinly populated and had few roads, which favored irregular warfare, and cattle ranching provided a livelihood and a source of supplies. Similar bands operated in Tolima, Santander, and elsewhere.

By 1953, the state faced a strain on several fronts. The army was engaged in the plains and in Tolima, the Liberal leadership was alienated, and the Conservatives were divided. Some military officers and political leaders, including Liberals and ospinistas, began to consider the removal of Gómez. Gómez's attempt to resume full presidential authority in June 1953 provided the occasion. These circumstances preceded the military takeover and explain why it found support among civilians who feared the alternative.

Section 83.6

General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, the Coup of 13 June 1953, and the Promise of Pacification

On 13 June 1953, General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, commander of the armed forces, took power. The sequence is clear in outline. Gómez, who had been recovering, announced that he was resuming the presidency from Urdaneta Arbeláez and signaled that he would dismiss Rojas. Rojas, with the support of other officers, arrested Gómez, who had spent the previous period in a conflict with the military, and assumed the presidency. This is properly called a coup, since the military removed a constitutionally elected president, but it differed from later Latin American military takeovers in that a broad cross-section of civilian leaders initially accepted it, and Gómez's own party was split between his followers and those who welcomed his removal.

Why did elements of the political establishment accept it? Many Liberals saw it as an end to Conservative persecution. Ospinistas and others saw it as removal of a leader who had pushed the country toward catastrophe. The Church hierarchy, which had been close to Gómez, accepted the new government, and business leaders hoped for order. Rojas described his government as a "military movement" not a conventional dictatorship, and his slogan was "Peace, Justice, and Liberty." He promised an end to partisan violence, reconciliation between the parties, and modernization. His supporters presented him as a nonpartisan arbiter, and for a time he enjoyed genuine popularity.

The peace policy was the most important of his early initiatives. The government offered amnesty and pardon to guerrillas who surrendered, accompanied by promises of safe conduct and aid. Decrees of 1953 and 1954 formalized amnesty. The most visible demobilization took place in the eastern plains in the second half of 1953, when the Liberal guerrilla commanders, including Guadalupe Salcedo, negotiated with military emissaries and surrendered weapons in public ceremonies. According to the Truth Commission's account, these negotiations involved commanders of the Llanos guerrillas who sought guarantees and also political recognition, and the disarmament was incomplete in consequences. Thousands of fighters in the plains and other regions took part in some form of surrender, and violence fell for a time in some areas.

It is important to distinguish negotiated demobilization from lasting reintegration. Surrender ended organized armed resistance in some districts but did not necessarily address compensation for lost property, protection against reprisals, return of land, or political participation. Many former guerrillas returned to communities where local officials or rivals remained hostile. Promises of credit and land were fulfilled unevenly. Some former combatants were later arrested, killed, or driven back to arms. Guadalupe Salcedo himself was shot dead by police in Bogotá in June 1957, an episode that former guerrillas and others remembered as proof that guarantees could not be trusted, though the circumstances remain debated.

Why did some groups accept the proposals and others not? Llanos commanders, whose organization was strongly Liberal and who had seen the Conservative leadership removed, had reason to trust a government that had overthrown Gómez. Communist-aligned self-defense groups in Tolima and Sumapaz had a different political situation. Their organizations were tied to agrarian demands and to a party that the government viewed with suspicion, and they had reasons to keep defensive structures. Bandit groups with mixed motives made their own calculations. The result was a partial settlement: significant demobilization in some regions, and continued or renewed fighting in others.

Section 83.7

The Limits of Rojas Pinilla's Peace: Villarrica, Communist Self-Defense, and Renewed Military Violence

Rojas Pinilla's pacification did not reach the communist-influenced agrarian communities of southern Tolima, Sumapaz, and neighboring districts. These communities had roots in the agrarian leagues of the 1930s and 1940s. In Sumapaz, a high plateau south of Bogotá, tenant and settler organizing had a long history and was associated with leaders such as Juan de la Cruz Varela, who combined Liberal and Communist affiliations. During La Violencia, communities in this region organized self-defense against Conservative police and irregular groups, and some of their members joined or allied with Communist networks. Their aims were local: protection of families, defense of land claims, and continued agrarian organizing. They were not at this stage a national guerrilla army.

Relations between the military government and these communities deteriorated in 1954 and 1955. The government, increasingly anti-Communist in rhetoric and responsive to the Cold War climate, outlawed the Communist Party in 1954 and viewed armed rural communities with suspicion. In 1955 it launched a major military operation in eastern Tolima, around Villarrica, declaring the region a military zone. Troops, aircraft, and bombing were used, and thousands of civilians fled. This was a major episode of the period, and scholars regard it as a turning point because it showed that the military regime would use force against agrarian organizations that had not accepted its terms.

The consequences for rural settlements were large. Families fled from Villarrica and nearby districts toward other zones, including areas of southern Tolima, Huila, the Sumapaz region, and the frontier lands that would later be associated with Marquetalia, El Pato, and Guayabero. Some of these movements formed part of a wider pattern of agricultural colonization, in which displaced families cleared land in remote areas with weak state presence. There they re-created community institutions, and some maintained armed self-defense. The military pressure thus produced a geographic dispersal of communist-influenced campesino communities and reinforced their sense that the state was hostile. This is an important continuity between La Violencia and later insurgencies.

The regime meanwhile grew more repressive and less popular in the cities. The government restricted the press, closing El Tiempo in 1955 and suspending El Espectador repeatedly. In June 1954, army troops fired on student demonstrators in Bogotá, killing several, an episode that damaged the regime's reputation. In February 1956, spectators at a bullring who had jeered a government figure were attacked by security forces. At the same time Rojas Pinilla built a base of support through public works, a social assistance agency run by his daughter, the introduction of television in 1954, and appeals to workers and to a populist rhetoric of a union of the people and the armed forces. He also tried to create a third political force, a "Third Force" combining military and popular support, which alarmed both parties.

The emphasis for this chapter is on continuity. Rojas Pinilla's pacification ended organized Liberal guerrilla warfare in parts of the plains, yet the partial nature of the peace, together with renewed state offensives in Tolima, kept alive the networks, grievances, and armed structures from which later insurgencies would draw. The Villarrica operation is therefore best understood neither as a mere incident nor as the start of the FARC, but as a link in a chain connecting earlier agrarian organizing, the violence of the early 1950s, and the campesino communities of the 1960s.

Section 83.8

The Fall of Rojas Pinilla, the Benidorm and Sitges Agreements, and the Plebiscite of 1957

As Rojas Pinilla's government grew authoritarian, the two traditional parties, hitherto rivals, came to see him as a common threat. The decisive figures were Alberto Lleras Camargo, a Liberal and former president who had been the first secretary-general of the Organization of American States, and Laureano Gómez, the Conservative leader in exile in Spain. Their meeting at Benidorm, on the Spanish coast, in July 1956 produced a declaration in which the two leaders agreed to cooperate against the dictatorship and to work toward a return to civilian rule through a joint arrangement between the parties. Gómez's agreement was notable, since he had been the leading figure of Conservative militancy and a bitter adversary of the Liberals.

The Benidorm declaration was a statement of intent. By 1957 the opposition had broadened. Rojas Pinilla, whose original term was to end in 1954 but who had been confirmed by a National Constituent Assembly, sought to extend his rule to 1962. Students, Church figures, business groups, bankers, and political leaders joined in a civic strike in early May 1957. On 10 May 1957, Rojas Pinilla resigned and left the country, handing power to a military junta of five officers, led by General Gabriel París Gordillo. The junta was transitional and promised to return the government to civilian hands.

In July 1957 Lleras Camargo and Gómez met again, this time at Sitges, Spain, and articulated a more detailed political bargain. Their negotiations contemplated bipartisan parity and presidential alternation, supported by constitutional reforms and a popular plebiscite. These were political agreements, however, and the provisions later enacted did not all enter the constitution at once. The 1957 plebiscite established foundational parity arrangements; an additional constitutional act in September 1959 expressly fixed mandatory party alternation for the presidential terms from 1962 to 1974 and extended the parity framework. Distinguishing proposals, the public vote, and the later constitutional amendment is essential to understanding how the National Front was made.

The constitutional plebiscite took place on 1 December 1957, following Decreto Legislativo 0247 of 4 October 1957, as amended by Decreto Legislativo 0251 of 9 October. The indivisible package provided equal Liberal and Conservative representation in elected public corporations, provisions for shared executive appointments and public service, education commitments, and equal political rights for women. An overwhelming majority of participating voters approved it. The ballot did not itself enact the specific 1962–1974 mandatory presidential alternation rule: that rule was supplied by Acto Legislativo 1 of 15 September 1959. The distinction does not diminish the plebiscite's significance; it separates its actual legal content from the wider political settlement that it helped legitimate.

The plebiscite deserves particular attention for women's participation, and here two distinct developments must be kept apart. Women's national voting rights were recognized in a constitutional amendment (Acto Legislativo) of 1954, under Rojas Pinilla, but no national election was held in which they could vote. The plebiscite of 1 December 1957 was the first nationwide occasion on which Colombian women exercised those rights. Women's suffrage had been promoted by women's organizations and by Liberal and Conservative figures for years, and the 1954 amendment was adopted by a government whose own legitimacy was disputed. The plebiscite also wrote women's equal political rights into the constitutional text. Magdala Velásquez Toro and Lola Luna have shown how Colombian women's political participation grew through earlier decades of legal and social struggle and not as a gift of any one government.

Why did the two dominant parties regard power-sharing as a solution? Their leaders had experienced the cost of partisan retaliation: a transfer of power from one party to another had repeatedly brought local violence. By guaranteeing each party a share of offices and the presidency in turn, the arrangement removed the incentive to use the state to destroy the other side. It also protected elites from the military and from the populist challenge Rojas had represented. In this sense the National Front was a solution to a problem that elites defined in their own terms, and it reduced the conflict that most threatened them.

Section 83.9

The National Front, Presidential Alternation, and the Restoration of Civilian Government

The Frente Nacional began on 7 August 1958, when Alberto Lleras Camargo was inaugurated after winning the May 1958 election as a joint bipartisan candidate. Its constitutional framework was established in stages: the 1957 plebiscite instituted foundational bipartisan paridad, whereas Acto Legislativo 1 of 15 September 1959 expressly required party alternación in the presidency for the three terms beginning in 1962 and ending in 1974, and continued legislative parity. Parity divided representation in elected corporations and many appointments between Liberals and Conservatives; alternation meant that the presidency would pass between parties every four years. The 1958–1962 Lleras Camargo administration was the initial, separately agreed presidency, before the 1959 alternation rule took effect. The requirement of supermajorities for many legislative decisions strengthened each party's capacity to block the other.

The intended benefits were substantial and, in important respects, achieved. The arrangement removed the incentive for the party in power to use police and appointments to punish the other, which had driven local violence. Lleras Camargo's government created institutions to investigate the violence and began programs of rehabilitation in affected regions. The system allowed technocratic planning and encouraged the creation of development agencies, and it coincided with growth in industry, education, and public services. Jonathan Hartlyn's study of coalition rule emphasizes that the arrangement was a conscious elite design to prevent a return to partisan war, and that it largely succeeded in its principal aim.

Electoral competition continued, but within limits. Liberals and Conservatives competed with one another inside the framework, and the party whose turn it was to hold the presidency could present only candidates from that party. Voters could choose between factions within each party, and within-party rivalry became the real contest. Since the seats were fixed by parity, an election did not change the balance of power between parties. In the congressional elections of 1958 and later, voters chose the individuals within each party's allocation. Robert Dix and Hartlyn both note that the system turned competition between parties into competition within them, and that it strengthened regional bosses and factions.

The distinction between holding elections and permitting full competition among alternative parties is the key analytical point. Elections were regular, relatively open within the framework, and widely accepted as legitimate by the two parties. But groups outside the two parties could not win legislative seats as separate forces on equal terms, and could participate only by running under Liberal or Conservative labels. Presidents also governed under prolonged states of siege, which gave the executive extraordinary powers. This arrangement was neither a continuation of dictatorship nor a full democracy. It was a restricted, pacted regime that guaranteed civilian rule and party survival and constrained the representation of other voices.

Interpreting the National Front as simply fictitious democracy therefore misses its significance. It was a real political settlement with genuine achievements: the end of the destructive war between the parties, the restoration of civilian government, and a degree of institutional continuity unusual in Latin America at the time. Colombia avoided the military regimes that overtook Brazil, Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay in the 1960s and 1970s. Yet the same arrangement narrowed political participation, and these two features must be held together.

Historical Vocabulary
Paridad y alternación

Paridad (parity) and alternación (alternation) were the two principles of the National Front. Parity gave Liberals and Conservatives equal shares of public offices and legislative seats, and alternation reserved the presidency for each party in turn. Parity entered the constitutional framework in the 1957 plebiscite; mandatory alternation for the three presidential terms beginning in 1962 was expressly established by Acto Legislativo 1 of 1959.

The terms matter because they describe a form of consociational or pacted government in which political peace was purchased by guaranteeing each party a share of power. They should not be confused with proportional representation or with the rotation of parties through electoral victory, since the shares were fixed in advance and voters could not alter them. Using the words precisely helps students see why the system reduced partisan violence and also why it limited competition.

Section 83.10

Political Exclusion, the MRL, ANAPO, and Opposition to the Two-Party System

The National Front's rules restricted opportunities for movements outside the two traditional parties. Because legislative seats were divided between Liberals and Conservatives and offices were allocated by party, a third party had no guaranteed place. The practical effect was that dissent had to find expression within the party labels, in activity outside electoral channels, or in opposition from outside the system. Three kinds of response can be distinguished: dissident movements that ran under party labels, parties that participated in a limited way, and groups that rejected the system and turned to revolutionary struggle.

The Movimiento Revolucionario Liberal (MRL) was the first major dissident movement. Led by Alfonso López Michelsen, son of the reformist president of the 1930s, it emerged in 1959 and criticized the National Front for freezing politics and ignoring social reform. It drew support from younger Liberals, some on the left, and from regional constituencies. Since it ran its candidates under Liberal labels, it won seats within the Liberal allocation. It was a loyal opposition in the sense that it worked through electoral competition, and in 1967 López Michelsen reunited with the official Liberal Party and later became president (1974–1978).

The Alianza Nacional Popular (ANAPO) was a more durable movement. Founded in 1961 around Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, who had returned to political life after his trial before the Senate in 1959 stripped him of political rights (rights later restored), it combined populist appeals to workers and the poor with nostalgia for his government, and it grew in the late 1960s, with Conservative and Liberal dissidents joining. Ayala Diago's study of ANAPO's origins shows how it organized opposition to the National Front within the system. Under party labels it won seats, and in 1970 its candidate came close to winning the presidency, a matter discussed in Section 83.18.

Socialists, communists, labor organizations, student groups, and regional reform movements also took part in political life. The Colombian Communist Party was illegal in some periods and constrained in others, but it kept a presence in unions, in rural communities, and in electoral politics through allied formations. Labor federations such as the CTC and the Unión de Trabajadores de Colombia (UTC), the latter linked to the Church, operated within a restrictive legal framework in which strikes could be declared illegal. Student movements were active in the public universities and politicized by the Cuban Revolution and by university reform debates.

The effect of emergency powers and local elites also mattered. Governments used the state of siege for long periods, which allowed them to restrict public gatherings, try civilians in military courts in some circumstances, and issue decrees with force of law. At the local level, traditional party bosses retained control of municipal office and patronage, which limited the entry of new forces in many regions.

Two historical safeguards apply. It is false that all political opposition was prohibited, since the MRL and ANAPO competed, elections were held, and public debate was lively. It is also false that all groups excluded from the National Front's arrangements became guerrillas. Most dissatisfied Colombians channeled their views through voting, unions, student activism, and the dissident movements. The system therefore produced both stability and dissatisfaction, and the relationship between exclusion and armed struggle was indirect and varied. Historians generally argue that exclusion contributed to the climate in which some groups turned to arms, alongside land conflicts, earlier rural violence, ideological divisions, organizational decisions, and Cold War conditions, and not as a sole cause.

Section 83.11

Agrarian Reform, Law 135 of 1961, INCORA, and Organized Campesino Mobilization

The land question had been unresolved for decades. Colombian rural property was highly concentrated in many regions: large estates, some of them poorly used, occupied fertile valley lands, while smallholders and tenants cultivated hillsides and settlers pushed into the frontier. Tenancy and sharecropping remained widespread, particularly on coffee and cattle estates. At the frontier, colonists cleared land and frequently faced claims by estate owners or speculators with titles. Legislation from 1936 had not resolved these conflicts, and La Violencia had added a layer of displacement, abandoned property, and illegal acquisition.

Law 135 of 15 December 1961, enacted under Lleras Camargo, established a program of "social agrarian reform" and created the Instituto Colombiano de la Reforma Agraria (INCORA). The law's stated objectives included reducing excessive land concentration, giving land to farmers who lacked it, improving agricultural production, and addressing inefficient land use. INCORA was empowered to buy land, to expropriate land that was inadequately exploited with compensation in bonds, to distribute property, to allocate public lands, to provide credit and technical help, and to organize settlement projects. The reform drew on the climate of the Alliance for Progress, the U.S.-sponsored program launched in 1961, which encouraged land reform in Latin America as a response to Cuba, but Colombian reformers, many with their own earlier commitments, also had domestic motives, including the desire to stabilize rural areas after La Violencia.

The legal program and its results differed sharply. INCORA's expropriation procedures were slow and legally complex, and landowners were politically powerful in Congress, where under the National Front's party parity and fractional politics they were well represented. Administrators also lacked resources. Much of INCORA's activity involved titling public lands, supporting colonization, and small-scale purchase, and relatively little involved expropriation of productive private estates. Studies of the period, including those of Albert Berry and Leon Zamosc, document limited redistribution compared with the scale of landlessness. Much of the more productive land remained in large holdings, and the commercialization of agriculture in some regions displaced tenants.

The influence of landowners, administrators, campesino organizations, and rural workers shaped outcomes in different ways. Landowners' associations lobbied against expropriation. Government administrators varied in commitment. Peasant organizations demanded faster action. Under President Carlos Lleras Restrepo (1966–1970), the government took a more active line. Believing that reform needed a political base among the peasants, he issued decrees in 1967 to create an organization of rural users of state services. This initiative led, through organizing in 1967 and 1968, to the formation of the Asociación Nacional de Usuarios Campesinos (ANUC), which held its first national congress in 1970.

The ANUC grew rapidly and developed in ways the government had not planned. Leon Zamosc's study shows that the organization, initially encouraged by the state as a channel for rural users, became an assertive movement that organized land occupations and demanded redistribution. Under Pastrana, officials and rural elites negotiated the Pact of Chicoral on 9 January 1972, a political agreement to limit the radical reach of agrarian reform; later legislative measures gave some of its direction legal effect. The pact falls within this chapter's 1946–1974 period. The movement's subsequent internal divisions also reflected different positions toward state policy and independent mobilization.

The agrarian question therefore remained at the center of Colombian politics even after the National Front began. A law had been passed, an agency created, and a peasant movement mobilized, yet land concentration and conflict persisted, and rural grievances continued to supply a basis for organizing, protest, and in some regions armed groups.

Section 83.12

Urbanization, Economic Development, Social Change, and the Limits of Political Reconciliation

The National Front years were a period of rapid social change. Colombia's population grew quickly, and by the mid-1960s roughly half of Colombians lived in towns and cities, a sharp change from earlier decades. Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, and Barranquilla expanded, with new neighborhoods, many of them informal, rising on city edges. Industry grew, especially in textiles, food processing, and consumer goods in Medellín and Cali, and the state expanded services in education, health, and infrastructure. Roads and air transport reduced regional isolation, though mountain geography still limited connections.

Rural-to-urban migration had several causes. Some migrants moved because cities offered wages higher than those in agriculture, and some because rural families had been displaced by violence. Historians dispute how much of urban growth was caused by La Violencia, since migration had begun before 1948 and the demographic expansion was itself a powerful cause. What can be said is that for many families, the decision to leave was shaped by both insecurity and opportunity, and the sources do not always allow the two to be separated.

Labor organization and professional employment also changed. A growing urban working class and a larger professional and white-collar sector emerged. Public universities expanded, and student numbers grew, producing a politicized student population. Women's participation in the labor force, in universities, and in civic organizations increased, and women, having voted for the first time in 1957, participated in elections thereafter, though women in elected office remained few.

The unevenness of development was striking. Prosperous urban regions and coffee zones contrasted with peripheral areas where state services, roads, and effective political authority remained limited. Large parts of the Llanos, the Amazonian south, the Pacific coast, and frontier zones of the Andes had few schools, clinics, or courts. The government's capacity to implement policy was constrained by difficult geography, unequal administrative resources, and powerful local interests, and national policy was often negotiated with regional elites.

National reconciliation between the parties did not automatically end conflicts over land, labor, local authority, or ideology. Banditry, a legacy of La Violencia, persisted in the late 1950s and 1960s in several regions, with armed groups of mixed political and criminal character. Sánchez and Meertens describe how such bandit chiefs, some of whom had begun as partisan fighters, were eventually hunted down by the army and police. Labor conflicts continued, and rural protest grew. The National Front thus presided over genuine economic and institutional change while leaving significant sources of political and social conflict unresolved.

Section 83.13

Communist Campesino Self-Defense, Agricultural Colonization, and the Independent Republics Controversy

The communist-associated rural communities that later became the core of the FARC were products of a particular combination of circumstances: agrarian organizing before 1948, persecution during La Violencia, displacement after the Villarrica offensive, and settlement of remote frontier land. Their principal locations were southern Tolima and the Sumapaz highlands, and, further south and west, settlement zones such as Marquetalia (in southern Tolima, near the border with Huila and Cauca), Riochiquito (in Cauca), El Pato (in Caquetá and Huila), and Guayabero (in the Meta frontier). These places were mountainous or forested, with few roads, and state presence was mostly limited to occasional police or army patrols.

The relationships among persecution, land, and organization were close. Families came to these areas because they had been driven from other regions, or sought land of their own, or both. Communist Party organizers, and local leaders aligned with the party, helped communities structure settlement, allocate plots, build trails and schools, and maintain security. Defense was part of daily life, since settlers feared attack by police, irregular Conservative groups, bandits, or the army. These were communities of farmers who also bore arms, and participants described themselves as self-defense groups (autodefensas campesinas) rather than guerrillas. Eduardo Pizarro's study of the origins of the FARC, titled De la autodefensa a la combinación de todas las formas de lucha, traces precisely this transition.

Two figures are central. Manuel Marulanda Vélez was the nom de guerre of Pedro Antonio Marín, born in 1930 in the Quindío coffee region, who had been a Liberal guerrilla fighter in La Violencia and became a leader of the Marquetalia community; he was later known by the nickname "Tirofijo." Jacobo Arenas, whose real name was Luis Alberto Morantes, was a Communist Party organizer who worked with the campesino groups and became the movement's principal political ideologue. Their histories and roles differed. Marulanda was a peasant commander with local authority and military experience, while Arenas was a party cadre with political training, and their partnership shaped the organization's blend of rural self-defense and party strategy. The Communist Party itself, in the mid-1960s, formally endorsed the idea of the "combination of all forms of struggle," meaning electoral, legal, and armed activity together, though its leaders varied in how they understood the formula.

The communities developed local arrangements for security, farming, and political organization. They held assemblies, established rules for land use, organized cooperative labor, and set up armed patrols. Their numbers are uncertain, since the sources, mostly government reports, Communist publications, and later memoirs, offer different estimates, and the populations varied as people moved in and out. In government and press discourse, these zones were increasingly portrayed as a threat.

The phrase "independent republics" was popularized by critics. In October 1961, the Conservative leader Álvaro Gómez Hurtado, son of Laureano Gómez, gave a speech in the Senate denouncing the existence of areas where, he said, the state's authority was not exercised and communist-led armed communities held sway. The label stuck and was used by army officers and politicians to justify military action. It was a political characterization, not a legal description. These communities did not claim to be sovereign states, did not issue currency or conduct foreign policy, and many of their residents considered themselves Colombian citizens seeking land and security. At the same time, the label pointed to a real condition: in these zones, local organizations exercised authority that the state did not.

Disputes over sovereignty, armed self-defense, and the state's perceived need to reestablish control framed the conflict. For the government and the army, the existence of armed groups outside state control was unacceptable, particularly in the Cold War context after the Cuban Revolution. For the communities, abandonment by the state and its earlier hostility were reasons to maintain their defenses. The continuity between earlier rural conflict and the later formation of communist guerrilla organizations is evident here, but it was not inevitable, and many residents of these zones were neither Communists nor guerrillas.

Historical Vocabulary
"Repúblicas independientes" (Independent Republics)

The term independent republics was used by critics, especially after 1961, to describe rural zones in which communist-aligned campesino communities maintained armed self-defense and local government outside regular state control. It was a polemical label used in Congress, the press, and the military to argue that the state needed to reassert authority.

Its meaning is political. It should not be taken as proof that these communities proclaimed or sought legal independence. Historians use it to analyze how the zones were portrayed and how that portrayal justified military action, while describing the communities by their own terms, such as self-defense zones or colonization settlements. The phrase also illustrates how Cold War language could turn a local agrarian conflict into a question of national security.

Section 83.14

Operation Soberanía, Marquetalia in 1964, and the Formation of the FARC in 1966

In 1964, under President Guillermo León Valencia, the Colombian army launched a campaign against the Marquetalia enclave, commonly called Operation Soberanía ("Sovereignty"), often discussed together with the broader counterinsurgency planning of the early 1960s. The government's objective was to reestablish state control over areas it regarded as "independent republics." The Defense Ministry under General Alberto Ruiz Novoa had developed civic-military approaches earlier, but the Marquetalia operation was predominantly military. Officials interpreted the armed communities as communist enclaves linked to the Cuban example, while the communities understood the campaign as an attack on settlements that had sought only land and peace.

The scale of the operation and the numbers involved are disputed. Government and insurgent narratives give very different figures, and the often-repeated contrast between a very large army force and a few dozen guerrillas comes largely from insurgent and journalistic tradition. The sources agree that a significant military force, supported by aircraft, advanced on the area in May and June 1964, and that a small armed group under Marulanda and others withdrew and avoided destruction. The area was occupied by the army. Studies of the operation emphasize that the military action did not destroy the armed group and that its political results ran against the government's aims.

Civilians experienced the campaign through displacement and fear. Families fled their homes, some to other settlement zones, some to towns, and some followed the armed groups. Houses and crops were damaged. The army's occupation brought control over the immediate area, but people and armed men dispersed to other parts of the region, which spread rather than eliminated the communities' structures. The consequences were organizational. The armed core had to reorganize, to move, and to rely on networks of supporters in several zones.

Three stages should be distinguished. First, the Marquetalia operation of May–June 1964 and the withdrawal and reorganization of armed campesino groups. Second, the 20 July 1964 agrarian program articulated their land and political demands; organizational meetings of the emerging Bloque Sur followed. Accounts differ over the dating and description of its first coordinating conference: some situate a first conference in late 1964 and others in 1965. The date of the agrarian program must not be treated as a fixed date for that conference. Third, the Second Guerrilla Conference of April–May 1966 formally adopted the name Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia (FARC). The later designation FARC-EP (Ejército del Pueblo) belongs to the 1980s and must not be used for the original organization.

The shift from localized self-defense to a more organized guerrilla movement was gradual and contested. The movement adopted a structure of fronts, a political program, and links with the Communist Party. It remained small for years, based in rural zones, and relied on campesino support and on a strategy that Pizarro describes as defensive rather than offensive in the 1960s. The explanation of the FARC's formation therefore lies in a longer process rather than in an immediate consequence of the Cuban Revolution of 1959. Its roots lay in earlier agrarian organizing, La Violencia, displacement, and the state's refusal or failure to incorporate these communities, together with Communist Party strategy and Cold War conditions. Cuba's example, as well as Soviet-aligned and Chinese-aligned currents, formed part of the ideological environment, but the FARC's core was Colombian.

Section 83.15

The ELN, the Simacota Manifesto, Camilo Torres, and Revolutionary Nationalism

The Ejército de Liberación Nacional (ELN) had a different origin from the FARC. It was formed in 1964 in the department of Santander, in the Magdalena Medio region around San Vicente de Chucurí and Barrancabermeja, an area with an oil-workers' union tradition, radical Liberalism, a history of agrarian protest, and a student population in the nearby university city of Bucaramanga. Its founders included Fabio Vásquez Castaño, who became its principal commander, and young activists, many from the Liberal tradition or from student and youth organizations, several of whom had been to Cuba in the early 1960s. The Cuban Revolution was a direct influence, in contrast to the case of the FARC, and the ELN drew on the idea that a small armed group could catalyze revolution, a theory discussed in Chapter 82.

The ELN's social composition differed from the communist self-defense networks. It combined university students and intellectuals, workers from the oil sector, and campesinos from Santander. Its political outlook was revolutionary nationalism, influenced by Cuban ideas, anti-imperialism, and a critique of the National Front. It was not tied to the Communist Party and was in some tension with it. The first armed group is said to have taken a name honoring José Antonio Galán, the leader of the Comunero revolt of 1781, which signals the organization's effort to place itself in a Colombian tradition of popular rebellion.

The ELN made its public appearance on 7 January 1965, when a group of its fighters seized the small town of Simacota in Santander, occupied the town hall, and read and distributed a manifesto, the Manifiesto de Simacota. The action was small in military terms but significant as a declaration. The manifesto, which Alejo Vargas Velázquez and other Colombian scholars have studied, presented the ELN as a movement of liberation fighting on behalf of the Colombian people against the oligarchy, imperialist domination, and a political system dominated by the two parties. It spoke of land, national sovereignty, social inequality, and the need for a revolutionary change, and addressed peasants, workers, students, professionals, and others.

Camilo Torres Restrepo became the ELN's most famous figure. Born in 1929 into a Bogotá professional family, he studied for the priesthood, was ordained in 1954, and studied sociology at the Catholic University of Louvain in Belgium. Returning to Colombia, he helped found, with Orlando Fals Borda, the sociology program at the National University and worked on studies of rural and urban poverty. He became a chaplain and a critic of social inequality. By 1964–1965 he advocated popular political unity as the way to change, and in 1965 he launched the Frente Unido del Pueblo Colombiano (United Front), an effort to unite diverse groups around a platform of social reform. He requested laicization (release from priestly duties) in 1965, saying, in the accounts of those who knew him, that he could not best serve the poor through his priestly functions in the circumstances.

Torres's path to the ELN was gradual. As the Frente Unido faced obstacles, including government harassment and divisions among its supporters, he concluded that electoral and organizational avenues were closed. In late 1965 he joined the ELN in Santander, and on 15 February 1966 he was killed in his first combat, an ambush against an army patrol. His death turned him into a symbol across Latin America, reproduced on posters and discussed by Christians and Marxists alike. Walter Broderick's biography and the posthumous collection Cristianismo y revolución are sources for his thought.

A historical safeguard is needed: Torres's thought should not be reduced to a claim that Christianity necessarily required armed revolution. He distinguished his social criticism, which drew on sociology and Catholic social teaching, from his political organizing through the Frente Unido, from his religious convictions, and finally from his decision to join the insurgency, which he presented as a last resort. Many Catholics, including other priests who shared his criticism of inequality, did not follow him into armed struggle, and the Church hierarchy rejected his choice. His death in 1966 made him a martyr to supporters and a cautionary figure to critics.

Section 83.16

The EPL, Communist Divisions, and Competing Colombian Revolutionary Strategies

The Ejército Popular de Liberación (EPL) was created in 1967 and had roots in a split within the Colombian communist movement. The wider Sino-Soviet split of the early 1960s divided communist parties worldwide between those aligned with Moscow and those drawn to Beijing's critique of Soviet "revisionism" and its emphasis on peasant-based revolutionary war. In Colombia, a pro-Chinese faction broke from the Communist Party and in 1965 founded the Partido Comunista de Colombia (Marxista-Leninista), usually abbreviated PC-ML. The party's armed wing, the EPL, was announced in December 1967. The Truth Commission confirms the EPL's 1967 establishment and its distinct Maoist orientation.

The EPL's early development took place in the northwest, in southern Córdoba, in the Alto Sinú and Alto San Jorge regions, areas of recent settlement, cattle estates, and peasant colonization, where land disputes were sharp. The party's strategy, inspired by Maoist ideas of protracted people's war, called for establishing rural bases and organizing peasants over a long period rather than seeking rapid military results. The relationship between communist politics and local agrarian conflict shaped its growth, as it recruited among settlers and tenants. The organization suffered setbacks, including the deaths of early leaders, and remained small in the 1960s and early 1970s.

The three organizations can be compared along several dimensions. The FARC originated in campesino self-defense communities shaped by La Violencia and linked to the pro-Soviet Colombian Communist Party, and its early strategy was defensive. The ELN originated in revolutionary nationalism, student activism, and Cuban inspiration, with a mixed social base and a vanguardist idea of armed struggle, outside the Communist Party. The EPL originated in the pro-Chinese split, with a Maoist ideology emphasizing rural people's war. These differences affected recruitment, strategy, relations with civilians, and relations with one another. The organizations sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed, and they rarely achieved the unity that Cuban-influenced theories of continental revolution assumed.

Section 83.17

State Security, Counterinsurgency, U.S. Assistance, and the Expansion of Military Authority

Colombian governments responded to continuing insurgency with a mix of military operations, intelligence work, police action, emergency powers, and development programs. The army, which had been engaged in partisan conflict and in the Rojas Pinilla period, was reoriented in the 1960s toward internal security. Intelligence services and the police expanded, and the executive relied on the state of siege to issue security decrees. The campaigns of the early 1960s against bandits and against armed rural enclaves were presented as restoring state authority, and they were the first stages of a long counterinsurgent tradition.

The Cold War context shaped these responses. After the Cuban Revolution, the United States expanded military assistance and counterinsurgency advice in Latin America. A special U.S. Army survey mission led by General William P. Yarborough visited Colombia in early 1962; later American advisers assisted Colombian officers in planning a comprehensive program commonly called Plan Lazo. Some subsequent accounts use Plan LASO or LASSO, sometimes expanding the label as “Latin American Security Operation”; its status as an original official acronym is disputed, and this chapter does not present that expansion as an established contemporary designation. The program included military coordination, intelligence, civic action, and rural development measures. Colombian officers and presidents exercised agency over its implementation, while U.S. training and assistance influenced doctrine and resources.

Counterinsurgency combined military and civic components. Colombian officers developed civic-military action programs in which soldiers built roads, schools, and clinics in rural areas, aiming to win support and extend the government's presence. General Alberto Ruiz Novoa, defense minister from 1962 to 1965, promoted this approach, though he was later dismissed after disputes with the president and conservative officers. In practice, the military dimension predominated in operations like Marquetalia, and development efforts reached only limited areas.

The difficulty of distinguishing armed combatants from civilians was central. In rural zones where campesinos kept weapons for hunting and self-defense, where organizations combined agricultural cooperation with political affiliation, and where people had family ties to armed men, officers often treated entire communities as suspect. Civilians suspected of collaboration faced detention, harassment, displacement, or violence. At the same time, insurgents also committed abuses, including killing alleged informers and coercing local populations. Documented state violence and detention practices of this period were smaller in scale than those of later decades, but they shaped local memories and trust in the state.

Emergency legislation also expanded military authority. Decree 3398 of 1965, later made permanent through Law 48 of 1968, created a national-defense framework. Article 25 provided that civilians not called for compulsory military service could be assigned activities in support of restoring normality; paragraph 3 of Article 33 allowed authorization of certain weapons otherwise reserved for military forces under defined administrative arrangements. Historians treat these provisions as part of the legal background of subsequent civilian participation in security operations. But the decree must not be represented as itself creating the much later paramilitary system of the 1980s and 1990s.

A final safeguard concerns agency. Colombian security policy was not designed wholly in Washington. Colombian presidents, defense ministers, army officers, landowners, party bosses, and regional authorities had their own motives, which included anti-communism, the defense of property, institutional ambitions, and genuine concern for order. U.S. assistance influenced doctrine and resources, but Colombian actors decided how to use them and bore responsibility for their choices.

Section 83.18

The Election of 19 April 1970, ANAPO, and the Origins of the M-19

The presidential election of 19 April 1970 was the last held under the alternation rule. It was the Conservatives' turn, and the official National Front candidate was Misael Pastrana Borrero, a Conservative who had served in Carlos Lleras Restrepo's government. His main opponent was Gustavo Rojas Pinilla, now seventy years old, who ran under the banner of ANAPO and had drawn support from workers, the urban poor, and dissidents of both parties, even though the rules in principle reserved the presidency for a Conservative candidate. Rojas ran as a Conservative dissident, and in the eyes of his supporters his campaign was a popular challenge to the two-party arrangement. Other candidates received fewer votes.

The vote count produced the controversy. As results came in on election night, early returns reportedly favored Rojas in major cities, and the government interrupted the broadcast of returns. A curfew was imposed in several cities. The official final tally, reproduced in historical electoral records, awarded 1,625,025 votes to Pastrana and 1,561,468 to Rojas Pinilla, a difference of 63,557. Rojas and ANAPO's leaders denounced the count as fraudulent, and protests followed. The official figures establish what the electoral authorities certified; they do not alone prove or disprove the separate allegations of manipulation.

Students should distinguish carefully among several kinds of evidence. The official electoral results establish the declared winner and margin. Contemporary allegations by ANAPO and its supporters claimed manipulation of the count, the interruption of the flow of returns, and irregularities in the scrutiny. Documented irregularities, such as the suspension of returns and the delays, are part of the record, but the existence of serious accusations does not by itself establish that every specific claim of fraud was proven. Later political interpretations vary: some accounts treat the election as stolen, others emphasize irregularities without concluding that the outcome was changed, and defenders of the result point to the lack of definitive proof. The evidence, including electoral records and press coverage, has been examined by historians, and the question remains debated.

Whatever the truth about the count, the controversy deepened dissatisfaction with the political settlement. For ANAPO's supporters, many of them poor and excluded from the benefits of National Front stability, the election appeared to confirm that the system would not permit a challenge to the two parties. The perception of a stolen election mattered politically, whether or not each allegation could be proved.

That perception led to the emergence of the Movimiento 19 de Abril (M-19), named after the date of the 1970 election. The M-19 emerged publicly in January 1974 with a symbolic action, the removal of Simón Bolívar's sword from a museum in Bogotá, and its leaders included Jaime Bateman Cayón, who had earlier belonged to the FARC and to the Communist Youth, and others who had been active in ANAPO. The Banco de la República identifies the 1970 controversy and ANAPO opposition as central to its emergence. The M-19 was distinctive. Its emphasis fell on political legitimacy, nationalism, and urban action, with a populist style and an appeal to ANAPO's followers, in contrast to the rural bases and rigid ideologies of the FARC, ELN, and EPL. It shows that disputes over electoral legitimacy could produce a different form of insurgent politics. Its later history belongs to subsequent chapters.

Section 83.19

Women, Displaced Families, Indigenous Communities, and Everyday Life amid Conflict

The social history of the period begins with families displaced by La Violencia, whose experience did not end in 1958. Many remained in cities or new settlement zones, and some never recovered their land. In rural districts, insecurity continued in the National Front years through banditry, army operations, and local disputes. Households had to adapt, relying on kin networks, informal work, and, in towns, neighborhood solidarity. Displacement produced new urban neighborhoods and colonization settlements whose residents carried memories of loss and sometimes distrust of authorities.

Women's experience was varied. In rural areas they worked in agriculture, processed food, raised children, and managed households that often lost male members to death, flight, or armed service. They were also organizers in communities, participated in cooperatives and committees, and in some cases supported or joined armed groups. In towns and cities, many worked as domestic servants, in textile factories, in small commerce, and increasingly in clerical and professional jobs. Newly enfranchised after 1957, women voted in elections, and political parties courted them, though few reached high office. Lola Luna and Magdala Velásquez Toro show that women's civic participation also took the form of charitable organizations, unions, and associations that predated and continued beyond the vote.

Conflict affected children and services. Rural schools closed or lost teachers during periods of violence, children dropped out or worked, and health services were scarce in conflict zones. Families lost property, documents, and animals. The long-term effects included lower schooling and the loss of rural land, which reduced economic opportunities.

Regional differences affected Indigenous and Afro-Colombian populations. In Cauca and Tolima, Nasa and other Indigenous communities struggled to protect resguardo lands against encroachment by landowners and, in some places, colonists. The Consejo Regional Indígena del Cauca (CRIC) was founded on 24 February 1971 in Toribío, providing a regional organizational voice for land and cultural rights within the chapter's period. Afro-Colombian communities on the Pacific coast and in the Cauca valley faced unequal access to political institutions, land titles, and services. Their experiences of conflict, often distinct from the main partisan fighting, remain less documented in the conventional political record; neither group should be represented only as a passive victim of outsiders' decisions.

Many communities sought to stay outside armed movements while negotiating with state officials, landowners, guerrillas, and local authorities. They paid contributions, provided information selectively, and moved when necessary. Such strategies of survival reveal the agency of ordinary people and the difficulty of classifying them as supporters of one side or another.

Historians recover these experiences from sources that differ from conventional political histories: oral histories collected by scholars such as Alfredo Molano, court documents, petitions and letters to authorities, official investigations, parish and notarial records, and personal recollections. Each has limits. Oral histories reflect later memory, official investigations reflect state priorities, and petitions follow conventions that shaped what could be said. Together they help correct a history told only through presidents, generals, and insurgent organizations.

Section 83.20

From La Violencia to Prolonged Guerrilla War: The Limits of the National Front Settlement

The National Front's formal presidential alternation ended in 1974. In that year, after sixteen years of alternating Liberal and Conservative administrations, Colombia held a presidential election open to broader competition, which was won by the Liberal Alfonso López Michelsen, the former MRL leader. The settlement had achieved what its architects most wanted. The destructive warfare between the traditional parties had largely ended, civilian institutions were restored, and the presidency had been stabilized through four complete terms, an achievement not matched in several neighboring countries.

Against these achievements stood persistent problems. Rural land disputes were unresolved, since Law 135 and INCORA had produced limited redistribution and the political momentum for reform had faded by 1972. Political participation remained unequal, with the two parties holding a protected position and dissident movements having to work within or outside the system. State institutions were weak in many local areas, where effective authority rested with local elites, armed groups, or no one. And armed revolutionary organizations existed, though still small in 1974.

The earlier Liberal–Conservative conflict must be distinguished from the revolutionary insurgencies of the 1960s. The first was a partisan struggle in which both sides belonged to the established parties and fought over local control; the second involved organizations that rejected the political system, adopted revolutionary programs, and drew on different ideologies. The two were connected, since some insurgent leaders and communities had passed through La Violencia, but they were not the same conflict, and the relationship was one of historical continuity with transformation, not identity.

The FARC, ELN, EPL, and M-19 differed in origin, goals, social relationships, and organization. The FARC grew from campesino self-defense and the Communist Party and sought land and political change, with a defensive strategy at first. The ELN combined students, workers, and peasants in a Cuban-inspired nationalism. The EPL followed Maoist doctrine in rural Córdoba. The M-19 grew from the electoral dispute of 1970 and emphasized political legitimacy. Their differences shaped their later histories and their difficulty in uniting.

Several factors affected these developments. Geography, with its mountains, forests, and distant frontiers, gave armed groups refuge and left the state with limited reach. Local political authority and patronage structures shaped who held power in the districts. Rural settlement patterns, especially colonization of frontier areas, created communities outside effective state control. State security policy, with its mix of force and development, sometimes intensified conflict. International ideological influences, including the Cuban Revolution, the Sino-Soviet split, and the Cold War, provided models, resources, and justifications, but they operated on Colombian conditions and did not create them.

The chapter's chronology stops in 1974, and subsequent history lies beyond it. The expansion of guerrilla warfare, the cocaine economy and its financing of armed groups, the rise of paramilitary violence, and later peace efforts, including the accord of 2016, are discussed in Chapters 97 through 100. They should be understood as consequences that developed from these origins and from later changes, and not as outcomes that were predetermined by 1974.

The closing question is how a political agreement designed to end partisan warfare could restore national stability while leaving important conditions for future armed conflict unresolved. The answer lies in the settlement's design. It addressed the conflict that most threatened political elites and left in place the land structures, regional inequalities, and exclusions that had always been separate from party rivalry. To understand the later conflict one has to see its distinct earlier stages: party warfare, rural self-defense, agrarian reform, political reconciliation, and ideological radicalization.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did the National Front Resolve Colombia's Political Crisis or Transform It into a Different Kind of Conflict?

The question: Did the National Front end the crisis that produced La Violencia, or did it convert a partisan conflict into a conflict between the political system and those excluded from or unaffected by it?

Interpretation A — A successful, if limited, resolution: Scholars such as Jonathan Hartlyn emphasize that the arrangement achieved its central aim. It ended interparty warfare, avoided prolonged military rule, and produced institutional stability. On this view, later insurgencies had additional causes, including rural inequality, ideological currents, and organizational choices, and cannot be blamed on the settlement.
Interpretation B — A transformation of the conflict: Other scholars, and the Truth Commission's emphasis on continuities, stress that the settlement left rural grievances unaddressed and narrowed political competition, so that conflict moved from partisan violence to insurgency and counterinsurgency. On this view, reconciliation among elites coexisted with the exclusion of rural and popular groups.

Evidence: Both views draw on electoral records, institutional studies, the history of Law 135 and its results, accounts of campesino communities, and guerrilla documents. Interpretation A relies on comparisons with other Latin American countries and on declines in interparty violence. Interpretation B relies on continuities of people, places, and grievances from the 1950s to the 1960s. The interpretations overlap, since a settlement can end one conflict and leave others untouched.

Why it matters: The question affects how Colombians and outsiders understand the origins of the later war and what lessons they draw about power-sharing, reform, and inclusion.

Student task: Write a short argument for each view, then identify what evidence, such as regional comparisons of violence before and after 1958, would best test them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Colombia's mid-century violence, its negotiated political settlement, and its guerrilla movements were connected but distinct developments. It began with the killing of Jorge Eliécer Gaitán on 9 April 1948, and then showed that the crisis had deeper roots in party patronage, local control of offices and police, and rural land conflict, and that neither La Violencia nor the later insurgencies were inevitable.

The Liberal Republic of 1930–1946 brought reform and reaction. Its programs, including the constitutional changes and Law 200 of 1936, raised expectations and provoked resistance, and left unresolved conflicts over land. The election of 1946, in which a divided Liberal Party lost to the Conservative Mariano Ospina Pérez, shifted control of local offices and police, and in a system where such control meant safety, it intensified conflict in towns and rural districts.

Gaitán, a populist Liberal who spoke of the excluded "national country," led the March of Silence in February 1948 and was assassinated in April. The Bogotazo that followed was a major but brief urban uprising, and the identity and motives of the killers remain unproven. The longer conflict took place mostly in the countryside, where police, irregular partisan groups, Liberal guerrillas, and private armed men acted, and where local feuds and land disputes became entangled with party labels. Its intensity varied sharply by region.

The human consequences were severe: displacement, abandoned property, destroyed hamlets, interrupted schooling, and loss of confidence in police, courts, and municipalities. Estimates of deaths range widely. The traditional figure of around 200,000, or in popular memory 300,000, has been challenged by a demographic study of the Banco de la República that estimates a maximum of about 57,700 violent deaths for 1949–1958 and about 113,000 for 1949–1966. No single figure is uncontested.

Political breakdown followed: the closing of Congress in 1949, Liberal abstention, the presidency of Laureano Gómez, and Liberal guerrilla organization in the Llanos. The coup of 13 June 1953 brought General Gustavo Rojas Pinilla to power with the acceptance of many civilians. His amnesty and the demobilization of Llanos guerrillas reduced violence in some regions but did not secure lasting reintegration, and his government's military offensive at Villarrica in 1955 and its growing authoritarianism showed the limits of his peace.

Liberal and Conservative leaders, especially Alberto Lleras Camargo and Laureano Gómez, agreed at Benidorm in 1956 and Sitges in 1957 to share power. After Rojas resigned on 10 May 1957, a plebiscite on 1 December 1957 approved foundational bipartisan parity; mandatory alternation of the presidency for 1962–1974 was enacted separately in 1959. Women's suffrage, recognized in 1954, was first exercised nationally in that plebiscite. The National Front that began in 1958 reduced interparty warfare and restored civilian government, yet limited competition by restricting political space for forces outside the two parties, though dissident movements such as the MRL and ANAPO competed within it.

Land remained central. Law 135 of 1961 created INCORA and declared social agrarian reform, but implementation was limited, and the ANUC, created with state encouragement from 1967 and holding its first congress in 1970, developed its own demands before the reform was reversed in the early 1970s. Meanwhile Colombia urbanized and developed economically, while peripheral regions remained thinly governed.

The guerrilla organizations emerged from different histories. The FARC grew from communist-influenced campesino self-defense communities shaped by La Violencia and displaced by the Villarrica offensive; the 1964 Marquetalia operation, the Southern Bloc, and the formal naming of the FARC in 1966 were stages in a longer process. The ELN, formed in 1964 and public at Simacota in January 1965, drew on revolutionary nationalism and Cuban influence, and Camilo Torres, who died in combat on 15 February 1966, became its most famous figure. The EPL, formed in 1967, came from a Maoist split in the communist movement.

Counterinsurgency combined force, civic action, emergency powers, and U.S. assistance, and Colombian officials had their own motives and responsibilities. The disputed election of 19 April 1970 deepened dissatisfaction with the settlement and, with ANAPO's followers, led to the M-19, which emerged publicly in 1974 with an emphasis on political legitimacy that distinguished it from the other organizations.

The chapter's central conclusion is that the National Front succeeded in the task its architects set, ending warfare between the traditional parties, while leaving unresolved the land conflicts, regional inequalities, and exclusions that fed other forms of violence. Later developments, including the expansion of guerrilla war, narcotics financing, paramilitary violence, and the 2016 peace accord, belong to subsequent chapters and should not be read back into 1974.

Key Terms

La Violencia
Mid-century wave of partisan and rural violence in Colombia, with debated dates and uneven regional character.
Liberal Republic
The Liberal governments of 1930–1946, associated with reforms and elite resistance.
Revolución en Marcha
López Pumarejo's reform program of the 1930s.
Gaitanismo
Populist movement around Jorge Eliécer Gaitán, appealing to the excluded "national country."
Bogotazo
Urban uprising in Bogotá following Gaitán's assassination on 9 April 1948.
Oración por la Paz
Gaitán's speech at the March of Silence, 7 February 1948.
Chulavitas
Popular term for Conservative-aligned police recruits, tied to Boyacá; used with partisan connotations.
Pájaros
Conservative-aligned irregular killers, especially in the Cauca valley.
Liberal guerrillas
Armed Liberal groups, notably in the Llanos Orientales, formed for resistance and defense.
Estado de sitio
Constitutional state of siege expanding executive power, used heavily in this period.
Rojas Pinilla coup
The military takeover of 13 June 1953 that removed Laureano Gómez.
Amnesty and demobilization
1953–1954 offers of pardon and surrender, which ended some armed resistance without ensuring reintegration.
Villarrica offensive
1955 military operation in eastern Tolima that displaced agrarian communities.
Benidorm and Sitges
1956 and 1957 agreements between Lleras Camargo and Gómez to create a joint civilian settlement.
Plebiscite of 1957
National vote of 1 December 1957 that enacted bipartisan parity and other reforms; mandatory presidential alternation was set by a separate 1959 amendment.
Frente Nacional
1958–1974 arrangement of parity and presidential alternation between Liberals and Conservatives.
Paridad y alternación
Party parity established by the 1957 plebiscite; compulsory alternating presidencies for 1962–1974 formalized by Acto Legislativo 1 de 1959.
MRL
Movimiento Revolucionario Liberal, a Liberal dissident movement led by López Michelsen.
ANAPO
Alianza Nacional Popular, the populist opposition movement of Rojas Pinilla.
Law 135 of 1961
Agrarian reform statute that created INCORA.
INCORA
Colombian Institute of Agrarian Reform, with powers to buy, expropriate, and distribute land.
ANUC
National Association of Peasant Users, organized from 1967 with state encouragement, first congress 1970.
Autodefensa campesina
Organized armed or collective rural self-defense.
"Independent republics"
Critics' label, popularized in 1961, for self-defense zones; a political characterization, not a legal fact.
Marquetalia
Settlement zone in southern Tolima, target of the 1964 operation.
Bloque Sur
Southern Bloc, the 1964 coordination of armed campesino groups preceding the FARC's formal naming.
FARC
Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia, named at the Second Guerrilla Conference in 1966; "FARC-EP" is a later designation.
ELN
Ejército de Liberación Nacional, founded 1964, public at Simacota in 1965.
EPL
Ejército Popular de Liberación, armed wing of the Maoist PC-ML, formed 1967.
M-19
Movimiento 19 de Abril, named for the 1970 election date and emerging publicly in 1974.
Plan Lazo (also called LASO/LASSO)
Colombian counterinsurgency and civic-action planning developed with U.S. advice after a 1962 survey mission. “LASO/LASSO” occurs in some later accounts; its supposed acronym expansion is contested.
Counterinsurgency
Combined military, political, and development measures against insurgents.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an apparent success can coexist with the conditions of a later crisis. The National Front ended a ruinous contest between two parties, and Colombians avoided the military regimes that dominated much of South America in the 1960s and 1970s. Yet the same settlement left the countryside, and those who felt shut out of national politics, largely unaddressed.

The chapter also qualifies a familiar story. Part XVIII shows how the Cuban Revolution challenged Latin America, and Colombia's ELN fits that story. But the FARC, EPL, and M-19 reveal other sources: agrarian self-defense, communist divisions, and disputed elections. Latin American insurgency had domestic roots and varied forms, and Chapter 82's continental theories met local histories here.

Several continuities reach beyond 1974: the thinness of state authority in peripheral regions, the unresolved agrarian question, and the memory of broken guarantees among rural communities. Later chapters on cocaine, paramilitaries, Plan Colombia, and the peace accord of 2016 build on these foundations while adding new forces, and students should resist reading them back into the period studied here.

Chapter 84 turns to Brazil under military rule, where a different path led to dictatorship. Colombia's case invites comparison: a pacted civilian regime and a persistent insurgency on one side, a military regime and a different repression on the other. Both show that Cold War conflicts in Latin America were shaped by domestic institutions as much as by global rivalry.
