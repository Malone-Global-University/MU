Mexico from Empire to Republic

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Country That Kept Rewriting Itself

Between 1821 and 1845 Mexico had an emperor, a triumvirate, a federal republic, a divided presidency, a centralist republic governed by seven constitutional laws, and a second centralist settlement written in 1843. It lost Texas, fought a war with France, and saw governments overthrown by garrison proclamations again and again. The central question is why a country with real resources and constant constitutional invention could not make any one settlement last.

On 27 September 1821 the Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City, and the next day a provisional junta declared the independence of the Mexican Empire. Eleven years of insurgency and counterinsurgency, described in Chapter 52, had ended not with the victory of the popular movements led by Hidalgo and Morelos but with a negotiated alliance between a royalist officer turned insurgent, Agustín de Iturbide, and the insurgent commander Vicente Guerrero. Within three years the monarchy it created had collapsed, its founder had been executed, and the country had been reconstituted as a federal republic. Within fifteen years that republic had itself been replaced by a centralist one. This chapter follows that sequence from 1821 to the threshold of the war with the United States in 1845.

The chapter's organizing question is why Mexico struggled to establish a durable political order after independence despite substantial constitutional experimentation, considerable economic resources, and repeated attempts at reform. The answer developed here does not rest on a claim that Mexicans were uniquely prone to disorder, a notion that older foreign and some Mexican writers promoted and that this textbook rejects. Nor does it reduce the period to the ambitions of Antonio López de Santa Anna, although he dominated national politics from the late 1820s. The chapter instead develops five interlocking explanations: the difficulty of designing institutions for a vast, regionally diverse country that had inherited a colonial state but not its legitimacy; the chronic problem of financing government; the strength of regional political communities that regarded themselves as sovereign; the political centrality of armed forces and of the pronunciamiento, the garrison proclamation that served as a mechanism of political bargaining; and the unequal effects of state formation on Indigenous communities, enslaved and free people of African descent, women, workers, and frontier populations.

Texas receives the longest division of the chapter because it joins several of these themes at once. It was a Mexican frontier province whose Tejano communities, Comanche and other Indigenous nations, Anglo-American settlers, and enslaved people each had their own stake in what the republic would become. The Texas Revolution is treated here principally as a conflict within Mexican and borderland history, a collision over federalism, immigration, slavery, and sovereignty, rather than only as a prelude to United States expansion. At the same time, the chapter does not allow Texas or the United States to stand for the whole of Mexican history in these years. Yucatán, Zacatecas, Oaxaca, the Pacific coast, the Gulf ports, the mining districts, and the villages of the central valleys were the settings in which most Mexicans actually experienced the republic.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Constitutional texts, congressional debates, pronunciamientos, state legislative records, customs accounts, foreign diplomatic correspondence, newspapers, pamphlets, and memoirs survive in quantity. Almost all of them were written in the heat of factional struggle. Lucas Alamán wrote a conservative history of Mexico decades before many events had settled; José María Luis Mora and Lorenzo de Zavala, both liberals, wrote accounts that shaped later liberal memory; foreign ministers such as Joel Poinsett and Henry Ward described Mexican politics through their own national interests. Records of ordinary people, especially Indigenous villagers and women, survive mainly in legal petitions, notarial documents, and parish registers rather than in narratives. The chapter therefore reads political texts as arguments and asks what interests each served.

The chapter is bounded by its neighbors in the textbook. Chapter 52 told the story of Hidalgo, Morelos, and the achievement of independence, and Chapter 58 compared the republican experiments of the whole region. Chapters 57 and 62 treat the separate fate of Central America. Chapter 63 will take up the U.S.–Mexican War, La Reforma, the French intervention, and the restoration of the republic. Chapter 59 therefore stops in 1845, when the United States annexation of Texas created the immediate crisis that led toward war. It narrates the Pastry War and the Yucatán rebellions as episodes of the centralist republic, but it does not narrate the campaigns of 1846–1848.

A word on terms. Contemporaries used federalist and centralist for those who preferred a federation of states or a unitary republic, but the labels hid much overlap. Many federalists wanted a strong central executive; many centralists defended local autonomy in practice. The labels liberal and conservative also shifted. The chapter uses them as descriptions of tendencies in the 1830s and 1840s and warns against treating them as stable parties with modern platforms.

Learning Objectives

Historical Context

The viceroyalty of New Spain was, in 1810, the richest and most populous Spanish colony, with a population commonly estimated at between six and seven million people, a majority of them Indigenous. Its wealth rested on silver mining in Guanajuato, Zacatecas, and elsewhere, on agriculture and ranching organized through haciendas, villages, and ranchos, and on a bureaucracy that taxed commerce and monopolies. Chapter 40 described that system. Its institutions included the viceroy, the audiencias, intendancies created by the Bourbon reforms of Chapter 48, municipal councils, a powerful Church with its own courts and enormous property holdings, and corporate privileges, or fueros, for clergy and soldiers.

Eleven years of war damaged this system without destroying it. Mines were flooded or abandoned, haciendas were raided, trade routes were interrupted, and the viceregal treasury was drained, much of it remitted to Spain or spent on the royalist army. At the same time, war transformed politics. The Spanish liberal Constitution of Cádiz, adopted in 1812 and restored in 1820, created elected municipal councils and provincial deputations that gave regional elites experience in self-government. Royalist militias, expanded during the insurgency, gave provincial officers an independent armed base. The provincial deputations, in particular, would later claim a sovereignty of their own.

The independence of 1821 resulted from a political shift in Spain. The liberal revolution of 1820 alarmed conservative clergy and officers in New Spain, who feared the loss of privileges, and Iturbide proposed a program, the Plan of Iguala, that promised independence under a monarchy, the protection of the Catholic religion, and union between Americans and Europeans. Chapter 52 told that story. What matters here is that independence was achieved through an alliance of former enemies who did not share a single vision of the new state. The insurgent tradition of Hidalgo and Morelos, with its social and agrarian currents, had been marginalized, while royalist officers and creole elites assumed leadership.

Mexico also began independence with a distinctive geography and a weak transport network. The country stretched from Central America to the northern borderlands of Texas, New Mexico, and California. Mountain ranges, deserts, and a lack of navigable rivers made communication slow and expensive, and mule trains linked mining centers, ports, and cities. Regions such as Yucatán, Oaxaca, Jalisco, and the northeast looked toward their own ports and markets rather than the capital. Chapter 58 described how this regionalism shaped republican design across Latin America. In Mexico it was acute.

Finally, Mexico's neighbors and rivals were changing. Spain refused to recognize independence and held the fortress of San Juan de Ulúa off Veracruz until 1825. Britain, seeking markets and mining investment, recognized Mexico in the mid-1820s and lent it money. The United States, which had agreed to a boundary with Spain in 1819, sought to buy territory and to shape Mexican politics through its ministers. France pressed commercial claims. Mexican leaders had to govern in a world where recognition, credit, and borders were all uncertain.

Division I — The First Mexican Empire, 1821–1823

This division explains why the independence settlement produced a monarchy and why that arrangement collapsed so rapidly. It begins where Chapter 52 ends and does not retell the wars of Hidalgo and Morelos.

Section 59.1

Mexico after Independence: An Empire without Stable Institutions

The independence of Mexico was proclaimed on 28 September 1821 by a Provisional Governing Junta of thirty-six men, most of them senior clergy, officers, officials, and wealthy landowners who had held places under the viceroyalty. Agustín de Iturbide presided over it. The junta then named a Regency of five, also headed by Iturbide, and issued an Act of Independence that described the new state as a sovereign empire. The speed of this transition obscured how little had been decided. The treaty signed at Córdoba in August 1821 between Iturbide and Juan O'Donojú, the Spanish official who had arrived as the last political chief of New Spain, offered the Mexican throne to Ferdinand VII or to another Bourbon prince. O'Donojú died in October 1821, and the Spanish government rejected the treaty in February 1822. The empire therefore had a throne without a claimant.

Administrative continuity was both an asset and a liability. Most local officials, tax collectors, judges, and military commanders stayed at their posts, which allowed the new government to function without dismantling the viceregal bureaucracy. The same continuity meant that independence did not alter the structure of colonial taxation, the privileges of the Church and army, or the legal separation of Indigenous communities from other populations, although the Plan of Iguala had declared all inhabitants equal and ended formal distinctions among castas in official documents. Many Spanish-born officials left the country, taking capital with them, and the departure of peninsular merchants and officials weakened commercial networks and deprived the treasury of expertise.

Economic damage framed every decision. Mining output had fallen sharply during the insurgency, as flooded mines, destroyed machinery, and scarce mercury reduced production in Guanajuato and Zacatecas. Agriculture had suffered in the Bajío and in parts of the south. The treasury inherited debts from the royalist government and faced a large standing army that Iturbide had promised to keep and pay, a commitment that consumed much of the available revenue. Customs duties and the colonial tobacco monopoly, which together provided much of the income, depended on a commerce that had not yet recovered.

Claims to legitimacy competed. Iturbide and his supporters rested on the army, whose officers had joined the Plan of Iguala, and on the prestige of having achieved independence without further civil war. Former insurgents such as Guerrero, Guadalupe Victoria, and Nicolás Bravo claimed the legacy of a decade of insurgency. Provincial elites looked to the provincial deputations created under the Spanish constitution. Republicans, Bourbonists, and supporters of Iturbide differed over what kind of state Mexico should be, and none could appeal to a tradition of national self-government because none existed. Independence had created a sovereign state in law, but who exercised sovereignty, the emperor, the Congress, or the provinces, remained the open question of the next two years.

Section 59.2

The Plan of Iguala, the Regency, and the Constituent Congress

The Plan of Iguala of 24 February 1821 had offered three guarantees: the preservation of the Catholic religion, independence, and union between Europeans and Americans. It also promised a constitutional monarchy and a Congress that would draft a constitution suited to the country. The Three Guarantees gave different groups reasons to support it. Clergy saw a defense of the Church against Spanish liberal anticlericalism, officers saw their positions protected, creoles saw access to office, and former insurgents saw independence at last. The plan's ambiguity about the actual powers of the monarch and of Congress allowed each to read it favorably, and the same ambiguity ensured conflict once the assembly met.

The Congress that convened in February 1822 was elected through a complicated system based on the indirect elections of the Cádiz constitution. Its deputies, representing the provinces, included clergy, lawyers, officers, and landowners, and they divided into tendencies. Some wished to offer the crown to a European prince under the Treaty of Córdoba, others favored a monarchy with strict limits on royal power, and a growing group, among them Servando Teresa de Mier and Carlos María de Bustamante in different ways, defended congressional authority against both emperor and army. The deputies insisted that the Congress was sovereign and that it alone could define executive powers. Iturbide, who headed the Regency, regarded himself as the architect of independence and as entitled to a leading role.

The Regency's financial problems sharpened the dispute. Iturbide asked Congress for funds to pay the army, which had swelled with the incorporation of insurgent and royalist units, and Congress, wary of a standing force loyal to him, resisted. Deputies debated reducing the army and the pay of senior officers. They also debated how to divide executive authority and whether the Regency should remain a collective body. Behind these arguments lay the question of whether sovereignty belonged to the nation represented in Congress or to the person who had brought independence about.

The conflict was not simply one between a would-be tyrant and a representative assembly. Many deputies were conservative and some were royalists reconciled to independence, while Iturbide drew on popular enthusiasm in the capital and in the barracks. The Congress was inexperienced, divided, and slow, and it faced a general with a large following and a treasury it did not control. Whatever its merits, it had little means of enforcing its decisions against the army. That imbalance explains why the next step, the proclamation of the emperor, came not from the Congress but from a crowd and a regiment.

Section 59.3

Agustín I: Coronation, Monarchical Authority, and Imperial Government

On the night of 18 May 1822, Sergeant Pío Marcha and soldiers of a regiment in Mexico City began shouting for Iturbide to become emperor, and crowds in the streets took up the cry. The next day, with a mob outside and Iturbide's supporters in the galleries, the Congress met in an emergency session and voted, by a majority of the deputies present, to make him emperor. Sources disagree on how spontaneous the demonstration was; Iturbide's opponents later charged that he had arranged it, while his supporters called it a popular acclamation. Either way, the Congress's vote took place under pressure, and its legitimacy was questioned from the start. Iturbide was crowned Agustín I on 21 July 1822 in the cathedral of Mexico City in a ceremony modeled partly on European precedent and partly on the improvisations of a new court.

The imperial government tried to build institutions quickly. A nobility was created, with titles and orders, ministers were named, and a court with ceremonial offices appeared. Iturbide appointed ministers of state and relations, of the treasury, and of war, but the empire lacked experienced administrators and relied heavily on former royal officials. The emperor sought support among the military by promotions, among the clergy by defending the Church's privileges, and among provincial elites by naming them to office. He could not satisfy all of them at once, and the cost of the court and the army deepened a fiscal crisis that was already severe.

Finance was the empire's most visible failure. Customs revenue was lower than expected because trade had not recovered and because many ports were slow to remit funds to the capital. Iturbide's government resorted to forced loans from merchants and from the Church, to the issue of paper money, and to the confiscation of funds, including a controversial seizure of a shipment of silver. These measures alienated merchants, clergy, and the provinces whose revenues were drawn upon. They also showed how a state with a large army and few reliable taxes tended to turn to coercion and emergency expedients, a pattern that would recur under later governments.

The empire also had territorial ambitions. In early 1822 the authorities of Guatemala, after a debate that divided the leaders of the captaincy general, voted to join the Mexican Empire, and Mexican troops under Vicente Filísola entered the region. Chiapas, which had declared for Mexico, remained within the empire, and the extension briefly gave Mexico a territory stretching to Panama's border. Chapters 57 and 62 narrate how Central America separated in 1823. For present purposes the point is that the empire claimed a vast space it had no capacity to govern, and that provinces from Guatemala to Oaxaca had their own views about their relationship to Mexico City.

Section 59.4

Congressional Opposition, Military Dissent, and the Crisis of 1822

Relations between the emperor and the Congress deteriorated within weeks of the coronation. Deputies who had voted for Iturbide under pressure now sought to limit his powers, arguing that he was a constitutional monarch bound by the legislature. In August 1822, the government arrested a number of deputies and other critics, accusing them of conspiracy, and it detained several for months. Iturbide defended these arrests as necessary to defend public order. The Congress protested that the arrested deputies enjoyed legislative immunity, and the dispute turned into a contest over whether the emperor could be held accountable to the assembly at all.

Matters came to a head on 31 October 1822, when Iturbide dissolved the Congress by force. He replaced it with a National Instituent Junta of his own selection, charged with preparing a constitution and with serving as a legislature in the meantime. The act destroyed whatever constitutional basis the empire had possessed. It also removed the one body that could plausibly claim to represent all the provinces. Opponents argued that a ruler who dissolved the nation's representatives had forfeited his authority, while supporters answered that a divided and obstructive Congress had endangered the new state.

Opposition grew in the provinces and in the army. Republican and federalist currents in Veracruz, Jalisco, Oaxaca, Yucatán, and elsewhere rejected the dissolution. The Masonic and reading circles that had begun to appear in cities spread criticism, and pamphlets circulated in the capital. Garrisons in the provinces were unpaid, and many officers resented Iturbide's favorites. Opposition was also regional: provinces that had been accustomed to govern themselves under their deputations feared that a centralized empire would draw off their revenue and send officials from the capital.

The man who gave the discontent a military voice was Antonio López de Santa Anna, then a young colonel and commander at Veracruz who had earlier joined the Plan of Iguala and won Iturbide's favor. In early December 1822 he issued a pronouncement at Veracruz proclaiming a republic, though his stated demands at first were more limited and focused on restoring Congress. His motives, like those of many officers, mixed ideology, ambition, and a reading of where power was moving. Guadalupe Victoria and Nicolás Bravo, veteran insurgents, joined the movement, and the government's first forces sent against Santa Anna failed to crush it. The initial rebellion was unsuccessful in itself. What turned it into a decisive challenge was the adhesion of imperial generals who had been sent to suppress it.

Section 59.5

The Plan of Casa Mata and the Fall of Iturbide

The imperial commanders sent against Veracruz, including José Antonio Echávarri and others, concluded in the course of the campaign that the empire could not be saved by force. On 1 February 1823 they signed, at an estate called Casa Mata near Veracruz, a plan that called for the election of a new Congress and recognized the sovereignty of the provinces. The plan did not explicitly call for a republic or demand Iturbide's removal. Its significance lay in what it implied: that the nation, represented in a new Congress chosen by the provinces, should decide its form of government, and that provincial authorities had the right to act when the central government had violated that principle.

The plan spread quickly through the country. Provincial deputations in Puebla, Oaxaca, Guadalajara, Zacatecas, and elsewhere adhered to it, in effect asserting that they, rather than Mexico City, were the bearers of political authority. Some deputations raised troops and began collecting taxes themselves, withholding revenue from the capital. The empire had not lost a battle of consequence. It lost the obedience of the provinces, and without provincial revenue and recruits the government could not pay its army. Iturbide's collapse was thus as much administrative and fiscal as military.

Iturbide attempted to save his position by reinstating the Congress he had dissolved, which met again in March 1823. The deputies, now with the provinces behind them, demanded his removal. On 19 March 1823 he abdicated, arguing that the crown had been forced on him, and the Congress declared the coronation void and the Plan of Iguala and the Treaty of Córdoba without effect, which cleared the way for the nation to choose its own form of government. It allowed him to leave the country with a pension, and he sailed for Italy in May and later reached England. Congress in the meantime named a provisional executive.

The final act was Iturbide's return. Hearing in exile that Spain was planning a reconquest and that Mexico was in disorder, he sailed back in 1824 without knowing that the Congress had declared him an outlaw who would be treated as a traitor if he reappeared on Mexican soil. He landed at Soto la Marina, in Tamaulipas, in July 1824, was arrested by local authorities and brought before the legislature of the state, which ordered his execution. He was shot at Padilla on 19 July 1824. The episode showed that regional legislatures now acted as sovereign bodies, and that the republic's first generation of leaders were willing to use lethal force against a founder. The central lesson of the collapse was that the empire fell because its authority did not rest on the consent of the provinces, a lesson that shaped the federal settlement discussed in Division II.

Historical Vocabulary
Pronunciamiento

A pronunciamiento (from pronunciar, "to declare") was a public declaration, usually by an army commander and often joined by civilian notables or a municipal council, that withdrew support from a government or demanded a political change. Most were issued as a "plan," named for the town where it was proclaimed, such as the Plan of Casa Mata, the Plan of Jalapa, or the Plan of Cuernavaca.

A pronunciamiento was not always a coup in the narrow sense. Many ended in negotiation rather than battle. Garrisons, local authorities, and other communities could "adhere" to a plan, which turned it into a measure of how much support a government retained. The practice reflected the absence of a generally accepted method for changing governments or settling constitutional disputes, and it gave army officers a central political role. Students should avoid treating it as simple military ambition. It was a recognized, if destabilizing, form of political bargaining.

Division II — Founding the Federal Republic, 1823–1824

The collapse of the empire left a vacuum that provinces, a new Congress, and a provisional executive had to fill. This division traces how federalism became the framework of the republic.

Section 59.6

The Supreme Executive Power and the Republican Transition

After Iturbide's abdication the reinstated Congress needed an executive that would not recall the empire. On 31 March 1823 it created a Supreme Executive Power of three members, Guadalupe Victoria, Nicolás Bravo, and Pedro Celestino Negrete, with Mariano Michelena, Miguel Domínguez, and Vicente Guerrero as alternates. The choice balanced tendencies. Victoria and Bravo were insurgent veterans associated with republican sentiment, and Negrete was a former royalist officer who had joined the Trigarante Army. A triumvirate was a deliberate rejection of one-man rule, and it reflected the fear that any single executive would become a new Iturbide.

The provisional government faced a divided country. Many provinces had treated the Plan of Casa Mata as permission to govern themselves, and some did not at first accept the authority of a Congress that sat in Mexico City, the same Congress that Iturbide had once dissolved and reinstated. Jalisco, under Luis Quintanar, declared itself a free and sovereign state in mid-1823 and refused to obey orders from the capital that it considered contrary to its rights. Oaxaca, Yucatán, and Zacatecas took similar positions, and several provinces raised their own militias and kept customs and tax revenue for themselves. The central government had little capacity to coerce them, since its army was unpaid and unreliable.

The triumvirate's task was to hold the country together while a new constituent assembly was organized. It negotiated rather than commanded. Negrete's military experience was used to pacify the provinces, and the government sent commissioners to persuade rather than to compel. It also confronted the problem of the army inherited from the empire, which was large, expensive, and politically restless, and it faced plots by Iturbide's remaining supporters as well as agitation from radical republicans.

Congress, for its part, concluded that it could not settle the constitution itself, since it had been elected under the empire's rules. It called a new constituent assembly, based on a wider and more representative election law, which met on 7 November 1823. The change of Congress mattered. The first constituent body had been elected under imperial conditions and had been associated with Iturbide's drama. The second was elected under the provincial claims of the Casa Mata moment, and its members arrived with instructions from their regions. The question of the republic's form was therefore taken up by a body whose members largely regarded themselves as representatives of provinces first and of the nation second.

Section 59.7

Provincial Sovereignty and the Constitutive Act of 1824

Federalism gained support in 1823 for reasons that were practical as much as ideological. The Spanish constitution of 1812 had created provincial deputations that, after 1821, continued to exercise real authority. Provincial elites wished to retain control of local taxes, militias, and appointments, and many had come to distrust a capital that had sent them an emperor, an unpaid army, and tax demands. The example of the United States, which Mexican liberals studied and admired, offered a model of a federal republic, although its history and conditions differed from Mexico's. Many Mexicans also believed that Spain's centralized rule had caused the disasters of the colonial period, and a federation seemed a way to avoid repeating them.

Not everyone agreed. Servando Teresa de Mier, a veteran of the insurgency and a former exile, delivered a famous speech in December 1823 warning that a loose federation of sovereign states would invite disintegration, and he recommended a compact, moderate system, a federal republic with a strong center. Others, including Lucas Alamán later and many conservatives, doubted that Mexico had the resources or the political culture to support thirty or so state governments. Supporters of a strong federation, led by Miguel Ramos Arizpe of Coahuila and by deputies from Jalisco, Zacatecas, Yucatán, and Oaxaca, answered that the provinces were already acting as sovereign communities and that the national government could only be constructed by their agreement.

The compromise that emerged was the Constitutive Act of the Mexican Federation, signed on 31 January 1824. It was a short document, intended to settle the fundamental structure of the nation before the full constitution was completed. It declared that sovereignty resided radically and essentially in the nation, adopted the representative popular federal republic as the form of government, established the Catholic religion as the religion of the state to the exclusion of any other, and divided power among legislative, executive, and judicial branches. It also listed the states of the federation and declared them free, sovereign, and independent in their internal government.

The act's wording gave rise to later quarrels. Federalists could point to the declaration that the states were sovereign as proof that the republic rested on a compact among them. Centralists could point to the statement that sovereignty resided in the nation as a whole. The text deliberately left unclear how far state sovereignty extended and what powers the central government held over armies, taxes, and trade. The act is important because it settled the form of the republic, federal and representative, months before the constitution that would give it institutional content, and because it showed how deeply the provinces' claims had shaped the new order.

Section 59.8

The Federal Constitution of October 4, 1824

The Federal Constitution of the United Mexican States was promulgated on 4 October 1824 after months of debate. It was a long document, with 171 articles, that borrowed from the United States Constitution and from the Spanish Constitution of Cádiz, combining a federal structure and a strong presidency with elements of Spanish constitutional practice. It confirmed the federal republic and the exclusive establishment of the Roman Catholic religion, forbidding the exercise of any other. It listed the states, nineteen in number, together with several territories administered by the federal government, among them Alta California, Baja California, Colima, Santa Fe de Nuevo México, and, shortly afterward, Tlaxcala, and it provided for a Federal District to house the national capital, a status given to Mexico City soon after.

The legislative branch was bicameral. The Chamber of Deputies was elected in proportion to population, while the Senate consisted of two senators from each state chosen by the state legislatures. Congress held powers over taxation, defense, trade, and foreign relations, and it chose the president's cabinet only indirectly through its control of funds. The executive was entrusted to a president and a vice president elected by the state legislatures, each state casting one vote, with the candidate receiving the most votes becoming president and the runner-up vice president. That system gave state legislatures immense influence and made the vice president a potential rival of the president, a feature that proved dangerous in practice.

The judiciary was headed by a Supreme Court of eleven ministers and an attorney general, with circuit and district courts. The constitution preserved the fueros of the clergy and the military, which kept ecclesiastical and military courts for cases involving their members. This continuity with colonial law was deliberate. Many delegates considered the Church and army indispensable supports of order, and the constitution did not touch the Church's property or its role in education and civil records. The text also declared certain articles unamendable, including those on religion, the form of government, the freedom of the press, and the division of powers, which made later reform politically fraught.

The constitution had notable omissions. It said little about the rights of Indigenous communities, about slavery, or about the status of women, who were not citizens in the political sense. Citizenship and the franchise were left largely to the states, which set their own requirements for voting and for office, and many adopted property or income qualifications. The constitution's silences allowed the continuation of colonial arrangements, such as corporate village landholding and household hierarchies, while the formal abolition of caste categories in official documents did not end racial discrimination in practice. Before the constitution was promulgated, the constituent Congress had enacted a law on 13 July 1824 prohibiting the international slave trade. The constitution therefore set a framework for politics among property-holding men while leaving most other questions to the states and to custom.

Historical Vocabulary
Federalism and Centralism

Federalism in the Mexican context meant a union of states that kept control of their internal affairs, local taxation, and militias while delegating foreign relations, defense, and some taxation to a national government. Centralism meant a unitary republic in which the central government appointed regional officials and determined policy for the whole country.

These labels did not mark stable parties. Many federalists sought a strong executive, and many centralists wished to preserve provincial customs. The terms also overlapped with conflicts over the Church, the army, and property, so that a "federalist" in 1833 and a "federalist" in 1846 might hold very different positions on social questions. Students should treat the terms as describing preferences about the location of authority, not as complete political programs.

Section 59.9

States, Municipalities, and the Fiscal Federal Compact

Each state drafted its own constitution in the years after 1824, and these documents varied. Some, like that of Jalisco, created strong governors, while others, like Zacatecas, favored powerful legislatures. State constitutions set suffrage rules, organized municipal government, regulated the Church within their territory in limited ways, and created state courts. Municipal councils, or ayuntamientos, which the Spanish constitution had spread widely, remained the basic units of local government, and in many places they became centers of political competition among local elites. Indigenous villages often kept their own officials within or alongside these structures.

State militias, known as the civic militia, were a central feature of the new order. Created in the early 1820s and expanded by many states, they were local forces under the control of governors and legislatures, manned by citizens, and intended as a counterweight to a standing army. For federalists the militia was a safeguard of liberty. For centralists and many army officers it was a threat, because it armed regional politicians and could be used against the national government. Zacatecas, whose militia was among the best organized, would later pay for this reputation.

The fiscal arrangements of the federation were its weakest point. The federal government received customs duties on foreign trade, the income from the tobacco monopoly, postal revenue, and some other sources, while the states kept the alcabala, the internal sales tax that had been a major colonial revenue. To make up the federal shortfall, a federal law of 1824 fixed a quota, called the contingente, that each state would contribute from its revenue. Many states paid little or nothing, since they needed the money for their own militias and governments and had no reason to feel that the capital's needs came first. The federal treasury thus depended on customs revenue collected at a handful of ports, above all Veracruz, and was vulnerable to any interruption of trade.

These arrangements mattered because the Mexican state faced the same fundamental problem as many new governments: it needed money to pay its army, service its debts, and govern, but the people best able to pay, landowners, merchants, and the Church, were represented in the bodies that voted taxes. The federal compact therefore shaped not only constitutional politics but fiscal capacity. A government that could not enforce its tax claims on the states had to borrow, and a government that borrowed on poor terms had to rely heavily on customs duties. Many of the political conflicts of the next two decades turned on this arrangement, even when contemporaries described them in the language of principle.

Section 59.10

Territory, Chiapas, and the Problem of National Integration

The republic inherited an immense territory with uneven connections to the center. Chiapas and Soconusco, historically part of the captaincy general of Guatemala, had declared for Mexico in 1821 and remained part of the empire when the rest of Central America separated in July 1823. In September 1824 a consultation in Chiapas ratified union with Mexico, though the process was contested between factions that favored Mexico and those that preferred Guatemala. Soconusco, the coastal cacao district, was claimed by both countries, and its status remained disputed for decades, a reminder that the border with Central America was not settled at independence.

The new federal territories reflected the government's limits. Alta and Baja California, Santa Fe de Nuevo México, and Colima were governed from the capital, with little revenue and few resources. The population of California was tiny and dispersed among missions, presidios, and ranches, while New Mexico was linked more to the Santa Fe trail and to Chihuahua than to Mexico City. These regions were too poor to support state governments, and the center was too weak to govern them effectively. Their inhabitants, including Indigenous communities and settlers, negotiated their own relations with distant authorities and sometimes challenged them, as in New Mexico and California in the 1830s.

Mexico City itself posed a political problem. As the largest city and the seat of the national government, it dominated the central valley, yet the constitution made it a federal district rather than part of any state. This provoked friction with the State of Mexico, which had to relocate its own capital, and it gave the capital a political character of its own, with crowds, newspapers, and garrisons capable of influencing national politics. Communication with the periphery remained slow: a letter to Chihuahua or Sonora took weeks, and rainy seasons could close roads.

The central problem of integration was thus not only constitutional but material. Governing a country of several million people across mountain ranges and deserts required roads, couriers, tax collectors, and soldiers, all of which required money. The republic spent far more on its army than on any infrastructure, and the army itself was scattered in garrisons that often depended on local authorities for supplies. In many regions, especially in the north, effective power lay with ranching families, local militias, or Indigenous nations such as the Comanche and Apache, who were not under Mexican authority at all. The constitution described a nation, but in practice a nation of many regional societies had to be built, and the federal republic was the first of several attempts to do so.

Division III — Guadalupe Victoria and the First Federal Republic, 1824–1828

The first constitutional presidency was the most stable period of the early republic, yet its stability concealed fiscal fragility, factional rivalry, and a deepening argument over who belonged to the nation.

Section 59.11

Guadalupe Victoria and the First Constitutional Presidency

Guadalupe Victoria, born Manuel Félix Fernández, took office on 10 October 1824 as the first president under the federal constitution, with Nicolás Bravo as vice president. Victoria had fought in the insurgency, survived years of hiding in the countryside after the defeat of Morelos, and enjoyed a reputation for integrity that made him acceptable across factions. He served until the end of his term in March 1829, the only president of the early republic to complete the constitutional period. That achievement reflected his willingness to compromise more than any strength of the institutions he led.

His cabinet included figures from different tendencies. Lucas Alamán, a conservative with a mining and administrative background, served as minister of interior and foreign relations in the first years, while Miguel Ramos Arizpe, a leading federalist and priest, held the ministry of justice and ecclesiastical affairs. Others, including officers from the insurgent and royalist traditions, held posts in the treasury and war ministries. The ministry was therefore a coalition, and its internal rivalries were as important as the opposition outside. Victoria's approach was to avoid confrontation with the Church and the army, to conciliate the states, and to defer difficult questions.

Security and military authority remained concerns. The army of several tens of thousands, inherited from the war, consumed much of the budget and was dispersed in garrisons under commanders who were often politically ambitious. The government continued to face Spanish military presence at San Juan de Ulúa, the fortress guarding Veracruz, which did not surrender until November 1825, and it feared that Spain might attempt a reconquest, a fear that was not baseless. The navy was tiny and improvised, and Mexico bought and chartered ships to blockade the fortress. The final surrender of Ulúa was a symbolic as well as a strategic victory, removing the last Spanish foothold.

Foreign recognition proceeded slowly. The United States had recognized Mexico in 1822, and Britain, after debate and pressure from commercial interests, recognized it formally at the end of 1824 and signed a commercial treaty in 1826. Other European states followed more reluctantly, and Spain would not recognize Mexican independence until 1836. The national symbols of the new state, including the flag with its eagle and serpent and the rituals of independence celebrations, were developed in these years, and the government used festivals and a reorganized national museum to cultivate identification with the republic. These efforts reached primarily the literate and urban populations, while many villagers continued to identify with their communities and parishes.

Section 59.12

Public Finance, British Loans, Customs, and Debt

The central economic puzzle of these years is why a government ruling a country widely believed to be rich could not obtain usable revenue or credit. Several factors combined. The silver mines that had underwritten the colonial economy were in ruin, and recovery required investment, machinery, and mercury that Mexican capital could not supply. Colonial taxes had been partly abolished or reduced: the Indigenous tribute had ended, some monopolies were curtailed, and the Church's tithe continued but was not at the state's disposal. The most reliable revenue came from import duties, which rose and fell with trade and were collected at ports. The government's expenditure, by contrast, was dominated by the army, which absorbed a majority of the budget in most years.

Foreign credit seemed to offer an escape. In 1824 and 1825 the Mexican government contracted two loans in London, one with the house of Goldschmidt and one with Barclay, Herring, Richardson and Company, each with a nominal value of about three million pounds at an interest of five percent. The bonds were sold at a discount, and fees and commissions took a large share, so that the sum actually received by the government was considerably less than the nominal amount. British investors also poured money into Mexican mining companies, sending engineers and miners, including Cornish workers at Real del Monte, and the early years of the decade saw a speculative boom that raised expectations of a quick recovery.

The boom ended in the financial crisis of 1825–1826, when the bursting of the speculative bubble in London damaged Latin American borrowers generally. The Goldschmidt firm failed, and the remaining funds from the loans were not fully delivered. Mining companies, which had overestimated yields and underestimated the difficulty of draining the old mines, struggled. Mexico stopped paying interest on its London loans in 1827, joining other Latin American states in default. The default closed European credit for years, and later governments would resort to domestic lenders, called agiotistas, who advanced funds at ruinous rates against the future customs revenue.

Customs revenue itself was affected by policy. Tariffs were protective in some periods, banning certain imported goods to protect domestic manufactures, and these bans encouraged smuggling, which reduced revenue. Customs houses were often controlled by officials who diverted funds, and the extent of smuggling along the coasts and the Rio Grande could not be measured by the government. The mixture of unstable tariffs, political pressure, and weak administration meant that an expanding foreign trade did not translate into a reliable income. The fiscal weakness of these years was therefore not a mystery of Mexican character but the result of identifiable features: a destroyed mining sector, a fragmented tax system under the federal compact, an oversized military, a dependence on volatile customs revenue, and a loss of access to credit after 1827.

Section 59.13

Society beneath the Federal Constitution

Most Mexicans lived in villages, haciendas, ranchos, and small towns, and for them the constitutional drama in the capital often mattered less than the price of maize, the demands of tax collectors, and the intentions of the local landowner. Indigenous peasants formed the majority of the population in the center and south. Their villages held communal lands, used for farming, grazing, and wood gathering, and were governed by local officers who negotiated with the state. The Plan of Iguala's declaration of equality and the formal abolition of caste categories altered legal vocabulary and ended Indigenous tribute and the special "republic of Indians" structure in law, but they did not give villagers secure rights. Local ayuntamientos replaced the older village councils in many places, and their composition often favored non-Indigenous neighbors.

Land was the central issue. Liberal thinkers argued that communal landholding discouraged initiative and that private property should be encouraged, and some states in these years began to discuss or enact measures to divide communal lands among individual families. The implementation of such measures was gradual and uneven in this period, and the major liberal attack on corporate landholding would come later, but the ideological shift had begun. Indigenous communities responded through litigation, petitions, and sometimes resistance. In Sonora and Sinaloa, Yaqui and Mayo leaders, including Juan Banderas, mobilized against local authorities in the 1820s and early 1830s, demanding recognition of their territory, and similar movements arose elsewhere. These rebellions were not simply rejections of the republic but assertions of Indigenous rights within and against it.

Racial hierarchy persisted under new names. Persons of African descent, who in some regions such as the Pacific coast of Guerrero and Oaxaca and the Gulf coast near Veracruz formed a substantial part of the population, were legally equal but often held the lowest-paid and most arduous work, including cattle herding, sugar labor, and muleteering. Slavery, though small in scale compared with Brazil or the Caribbean, still existed in some regions, a subject taken up again in the discussion of Guerrero's decree. Citizenship was defined by the state constitutions, which usually required residence, property or occupation, and sometimes literacy, excluding many poor and Indigenous people from formal participation, although local elections drew in wider groups than the formal rules suggest.

Women's work and legal position illustrate the limits of change. Under Spanish civil law, which the republic retained, married women were subject to their husbands' authority but could hold property, including dowries, and could inherit. Women sold food, pulque, and cloth in markets, ran small shops and ranches as widows, worked as domestic servants, and labored in textile workshops. Thousands worked in the tobacco factory in Mexico City, which employed many women as cigarette makers. Women could not vote or hold office, but they participated in politics through petitions, salons, and correspondence. Leona Vicario, a veteran of the insurgency who had been imprisoned for aiding the cause, continued to write and to defend her views in the republic's press. The constitution's silence on women's status meant that colonial legal norms continued largely unchanged.

What Changed

  • Spanish sovereignty ended, and new offices, elections, and constitutions were created
  • Indigenous tribute and formal caste categories were abolished in law
  • The slave trade was prohibited in 1824, and municipal and state governments gained new powers
  • Foreign trade and credit opened to Britain and the United States

  • The Church's wealth, tithe, and fuero, and the military's fuero
  • Hacienda and village landholding, and the dependence of rural workers on landowners
  • Spanish civil law on family and property, and the exclusion of women from politics
  • Reliance on customs duties, the alcabala, and the tobacco monopoly for revenue

Who experienced change most directly: Provincial elites, officers, and urban merchants gained political voice and commercial opportunity. Who experienced greater continuity: Indigenous villagers, enslaved and free workers of African descent, and most women lived under the old hierarchies. Why the pattern matters: The republic changed political forms faster than social relations, and later reform conflicts, especially over the Church and corporate property, grew from that gap.

Section 59.14

Yorkinos, Escoceses, Newspapers, and Foreign Diplomacy

In the absence of organized parties, Masonic lodges became the vehicles of political organization. The Scottish rite lodges, called Escoceses, had been introduced during the Spanish liberal period and drew in many creole and former royalist elites, who tended to favor order, the protection of property, and a more moderate or centralizing constitutionalism. Vice President Bravo was associated with them. In 1825 a new network of York rite lodges, the Yorkinos, was formed, attracting more recruits from the middle ranks, former insurgents, army officers, and provincial politicians, and it quickly became a broader popular movement. Yorkinos favored federalism, anti-Spanish measures, and a more open politics, and leaders such as Vicente Guerrero, Lorenzo de Zavala, and Santa Anna became prominent in their ranks.

The lodges worked alongside the press. Newspapers multiplied after independence, some tied to factions, such as El Sol, associated with the Escoceses, and El Águila Mexicana, associated with liberals and Yorkinos. Pamphlets, broadsheets, and satirical prints circulated in the cities, reaching literate readers and, through public readings and conversation, wider audiences. The press accused opponents of treason, corruption, and subservience to foreign powers, and its tone helped harden factions. Freedom of the press was constitutionally protected, but libel prosecutions and state restrictions occurred, and printers operated under financial insecurity.

Foreign diplomats entered these struggles. Joel Roberts Poinsett, who arrived in 1825 as the first United States minister, believed that republican federalism was the proper course for Mexico and associated himself with the Yorkinos, a connection his critics exaggerated and he did not entirely deny. He pressed for a commercial treaty, for a boundary settlement with the United States, and, according to his instructions, for the purchase of Texas, offers that Mexican governments refused. Henry George Ward, the British chargé and later minister, represented commercial and financial interests and was often at odds with Poinsett. Mexican politicians used the rivalry for their own purposes, and Escoceses denounced Poinsett as a foreign meddler. A boundary treaty confirming the line agreed with Spain in 1819 was signed in 1828, though it was ratified only later.

The relationship between factions and elections was reciprocal. The lodges mobilized voters and legislators, and state legislatures, which chose the president and vice president, could be won through networks of patronage. By the mid-1820s the Yorkinos had a substantial presence in the state legislatures and in Congress, and the Escoceses, weakened and increasingly identified with the Spanish and the rich, saw their influence decline. That shift left the republic with a dominant faction and a resentful opposition, which, lacking an institutional avenue of recourse, turned to arms. It is a mistake to describe the lodges as modern parties. They were secret societies with shifting memberships and limited programs, but they gave political life a form of organization that the constitution did not.

Section 59.15

Anti-Spanish Politics, the Montaño Revolt, and Republican Polarization

The Spaniards who remained in Mexico after independence, some thousands of merchants, clergy, and landowners, became targets of suspicion. Their loyalty was questioned because Spain refused to recognize independence, held Ulúa until 1825, and was widely believed to be planning a reconquest. Many Mexicans also resented the commercial and landholding power of peninsulares, and the political rhetoric of Yorkino liberals identified them with the old regime. Pamphlets accused them of hoarding wealth and plotting. The attacks combined legitimate security concerns with scapegoating, and they shaded into a broader language that defined the true Mexican by opposition to the Spaniard.

In January 1827 the discovery of a conspiracy led by a Spanish Franciscan, Joaquín Arenas, who planned to restore Spanish rule, gave the anti-Spanish movement a pretext. The plot was small and poorly organized, but it was treated as proof of a vast conspiracy. State legislatures began to pass laws expelling Spaniards, and a federal law at the end of 1827 barred Spaniards from many public offices and mandated expulsions, with exceptions for those with family ties, long service, or physical incapacity. A second, stricter expulsion law followed in March 1829. Thousands of Spaniards left, taking capital and commercial networks with them, which damaged trade and deprived the treasury of tax payers.

Opposition to the Yorkinos and to Poinsett erupted in the Plan of Montaño, proclaimed in December 1827 by Lieutenant Colonel Manuel Montaño at Otumba, which demanded the suppression of secret societies, the dismissal of Poinsett, and a change of the ministry. Vice President Bravo, a leading Escocés, supported the plan and took the field. Guerrero, as a Yorkino leader and soldier, was sent against him and defeated his forces at Tulancingo in January 1828. Bravo was captured and sent into exile, together with other participants. The episode destroyed the Escoceses as an organized force and showed that a vice president could rebel against the president whose government he served.

The consequences were important. The Yorkinos emerged triumphant, but their victory deepened polarization, with moderates and conservatives alienated by the anti-Spanish campaign and by the use of military force against a vice president. The expulsion of Spaniards damaged the economy, and the political language of popular republicanism, associated with Guerrero, now stood against a conservative defense of order, property, and the Church. The election of 1828, narrated in Division IV, took place in this charged atmosphere, with the memory of Montaño and Arenas fresh and with a political class that treated its opponents as enemies of the nation.

Division IV — Electoral Crisis, Vicente Guerrero, and Military Intervention, 1828–1832

The disputed election of 1828 inaugurated a period in which presidents were removed by armed force, a former insurgent was executed, and Santa Anna became a national actor. The division asks how each crisis grew from the last.

Section 59.16

The Election of 1828 and the Acordada Uprising

The presidential election of 1828 was the first contested succession under the constitution. The state legislatures chose among candidates in September, and the main contenders were Manuel Gómez Pedraza, the minister of war, and Vicente Guerrero. Gómez Pedraza was a former royalist officer who had joined the independence movement, regarded as an able administrator and favored by moderates, army officers, and many Escoceses seeking an alternative to the Yorkino leadership. Guerrero, a hero of the insurgency from the south with a large following among former insurgents, the lower ranks of the army, and urban artisans, was the Yorkino candidate. The vote, by state legislatures each casting a single vote, gave Gómez Pedraza a narrow victory, with eleven states to Guerrero's nine in the figures usually cited.

Guerrero's supporters refused to accept the result. Santa Anna, then governor of Veracruz, pronounced at Perote in September 1828 in favor of Guerrero, alleging that the election had been manipulated, and marched toward Oaxaca, where he was besieged. His movement failed militarily, but it set a precedent: an officer who lost at the ballot box could appeal to arms in the name of the people's will. In the capital, tension rose as Yorkino leaders, including Lorenzo de Zavala, plotted a rising. On 30 November 1828, Zavala and Colonel José María Lobato seized the Acordada, an armory and former prison in Mexico City, with the support of regiments and crowds.

The fighting in the capital lasted several days, and it ended in a sack of the Parián, the large market in the central square where many wealthy merchants, including Spaniards, kept stalls, on 4 and 5 December. Crowds, in part provoked by anti-Spanish sentiment and in part simply seizing the opportunity, looted the stalls, and the damage was heavy. The episode revealed the social bitterness in the city, among poor residents who resented the merchant elite, and it frightened propertied men of all factions. Contemporary and later commentators used the Parián to argue that Guerrero's movement had unleashed the mob, a charge his supporters denied, while liberals pointed to the sack as the product of the government's provocations.

Gómez Pedraza, who had not yet taken office, resigned and left the country. Congress annulled the election of 1828 in January 1829 and declared Guerrero president, with Anastasio Bustamante, a general of different tendencies, as vice president. The constitutional order had been broken. A candidate who had lost the election gained the presidency through a barracks revolt and urban riot, and a constitutional majority in Congress ratified it afterward. The episode taught the political class that elections could be overturned by force, a lesson that officers and politicians of all factions would apply repeatedly, and it showed how quickly a procedural dispute could fuse with social grievance.

Section 59.17

Vicente Guerrero's Presidency and the Limits of Republican Equality

Vicente Guerrero took office on 1 April 1829. Born in the southern town of Tixtla, he was widely described as being of mixed African and Indigenous ancestry, and he came from a humble background, having been a muleteer before joining the insurgency. He was the first president of Mexico of non-elite origin, and many poor Mexicans, especially in the south and in the cities, saw him as their representative. His elite opponents derided his lack of education and his origins, using language that exposed the racial prejudices still pervasive in the republic. Guerrero's presidency thus tested whether the formal equality proclaimed at Iguala could translate into real access to power.

His government included Zavala as minister of the treasury, who proposed new taxes on incomes and wealth, tariff measures, and the confiscation of Spanish property, measures that threatened established interests and that Congress and the states met with hostility. Zavala's financial reforms were unrealistic in the circumstances, since the treasury was empty and the means of collecting direct taxes were lacking. The government also pursued the expulsion of Spaniards and supported popular measures such as protection of local manufactures. Its opponents, among them conservatives, much of the clergy, many moderates, and officers who resented Yorkino influence, grew steadily bolder.

The abolition of slavery was the most notable social measure. On 15 September 1829, four days after the Spanish expedition under Barradas capitulated at Tampico, and while Guerrero still held extraordinary powers granted by Congress during the invasion, he issued a decree abolishing slavery in the republic, with compensation for owners to be paid when the treasury permitted. The decree built on earlier steps: Hidalgo and Morelos had proclaimed abolition in the insurgency, Congress had prohibited the slave trade in 1824, and several state constitutions had adopted free-birth provisions. The number of enslaved people in Mexico was relatively small, concentrated in sugar and cattle regions of the coasts, in domestic service in cities, and in Texas, but the decree had symbolic force, and it placed Mexico among the first American states to abolish slavery nationally.

Implementation was contested and incomplete. In Texas, where Anglo-American settlers had brought enslaved people, the decree provoked alarm, and officials, led by empresario Stephen F. Austin and the state authorities, sought exemption. Scholars note that Texas received a form of exemption or suspension in practice, and that settlers continued to hold people in bondage, sometimes under contracts of indenture that obscured the status of the laborers. Elsewhere the decree's effect depended on local authority and on the enslaved themselves, who could appeal to it. The decree's limits are also clear: it did not address the dependence of free workers of African descent on landowners, and it did not end racial prejudice. It illustrates both the radical potential of Guerrero's presidency and the constraints on it, since a government that could not pay its soldiers could hardly enforce emancipation in distant provinces.

Section 59.18

The Spanish Reconquest Attempt of 1829

In July 1829, a Spanish expeditionary force of roughly three thousand men under Brigadier Isidro Barradas, sailing from Havana, landed on the coast of Tamaulipas near Cabo Rojo and advanced toward Tampico. Spain had never accepted Mexican independence, and Ferdinand VII's government hoped that the divided and impoverished republic might welcome the restoration of royal authority. The expedition counted on popular support that did not materialize, as the Mexican population, whatever its quarrels, showed little desire to return to Spanish rule, and Mexican forces and local militia gathered to oppose the invaders.

The campaign combined military and environmental factors. The Spanish troops, landing in the hot, humid season, were struck by yellow fever, malaria, and dysentery, which killed many and weakened others. Supplies were short, and the expedition could not move inland. Mexican commanders, including Manuel Mier y Terán, then in charge of the eastern region, and Santa Anna, who marched from Veracruz at the head of troops and volunteers, converged on Tampico. After a series of engagements around the port, Barradas capitulated on 11 September 1829 at Pueblo Viejo, and the surviving Spaniards were allowed to leave. Spain made no further serious attempt to reconquer Mexico.

The victory had large political consequences. Santa Anna, who had been defeated in Oaxaca the year before, now emerged as the "Hero of Tampico," a title that enhanced his reputation and made him the most popular military figure in the republic, although the contribution of Mier y Terán and the effects of disease on the Spanish army were at least as important. The invasion also strengthened the hand of the government in the short term, since it justified extraordinary powers, the expulsion of Spaniards, and the abolition decree. It rallied national sentiment around the republic, though not for long.

In the longer view, the failed reconquest had both military and diplomatic effects. It confirmed that Spain could not recover Mexico by force, which encouraged other European states to treat the republic as permanent. It also deepened the fiscal strain of mobilization, forcing the government to raise and equip troops and to expand its army, which later became an instrument against the government itself. The Army of Reserve assembled at Jalapa under Vice President Bustamante in the aftermath of the crisis, nominally to defend against new invasions, became the instrument of the next overthrow.

Section 59.19

The Plan of Jalapa, Bustamante's Government, and Guerrero's Execution

In early December 1829, units of the Army of Reserve at Jalapa pronounced against the government of Guerrero. The Plan of Jalapa, issued on 4 December, charged that the president had exceeded his powers by using the extraordinary faculties granted for the emergency, demanded the revocation of those powers, the observance of the constitution and the laws, and the removal of officials who had abused their authority. It was a conservative and legalist protest, criticizing the executive for unconstitutional rule while itself resting on a military revolt. Vice President Bustamante took command of the movement. Guerrero, lacking reliable forces in the capital, left Mexico City to rally support in the south, and in January 1830 Bustamante assumed the executive.

Congress, now under the control of Bustamante's allies, declared Guerrero morally unable to govern, a formula that gave the transfer a legal veneer. The new government drew on conservatives, moderate liberals, and army officers, among them Lucas Alamán, who as minister of interior and foreign relations became its most influential figure. It restored a measure of order, curbed the press, persecuted Yorkino leaders, and returned many Spaniards to favor, while keeping the formal framework of the federal constitution. It also prosecuted political enemies, and several states that remained loyal to Guerrero, especially in the south, rejected its legitimacy.

Guerrero's fate was the grimmest outcome of these struggles. After raising a rebellion in the southern mountains that proved hard to suppress, he was betrayed in early 1831 by Francesco Picaluga, a Genoese ship captain who was paid to lure him aboard his vessel, the Colombo, at Acapulco and deliver him to government forces. Guerrero was brought to Oaxaca, court-martialed, and shot at Cuilápam on 14 February 1831. The execution of a former president who had been a hero of independence stunned many and alienated liberals and southern communities, who regarded it as judicial murder. Alamán and his ministry bore responsibility, though historians debate the degree to which Alamán personally planned the betrayal.

The affair raised questions of constitutional legitimacy that haunted the rest of the period. The Plan of Jalapa claimed to defend the constitution but replaced a president by military pronouncement. The government that followed claimed to restore legality but used the machinery of the state to kill its predecessor. Conservatives argued that Guerrero had violated the constitution and endangered order, while liberals argued that Bustamante's government was founded on rebellion and tyranny. Both claims contained truth, which is why the episode is a useful test of how historians separate constitutional argument from partisan interest. For the south, where Guerrero's memory remained strong and where Juan Álvarez would later lead a long liberal movement, the execution created enduring bitterness.

Section 59.20

Lucas Alamán, Industrial Policy, and the Civil War of 1832

Lucas Alamán, born in Guanajuato and educated in mining and the sciences in Europe, was the most capable administrator of Bustamante's government and a leading conservative thinker. He believed that Mexico needed order, a strong executive, a close relationship with the Church, and economic development to sustain independence. His policies combined protection of the established social order with an ambitious program of modernization, and he is often described as having tried to build a conservative developmental state. He also administered with a heavy hand, curbing the press, ordering the arrest of opponents, and relying on the army.

The centerpiece of his economic program was the Banco de Avío, established in October 1830. Funded by a portion of the duties on imported cotton goods, it offered loans to entrepreneurs who wished to establish textile and other industrial enterprises, and it imported machinery and technicians from Europe. The bank helped to establish spinning mills in Puebla, Veracruz, and elsewhere, and it fostered the beginnings of mechanized production. Its achievements were modest, since many loans were not repaid and political instability limited its capital, and it was abolished in 1842. Still, it represented an early attempt to use state credit to promote industry, in a country where most capital was tied up in land, mining, commerce, and the Church.

Opposition to Bustamante's government grew. Liberals resented the repression and the death of Guerrero, federalists in states such as Zacatecas and Jalisco resented the government's centralizing tendencies, and ambitious generals saw opportunity. In January 1832, Santa Anna pronounced at Veracruz against Bustamante's ministers, claiming to defend the constitution against a tyrannical cabinet. The movement spread, and a civil war followed through 1832, with battles in Veracruz, San Luis Potosí, and elsewhere. Federal forces won some engagements but could not suppress the rising. The government's position was weakened by the death of Mier y Terán, one of its capable officers, who took his own life in July 1832 in Padilla, Tamaulipas.

The civil war was settled by negotiation. In December 1832, the Convention of Zavaleta, signed between Santa Anna's forces and the government's commanders, recognized Manuel Gómez Pedraza, the winner of the election of 1828, as president for the three months remaining of his term, in effect restoring the result that had been overturned in 1828. Gómez Pedraza returned from exile and took office in January 1833. Elections held in the spring chose Santa Anna as president and Valentín Gómez Farías as vice president. The resolution showed how the pronunciamiento could end in compromise, and how a general who had overthrown one government could present himself as the guardian of constitutional legality. It also set the stage for the experiment in liberal reform that Gómez Farías would attempt in 1833.

Division V — Liberal Reform, Conservative Reaction, and Centralism, 1833–1836

The centralist republic did not appear suddenly in December 1836. It grew from the reversal of a reform program, from military conflict with the states, and from a series of constitutional measures between 1834 and 1836.

Section 59.21

Santa Anna and Valentín Gómez Farías: The Divided Executive

The elections of early 1833 produced an unusual executive. Santa Anna, by now the republic's most popular general, was elected president, and Valentín Gómez Farías, a physician from Guadalajara and a leading liberal, was elected vice president. The state legislatures that chose them were dominated by supporters of the movement that had overthrown Bustamante and by federalist politicians who wanted reform. Santa Anna took the oath on 1 April 1833, but almost immediately withdrew to his hacienda at Manga de Clavo in Veracruz, leaving the government in the hands of his vice president. He returned to the capital at intervals and left again, so that authority alternated between the two men over the following year.

The arrangement served both. Santa Anna preferred to hold the prestige of the presidency without the burdens of daily administration, and he appeared willing to let others bear the political cost of unpopular measures. He may also have wished to see how the political winds blew before committing himself. Gómez Farías, for his part, used the opportunity to advance an ambitious program, supported by the intellectual leadership of José María Luis Mora, a priest turned liberal theorist, and by Zavala, who had returned to politics. The relationship between president and vice president was one of mutual convenience, not shared conviction, and its fragility contained the seeds of the reaction that followed.

Behind the executive lay competing visions of the state. Liberals of Mora's school believed that the colonial inheritance, above all the privileges of the Church and army and the corporate organization of society, blocked progress. They wished to create a society of individual citizens governed by laws, with public education controlled by the state and the army's political power curbed. Conservatives and many moderates saw the Church and army as the foundation of order and national identity and feared that attacking them would unleash social conflict. Between them stood many who supported federalism and republican forms but disliked the pace and style of the reformers.

The social setting of 1833 was grim. A cholera epidemic that arrived in the summer of 1833 killed thousands in Mexico City and in other towns, and the government's response, along with the Church's, shaped public opinion. Many clergy and ordinary Catholics interpreted the epidemic in religious terms, while the government, relying on public health measures and dismissing the more providential explanations, appeared to some as heedless of popular piety. Economic hardship continued, with the treasury empty and the army unpaid, and the reformers had to act in an atmosphere of fear and discontent.

Section 59.22

The Liberal Reform Program of 1833

Between mid-1833 and early 1834, the Congress and Gómez Farías enacted a series of measures aimed at reducing the power of the Church and the army and at promoting secular public education. In October 1833 the government ended the civil enforcement of tithe payments, which made tithing voluntary in law and deprived the Church of a crucial source of income backed by the state. It ended the civil enforcement of monastic vows, so that individuals could leave convents and monasteries without being compelled by state authority to return. It asserted the right of the government to make or approve clerical appointments, claiming the patronato, the royal patronage of the colonial era, as inherited by the republic, over the objections of bishops who argued that only the pope could confer such authority.

The reformers also overhauled education. In October 1833 they closed the Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico, regarding it as a stronghold of colonial scholasticism, and created a Directorate of Public Instruction to oversee a new system of schools and colleges, with the aim of teaching modern sciences and fostering civic values. They secularized the missions of California by a law of August 1833, which sought to transfer the missions' lands and Indigenous laborers to civil administration, a measure whose consequences for Indigenous people in the region were severe, as mission lands were taken by ranchers and the former mission residents lost protection. The reformers also attacked the military's privileges, by proposing to reduce the army and to curb the military fuero, and they expelled certain political opponents by a law of June 1833 that listed named individuals.

The fiscal implications were important. The reformers hoped that the Church's vast property, estimated in some accounts to include a large portion of urban real estate and many rural estates, might provide revenue for the state, and they debated measures to bring clerical property under national control. Most proposals for confiscation were not enacted in this period, but the threat alarmed the Church and its supporters, and it foreshadowed the later reforms of the 1850s. The reforms also showed the limits of the state's capacity: lacking an administrative apparatus to take over Church functions such as education and civil registration, the government could dismantle old institutions faster than it could build new ones.

The reforms met strong opposition. Bishops, cathedral chapters, and religious orders protested, and many parish priests preached against the government. The ranks of the army saw the attack on the fuero as an attack on their status. Conservative politicians and many moderates, even among those who favored limited reform, thought that the measures were too radical and too hasty. Some ordinary Catholics, particularly in rural areas, regarded the closure of convents and the attacks on clergy as impious. Others, particularly in the cities, supported the reformers, so the picture was not a simple contest between pious masses and anticlerical elites. The reform program's strength lay with a small group of intellectuals and politicians, and its weakness lay in its lack of an organized popular base or of an army willing to enforce it.

Historical Vocabulary
Fuero

A fuero was a legal privilege or jurisdiction granted to a corporate group, the best known being the fuero eclesiástico, which allowed clergy to be tried in Church courts, and the fuero militar, which gave soldiers their own tribunals. Other corporate bodies, such as merchants' guilds and miners, had their own jurisdictions in the colonial period.

Fueros embodied a society of corporations rather than equal individual citizens. Liberals attacked them as incompatible with equality before the law, while defenders considered them necessary safeguards of the Church's independence and of military discipline. The slogan "religion and fueros," heard in 1834, tied the defense of the faith to the defense of privileges. Students should not read the term as "rights" in the modern sense. Fueros were privileges attached to membership in particular groups.

Section 59.23

The Plan of Cuernavaca and the Conservative Reaction of 1834

Opposition crystallized in the first months of 1834. In several towns, garrison commanders and local notables issued pronouncements calling for the repeal of the reform laws and the defense of religion and the fueros. The most important was the Plan of Cuernavaca, proclaimed on 25 May 1834 in that town to the south of the capital, which denounced recent religious-reform and political-proscription laws, appealed to Santa Anna to protect its demands, and called on the legislators and officials responsible to leave their posts. The broader movement rallied behind religion and corporate privileges, while the plan itself presented the legislature as having exceeded its constitutional authority and appealed to the president for protection.

Santa Anna had returned to the capital in April 1834 and now played a double game with characteristic skill. He had allowed the reforms to proceed while he was away, and he now accepted the call of the garrisons, distancing himself from Gómez Farías and from the measures. He displaced Gómez Farías from the exercise of executive power, dismissed reformist ministers, annulled many of the laws, and at the end of May 1834 shut down the Congress that had passed them. Gómez Farías left for the United States. Santa Anna presented himself as the defender of order and the Church against radicals, though he had been elected on a liberal platform and had previously supported federalism.

Why did Santa Anna reverse course? Historians point to several factors. The opposition was strong in the army and the Church, which he needed. The reform program lacked a social base. Santa Anna's politics were pragmatic and personal, aimed at preserving his position as the arbiter of national politics, and he recognized that the conservatives could offer him greater support. Some observers, including liberals, saw the reversal as betrayal, while others argued that he merely followed the national mood. The episode illustrated both the opportunism of leading politicians and the genuine weight of the defense of the Church in Mexican society.

The consequences were significant. New elections in 1834 produced a Congress with a conservative majority, which met in early 1835, and the political center of gravity shifted away from federalism. The reaction defeated Gómez Farías's program but did not restore the old order, since the controversy over Church property and the limits of clerical privilege remained. It also reshaped the political spectrum, as moderate federalists who had supported reform now looked to Santa Anna, and liberals who favored federalism and secularization were driven into opposition. When Santa Anna took leave in January 1835, Miguel Barragán served as interim president, and the government moved steadily toward a constitution that would abandon federalism.

Section 59.24

Federalist Resistance and the Zacatecas Campaign of 1835

Federalism did not give way without resistance. Several states, led by Zacatecas, Jalisco, Coahuila, and Durango, continued to defend the federal system and the 1824 constitution against the conservative Congress and Santa Anna's government. The center of resistance was Zacatecas, a rich mining state where former governor Francisco García Salinas (1829–1834) had developed the state’s administration and militia. Though no longer governor, he commanded its militia forces during the 1835 crisis. Zacatecas became a model for federalists, and a threat for centralizers, who saw in it a state able to defy the capital.

The conflict came to a head over the militias. In March 1835, the national Congress, acting at Santa Anna's urging, passed a law reducing the civic militias to a small fraction of their size, about one man per five hundred inhabitants, and ordering the surplus to disarm. For federalists the measure was an assault on state sovereignty, depriving the states of their means of defense. The legislature of Zacatecas refused to comply and mobilized its militia, which numbered several thousand men, to resist. Other states, wary of confronting the government, largely complied, and Zacatecas was left alone.

Santa Anna marched against Zacatecas at the head of several thousand regular troops and, on 11 May 1835, defeated the state militia at the battle of Guadalupe, near the city. His soldiers then occupied and sacked Zacatecas, allowing looting by troops. The government dissolved the state's authorities, replaced them with compliant officials, and punished the state by detaching the district of Aguascalientes and making it a separate territory. The sack was brutal, with reports of killings and plunder, and it created a bitter memory that federalists would recall in the coming years. Resistance in other states collapsed or shrank.

The consequences for federalism were decisive. The defeat of the strongest state militia demonstrated that no state could resist a national army, and it removed the main armed defenders of the federal system. It also showed how closely the fate of federalism was tied to the control of armed force. For the Texans watching these events, the defeat of Zacatecas and the dissolution of state authority signaled that the constitutional framework under which they had settled was being dismantled, a point taken up in Division VI. Santa Anna emerged as the military victor of the conservative cause, with the means to impose a new constitutional order.

Section 59.25

The End of Federalism and the Seven Laws of 1836

The conservative Congress, elected in 1834 and convened in January 1835, turned itself into a constituent body in September 1835. It declared that the nation wished to change the form of government, though most deputies and some political leaders would have claimed that it was merely adjusting the constitution. On 23 October 1835 it adopted the Bases for a new constitution, which abolished the federal system, replacing the states with departments governed by officials appointed from the capital, and which continued the separation of powers while strengthening the executive. The bases set the framework for a more detailed text, which Congress prepared over the next year.

The new constitution, known as the Siete Leyes, or Seven Laws, was promulgated in stages, the first law in December 1835 and the remainder in December 1836. The first defined the rights and duties of Mexicans and the qualifications for citizenship, requiring an annual income of at least one hundred pesos for the vote and excluding domestic servants. The second created the Supreme Conservative Power, a body of five members empowered to nullify acts of the other branches, to suspend Congress, and to declare the president incapacitated, and answerable, according to the text, to no ordinary legal process. The third set up a bicameral legislature, with property qualifications for deputies and senators set far higher than for voters.

The fourth law established the executive, a president elected for eight years from lists drawn up by the president, the council of government, the Senate, and the Supreme Court, with a minimum annual-income qualification of four thousand pesos. The fifth reorganized the judiciary under a Supreme Court. The sixth divided the country into departments and districts, with governors appointed by the president from lists submitted by departmental juntas, and municipal governments reorganized with reduced independence. The seventh prohibited any changes to the constitution for six years. These provisions marked a deliberate move toward a strong, indirectly elected executive, a property-based citizenship, and a central bureaucracy.

The Seven Laws were the product of conservative thinkers who believed that the federal experiment had produced disorder and fiscal weakness and that Mexico needed a more disciplined structure. The Supreme Conservative Power reflected a distrust of both popular majorities and strong presidents, an attempt to create an arbiter above the factions, but in practice it proved ineffective and ill-defined. The reorganization also provoked resistance in the provinces, for departments had less autonomy than states, and the system relied on the cooperation of local elites who often lacked enthusiasm. The new order was bound to face rebellion from federalists, as in Texas, Yucatán, and the northeast, and it would last only a few years.

Division VI — Mexican Texas: Settlement, Sovereignty, Slavery, and Revolution, 1821–1836

Texas joins Mexican state-building, immigration, slavery, Indigenous sovereignty, federalism, armed conflict, and international relations. It is treated here principally as a conflict within Mexican and borderland history, not merely as a prelude to United States expansion.

Section 59.26

The Northern Borderlands and Tejano Society

When Mexico became independent, Texas was a thinly settled province of the far north, administratively joined after 1824 with Coahuila as the state of Coahuila y Texas, with its capital at Saltillo and later Monclova. Its Spanish-speaking population, the Tejanos, numbered only a few thousand and lived chiefly in three clusters: San Antonio de Béxar, the largest settlement, with its missions and presidio; La Bahía, near Goliad on the lower San Antonio River; and Nacogdoches in the east, near the old border with Louisiana. Many Tejano families had lived in the province for generations, raised cattle and horses on large ranches, and traded with Coahuila and Louisiana. Their economy depended on ranching and on local commerce, and they maintained a society of extended families and patrons.

The province was never a space governed only by Mexicans. Much of Texas lay within the domain of independent Indigenous nations, above all the Comanche, whose power had expanded across the southern plains in the eighteenth century and who dominated the trade in horses, hides, and captives. Apache groups, Wichita and other peoples, and the Karankawa of the coast also lived in or around the province, and some eastern Indigenous nations displaced from the United States, such as the Cherokee, Alabama, and Coushatta, had settled in East Texas. Spain had maintained a precarious peace with the Comanche from the 1780s through gifts and trade, but this system weakened after independence as the Mexican government lacked the means to continue it, and raids and counter-raids multiplied in the 1820s and 1830s.

Frontier defense was thus a central concern of Tejanos and of Mexican authorities. Presidial garrisons were small and poorly supplied, and Tejano families relied on local militias and their own arms. Comanche raids reached deep into Coahuila, Nuevo León, Durango, and Chihuahua, causing losses of livestock and lives, and northern states experimented with policies such as bounties for scalps and attempts to negotiate peace. In this context, the arrival of settlers who might strengthen the population and defend the frontier appeared attractive to Mexican officials, a calculation that would later prove costly.

Tejano political life was shaped by the Spanish constitutional experience and by local family networks. Leading figures such as Erasmo Seguín of Béxar and his son Juan, José Antonio Navarro, and José Francisco Ruiz participated in provincial politics, served as municipal officials, and later in the national Congress. Women of Tejano households held property under Spanish civil law, ran ranches as widows, and managed family estates, and some founded settlements, as did Patricia de la Garza de León at Victoria. Indigenous and mixed-ancestry people also formed part of Tejano communities, and the province's social hierarchy, though less rigid than in central Mexico, still privileged landowning families. The Tejanos were not a homogeneous group, and their divisions of interest and loyalty would prove crucial in the crisis of the 1830s.

Section 59.27

Empresarios, Land Grants, and Anglo-American Colonization

The Spanish crown had already begun to consider settlement of Texas by outsiders, and in 1821 Moses Austin, a Missouri entrepreneur, received permission from the colonial authorities to bring three hundred families. He died in June 1821, and his son Stephen F. Austin took up the project, obtaining confirmation from the new Mexican government. In 1823 the imperial government and then Congress passed colonization measures that offered land to settlers who swore to obey Mexican laws and to profess the Catholic religion, and Austin received a grant for his colony, settling the first families, known as the "Old Three Hundred," along the Brazos and Colorado rivers.

The national colonization law of 18 August 1824 set the framework. It invited foreign colonists, left the details of settlement to the states, prohibited colonization within a set distance of the national boundary and the coast without the approval of the federal government, and gave preferences to Mexican settlers. Coahuila y Texas passed its own colonization law in March 1825, providing for contracts with empresarios, agents who undertook to bring a specified number of families in return for land premiums. Several empresarios obtained contracts, including Green DeWitt, Haden Edwards, and others. Settlers received large grants by the standards of the United States, often a league of ranching land or a smaller farm labor, for modest fees.

Mexican officials had several reasons to encourage settlement. They hoped to populate a vast, nearly empty province, create a buffer against Comanche raids, develop an export economy in cotton, and strengthen ties to the nation. Many believed that settlers who accepted Mexican citizenship and laws would assimilate. They also feared the alternative, that the United States would expand into the region. The Mexican government declined offers by Poinsett and others to buy Texas, but its ability to control settlement was limited by distance, lack of funds, and corruption.

The settlers who came were overwhelmingly from the southern United States, drawn by cheap land and the prospect of cotton cultivation. Many brought enslaved people. Most were Protestants who nominally accepted Catholicism, and few learned Spanish, though some Anglo men married Tejano women and became integrated into local families. By the late 1820s, tensions had appeared. The Fredonian Rebellion of 1826–1827, in which Haden Edwards and a small group tried to set up an independent republic at Nacogdoches after a dispute over land titles with Tejano landowners, was suppressed with the help of Austin's colonists and Mexican troops, but it confirmed Mexican fears. In 1828 Manuel de Mier y Terán, sent to inspect the frontier, reported that Anglo influence was growing dangerously and recommended measures to restrict immigration, promote Mexican and European settlement, and strengthen garrisons, a report that influenced the law of 1830.

Historical Vocabulary
Empresario

An empresario (Spanish, "entrepreneur" or "contractor") was an agent who contracted with a Mexican state government to bring a specified number of families to settle a defined tract of land, receiving land premiums in return. Moses and Stephen F. Austin, Green DeWitt, and Haden Edwards were among the empresarios in Texas.

The system was a Mexican policy adapted from Spanish practice, and empresarios were responsible to state authorities for the character and loyalty of their settlers. It is therefore misleading to describe the settlers as simple invaders. They entered under Mexican law, though their numbers, customs, and concentration made them a distinctive community. Students should distinguish the legal framework of colonization from the later political conflicts that arose within it.

Section 59.28

Slavery, Immigration Restrictions, and the Law of April 6, 1830

Slavery was the most explosive issue in Texas. The Mexican government had moved toward restriction from independence, with the prohibition of the slave trade in 1824 and various state measures. The constitution of Coahuila y Texas, adopted in 1827, declared that no one would be born a slave in the state, and banned the introduction of slaves after six months, though it did not free those already held. Settlers sought ways around these rules. A common device was to bring enslaved people under indenture contracts, signed in the United States or on arrival, by which individuals agreed to serve for very long terms, sometimes ninety-nine years, and the legislature of Coahuila y Texas tolerated or even regulated such contracts. Slaveholders thus preserved bondage under another name.

Guerrero's abolition decree of September 1829 alarmed Texas slaveholders, who protested, and Austin and local officials sought exemption. The central government in the end allowed Texas to be excluded or the decree to be suspended in the province in practice, a decision whose precise form is discussed by historians. Slavery remained legal in practice in Texas, which meant that an issue on which the republic had taken a stand was left to local accommodation. The fact that the largest group of enslaved people in Mexico lived in the one province where Anglo-American settlement was growing most quickly gave the issue disproportionate weight.

The Law of 6 April 1830, associated with Lucas Alamán and based partly on Mier y Terán's report, was the central government's attempt to regain control. It prohibited further immigration from the United States into Texas, with exceptions for those already contracted, called for the settlement of Mexican families and convicts, strengthened garrisons, authorized new customs houses at ports such as Anahuac, and ended the exemption from customs duties that settlers had enjoyed. It also provided for the prohibition of the further introduction of slaves. The law sought to reassert federal authority and to slow the demographic shift that threatened Mexican control.

The law's results were mixed. Its enforcement was weak, since Mexico lacked the troops, money, and administrative capacity to prevent entry across the Sabine and along the coast, and speculators found ways around the restrictions. Colonists reacted angrily to the customs duties and to garrisons, especially at Anahuac, where disputes with the commander, Colonel Juan Davis Bradburn, led to armed clashes in 1832. In 1833, following petitions led by Austin, the central government repealed the restriction on immigration, effective in 1834. The episode demonstrated the dilemma of Mexican policy: restricting colonization alienated settlers, while allowing it increased their number. The law's enforcement problem also reveals how fiscal and administrative weakness shaped even the most sensitive questions of sovereignty.

Section 59.29

Tejano Politics, Federalist Resistance, and the Crisis of 1832–1835

The political crisis in Texas developed in the context of the national conflicts of the early 1830s. In 1832, when Santa Anna pronounced against Bustamante in the name of the constitution, settlers at Anahuac and elsewhere declared their support for him, using the language of federalism and constitutional defense to justify action against local commanders. The Mexican garrisons in Texas withdrew or were expelled, and for a time Texas was practically governed by its own committees. Settlers presented their grievances as a demand for the restoration of constitutional order, an argument that resonated with Mexican federalists, and in this early phase many Tejanos and Anglo settlers acted in parallel.

Conventions of settlers met in October 1832 and April 1833 to petition the government. They asked for the repeal of the immigration restrictions, for tariff relief, for better protection against Indigenous raids, and, in 1833, for separate statehood for Texas within the Mexican federation, so that the province would no longer be joined to Coahuila, whose legislature was seen as distant and hostile to Texan interests. Stephen F. Austin carried the petition to Mexico City in 1833 and negotiated with the government of Gómez Farías and Santa Anna, obtaining the repeal of the immigration ban and some administrative reforms, though not statehood. On his return he wrote a letter to the municipality of Béxar urging the organization of a state government, which was intercepted, and he was arrested in January 1834 and held for many months without trial in Mexico City.

Tejano opinion was diverse. Some Tejano leaders, including Navarro and Seguín, favored the federalist cause, supported local autonomy, and shared some of the settlers' grievances against the Coahuila legislature and the centralizing trend in Mexico City. Others remained loyal to the government or were wary of the Anglo settlers' growing numbers and their land hunger. The land policies of the Coahuila y Texas legislature, which sold large tracts to speculators in 1834–1835, angered many of both communities. Meanwhile among the settlers a "peace party" under Austin favored negotiation, while a "war party" of younger and more radical men pressed for confrontation.

The transition from constitutional protest to armed rebellion occurred in 1835. The Zacatecas campaign and the dissolution of state legislatures convinced many that the constitutional guarantees were being destroyed. Santa Anna's centralizing measures, along with the arrival of troops and the arrest of political leaders, heightened fears. Austin, released in July 1835 and having returned to Texas, concluded that war was unavoidable. His shift illustrates how many settlers moved from loyalty to the federal constitution to the conviction that a centralist Mexico no longer offered a place for their interests. It also shows that the Texas rebellion began as part of the wider federalist resistance, though it would end as a separatist revolution.

Section 59.30

The Texas Revolution: Gonzales, Béxar, the Alamo, and Goliad

The fighting began on 2 October 1835 at Gonzales, where townspeople refused to return a small cannon that Mexican authorities had lent them for defense against Comanche raids and resisted a detachment sent to retrieve it. The skirmish was minor, but it triggered mobilization. Volunteers, many from the settlements and some from the United States, gathered and marched on San Antonio de Béxar, which they besieged for two months. In December 1835, after a fierce street fight in which Ben Milam died, the Mexican garrison under General Martín Perfecto de Cos capitulated. A provisional government, formed at a consultation in November, declared its adherence to the Constitution of 1824 and its resistance to centralist rule, a position that attracted federalist and Tejano support.

Santa Anna, determined to crush the rebellion, assembled an army of several thousand men and marched north in the winter of 1835–1836, in cold and with poor supplies. On 23 February 1836 he arrived at Béxar and besieged the old mission of the Alamo, where a garrison of roughly two hundred volunteers and regulars, including Tejanos, had remained under William Barret Travis and James Bowie, with David Crockett among them. After thirteen days the Mexican army stormed the fortification on 6 March 1836 and killed almost all the defenders. A few survivors, including Susanna Dickinson and her child, and enslaved people including Joe, who belonged to Travis, were spared. The assault cost the attackers heavy casualties, and the event became a powerful symbol in Texan and American memory, though its significance in the military campaign was limited.

Meanwhile the convention at Washington-on-the-Brazos declared independence on 2 March 1836. The delegates, who included Tejanos such as Navarro and Lorenzo de Zavala, the former Mexican liberal who now served as vice president of the ad interim government, drafted a constitution that protected slavery and restricted the rights of free Black people, and they made Sam Houston commander of the army. The declaration of independence listed grievances against Santa Anna and the centralist government, including the abolition of the federal constitution, the lack of trial by jury, and the failure to provide education and security, and it invoked the right of a people to form a new government. Its justification rested largely on the claim that Mexico had broken the compact of 1824, a claim that also had force among Mexican federalists.

At Goliad the Texan force of about three hundred and fifty under James Fannin, which had hesitated to leave its position, was caught by Mexican troops on the open prairie and surrendered on 20 March. On Santa Anna's order, carried out on 27 March, most of the prisoners, some three hundred and forty, were shot, an act that Texan opinion treated as a massacre and that later fueled the cry of vengeance. Civilians suffered as well. As the Mexican army advanced, Texan families fled eastward in the "Runaway Scrape," while Tejano residents of Béxar and the countryside faced suspicion from both sides and loss of property. The campaign demonstrated both the military capacity of the Mexican army and the fragility of its supply lines, which were stretched across hundreds of miles of difficult terrain.

Section 59.31

San Jacinto, the Treaties of Velasco, and the Unresolved Texas Question

Santa Anna divided his army to pursue the fleeing Texan government and the army under Houston, who retreated eastward for weeks, to the dismay of many of his own men. On 21 April 1836, Houston's force of roughly nine hundred men struck the Mexican camp at San Jacinto, near present-day Houston, catching Santa Anna's troops during their afternoon rest. The battle lasted perhaps twenty minutes, followed by a slaughter of retreating Mexican soldiers, with hundreds killed and many captured, in revenge for the Alamo and Goliad. Santa Anna was captured the next day, disguised as a common soldier. The defeat of this portion of the army, commanded by Santa Anna in person, ended the campaign as other Mexican forces under generals such as Vicente Filisola withdrew south.

Santa Anna, held prisoner, signed the Treaties of Velasco on 14 May 1836, a public treaty and a secret one, by which he agreed to end hostilities, withdraw Mexican troops beyond the Rio Grande, and, in the secret provisions, to work for the recognition of Texan independence. He later returned to Mexico in 1837 after a visit to Washington. The Mexican government and Congress repudiated the treaties as signed under duress by a captive who had no authority to cede territory, and Mexico refused to recognize Texas. The territorial claim was also disputed: Texas claimed the Rio Grande as its southern boundary, while Mexico considered the Nueces the historical limit of the province.

The consequences varied by group. For Anglo-American settlers, victory secured independence, and the Republic of Texas legalized slavery and encouraged immigration, with the result that the enslaved population grew. For Tejanos, the outcome was ambiguous and often harsh. Some, such as Seguín, who commanded a company at San Jacinto and later served as mayor of San Antonio, were pressured by newcomers and forced to leave amid hostility and accusations of disloyalty, while many lost land to speculators and immigrants. For the enslaved, the new republic offered little hope of freedom, though some sought refuge across the Rio Grande in Mexico, where slavery was illegal. For Indigenous communities, the republic adopted policies of dispossession and war, including against the Cherokee in 1839, and the Comanche continued to resist.

For Mexico, the loss of effective control over Texas was a severe blow to national prestige and an unresolved problem. Successive governments proclaimed their intention to reconquer the province but lacked the funds and unity to do so, and the raids and expeditions of the early 1840s, including Mexican forays to San Antonio in 1842 and the failed Texan Santa Fe and Mier expeditions, kept the conflict alive without resolving it. The United States, which recognized Texas in 1837, and European powers including Britain and France, which later recognized it, became involved in the question of the republic's future. The Texas question thus became a permanent feature of Mexican politics and of the relationship with the United States, a subject that returns in the final division and in Chapter 63.

Division VII — The Centralist Republic and Regional Resistance, 1837–1843

The centralist system promised discipline and order. It delivered neither, because it inherited the fiscal weakness of its predecessor, faced a foreign blockade, and provoked regional revolts in the very provinces it sought to integrate.

Section 59.32

Governing under Centralism: Debt, Administration, and Political Opposition

Anastasio Bustamante, returned from exile in Europe, was elected president under the Seven Laws and took office in April 1837, serving until 1841. His government tried to operate the new institutions: departments replaced states, governors were appointed from lists, departmental juntas advised them, and the Supreme Conservative Power sat as constitutional arbiter. In practice, the system depended on officials who often lacked local roots or authority. Many former state politicians, deprived of their legislatures, remained influential in their regions and used the local press and municipal councils to criticize the new order, while others cooperated in the hope of preserving what they could.

Fiscal pressures were relentless. The government inherited a defaulted foreign debt and a large internal debt, and it faced the military costs of the Texas war, the maintenance of the standing army, and the suppression of rebellions. Revenue collection was difficult. The centralist reorganization was supposed to give the national government control over taxation, but departmental authorities often retained funds for local needs, and the customs houses on which the treasury depended were subject to smuggling and to the political interests of their administrators. The government turned to forced loans, to the sale of the right to collect taxes, and to advances from agiotistas, lenders who charged high interest and acquired influence over fiscal policy.

The army remained both the government's mainstay and its chief danger. Centralism had been established with military backing, and it needed soldiers to put down revolts in Texas, the northeast, and the south, to guard against the French, and to maintain order. Officers received a large share of the budget, and recruitment relied on forced conscription, the leva, which fell on the poor and on villagers and provoked resistance. At the same time, ambitious generals, including Santa Anna, Mariano Paredes y Arrillaga, and Gabriel Valencia, held independent commands, and the executive could not take their loyalty for granted.

Opposition came from several directions. Federalists, who had lost the states, conspired and rebelled. Liberals attacked the property qualifications and the Supreme Conservative Power. Some conservatives were disappointed that the new constitution had not produced stability or prosperity. Provincial resentment arose in places that had enjoyed real autonomy, such as Yucatán, Zacatecas, and Jalisco, and in the north, where distance and Indigenous raiding made central direction appear irrelevant. Uprisings in California in 1836 and in New Mexico in 1837, where Governor Albino Pérez was killed in a revolt in which Pueblo and Hispano rebels briefly took power, showed that the new departments could not be assumed to accept direction from the capital. The centralist system, designed to curb disorder, proved unable to command the obedience on which it depended.

Section 59.33

The Pastry War and French Intervention, 1838–1839

The conflict with France began with the claims of foreign residents for damages suffered during Mexico's disorders. French merchants and others had presented demands for compensation for property destroyed or looted during riots and pronunciamientos, and among them was the claim of a pastry cook, Remontel, in Tacubaya, whose shop had been sacked by soldiers, which gave the war its mocking name. Other claims came from French businessmen. The Mexican government, short of funds and distracted by other crises, delayed settlement. The French government, under King Louis Philippe, took up the claims as a matter of national honor and commercial interest, and its representative, Baron Deffaudis, presented an ultimatum in early 1838 demanding a large indemnity, in the neighborhood of six hundred thousand pesos.

When Mexico refused to yield to the threat, France sent a naval squadron that blockaded Mexican ports on the Gulf beginning in April 1838. The blockade crippled customs revenue, on which the treasury depended, and encouraged smuggling through Texas and other routes. In November 1838, a French fleet under Rear Admiral Charles Baudin bombarded the fortress of San Juan de Ulúa, which surrendered after several hours, and on 5 December French landing parties seized Veracruz. Mexico declared war in the same month. The French action shows how a European power could exploit Mexican weakness to press commercial claims, although the extent of French ambitions was limited.

Santa Anna, who had been in political retirement at Manga de Clavo since the fall of federalism and the Texas disaster, hurried to Veracruz and took part in the fighting. In a skirmish against French troops during their withdrawal on 5 December, he was wounded and lost his left leg below the knee. He used the event with great effect, presenting himself as a patriot who had sacrificed himself for the nation, and the leg was later buried with solemn honors in Mexico City. The episode helped to rehabilitate his reputation after Texas, and he returned to the center of national politics. Critics were skeptical, but the public valued the image of a general who had fought a foreign enemy.

British mediation, motivated by trade interests, ended the war. A treaty signed on 9 March 1839 required Mexico to pay an indemnity of six hundred thousand pesos to settle French claims, and the French withdrew. Mexico had suffered economic damage from the blockade and had again displayed its military weakness, while France had achieved its financial aim. The war's political consequences were substantial: it discredited Bustamante's government, which had been unable to defend the coast, it strengthened Santa Anna's role as a national savior, and it worsened the financial crisis. It also illustrated the fragility of Mexico's position in a world where European powers used naval force to settle debts and claims.

Section 59.34

Yucatán, Tabasco, and the Federalist Challenge, 1839–1841

Regional resistance to centralism took its most serious form on the peripheries. In May 1839, a federalist revolt began in Yucatán, led by Santiago Imán at Tizimín, and it spread across the peninsula. Yucatán had a distinct regional identity and economy, based on maize cultivation, cattle, sugar, logwood, trade with Cuba and the Gulf, and a developing but not yet dominant henequen sector, and it felt little connection with Mexico City. Local elites resented the sending of troops to Texas and the tax demands of the centralist government, which seemed to benefit the capital at their expense, and they used the language of federalism and the 1824 constitution to defend their autonomy. Imán's rebels expelled centralist officials and, in 1841, Yucatán declared its independence from Mexico.

The Yucatecan revolt produced important political and legal innovations. In 1841, a constitution drafted by Manuel Crescencio Rejón and others established a liberal regime for the peninsula, with guarantees of individual rights, religious toleration, and an early form of the judicial remedy known as amparo, a protection of constitutional rights against government acts, which would later become a central institution of Mexican constitutional law. Yucatán negotiated with Texas, which sent a naval squadron to support it, and with foreign powers, showing that a Mexican region could conduct its own diplomacy. In 1843 the peninsula rejoined Mexico under conditions that preserved considerable autonomy, though separatist politics would resurface later.

Tabasco, to the west, saw a federalist movement in the same period, led by local leaders who also rejected centralist authority. In the northeast, federalists in Tamaulipas, Nuevo León, and Coahuila, among them Antonio Canales, declared the Republic of the Rio Grande in January 1840, aspiring to create an independent federal republic. The Republic of the Rio Grande lasted less than a year and collapsed after military defeats, but it illustrated the depth of regional discontent. In Zacatecas, Jalisco, and elsewhere, opposition was expressed through conspiracies and uprisings. Many of these movements were driven by local revenue interests as much as by ideology, since regional elites wanted to retain customs duties and taxes.

The uneven reach of national authority is the main lesson. Centralism assumed a capital able to direct the provinces, yet the provinces possessed their own economic ties, local militias, and political traditions. Where the national army was strong, as in the central valleys, the government prevailed. Where distance, sea routes, or local solidarity favored regional leaders, centralism was resisted or ignored. Indigenous communities also acted within these struggles, sometimes allied with one side, sometimes pursuing their own demands, a theme that would erupt dramatically in Yucatán in 1847 with the Caste War, outside this chapter's period. The federalist challenge forced the central government to consider concessions and weakened the legitimacy of the Seven Laws.

Section 59.35

The Bases of Tacubaya and the Organic Bases of 1843

Dissatisfaction with Bustamante's government culminated in 1841. In August, General Mariano Paredes y Arrillaga pronounced at Guadalajara against the administration, and Santa Anna and General Gabriel Valencia joined in, Valencia seizing the Ciudadela in Mexico City. After weeks of fighting and negotiation, the leaders of the movement agreed on a plan at Tacubaya, the Bases of Tacubaya, signed on 28 September 1841. The bases declared the powers of the existing government at an end, entrusted provisional authority to a junta of notables, and gave the provisional executive, in practice Santa Anna, very broad powers until a new constituent Congress could draw up a constitution. Bustamante yielded, and Santa Anna became provisional president in October 1841.

The Bases of Tacubaya were thus a temporary arrangement, not a constitution. They suspended the Seven Laws without replacing them with a permanent text and called for a Congress to write a new one. That Congress met in June 1842 and was elected on terms that produced a body with a substantial liberal and federalist element, including Mariano Otero and others who wished to restore a federal system with strong guarantees of individual rights. The deputies drafted several projects, including one favoring federalism, and clashed with Santa Anna and his supporters, who wanted a strong executive. Santa Anna, who had withdrawn from the capital to his estates, left the interim presidency to Nicolás Bravo and watched.

The struggle ended in a military solution. At the end of 1842, garrisons across the country pronounced against the Congress, and on 19 December 1842 the interim government dissolved it. In its place, Santa Anna's circle appointed a Junta of Notables, drawn largely from conservative landowners, clergy, officers, and officials, which drafted a new constitution. The resulting Bases Orgánicas, or Organic Bases of the Mexican Republic, were sanctioned in June 1843. They retained the centralist structure of departments and the exclusive status of the Catholic religion, abolished the Supreme Conservative Power, strengthened the executive with a veto and wide appointive powers, set higher property qualifications for officeholders, and restricted the press.

The difference between the Bases de Tacubaya of 1841 and the Bases Orgánicas of 1843 is essential. The first was a provisional political agreement that dissolved an existing arrangement and concentrated authority in a temporary executive, while the second was a full constitution drafted by an appointed body after the dissolution of an elected one. The 1843 settlement concentrated authority in the president more than the Seven Laws had, replacing the unpopular Supreme Conservative Power with executive prerogative, and it reflected the preferences of conservatives and of Santa Anna rather than of a representative assembly. Its history shows how constitutional change in these years was driven by pronunciamientos and by the dissolution of elected bodies rather than by durable consensus.

Section 59.36

The Republic from Below: Economy, Society, and Uneven State Authority

Most of the republic's roughly seven million inhabitants experienced politics through taxes, conscription, and the local balance of power. Indigenous villages, the majority of the rural population in the center and south, continued to farm communal and household plots, to supply labor to haciendas and mines, and to defend their lands through litigation. Repeated changes of regime meant that each government sought to collect taxes, and that village officials had to negotiate with whichever authorities held power. Some communities used the competition to advance their claims, while others suffered from the demands of recruiters and tax collectors. The state's reach was uneven: in the central valleys, it was an everyday presence, while in the remote sierras and deserts it was rare.

Towns and cities were arenas of economic and political life. Artisans, who made up a large share of urban populations, petitioned for protective tariffs against foreign textiles and manufactures, and they supported measures that kept out imports, though merchants and consumers favored lower duties. The tariff debates of the 1830s and 1840s pitted artisans and nascent manufacturers, supported by Alamán's allies, against importers and many liberals. Merchants, including a growing community of British, French, German, and North American traders in Veracruz and Mexico City, dominated commerce and credit, and the agiotistas who lent to the government amassed fortunes. Mining recovered slowly, and by the 1840s silver production approached earlier levels in some districts though with difficulty.

Women's lives reflected both continuity and the pressures of instability. They continued to supply the markets and workshops, to run households when men were conscripted or killed, and to petition authorities for pensions, for the return of requisitioned property, and for relief. Widows of officers, and sometimes of ordinary soldiers, submitted claims that reveal the human cost of civil conflict. The heavy reliance on forced conscription, drawing men from villages and towns into an army that might not pay them, left many households without male labor. Women also took part in religious life as organizers of confraternities, patrons of charity, and participants in processions, which anchored communities during political turbulence.

Landowners, soldiers, and frontier populations experienced the republic differently again. Large landowners generally benefited from the continued weakness of the state, protecting their estates against taxation and negotiating with whichever regime held power, though they also suffered from forced loans and the disruption of trade. Soldiers, many of them conscripts, endured poor pay and harsh discipline, while officers sought promotion and political influence. On the northern frontier, Comanche and Apache raiding, along with the withdrawal of resources toward the interior and Texas, left communities to defend themselves, and some states paid bounties for Indigenous scalps, practices that intensified violence. The republic existed unevenly across these social worlds, and its authority depended on negotiation more than command.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxes, Recruits, Markets, and Fiestas: Living through Instability

For villagers, the most direct contact with the state came through the tax collector and the recruiter. Alcabalas and customs duties raised the price of goods in markets, while the late 1830s and 1840s brought new levies, including head taxes and taxes on doors and windows that fell heavily on urban householders and provoked protest. The leva, the roundup of men for the army, struck poor laborers, artisans, and villagers, and families sought exemptions through influence or by paying substitutes. Armies moving through the countryside requisitioned grain, mules, and fodder, and bandits and deserters operated on the roads, so that civil conflict meant insecurity and loss for many who had no political voice.

Local life nevertheless continued around markets, parish churches, and municipal elections. Weekly markets linked villages to towns, with women prominent as sellers of food, cloth, and pottery. Religious festivals, held for patron saints and in the liturgical calendar, structured the year and expressed community identity, often with help from confraternities. Local elections, held under rules that excluded many people, still drew in groups of voters, and village officials negotiated with higher authorities over taxes, land, and labor. Evidence for these experiences comes from petitions, parish registers, notarial records, and court cases rather than from the pronunciamientos that dominate political narratives, and it shows people adapting to political change while keeping local institutions alive.

Section 59.37

Santa Anna's Return, Political Exhaustion, and the Crisis of 1844–1845

Under the Organic Bases, Santa Anna was elected president at the end of 1843 and ruled, with interruptions during which others held the office, until late 1844. His government was marked by extraordinary measures. It strengthened the army, raising expenditure sharply, and it enforced obedience in the press and in the departments. To pay for these commitments and for a possible campaign against Texas, it imposed new taxes, including a head tax on adults and levies on doors and windows, on property, and on professions. The taxes provoked protest in many departments, and the government's reliance on loans from agiotistas deepened the sense that it served moneylenders and soldiers. Pomp surrounded the president, including lavish ceremonies and the elaborate burial of his amputated leg and monuments in his honor.

Elite opposition grew. Conservatives who had supported him as a guarantor of order resented his autocratic style and taxes. Liberals, excluded from power, wrote pamphlets and used the press and Congress to criticize him. Army officers divided between those who benefited from his patronage and those who resented favorites. Regional leaders, including the commanders of Jalisco and other departments, chafed under central control. A Congress that met under the Organic Bases proved less compliant than he had hoped, and disputes over taxation and over the Texas question strained relations between the executive and the legislature.

The crisis came in the fall of 1844. In November, General Paredes y Arrillaga pronounced at Guadalajara against Santa Anna's government, and the movement spread. Congress, which Santa Anna sought to prorogue, supported the opposition, and in December crowds in Mexico City turned against the regime, tearing down his statues and, according to contemporary accounts, disinterring the remains of his leg. Santa Anna left the capital to confront the rebels but was abandoned by troops and captured in January 1845 near Xico in Veracruz. José Joaquín de Herrera, a moderate general who held the presidency of the council, assumed the executive in December 1844, and Santa Anna was ultimately exiled to Cuba.

Herrera's government pursued a cautious course, seeking to stabilize finances and to avoid war with the United States. It nevertheless faced the same underlying problems: an empty treasury, an army whose loyalty was uncertain, and regional resistance, along with a nationalist press that denounced any concession over Texas. Santa Anna's fall showed that even the strongest executive could not survive the combination of fiscal strain, elite desertion, and military pronouncement. It also showed that overthrowing a regime did not solve the structural problems that had undermined it, since the next government inherited them intact, and in December 1845 General Paredes would in turn overthrow Herrera in the name of national honor over Texas.

Section 59.38

Mexico on the Eve of War: The Unfinished Republic

By the end of 1845, Mexico had experimented with monarchy, federalism, a divided executive, centralism under the Seven Laws, a provisional dictatorship under the Bases of Tacubaya, and an executive-centered centralism under the Organic Bases. Each settlement had collapsed in the face of a pronunciamiento or a revolt, and none had commanded durable consent. Yet the experimentation was not pointless. It produced lasting institutions and ideas: the tradition of the 1824 constitution as a reference point for federalists, the amparo from Yucatán, the debate about the Church and corporate privileges that would shape La Reforma, and a growing national vocabulary of citizenship and sovereignty. The republic existed, and Mexicans on all sides continued to argue about its form, which is itself evidence of a shared national frame.

The five explanations developed in this chapter operate together. Institutional design mattered, because constitutions that placed the choice of presidents in state legislatures or created an arbiter above politics did not match the distribution of power. Fiscal weakness mattered because no government could pay its army, service its debt, and build administration at once. Regional political communities mattered because provinces, with their own economic interests and militias, did not accept direction from the capital without bargaining. The military mattered because the army was both the instrument of every government and the most reliable source of political change. And the unequal effects of state formation mattered because Indigenous communities, free and formerly enslaved people of African descent, women, workers, and frontier populations experienced independence and the republic in ways that varied widely and often disappointed their hopes. No single factor, and no single person, accounts for the pattern.

Texas and relations with the United States gave the crisis its immediate form. The Republic of Texas, recognized by the United States, France, and Britain, existed as an unresolved fact, and Mexican governments had neither recognized it nor reconquered it. The movement in the United States to annex Texas culminated in a joint resolution of Congress signed on 1 March 1845, after which the Mexican minister in Washington broke relations and the Texas convention and electorate accepted annexation, followed by admission to the Union at the end of the year. Mexican opinion regarded annexation as an act of aggression, while the unresolved boundary between the Nueces and the Rio Grande invited conflict. The war that followed, its campaigns, and the treaty that ended it belong to Chapter 63.

The chapter should not be read as a story of inevitable failure. In 1845 Mexico was a large republic with a rich cultural life, a recovering economy, vigorous regional societies, and a political class that debated fundamental questions in print and in assemblies. It was also a state without the resources, internal cohesion, or military strength to face a determined foreign adversary. The coming war would test those weaknesses severely and would drive Mexicans toward a more radical reexamination of their institutions. The unfinished republic of 1845 was both the product of two decades of improvisation and the starting point of the next.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Was Mexican Politics So Unstable after Independence?

The question: Was instability caused principally by constitutional weakness, military intervention, fiscal incapacity, regional interests, or competing political visions?

Scholarship does not divide neatly into two camps, and most historians combine several explanations. The two interpretations below mark the main poles of the discussion.

Interpretation A — Political and institutional causes: Some historians emphasize that elites disagreed about fundamental questions, such as federalism versus centralism, the role of the Church, and the status of corporate privileges, and that constitutions lacked legitimacy because they were imposed by victorious factions. On this view, the army's political role and the pronunciamiento filled a gap created by the lack of agreed procedures, and ideology and ambition drove events.
Interpretation B — Structural and fiscal causes: Other historians stress the economic ruin left by the independence wars, the collapse of mining, the weakness of tax collection, the dependence on customs revenue, and the strength of regions with separate economic ties. On this view, no government of any ideology could have financed itself or integrated the country, so political conflict reflected deeper material constraints, and the army became central because it was the largest institution the state could afford to maintain.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on constitutional texts, congressional debates, pronunciamientos, treasury reports, customs data, and foreign diplomatic correspondence, together with regional studies of local economies. Quantitative evidence is fragmentary, and fiscal estimates differ among sources, so conclusions depend on how evidence is weighed.

Why it matters: If political choices were decisive, then different decisions, such as accepting a moderate constitution or restraining the army, might have produced stability. If structural constraints dominated, the period's instability appears less contingent and more the common experience of new states with weak fiscal bases, a view reinforced by comparison with other Latin American republics in Chapter 58. Many historians now combine both, arguing that political decisions operated within severe structural limits.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Mexico's political history from the independence settlement of 1821 to the eve of the war with the United States in 1845, and has asked why a country with resources and constitutional creativity struggled to establish a durable political order. The argument is that no single cause explains the pattern. Institutional design, fiscal weakness, regional political communities, the central role of the army, and the unequal effects of state formation operated together.

Independence came through the alliance of Iturbide and Guerrero under the Plan of Iguala, and it produced a monarchy because the alliance rested on conservative guarantees: religion, independence, and union. The First Mexican Empire, proclaimed in May 1822, lacked a Bourbon claimant, a reliable treasury, and the consent of the provinces. Iturbide's dissolution of Congress, the pronouncement of Santa Anna at Veracruz, and the Plan of Casa Mata of February 1823 brought the empire down, because provincial deputations and army commanders withdrew obedience. Iturbide's abdication and his execution in 1824 on his return showed that provincial legislatures now acted as sovereign bodies.

The federal republic emerged from the provinces' claims. After a Supreme Executive Power governed provisionally, the Constitutive Act of January 1824 declared a representative popular federal republic, and the Federal Constitution of October 1824 gave it a bicameral Congress, a presidency chosen by state legislatures, a Supreme Court, and exclusive Catholic establishment, while preserving clerical and military fueros. The federal compact was fiscally weak, since states kept the alcabala and militias and paid little of their assigned contingente, and the federal treasury depended on customs duties.

Under Guadalupe Victoria, the republic enjoyed its most stable years, yet the British loans of 1824 and 1825 ended in default in 1827, the expulsion of Spaniards damaged commerce, and the Yorkino and Escocés lodges hardened factions. The Plan of Montaño and the defeat of Vice President Bravo in 1828 left a dominant faction and a resentful opposition. Beneath the constitutional surface, Indigenous villagers, free and enslaved people of African descent, and women lived largely under colonial hierarchies, with change in political forms outrunning change in social relations.

The election of 1828 inaugurated a period of armed intervention. Santa Anna's rising at Perote, the Acordada uprising, and the sack of the Parián brought Vicente Guerrero to the presidency, where his abolition decree of September 1829 gave independence a social meaning, though it was contested and effectively exempted Texas. The failed Spanish reconquest of 1829 made Santa Anna a hero, but the Plan of Jalapa overthrew Guerrero, and his execution in 1831 left bitter memories in the south. Alamán's Banco de Avío represented a conservative developmental program, and Santa Anna's rebellion of 1832 returned politics to a settlement that restored Gómez Pedraza.

The liberal reforms of 1833, led by Gómez Farías, attacked clerical and military privilege and Church control of education. The Plan of Cuernavaca and Santa Anna's reversal in 1834 repealed most of them. The defeat of the Zacatecas militia in May 1835 destroyed the armed basis of state autonomy, and the Bases of October 1835 and the Seven Laws of 1835–1836 replaced states with departments, created a Supreme Conservative Power, and tied citizenship to income. Centralism thus emerged gradually through the reversal of reform, military victory, and constitutional change.

Texas combined the chapter's themes. Mexican colonization policy brought Anglo-American settlers under the empresario system, amid Comanche power, Tejano ranching society, and a slavery question that Mexican law tried and largely failed to resolve. The law of 1830 reflected the government's concern but could not be enforced. Settlers and many Tejanos first protested in the name of federalism, then took up arms in 1835 as the federal system was dismantled. The Alamo and Goliad, the declaration of 2 March 1836, and San Jacinto ended Mexican effective control. Mexico refused to recognize the Treaties of Velasco, so the question stayed open, with different consequences for Anglo settlers, Tejanos, enslaved people, and Indigenous nations.

The centralist republic faced debt, blockade, and revolt. The Pastry War of 1838–1839 revealed fiscal and military weakness and brought Santa Anna back to prominence. Federalist movements in Yucatán, Tabasco, and the northeast, along with the Republic of the Rio Grande, showed the uneven reach of national authority, while Yucatán's 1841 constitution introduced the amparo. The Bases de Tacubaya of 1841, a provisional arrangement, and the Organic Bases of 1843, a full executive-centered constitution drafted by an appointed junta after the dissolution of an elected Congress, must be distinguished.

From below, the republic meant taxes, conscription, tariff disputes, market life, and negotiation with local authorities. Santa Anna's heavy taxation, elite desertion, and Paredes's pronouncement overthrew him in December 1844, and Herrera's moderate government inherited the same structural problems. The United States' annexation of Texas in 1845, accepted by Texas that year, created the immediate crisis leading to the war of 1846, which Chapter 63 narrates.

The significance of the period lies in what it produced as well as in what it failed to achieve. It established the vocabulary of federalism and centralism, the tradition of the 1824 constitution, the amparo, and the debate over Church property and corporate privilege that would shape La Reforma. It also revealed how a state with weak revenues, regional diversity, and a politicized army could not consolidate by constitutional design alone. Students should carry forward the lesson that instability was neither inevitable nor the result of one man's ambition.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Constitutional Projects: A Regional Study of Federalism and Centralism, 1824–1843

This project asks you to examine how one region experienced the shifts from federal states to centralist departments and back.

  1. Select a region. Choose one: Zacatecas, Yucatán, Texas and Coahuila, Oaxaca, or Jalisco.
  2. Gather constitutional texts. Locate the state constitution or relevant sections of the 1824 Constitution, the Seven Laws, and the Organic Bases in translated or Spanish editions.
  3. Collect political and social evidence. Use pronunciamientos, newspapers, petitions, treasury accounts, and secondary studies to describe local politics, taxation, militias, and the position of Indigenous communities and women.
  4. Compare. Identify how each constitutional change altered the region's authority, revenue, and military power, and note differences between formal text and actual practice.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay, explain what the regional case shows about the causes of instability and identify the limits of the available evidence.

Library resources to use: Collections of Mexican constitutional documents, regional histories, studies of the pronunciamiento and the early republic, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on nineteenth-century Mexico. The 1826 Tanner map of Mexico at the Library of Congress may be used as a cartographic source.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the first quarter-century of Mexican independence was a period of intense constitutional creativity, not mere disorder. Monarchy, federalism, and centralism were each serious answers to the problem of governing a vast and diverse country, and each foundered on the same obstacles: governments that could not raise enough revenue, regions that bargained rather than obeyed, an army that served as both pillar and solvent of authority, and a population whose experiences of the republic varied sharply with region, status, and gender.

The period also shaped what followed. The memory of the 1824 constitution fed federalist movements for decades, the debates of 1833 anticipated the Reform of the 1850s, the amparo from Yucatán became a central institution, and the loss of Texas defined relations with the United States. The pronunciamiento, the conflict over corporate privilege, and the question of how to finance a state persisted well beyond 1845. These continuities show that the early republic was a foundation, however unstable, for later Mexican history.

The chapter connects Mexico to wider patterns discussed elsewhere in Volume III. Chapter 58 treated republican experimentation across Latin America, and Mexico's problems of citizenship, federalism, and caudillismo had parallels in Gran Colombia, the Río de la Plata, and Central America. At the same time, Mexico's proximity to the United States and its northern frontier gave its crisis a particular geopolitical form.

Chapter 63 takes up the next stage: the annexation of Texas, the war with the United States, the territorial losses, and the liberal Reform that followed. Students should carry forward the view that Mexico entered that war not as a failed state but as an unfinished one, whose divisions, fiscal limits, and regional structures had been shaped by two decades of political experiment.
