The Peopling of the Americas

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Hemisphere Without Written Records

More than twelve thousand years before any written language existed anywhere in the Americas, small groups of human beings were already living, hunting, gathering, and raising children across a landscape stretching from the edge of the retreating ice sheets in the north to the cold beaches of the far south — a human presence recorded not in documents but in stone tools, footprints pressed into ancient mud, and the deep genetic inheritance carried by their descendants today.

At a place now called White Sands, in what is today the U.S. state of New Mexico, a series of human footprints was pressed into the soft mud of an ancient lakeshore by people walking, running, and carrying small children on their hips. Radiocarbon dating of seed layers above and below those footprints, published by a team of geologists and archaeologists beginning in 2021, suggested an age of roughly 21,000 to 23,000 years — a claim that, if accurate, would place human beings in North America thousands of years earlier than most twentieth-century archaeologists believed possible. At a place called Monte Verde, in the temperate forests of southern Chile, more than eight thousand miles to the south, another set of excavations uncovered wooden structures, hearths, worked stone, and preserved plant remains that the excavating team dated to roughly 14,500 years ago — a proposed age that, after decades of scholarly dispute and a widely publicized 1997 site visit by skeptical specialists, became broadly accepted by most archaeologists and helped overturn a model of American peopling that had dominated the discipline for half a century. As this chapter explains in detail, that broad acceptance was itself challenged again in 2026, when an independent research team argued for a far younger date, exerciseing a vigorous published rebuttal from the original excavators and reopening a debate that most archaeologists had considered closed for nearly three decades.

These two sites, separated by an entire hemisphere and by thousands of years of debate over their exact meaning, sit at the center of one of the most consequential and most actively revised questions in all of American history: when, how, and by what routes did human beings first arrive in the Western Hemisphere, and how did their descendants come to occupy every major environment from the Arctic to Tierra del Fuego? This is not a settled question with a single, textbook-ready answer. It is an active field of scientific inquiry in which archaeologists, geneticists, geologists, and Indigenous knowledge-holders continue to debate evidence, methods, and interpretation — sometimes revising long-accepted conclusions within a single generation of scholars.

This chapter does not claim to resolve that debate. Instead, it introduces students to the evidence, the competing models, and the historical process by which archaeological understanding of the peopling of the Americas has itself changed dramatically over the past century. It explains why the timing, routes, and pace of this migration remain contested; why earlier "Clovis-first" models that once seemed definitive have been substantially revised or abandoned; and why genetic, linguistic, and archaeological evidence must be read together, critically, rather than treated as though any single kind of evidence settles the question on its own.

It is essential to understand from the outset that the peoples who first settled the Americas did not think of themselves as engaged in a single, unified "peopling" event, any more than the term "Latin America," introduced in Chapter 1, would have meant anything to them. They were small kin groups and bands, moving across generations in search of game, plant foods, and viable places to live, adapting to environments ranging from Arctic tundra to tropical rainforest to high-altitude desert. Their genetic, cultural, and linguistic descendants would eventually give rise to the extraordinary diversity of Indigenous peoples examined throughout the rest of this volume — from the maize farmers of Mesoamerica to the fisher-foragers of the Pacific coast to the llama-herding communities of the high Andes. Understanding how, roughly, that diversification began is the necessary foundation for everything that follows.

This chapter also introduces students to a set of historical and scientific reasoning skills that will recur throughout the textbook: how to weigh disputed archaeological dates, how to distinguish a well-supported scientific consensus from an actively contested hypothesis, how genetic evidence complicates and refines purely archaeological narratives, and how new discoveries can force scholars to abandon models that had stood for decades. These skills are not unique to the study of ancient migration; they will matter again when this textbook considers, for example, competing colonial-era chronicles of conquest, disputed population estimates for the Atlantic slave trade, or contested casualty figures from twentieth-century dictatorships. Learning to read evidence carefully begins here, at the very beginning of the human story in the Americas.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold two ideas together without collapsing one into the other. First, the scientific study of ancient migration — using radiocarbon dating, genetic sequencing, and careful excavation — has produced real, evidence-based knowledge about the deep past, and that knowledge continues to be refined as new sites are discovered and new techniques are developed. Second, Indigenous peoples across the Americas have their own long traditions of origin, migration, and connection to specific homelands, traditions that are not simply "unscientific" stories to be dismissed but are themselves a form of historical memory deserving careful, respectful attention, discussed further in Chapter 4. This chapter focuses primarily on the archaeological and genetic evidence for the timing and routes of migration; the broader question of how historians integrate archaeology, oral tradition, and other forms of evidence in reconstructing Indigenous history is addressed more fully in Chapter 4.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established that "Latin America" is a relatively recent historical category applied to a much older and more diverse human world. This chapter reaches back to the very beginning of that human world — a period so distant that it must be studied without the aid of written records, oral chronicles preserved in their original form, or dateable monumental architecture. The evidence available for this period comes almost entirely from archaeology, geology, and, increasingly, genetics, and each of these fields has its own methods, strengths, and limitations that students must understand before evaluating competing claims about when people first arrived in the Americas.

The broad scientific consensus, developed over more than a century of research, holds that anatomically modern humans evolved in Africa and had spread across most of Europe, Asia, and Australia by roughly 40,000 years ago, but that the Americas — separated from Asia by the Bering Strait and from other continents by vast oceans — were the last major inhabited landmass to receive a permanent human population. Exactly when that population arrived, by what route or routes, and in how many distinct movements, remain genuinely unsettled questions actively debated among specialists. This is not a case of popular misunderstanding persisting alongside settled scientific fact; the scientific consensus itself has changed substantially over the past several decades, and further change is possible as new sites are excavated and new dating and genetic techniques are developed and applied.

Understanding this history requires some basic familiarity with the environmental conditions of the last glacial period, often called the Last Glacial Maximum, when enormous ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Eurasia and global sea levels were far lower than today because so much of the planet's water was locked in glacial ice. Under these conditions, the shallow continental shelf beneath the modern Bering Strait — the narrow body of water separating easternmost Siberia from Alaska — was exposed as dry land, forming a vast plain, not simply a narrow bridge, that connected Asia and North America. This exposed landmass and its associated ecosystem are known to archaeologists and geologists as Beringia, and it is central to nearly every model of how humans first reached the Americas.

This chapter also requires students to understand basic principles of archaeological dating, particularly radiocarbon dating, which measures the decay of a radioactive isotope of carbon absorbed by living organisms and allows scientists to estimate the age of organic materials such as charcoal, bone, wood, and plant remains found at archaeological sites. Radiocarbon dates are not infallible; they carry margins of error, can be affected by contamination, and require careful interpretation of the archaeological context in which dated materials were found — whether, for example, a piece of charcoal was actually associated with human activity or might have entered a site through natural processes unrelated to human presence. Much of the scholarly debate over sites discussed in this chapter turns on exactly these kinds of questions about context and reliability, not simply on the raw calendar dates that dating techniques produce.

Section 2.1

A Hemisphere Without Written Records

Every subsequent chapter in Volume I depends, to varying degrees, on evidence historians describe as "prehistoric" — a term that requires some care. "Prehistoric" does not mean a period without history; it means a period before the kinds of written documentation that allow historians to reconstruct events in the detailed, dated, and often multi-perspectival way possible for later periods. The peoples discussed in this chapter had no writing systems in the modern sense, though later civilizations examined in Parts II through V of Volume I — including the Maya, the Mexica, and Andean societies using the knotted-cord recording system known as quipu, examined in Chapter 4 — would develop sophisticated systems for recording information. For the period this chapter examines, roughly the last twenty-five thousand years down to the beginnings of settled agricultural life, historians and archaeologists must rely almost entirely on material evidence: stone tools, animal bones, plant remains, hearths, human skeletal remains, and, increasingly, the genetic information preserved within human DNA.

This absence of written records does not mean the period lacks historical significance or that it cannot be studied rigorously. Archaeology, geology, and genetics are all evidence-based disciplines with established methods for dating materials, interpreting context, and testing hypotheses against new discoveries. What it does mean is that conclusions about this period are unusually dependent on the interpretation of physical evidence, and that new discoveries — a newly excavated site, a refined dating technique, a genetic sample recovered from ancient remains — can meaningfully revise scholarly understanding in ways that would be far less likely for, say, the study of a well-documented nineteenth-century political event. Students should expect this chapter to describe genuine scientific debate rather than a single uncontested narrative, and should understand that this reflects the actual state of scholarship rather than any unusual uncertainty invented for this textbook.

Section 2.2

Ice Age Worlds: Beringia and the Pleistocene Environment

The environmental backdrop for the peopling of the Americas is the Pleistocene epoch, a long span of geological time characterized by repeated glacial and interglacial cycles, during which enormous ice sheets advanced and retreated across the Northern Hemisphere over tens of thousands of years. During the most recent and most extreme of these glacial episodes, known as the Last Glacial Maximum and reaching its peak intensity roughly 26,000 to 20,000 years ago, ice sheets more than a mile thick covered much of present-day Canada and the northern United States, while global sea levels fell by well over one hundred meters as enormous volumes of ocean water became locked in glacial ice.

This dramatic drop in sea level had a crucial consequence for the history of human migration: the shallow seafloor beneath the modern Bering Strait, which today separates easternmost Siberia from Alaska by roughly fifty miles of open water, became exposed as dry land. Geologists and archaeologists refer to this exposed region, along with adjacent portions of Siberia and Alaska that shared its ecology, as Beringia. Beringia was not a narrow, ice-covered causeway, as popular imagination sometimes suggests, but a vast subarctic landscape, in places nearly a thousand miles wide, supporting grasses, shrubs, and a range of large herbivores including mammoths, horses, bison, and other Ice Age megafauna, alongside the human and animal predators that depended on them.

Genetic and archaeological evidence increasingly suggests that human populations may have lived within Beringia itself for an extended period — potentially thousands of years — before moving further into the Americas, rather than passing through it in a single rapid crossing. This idea, sometimes called the "Beringian standstill" or "Beringian incubation" model, proposes that a population genetically distinct from either its Siberian ancestors or its later American descendants developed within Beringia during the height of the last glacial period, when conditions further south and east may have been less hospitable to further movement, before eventually expanding into the Americas as glacial conditions began to ease. This model remains one among several competing frameworks for understanding the timing and process of migration, discussed further in Section 2.8.

Whatever its precise chronology, Beringia's existence as dry, biologically productive land during the last glacial period is essential to understanding how human populations, who had already spread across much of Siberia by this period, could have moved into a previously uninhabited hemisphere without requiring sophisticated maritime technology, though maritime skills likely mattered a great deal to the coastal migration processes discussed in Section 2.5.

Section 2.3

The Long-Dominant Clovis-First Model

For much of the twentieth century, American archaeology was organized around a model now generally known as "Clovis-first." The model takes its name from a site near Clovis, New Mexico, where in the early 1930s archaeologists recovered distinctive, finely worked fluted projectile points — since found at hundreds of sites across North America — in direct association with the remains of mammoths and other now-extinct large mammals. These points, carefully shaped through a specialized flintknapping technique, became known as Clovis points, and radiocarbon dating eventually placed the Clovis tradition at roughly 13,000 to 12,700 years ago. This textbook uses the term "projectile point" rather than "spear point" because the stone points alone do not establish with certainty whether they tipped hand-held thrusting spears, darts thrown with a spear-thrower, or some other weapon; the point itself is far better documented archaeologically than the delivery system it was mounted on.

Because Clovis sites were, for decades, the oldest reliably dated evidence of human presence in the Americas, and because Clovis-style points appeared across a remarkably wide geographic area within a relatively narrow window of time, many archaeologists concluded that the makers of Clovis points represented the first human population to enter the Americas, having crossed Beringia and then moved south through an "ice-free corridor" that periodically opened between the retreating Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets covering much of Canada, before rapidly expanding across the entire hemisphere within a few centuries. On this model, virtually all subsequent Indigenous populations of North, Central, and South America would descend from this single, relatively late and rapid migration.

The Clovis-first model had genuine explanatory appeal. It fit the available dated evidence reasonably well for several decades, it offered a specific and testable mechanism — the ice-free corridor — for how people could have moved south of the ice sheets, and it provided a tidy, unified narrative for a complicated hemisphere. It also became, for a significant period, something close to orthodoxy within the discipline: archaeologists proposing older sites often faced intense skepticism, and claims of "pre-Clovis" human presence were frequently dismissed or subjected to unusually demanding scrutiny. This intellectual environment matters for understanding the following section, because it shaped how the scholarly community initially responded to evidence from Monte Verde.

Section 2.4

Monte Verde: A Landmark Site Reopened

Beginning in the 1970s, archaeologist Tom D. Dillehay led excavations at a site called Monte Verde, located near a small creek in the temperate forests of south-central Chile, far from the North American plains where Clovis-first thinking had developed. The site's waterlogged, low-oxygen soil conditions had preserved an extraordinary range of organic materials rarely recovered at drier sites: wooden foundation timbers from what appeared to be simple structures, wooden tools, preserved plant remains including seaweed apparently gathered from the Pacific coast, animal hides, and hearths containing charcoal suitable for radiocarbon dating.

Dillehay's team obtained radiocarbon dates suggesting an occupation, generally referred to as Monte Verde II, of roughly 14,500 years ago — more than a thousand years earlier than the oldest confirmed Clovis sites in North America, despite Monte Verde's location thousands of miles further south. If the dates and the human origin of the site's features were accurate, this posed a serious problem for the Clovis-first model: it would require that humans had already reached the far southern end of South America well before the appearance of Clovis technology in North America, meaning either that an earlier, pre-Clovis migration had occurred, that migration and subsequent movement south had happened far more rapidly than previously believed, or both.

The initial scholarly reception to Monte Verde was, in keeping with the disciplinary climate described in Section 2.3, highly skeptical. Some prominent archaeologists publicly doubted the site's dates, questioned whether some of the recovered materials were genuinely associated with human activity rather than natural deposition, or suggested contamination of the samples. In response, Dillehay and his collaborators pursued an unusually thorough process of documentation, publication, and eventually a formal site visit by a delegation of prominent skeptical archaeologists in 1997, organized specifically to evaluate the evidence firsthand. Following this visit, most of the delegation concluded that the site was genuinely of human origin and that the proposed age was credible, and for nearly three decades afterward, Monte Verde's roughly 14,500-year date functioned as one of the most widely cited anchors for pre-Clovis human presence in the Americas, playing a central role in the broader scholarly abandonment of strict Clovis-first thinking described in Section 2.8.

The 2026 reopening of the debate. In March 2026, a research team led by Todd A. Surovell, working in the vicinity of Monte Verde for the first time as an investigation genuinely independent of the original excavators, published a study arguing that the Monte Verde II archaeological component could not be older than the Middle Holocene, a period spanning roughly 8,200 to 4,200 years before the present — several thousand years younger than the traditional date, and, if correct, too recent to bear on the timing of the earliest human colonization of South America at all. The team based this argument primarily on geological and stratigraphic evidence collected from alluvial exposures near, though not directly at, the excavated archaeological component: radiocarbon and luminescence dates from these exposures, combined with the identification of a volcanic ash layer, or tephra, that the team dated to roughly 11,000 years ago and interpreted as lying stratigraphically beneath the archaeological horizon, led them to argue that the surface on which the Monte Verde II materials rest could not have existed until well into the Holocene epoch.

Dillehay and a group of collaborating geoarchaeologists published a detailed rebuttal later in 2026, along with supporting data, arguing that the Surovell team had not excavated the actual archaeological component of Monte Verde II, had misidentified or miscorrelated the tephra layer in question, and had proposed an implausible depositional scenario — a highly localized flooding event redistributing older wood onto a younger surface — that was not supported by the broader stratigraphic and geomorphological record built up over nearly five decades of research at and around the site. The rebuttal presented additional radiocarbon dates, including a substantial new series from cores taken near the original excavation area, which the original research team argued continued to support the traditional late Pleistocene age. Other geoarchaeologists not previously affiliated with either team likewise published independent assessments raising significant methodological objections to the Surovell team's stratigraphic interpretation, while acknowledging that renewed, transparent scientific scrutiny of a foundational site is, in principle, a healthy part of normal archaeological practice.

As of this textbook's writing in September 2026, the dispute remains genuinely unresolved. This chapter therefore describes Monte Verde's age as actively disputed rather than settled in either direction, and it declines to present either the traditional ~14,500-year chronology or the proposed Middle Holocene chronology as the established scholarly consensus. Students should understand that a site once treated in textbooks — including earlier editions of comparable texts — as a settled cornerstone of pre-Clovis archaeology is, at the moment this chapter was written, back at the center of an open scientific controversy, and that this reversal is itself an instructive illustration of how even long-accepted archaeological conclusions can be reopened by new fieldwork and new argument.

It is important to recognize what does not follow from this renewed uncertainty. Even if the traditional Monte Verde II chronology were ultimately revised downward, this would not, by itself, restore the strict Clovis-first model discussed in Section 2.3, because multiple other lines of archaeological and genetic evidence — including the Page-Ladson site in Florida and the genomic evidence discussed in Section 2.7, both examined further in Section 2.8 — independently support a pre-Clovis human presence in the Americas that does not depend on Monte Verde's specific chronology. Monte Verde's scientific and historical importance as the site most responsible for opening serious scholarly consideration of pre-Clovis migration is not erased by the 2026 controversy, even though the exact age of its principal archaeological component can no longer be presented to students as a settled fact.

Section 2.5

The Coastal Migration Hypothesis

Geological and environmental reconstruction indicates that the interior ice-free corridor described in Section 2.3, running between the retreating Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, did not become ecologically viable for sustained human travel — meaning that it supported enough plant life and game animals to make a long overland journey feasible rather than simply passable — until roughly 12,600 years ago, well after the earliest widely discussed pre-Clovis sites, including the traditional Monte Verde chronology and the Page-Ladson site discussed in Section 2.8, are dated. The corridor may still have served as an important route for later north-south movement of populations already established in the Americas, but most current specialists no longer regard it as a plausible route for the earliest southward dispersal.

In light of this timing problem, many archaeologists have increasingly favored what is generally called the Pacific coastal migration hypothesis, sometimes referred to informally as the "kelp highway" model. This hypothesis proposes that early populations moved south from Beringia not primarily through the North American interior but along the Pacific coastline, using boats or other watercraft to travel more rapidly along a near-shore marine ecosystem that, in many stretches, offered fish, shellfish, sea mammals, and productive kelp forests as reliable food sources.

The coastal route should not, however, be pictured as a single, continuously open, ecologically uniform corridor stretching smoothly from Beringia to southern Chile. Reconstructions of the ancient Pacific coastline indicate that Cordilleran glacial ice reached the shoreline and obstructed travel along some stretches of coast well into the period under discussion, and that the productivity of near-shore marine ecosystems varied considerably by latitude and by period, creating gaps and bottlenecks rather than a uniformly favorable route. The coastal migration hypothesis remains, in the judgment of most current specialists, a plausible and probably important explanation for how populations moved south relatively quickly, but it should be understood as a route that required real environmental adaptation and that was not equally hospitable at all times and places, rather than as an effortless highway.

The hypothesis also faces a significant evidentiary challenge: much of the coastline that early coastal migrants would have followed is today submerged beneath tens of meters of ocean water, because global sea levels have risen substantially since the end of the last glacial period as glacial ice melted. Any campsites, tools, or other evidence of coastal migration along this ancient shoreline would now lie underwater, in many cases far offshore, making them extraordinarily difficult and expensive to locate and excavate using underwater archaeological techniques. As a result, direct archaeological evidence for the coastal migration hypothesis remains more limited than the hypothesis's growing acceptance among specialists might suggest, and much of its support currently comes from indirect evidence — genetic patterns, the geographic distribution and age of early sites, and geological reconstruction of ancient coastlines — rather than from large numbers of directly excavated coastal migration sites.

Section 2.6

White Sands and the Question of an Earlier Arrival

A second major body of evidence in this debate involves a series of fossilized human footprints discovered at White Sands National Park in New Mexico, preserved in the sediment of an ancient lakebed. Beginning in 2021, a research team published radiocarbon dates obtained from layers of ditch grass seeds found stratigraphically above and below the footprint-bearing layers, proposing an age of approximately 21,000 to 23,000 years for the footprints. This range falls within the Last Glacial Maximum itself, discussed in Section 2.2, and would place humans in North America during the very period when the great ice sheets covering much of Canada reached their greatest extent — considerably earlier than either the traditional Clovis-first chronology or most versions of the coastal migration model had generally proposed.

This claim generated substantial scholarly excitement alongside serious methodological scrutiny. Some researchers raised concerns about whether aquatic plants such as the ditch grass used for the initial dating might have absorbed old carbon from the surrounding water in ways that could make the resulting radiocarbon dates appear older than the true age of the footprints, a phenomenon known as a "hard water" or reservoir effect. In response, the original research team and independent laboratories pursued a sustained, multi-year program of corroborating evidence. In 2023, researchers obtained dates from terrestrial conifer pollen, whose absorption of atmospheric carbon is not subject to the same reservoir effect, together with optically stimulated luminescence dates measuring when quartz grains in the footprint-bearing sediment were last exposed to sunlight — an entirely independent dating method unrelated to radiocarbon chemistry. Both approaches produced age estimates consistent with the original chronology. In 2025, a separate research team, working independently of the original excavators, obtained more than two dozen additional radiocarbon dates from a nearby paleolake sediment sequence that could be directly correlated with the track-bearing layers, again supporting a Last Glacial Maximum age.

With multiple independent chronological lines of evidence — radiocarbon dating applied to aquatic ditch-grass seeds, radiocarbon dating applied to terrestrial conifer pollen, optically stimulated luminescence dating of the sediment itself (a genuinely distinct dating method unrelated to radiocarbon chemistry), and, in 2025, an additional series of more than two dozen radiocarbon dates from a nearby paleolake sequence obtained by a research team independent of the original investigators — all converging on a broadly consistent age range, the White Sands footprints are now supported by evidence considerably stronger than the original 2021 publication alone provided. Some methodological and interpretive discussion continues, as it does for most major archaeological claims, but the accumulation of independently derived dates between 2021 and 2025 has moved White Sands from a provocative and contested claim to one of the more securely corroborated early chronologies currently available for the Americas — a notable contrast, as this chapter discusses further in Section 2.4, with the renewed uncertainty surrounding Monte Verde as of 2026.

Section 2.7

Genetics, Skeletal Evidence, and Population History

Alongside archaeological excavation, the study of human genetics has become an increasingly important source of evidence for understanding the peopling of the Americas. Researchers analyze both the DNA of living Indigenous populations across North, Central, and South America and, where ethically permissible and undertaken in careful partnership with descendant communities, ancient DNA recovered from human skeletal remains at archaeological sites, in order to reconstruct patterns of population movement, divergence, and relationship over time.

Broadly, genetic research supports the conclusion that the major founding ancestry of Indigenous peoples of the Americas traces to a population, or a small number of closely related populations, that formed in and around Beringia, with genetic evidence consistent with a period of relative population isolation there — the "Beringian standstill" model discussed in Section 2.2 — before subsequent expansion into the Americas and diversification into many distinct population lineages. Genetic studies have also identified evidence of at least one distinct and unusually early population branching event consistent with movement along a coastal route, lending indirect support to the coastal migration hypothesis discussed in Section 2.5, alongside evidence for later, separate migrations into the Arctic regions of North America associated with the ancestors of Inuit, Yupik, and related Arctic peoples, whose history is generally treated separately from the peopling processes that produced most other Indigenous populations of the Americas.

A large-scale genomic study published in 2026, analyzing more than a hundred high-coverage Indigenous American genomes, substantially complicated this picture for South America specifically. Rather than a single founding population simply spreading and branching, the study identified evidence for at least three principal population dispersals into South America at different periods, each followed by regional differentiation and, in many areas, long-term genetic continuity spanning thousands of years. The same research identified small but consistent genetic affinities between some South American Indigenous populations and Australasian populations, a pattern sometimes called Ypykuéra ancestry, most plausibly explained by an ancient admixture event involving a now-unsampled Asian population rather than by any direct migration from Australia or New Guinea. This research illustrates that the population history connecting Beringia to the Indigenous peoples examined throughout this textbook was not a single simple lineage but a more layered process involving multiple dispersals, later population movements, and regional continuity and change operating differently across South America.

Genetic evidence carries its own important limitations, however. DNA recovered from ancient skeletal remains is often degraded, contaminated, or available only from a limited number of individuals and sites, meaning that broad population-level conclusions must be drawn cautiously from what can be a genuinely small sample. Genetic research involving Indigenous ancestral remains also raises significant ethical questions regarding consent, cultural and religious protocols, and the rights of descendant Indigenous communities, and responsible contemporary research increasingly requires collaboration with, and consent from, the Indigenous nations most directly connected to the remains and sites under study. Genetic data can identify broad patterns of relationship and approximate timing of population divergence, but it generally cannot, by itself, specify exact migration routes, precise dates, or the particular lived historical experiences of the populations involved — for that, genetic evidence must be interpreted together with archaeological, geological, and, where available and appropriate, Indigenous oral historical evidence, a theme developed further in Chapter 4.

Section 2.8

Rethinking Routes: Interior Corridors, Coasts, and Multiple Waves

Taken together, the evidence discussed in the preceding sections has led most current specialists to abandon the older, simpler Clovis-first narrative in favor of more complex models. The well-corroborated White Sands evidence demonstrates that human populations were already established south of the continental ice sheets during the Last Glacial Maximum itself, roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years ago; because this evidence documents an established population rather than the moment of first arrival, it implies that initial entry into the Americas must have occurred earlier still, though White Sands does not by itself establish exactly how much earlier or by what route. Migration south of the ice sheets during this period may have involved coastal movement, since the interior ice-free corridor is not generally considered to have been ecologically viable for sustained travel until roughly 12,600 years ago, well after the period White Sands documents; however, the White Sands evidence does not itself identify a route, and the specific path or paths of the earliest dispersal remain unresolved. Under these revised models, the Clovis technological tradition that once seemed to mark the beginning of American prehistory is now understood instead as a widespread and archaeologically highly visible, but not necessarily earliest, technological horizon — one important and successful adaptation among what was likely a more complex and geographically varied process of initial settlement.

Because the traditional Monte Verde chronology discussed in Section 2.4 is, as of 2026, an actively disputed rather than settled data point, this chapter deliberately avoids resting the case for pre-Clovis migration on that single site. Other archaeological evidence points in the same direction independently. At Page-Ladson, a submerged sinkhole site along the Aucilla River in Florida, archaeologists recovered stone tools and cut-marked mastodon remains in an undisturbed stratigraphic context radiocarbon dated to roughly 14,550 years ago — a date derived from a different site, a different research team, and a different depositional environment than Monte Verde, and one that has not been subject to comparable challenge. Investigators describe the broader North American archaeological record for the period between roughly 15,000 and 14,000 years ago as sparse, reflecting how few people likely lived in the Americas at this early date and how difficult their traces are to find, but real, in the sense that multiple independently dated sites, not merely one, now support a human presence before the Clovis technological horizon. This means that even if the Surovell team's 2026 reassessment of Monte Verde were eventually to prevail over the Dillehay team's rebuttal, strict Clovis-first thinking would not automatically be restored, because the case for pre-Clovis migration no longer depends on Monte Verde alone.

Current scholarship also increasingly emphasizes that the peopling of the Americas may not have involved a single migration "event" at all, but rather an extended process potentially involving multiple movements of related, and in some cases genetically distinguishable, populations over a period of several thousand years, complicated further by the later, separate migrations associated with Arctic peoples noted in Section 2.7. Ancient-genome research, discussed further in Section 2.7, independently supports a more complex population history than a single founding migration, showing evidence of rapid branching and geographic expansion among early populations south of the continental ice sheets. Some researchers continue to argue for a role for the interior ice-free corridor once conditions there became viable, particularly for the later northward and southward movement of populations already established on the continent, even if it did not serve as the very first pathway of entry. Other open questions include the degree to which early Pacific coastal populations also moved into and adapted to interior environments relatively quickly after initial coastal dispersal, and whether currently unconfirmed sites elsewhere in the hemisphere might eventually provide evidence of a human presence even older than that documented at Page-Ladson or proposed at White Sands.

Students should take from this section not a single confident answer but an accurate picture of the current state of a genuinely unsettled and actively researched scientific question — one in which reasonable, well-informed specialists continue to disagree about specific dates, routes, and the number and relationship of early migrating populations, even as they broadly agree on the underlying reality of an ancient migration originating in Northeast Asia and eventually populating the entire hemisphere.

Section 2.9

From Foragers to Diverse Regional Societies

Whatever the precise timing and route of initial migration, the archaeological record makes clear that, by roughly 13,000 to 11,000 years ago, small human populations — commonly referred to by archaeologists using the term Paleoindian, a conventional label for Late Pleistocene and early Holocene populations and technological traditions, especially those around and after the Clovis horizon, whose chronological boundaries vary regionally and which does not imply that humans were absent from the Americas before this period — were present across an enormous range of environments throughout North, Central, and South America, from the grasslands and forests of temperate North America to the high plateaus of Mesoamerica, the coastal deserts and river valleys of the Andes, the vast tropical lowlands of Amazonia, and the cold southern reaches of Patagonia examined further in Chapter 24.

These early populations lived by hunting, fishing, and gathering wild plant foods, relying on stone, bone, wood, and other organic materials for tools and, in some regions, hunting animals belonging to the Ice Age megafauna community, including mammoths, giant ground sloths, native horses, and other large mammals, many of which became extinct across the Americas by roughly 10,000 years ago. The causes of these extinctions remain debated among specialists; climatic change associated with the end of the last glacial period and hunting pressure from expanding human populations are the two principal explanations currently offered, but their relative importance appears to have varied considerably by species and by region, and current research does not support a single universal mechanism applicable to every extinct species across the entire hemisphere.

As populations grew and spread into an increasingly wide range of environments over subsequent millennia, their subsistence strategies, tool technologies, settlement patterns, and social organization diversified accordingly, adapting to the specific resources, climates, and ecological conditions of each region — a process of regional diversification that forms the essential background for the development of the more complex, sedentary, and eventually urban societies examined in Parts II through V of this volume. This diversification should not be understood as a simple, linear progression from "primitive" to "advanced" societies; rather, it represents the successful adaptation of human populations to an extraordinarily wide range of environments, each of which posed distinct challenges and offered distinct opportunities, over a period of many thousands of years.

Section 2.10

The Archaic Period and the Origins of Agriculture

Archaeologists generally refer to the long period following the initial Paleoindian era, and extending from roughly 11,000 to somewhere between 4,000 and 3,000 years ago depending on the region, as the Archaic period. This period was marked by the beginning of the Holocene epoch — a geological period, continuing to the present day, marked by generally warmer global conditions than the terminal Pleistocene, though with substantial regional and millennial-scale climatic variability — and by a gradual, regionally uneven transition from highly mobile hunting and gathering toward more settled patterns of life, more intensive use of local plant and animal resources, and, in several key regions, the beginnings of plant cultivation and eventual domestication.

In Mesoamerica, Archaic-period communities began experimenting with the cultivation of wild plant ancestors of what would eventually become maize, beans, and squash — the agricultural foundation for the complex civilizations examined beginning in Part II of this volume — through a gradual process of selection and cultivation extending over thousands of years rather than a single moment of "discovery." In the Andes, Archaic-period communities in different ecological zones, from the arid Pacific coast to the high mountain valleys, began cultivating crops including varieties of squash, beans, and eventually the potato and other high-altitude tubers, alongside the early management and eventual domestication of camelids such as the llama and alpaca, developments examined further in Part IV of this volume. Similar processes of plant management and eventual domestication occurred independently in Amazonia and in other regions of the Americas, reflecting the varied environmental opportunities available to Archaic-period populations across the hemisphere.

These developments did not immediately produce settled village life, complex political organization, or the monumental architecture examined in later chapters; agriculture and settled life developed gradually and unevenly, often alongside continued hunting, fishing, and gathering, over a period of several thousand years. The detailed origins and development of agriculture in specific regions are examined in depth in Chapter 5 and throughout Part II of this volume; this chapter's purpose is to establish the broad chronological and environmental transition from Paleoindian foraging to the more regionally diverse and increasingly agricultural societies that form the subject of the chapters that follow.

Section 2.11

Population, Diversity, and the Eve of Civilization

By the end of the Archaic period, human populations across the Americas had grown substantially from the small founding populations discussed earlier in this chapter, though precise population figures for this era cannot be established with confidence and should not be treated as settled fact. These populations had also diversified linguistically, culturally, and technologically to a remarkable degree, laying the groundwork for the hundreds of distinct Indigenous peoples, languages, and eventually complex civilizations that this textbook examines throughout the remainder of Volume I and beyond.

It is important for students to resist two temptations at this point in the narrative. The first temptation is to imagine a simple, linear story in which small founding populations steadily and predictably "progressed" toward the large empires of Mesoamerica and the Andes, as though civilization were the inevitable destination of human historical development. In reality, the vast majority of Indigenous societies throughout the Americas, both before and after the rise of large states such as the Mexica and Inca empires examined later in this volume, continued to live in smaller-scale communities organized very differently from imperial states, and this diversity of political and social organization remained a defining feature of the Indigenous Americas throughout its history, not merely a "stage" that most societies eventually left behind.

The second temptation is to treat the deep history discussed in this chapter as somehow disconnected from, or merely a prelude to, the "real" history that begins with named civilizations and dated events in later chapters. This chapter's evidence — however incomplete and however contested in its details — documents real human lives, real historical processes of migration, adaptation, and innovation, and real ancestral connections to the Indigenous peoples who continue to inhabit the Americas today. The peopling of the Americas is not merely background; it is the first chapter of a continuous human historical narrative that runs, without interruption, through every subsequent chapter of this textbook.

Section 2.12

Why the Peopling Debate Still Matters

The scientific debate over the timing, route, and process of American migration might seem, at first glance, to be a narrow technical matter of interest mainly to specialists. In fact, it carries broader significance for how this textbook, and history more generally, should be studied and understood.

First, the history of the Clovis-first model and its eventual revision in light of Monte Verde and subsequent evidence offers a valuable lesson in historical and scientific reasoning: even a well-established, widely taught, and institutionally entrenched scholarly consensus can be substantially wrong, or at least substantially incomplete, and can be revised through the patient accumulation and rigorous scrutiny of new evidence. This lesson recurs throughout this textbook, whether in the revision of older narratives about the "discovery" of the Americas discussed in Chapter 1, debates over the origins of specific civilizations examined later in this volume, or contested interpretations of more recent political events examined in later volumes.

Second, the question of how and when the Americas were peopled has real political and cultural stakes in the present. Debates over the timing and character of early migration have, at various points, been invoked in contemporary political and legal contexts, including disputes over Indigenous land rights, cultural patrimony, and the treatment of ancestral human remains, underscoring why responsible archaeological and genetic research in this area increasingly requires meaningful collaboration with, and respect for the perspectives and rights of, contemporary Indigenous nations and communities.

Finally, this chapter's careful, evidence-based, and appropriately uncertain treatment of a genuinely unsettled scientific question models the kind of historical reasoning this textbook will ask of students throughout its 115 chapters: distinguishing well-supported conclusions from live hypotheses, taking seriously the limitations as well as the strengths of different kinds of evidence, and remaining open to revising one's understanding as new evidence becomes available, rather than treating any single account of the past as beyond question.

Historical Vocabulary
Beringia

Beringia refers to the region, including the now-submerged landmass beneath the modern Bering Strait, that connected northeastern Siberia and Alaska during periods of low global sea level associated with past glacial periods, most importantly the Last Glacial Maximum of roughly 26,000 to 20,000 years ago. Far from a narrow bridge, Beringia was, at its greatest extent, a vast subarctic plain supporting grasses, shrubs, large herbivores, and the human populations who depended on them.

Beringia is central to essentially every current scientific model of how humans first reached the Americas, whether by movement through its interior, along its coastline, or, according to the "Beringian standstill" hypothesis discussed in Section 2.2, by an extended period of habitation within Beringia itself before further movement into the hemisphere. As global sea levels rose with the melting of glacial ice at the end of the last glacial period, Beringia was gradually submerged, becoming the modern, much narrower Bering Strait familiar from contemporary maps.

Historical Vocabulary
Clovis Culture

Clovis culture refers to a distinctive stone-tool tradition, especially associated with finely worked, fluted projectile points, first identified near Clovis, New Mexico, and subsequently found at hundreds of sites across North America dating to roughly 13,000 to 12,700 years ago. For much of the twentieth century, Clovis sites represented the oldest confidently dated evidence of human presence in the Americas, leading to the "Clovis-first" model discussed in Section 2.3.

Following the broad, though as of 2026 renewed and contested, scholarly acceptance of Monte Verde's early chronology, together with independent pre-Clovis evidence from sites such as Page-Ladson and the well-corroborated Last Glacial Maximum dates from White Sands, most archaeologists no longer regard Clovis technology as marking the very beginning of human presence in the Americas. Instead, Clovis is now generally understood as a widespread and archaeologically prominent technological tradition that developed among populations already established in the Americas, representing an important adaptation to the environmental conditions of the period rather than the founding migration itself.

Historical Debate

What Is Monte Verde's True Age, and What Follows Either Way?

The question: Following the 2026 publication challenging Monte Verde's traditional chronology and the subsequent rebuttal defending it, how should historians and students evaluate the site's true age, and how much should the outcome of that specific dispute change the broader picture of early American migration established by White Sands and other independent evidence?

Interpretation A — The traditional ~14,500-year chronology for Monte Verde II remains correct: This view, defended by Dillehay and collaborating geoarchaeologists, holds that the Surovell team's 2026 reassessment examined sediments near, but not at, the actual archaeological component; misidentified or miscorrelated the volcanic ash layer central to its argument; and proposed a highly localized flooding scenario unsupported by nearly five decades of stratigraphic and geomorphological research at the site. Proponents point to additional radiocarbon dates presented in the 2026 rebuttal, along with independent critiques published by other geoarchaeologists questioning the reassessment's methods, as evidence that the original late Pleistocene chronology has not been successfully overturned.
Interpretation B — Monte Verde II is considerably younger than previously believed, dating to the Middle Holocene: This view, advanced by Surovell and colleagues, holds that geological evidence from alluvial exposures near the site — including radiocarbon and luminescence dates and the identification of an approximately 11,000-year-old tephra layer beneath the archaeological horizon — indicates that the ground surface on which the Monte Verde II materials rest did not exist until the Holocene, making a late Pleistocene age geologically implausible regardless of the ages obtained from organic materials directly associated with the archaeological finds. Proponents argue that removing Monte Verde as an anchor point substantially changes, though does not by itself disprove, the broader case for an early human colonization of South America.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on radiocarbon dating, luminescence dating, and stratigraphic analysis of the same general area, but disagree fundamentally about which sediments and features are properly correlated with the archaeological component, and about whether a proposed flooding or erosional event could account for the discrepancy between the two chronologies.

Why it matters, and what does not depend on the outcome: The resolution of this specific dispute affects how confidently historians can cite Monte Verde as evidence for a pre-Clovis human presence in South America specifically. It does not, on its own, resolve the broader question of when humans first reached the Americas, because that broader case now rests on multiple independent lines of evidence — most importantly the well-corroborated White Sands footprints, discussed in Section 2.6, and the Page-Ladson site in Florida, discussed in Section 2.8 — that are unaffected by the Monte Verde controversy and were dated by different teams using different methods in different locations. Students should therefore distinguish two separate questions that are easy to collapse into one: "Is Monte Verde II approximately 14,500 years old?" (unresolved as of 2026) and "Did humans reach the Americas before the Clovis technological horizon?" (supported by evidence independent of Monte Verde).

Student task: Using the evidence and reasoning presented in Sections 2.4 through 2.8, explain why a dispute over a single site's chronology can be scientifically significant without being decisive for the broader question of early American migration, and identify what kind of additional evidence would most effectively resolve the Monte Verde dispute specifically.

Multiple Perspectives

Interpreting the Same Evidence: Perspectives on the Peopling Debate

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns found across scholarship and public discussion. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of a mid-twentieth-century Clovis-first archaeologist: The consistency and wide distribution of Clovis-style points, combined with their association with now-extinct megafauna, provided compelling, testable evidence for a relatively late, rapid migration through an interior ice-free corridor, and claims of earlier "pre-Clovis" sites appeared, given the evidentiary standards of the time, more likely to reflect misdated or misattributed material than genuine earlier human presence.
From the perspective of a specialist advancing the coastal migration hypothesis: The well-corroborated Last Glacial Maximum date for White Sands, combined with genetic evidence for early population branching and the resource variability of Pacific coastal ecosystems, makes coastal migration one of the leading explanations for how humans moved south of the continental ice sheets, followed by later movement into interior regions such as the site of the White Sands footprints, even though much of the direct archaeological evidence for a coastal route now lies underwater and remains difficult to recover.
From the perspective of a geneticist studying ancient and modern Indigenous DNA: Genetic evidence offers a valuable, largely independent line of evidence for evaluating competing archaeological models, but it must be interpreted cautiously, given limited sample sizes for ancient remains and the ethical obligation to conduct such research in genuine partnership with, and with the consent of, contemporary Indigenous communities connected to the populations under study.
From the perspective of a contemporary Indigenous community member reflecting on migration narratives: Scientific models of migration are worth taking seriously as one valid form of evidence about the deep past, but they do not automatically take precedence over a community's own long-held traditions regarding origin and connection to a particular homeland, and responsible scholarship should treat both forms of knowledge with appropriate respect rather than assuming that one must simply replace the other.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From First Arrival to Regional Diversity

What Changed

  • Small, related founding populations diversified over thousands of years into hundreds of distinct linguistic, cultural, and eventually political communities across an enormous range of environments
  • Subsistence strategies shifted, unevenly and at different times in different regions, from highly mobile hunting and gathering toward more settled patterns of resource use and, eventually, agriculture
  • Tool technologies diversified regionally as populations adapted to distinct environments, from Arctic to tropical to high-altitude settings
  • Scientific understanding of this process has itself changed dramatically, from the Clovis-first model to a more complex, multi-route, and possibly multi-wave understanding of migration

What Stayed the Same

  • The major founding ancestry of Indigenous peoples of the Americas traces to populations that formed in and around Beringia, while later migrations, additional dispersals within South America, and, in some regions, further admixture contributed additional ancestry, as discussed in Section 2.7
  • Human beings have continuously inhabited the Americas without interruption from the period of initial migration to the present day
  • The reliance on careful archaeological, geological, and increasingly genetic evidence — rather than written records — remains the basis for reconstructing this period
  • Scientific debate over specific dates, routes, and processes, though its content has changed, has remained a constant feature of this field of study

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic diversification that followed initial migration and the underlying continuity of ancestral connection helps students avoid treating the earliest Americans as a single undifferentiated group, while still understanding the shared root from which later, immensely varied Indigenous societies developed.

Cause and Effect

Why the Americas Became So Environmentally and Culturally Diverse

Long-term causes

The enormous north-to-south extent of the Americas, spanning nearly the full range of Earth's climate zones, combined with a similarly wide range of coastal, mountain, forest, desert, and river environments available for human settlement.

Short-term causes

Successive waves or phases of population movement and expansion following initial migration, combined with the end-of-Pleistocene extinction of megafauna, which required subsistence adaptation in many regions.

Effect

The development, over thousands of years, of hundreds of distinct Indigenous languages, subsistence strategies, technologies, and eventually political systems, ranging from small mobile bands to the large imperial states examined later in this volume.

Historical significance

This diversity created the varied historical, environmental, and cultural foundations later encountered, misunderstood, and often violently disrupted by European conquest, examined beginning in Volume II.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the Americas' extraordinary Indigenous diversity; environmental variation, population history, and thousands of years of independent cultural development all interacted to produce it.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Daily Life of Early American Populations

Although this chapter has focused heavily on questions of dating, migration routes, and scientific method, the populations under discussion lived real daily lives that archaeology allows us to glimpse, however incompletely. Evidence from sites across the hemisphere suggests small kin-based bands, likely numbering in the dozens rather than hundreds of individuals, moving seasonally across defined territories in pursuit of game, fish, and plant foods, and returning periodically to favored camp locations, some of which, like Monte Verde, were occupied repeatedly over extended periods.

Archaeological evidence, including hearths, food remains, tools of varying specialization, and, at some sites, evidence of shelter construction, suggests a division of labor and knowledge within these early communities, though the details of gender roles, child-rearing practices, and social organization during this period remain difficult to reconstruct with confidence from material evidence alone. Later chapters, particularly those examining societies with richer archaeological and eventually documentary records, will be able to say considerably more about family life, gender, and social organization; this chapter's more limited evidentiary base is itself an important lesson about how the availability of evidence shapes what historians can and cannot confidently claim about the past.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined one of the most fundamental and most actively contested questions in the study of Latin American and broader American history: when, how, and by what routes did human beings first arrive in the Western Hemisphere? Rather than offering a single settled answer, the chapter has traced the historical development of scientific understanding on this question, from the mid-twentieth-century dominance of the Clovis-first model through its substantial revision following Tom Dillehay's excavations at Monte Verde in southern Chile, to the current, considerably more complex and, in important respects, still shifting picture shaped by coastal migration hypotheses, genetic research, the now well-corroborated evidence from White Sands, and the renewed 2026 controversy over Monte Verde's own chronology.

Students have learned that the environmental conditions of the Last Glacial Maximum, particularly the exposure of the Beringia landmass connecting Siberia and Alaska, created the essential precondition for human movement into a previously uninhabited hemisphere. They have examined how the Clovis-first model, based on the wide distribution of distinctive fluted projectile points dated to roughly 13,000 years ago, dominated archaeological thinking for decades before Dillehay's careful excavation at Monte Verde, and its broad acceptance following a 1997 site visit, forced a fundamental reconsideration of that model — an acceptance that was itself reopened in March 2026 when an independent research team argued for a far younger, Middle Holocene age, exerciseing a detailed rebuttal from the original excavators that leaves the site's precise chronology unresolved as of this textbook's writing.

The chapter has also introduced the coastal migration hypothesis as a leading current explanation for how populations might have moved south along the Pacific coastline, while emphasizing that this coastal route should be understood as environmentally variable rather than a uniformly easy corridor, and while acknowledging the significant evidentiary challenges posed by the fact that much of the ancient coastline in question now lies submerged. It has examined the White Sands footprints in detail, tracing how an initially contested 2021 claim was substantially strengthened by independent corroborating evidence published in 2023 and 2025, to the point that most specialists now regard the site as strong evidence for a human presence in North America during the Last Glacial Maximum itself — a notably different evidentiary trajectory than the one Monte Verde has followed since 2026. The chapter has also introduced the Page-Ladson site in Florida as further independent evidence that the case for pre-Clovis migration does not rest on Monte Verde alone.

Students have been introduced to the growing role of genetic evidence, including both modern population genetics and ancient DNA research, in reconstructing early American population history, while also learning to appreciate this evidence's limitations and the important ethical considerations involved in genetic research on Indigenous ancestral remains. The chapter has traced the broad transition from small, mobile Paleoindian foraging bands to the more regionally diverse societies of the Archaic period, including the gradual, regionally varied beginnings of plant cultivation that would eventually give rise to the agricultural foundations of later Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized that this period of deep history is not merely a prelude to "real" history beginning with named civilizations in later chapters, but the first chapter of a continuous historical narrative connecting the earliest human populations of the Americas to the Indigenous peoples who continue to inhabit the hemisphere today. It has also used the history of scientific revision discussed throughout the chapter — particularly the overturning of the once-dominant Clovis-first model — to model the kind of careful, evidence-based historical reasoning that this textbook will ask of students throughout its remaining 113 chapters.

Chapter 2 has asked how humans first came to inhabit the lands this textbook studies. Chapter 3 turns to the landscapes themselves — the diverse environments of Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia, the Caribbean, and other regions — that would shape the development of the societies and civilizations examined throughout the remainder of Volume I.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Beringia
The now-largely-submerged landmass that connected Siberia and Alaska during periods of low sea level associated with past glacial periods, central to models of American migration.
Last Glacial Maximum
The period of maximum global ice-sheet extent and lowest sea level during the most recent glacial period, occurring roughly 26,000 to 20,000 years ago.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch, ending roughly 11,700 years ago, characterized by repeated glacial and interglacial cycles.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning roughly 11,700 years ago and marked by generally warmer global conditions than the terminal Pleistocene, alongside substantial regional and millennial-scale climatic variability.
Clovis culture
A stone-tool tradition, especially associated with fluted projectile points, dated to roughly 13,000–12,700 years ago and once believed to mark the earliest human presence in the Americas.
Clovis-first model
The now largely abandoned twentieth-century model holding that the makers of Clovis points were the first human population to enter the Americas via an interior ice-free corridor.
Ice-free corridor
A proposed interior travel route between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, once considered the primary route for the peopling of the Americas.
Coastal migration hypothesis
The hypothesis that early populations moved south from Beringia primarily along the Pacific coastline, using watercraft and coastal marine resources; sometimes called the "kelp highway" model.
Radiocarbon dating
A scientific method for estimating the age of organic materials by measuring the decay of a radioactive carbon isotope, central to archaeological dating discussed throughout this chapter.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered deposits at archaeological or geological sites, used to establish relative chronology and context for dated materials.
Archaeological context
The precise physical and stratigraphic circumstances in which an artifact or sample is found, essential for correctly interpreting its age and significance.
Ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from ancient human or animal remains, used alongside archaeological evidence to reconstruct population history.
Population genetics
The scientific study of genetic variation within and between populations, used to reconstruct patterns of migration, divergence, and relationship.
Beringian standstill hypothesis
The proposal that a population lived within Beringia for an extended period before further expansion into the Americas, supported by genetic evidence of population isolation.
Megafauna
Large animal species, including mammoths and giant ground sloths, many of which became extinct in the Americas by roughly 10,000 years ago.
Younger Dryas
An abrupt cooling episode during the terminal Pleistocene, roughly 12,900 to 11,700 years ago, that interrupted the broader deglacial warming trend.
Archaic period
The long period following the initial Paleoindian era, marked by regional diversification of subsistence strategies and, in several regions, the beginnings of agriculture.
Paleoindian
A conventional archaeological term for Late Pleistocene and early Holocene populations and technological traditions in the Americas, especially those associated with and following the Clovis horizon; its chronological boundaries vary by region, and the term does not imply that humans were absent from the Americas before roughly 13,000 years ago.
Forager
A person or community subsisting primarily through hunting, fishing, and gathering wild food resources rather than agriculture.
Lithic technology
Stone-tool technology, a primary source of archaeological evidence for early American populations.
Scientific consensus
The collective judgment of the scientific community based on available evidence at a given time, which can change substantially as new evidence emerges.
Hypothesis
A proposed explanation for observed evidence, subject to further testing and revision, distinguished in this chapter from more firmly established scientific conclusions.
Reservoir effect
A phenomenon in which organisms, particularly aquatic plants, absorb older carbon from their environment, potentially skewing radiocarbon dates to appear older than their true age.
Domestication
The long-term process by which human selection and cultivation transform wild plant or animal species into forms dependent on human management.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Primary evidence
In this chapter's context, physical and genetic evidence produced directly by the historical processes under study, such as stone tools, footprints, and ancient DNA.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to begin the study of Latin American history at its earliest possible point: the arrival of the first human beings in a hemisphere that had, until that arrival, never known a human presence. It has done so while modeling a form of historical reasoning that will recur throughout this textbook — weighing incomplete and sometimes contradictory evidence, distinguishing well-supported conclusions from live hypotheses, and remaining genuinely open to revision as new evidence emerges.

The overturning of the Clovis-first model stands as one of the clearest examples in modern archaeology of how a widely accepted scholarly consensus can be substantially revised through patient, rigorous scholarship, and this chapter has used that episode deliberately to prepare students for similar moments of revision and debate that will appear throughout later volumes of this textbook, from contested population estimates for the Atlantic slave trade to disputed casualty figures from twentieth-century political violence.

The chapter has also established an essential principle that will recur throughout Volume I: Indigenous history in the Americas is extraordinarily long, environmentally diverse, and continuous, beginning many thousands of years before any of the civilizations, states, or historical categories examined in later chapters existed. The small Paleoindian bands and the more regionally diverse Archaic-period societies discussed in this chapter are not a prelude to "real" history; they are the ancestral populations from which the Olmec, Maya, Mexica, Inca, Taíno, Mapuche, and hundreds of other peoples this textbook will examine in the chapters ahead ultimately emerged, although, as the genetic evidence discussed in Section 2.7 makes clear, thousands of years of subsequent migration, admixture, and cultural transformation generally make it impossible to trace a direct, unbroken biological or cultural line from any specific early population to any specific later named society.

Understanding this deep chronology, and understanding the genuine scientific uncertainty that still surrounds its details, equips students to read the remainder of Volume I with appropriate historical humility: civilizations did not spring into being from nothing, but developed over many thousands of years of human adaptation, innovation, and regional diversification, a story that continues, chapter by chapter, throughout the rest of this book.
