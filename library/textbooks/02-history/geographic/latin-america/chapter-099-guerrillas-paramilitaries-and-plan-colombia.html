Guerrillas, Paramilitaries, and Plan Colombia

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: One Country, Several Wars

Colombia's armed conflict is often described as a war between the state and the guerrillas. The historical record shows something more tangled: insurgents, paramilitary confederations, state security forces, trafficking enterprises, landowners, local politicians, and above all civilians, whose lives were shaped by all of them.

In the late 1990s a rural family in Colombia might have encountered several armed organizations within a few years, sometimes within a few months. A guerrilla front might tax the harvest, a paramilitary column might accuse the village of collaboration, an army patrol might pass through, and a trafficking network might buy the coca leaf or coca paste that was often the only crop with a reliable buyer. Each actor claimed to bring order or justice. Each also used coercion. The National Center for Historical Memory (Centro Nacional de Memoria Histórica, CNMH), a Colombian state research institution, estimated roughly 220,000 conflict-related deaths between 1958 and 2012, the great majority of them civilians. That is a historically bounded estimate, not a current total, and it does not count the millions who were displaced, the kidnapped, the disappeared, or the recruited as minors.

This chapter explains how a conflict that began in the 1960s as rural insurgency became, by the 1990s, a multidimensional system of overlapping confrontations. It concentrates on three armed organizations of the left and right, the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC), the National Liberation Army (ELN), and the United Self-Defense Forces of Colombia (AUC), and on the state institutions that confronted, tolerated, and sometimes collaborated with them. It also examines the cocaine economy as a source of financing, without retelling the history of the cartels told in Chapters 97 and 98.

The chapter's second major task is to explain Plan Colombia. The plan began in 1999 as a Colombian government strategy for peace, institutional strengthening, economic development, and drug control. It became widely identified with the United States because of the large package of mostly security assistance that Washington approved in 2000. This chapter treats the Colombian plan and the American funding programs as related but distinct. It then follows the 2000s, when military reform, a more security-centered presidency under Álvaro Uribe, and sustained assistance shifted the balance between the state and the FARC, and when the state's conduct, including serious abuses, came under scrutiny.

The central argument is that Colombia's military transformation in the 2000s changed the balance of power between the state and the principal insurgents, but that military gains alone could not resolve the issues at the heart of the conflict: unequal rural landholding, uneven state presence, illicit economies, impunity, and the rights of victims. Those unresolved issues explain why, by 2012, both the government and the FARC could judge negotiation preferable to continued war. That moment of decision is where this chapter ends. Chapter 100 takes up the negotiations themselves.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually contested. Guerrilla communiqués and conference documents show what organizations wished to be seen as doing. Statutes and congressional records show what states authorized. Truth Commission and CNMH reports, court judgments, and the findings of the Special Jurisdiction for Peace (JEP) reconstruct violence through evidentiary processes that were not available to contemporaries. Government statistics on kidnappings, displacement, and coca cultivation come from different institutions with different methods. This chapter asks students to say what each source can establish, and to avoid adding categories of victimization together when the same person may appear in more than one.

The chapter also insists on distinctions that polemic erases. Guerrillas, paramilitaries, trafficking enterprises, and official state institutions were not interchangeable, though they sometimes overlapped or cooperated. Guerrilla atrocities, paramilitary massacres, and abuses by state personnel are each documented, and each is treated according to its own evidence. The chapter does not treat Colombia only as a land of violence. Its communities organized, litigated, petitioned, fled, returned, and rebuilt, and Colombian institutions including courts, a human-rights movement, and eventually a transitional-justice system emerged from the conflict.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Compare the origins, ideology, organization, geography, and financing of the FARC and the ELN.
  2. Explain how the FARC's 1982 Seventh Conference and the growth of its fronts changed its strategic ambitions.
  3. Distinguish protection fees, coercive taxation, kidnapping, and direct involvement in drug trafficking as sources of insurgent income.
  4. Trace the origins of paramilitarism from local self-defense and private armies to the confederated AUC of 1997.
  5. Analyze the Mapiripán and El Salado massacres as documented case studies of paramilitary violence and failures of state protection.
  6. Assess the relationship among paramilitaries, politicians, landowners, and land dispossession without presuming universal complicity.
  7. Explain forced displacement as a central strategy and consequence of the conflict, and the legal framework created by Law 387 of 1997.
  8. Evaluate why the Caguán negotiations of 1998–2002 failed and how that failure shaped Colombian politics.
  9. Distinguish the Colombian Plan Colombia of 1999 from the American assistance package of 2000.
  10. Assess the military reforms and Democratic Security policy of the 2000s, including security gains and extrajudicial killings.
  11. Interpret the Justice and Peace Law and the persistence of successor groups after AUC demobilization.
  12. Connect the changed military balance of 2008–2012 to the opening of peace negotiations in 2012.

Historical Context

Chapter 83 told the story of La Violencia, the partisan civil conflict between Liberals and Conservatives that killed a very large number of Colombians between the late 1940s and the 1960s, and of the National Front (1958–1974), the power-sharing arrangement that ended it by alternating the presidency between the two parties. The National Front restored civilian rule but excluded other political forces and left rural inequality largely untouched. Land was highly concentrated, frontier settlers were often without secure title, and large parts of the national territory had little regular presence of courts, police, schools, or roads. Armed groups grew in such regions because the state was thin there, not because the state was absent from the rest of the country.

Chapters 97 and 98 described the cocaine economy and the confrontation with Medellín and Cali. Their relevance here is structural. The cocaine business created enormous sums of money, an appetite for rural land, and a market for coca leaf and paste in frontier zones where other crops could not reach markets. It also taught armed groups that illicit economies could finance organization. The cartel wars did not cause the guerrilla conflict, which began in the 1960s, but they transformed its economics and its geography.

The 1991 Constitution, written by a Constituent Assembly after the demobilization of several smaller insurgencies including the M-19, expanded rights, recognized Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities, created a Constitutional Court, and strengthened the legal vocabulary available to victims. The FARC and ELN did not take part. The decade that followed therefore combined democratic constitutional reform with escalating armed conflict, a combination that students should hold in mind. Colombia's elected institutions remained continuous through the conflict, which distinguishes it from the military dictatorships of the Southern Cone.

Internationally, the end of the Cold War removed the Soviet and, after 1991, much of the Cuban support that had framed revolutionary movements, while narcotics replaced communism as the main organizing concern of United States policy in the Andes. Washington designated the FARC and ELN as Foreign Terrorist Organizations in 1997 and the AUC in 2001. Those are American legal designations, not universal scholarly classifications. The terminology of the conflict was itself contested: governments spoke of terrorism, narco-guerrillas, or self-defense forces; the organizations spoke of revolution or self-defense; Colombian scholars debated whether "armed conflict," "internal war," or "violence" best described the situation. This chapter uses "internal armed conflict," the term adopted by Colombian legislation and by the CNMH, while recognizing that its use carried legal consequences.

Guerrilla Organizations and the Transformation of the Conflict

How Colombia's principal insurgencies grew from earlier revolutionary movements into large, territorially organized forces.

Section 99.1

From La Violencia to Prolonged Insurgency: The FARC and ELN

The FARC and ELN emerged within a year of each other, in 1964 and 1965, but from different social worlds. The FARC grew from peasant communities in southern Tolima, Huila, and Cauca that had organized armed self-defense during La Violencia, often under the influence of the Colombian Communist Party. In 1964 the army launched a large offensive against settlements it described as "independent republics," the best known of which was Marquetalia. Survivors, led by Pedro Antonio Marín, who took the name Manuel Marulanda Vélez and was known as "Tirofijo," regrouped and formed a guerrilla organization that took the name Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia in 1966. Its origins were agrarian, its leadership was largely peasant, and its early outlook was defensive, concerned with protecting colonized land and communities.

The continuation of insurgency also reflected the uneven incorporation of rural territories into the constitutional state. A farmer in a recently colonized district might face contested claims to land, a long journey to the nearest judge, and competing demands for loyalty from armed actors. These circumstances neither made rebellion inevitable nor justified attacks on civilians. They help explain why the authority of an armed movement could become entangled with everyday disputes about roads, crops, debt, and land boundaries. Religious, trade-union, student, and campesino organizations offered other avenues of protest, sometimes facing threats themselves. Historical explanation therefore requires attention both to groups choosing armed struggle and to Colombians who sought reform through lawful civic organization. The latter were not passive spectators to a binary state–insurgent conflict.

The ELN began differently. A group of students and young activists, some of whom had received training in Cuba and were inspired by the Cuban Revolution's example of a small armed vanguard, established a guerrilla nucleus in Santander. Historians call this the foco approach, after the idea that a committed armed group could generate revolutionary conditions. The ELN announced itself on 7 January 1965 by occupying the town of Simacota and issuing a manifesto. Its early leader, Fabio Vásquez Castaño, built an organization of intellectuals, students, workers in the oil sector, and peasants. In late 1965 the priest and sociologist Camilo Torres Restrepo joined, and he died in his first combat in February 1966, becoming a lasting symbol.

Why did insurgency take root? The National Front had closed formal avenues for opposition parties, and the 1960s were a period of expanding agrarian conflict, urban migration, and student radicalism across Latin America. Land concentration and the lack of secure title on colonization frontiers created persistent grievances. But structural conditions alone do not explain which movements survived. The FARC survived because it was embedded in communities and in the Communist Party's networks; the ELN survived, after near destruction in the early 1970s, by finding new regional bases. Other insurgencies, including the Maoist-inspired EPL (1967) and the urban M-19 (1970s), rose and eventually entered negotiation, with most demobilizing in 1990–1991. The FARC and ELN remained outside that settlement.

Students should avoid two common simplifications. One treats the insurgents purely as bandits or terrorists with no political history; the other treats them purely as the armed voice of the rural poor. The historical record shows political programs rooted in real grievances and also a growing record of coercion against civilians, which later sections document. Chapter 83 told the foundational story, and this chapter picks it up as the organizations began to expand.

Historical Vocabulary
Insurgency, guerrilla warfare, and territorial control

An insurgency is an organized armed challenge to a government, usually combining military action with political organization. Guerrilla warfare ("little war," from Spanish) is a method: small, mobile units that avoid decisive battle, strike selectively, and depend on local knowledge and support or tolerance. Territorial control means the ability of an armed organization to regulate daily life in an area, for example by collecting taxes, settling disputes, or restricting movement.

Territorial control is a matter of degree. An armed group may be present in an area without governing it, and an area may be contested, with several actors exerting influence at different times. Students should be cautious about maps that paint regions as "controlled" by one group, a distinction the map studies below revisit.

Section 99.2

The FARC-EP: Organization, Expansion, and Revolutionary Strategy

For its first two decades the FARC was a modest rural force. A turning point came at its Seventh Conference in 1982, held in the southeastern frontier region. The conference adopted the designation Ejército del Pueblo (People's Army), giving the organization the name FARC-EP, and approved a strategic plan to multiply the number of fronts, extend into economically important regions, and prepare for eventual military and political advance toward the center of the country. Leadership rested with a Secretariat in which Manuel Marulanda was the senior military figure and Jacobo Arenas, a former Communist Party official, the principal ideologue until his death in 1990. Fronts were organized in blocs under regional commands, and the organization developed schools, training systems, and internal discipline codes.

The massacre and persecution of Unión Patriótica activists are particularly important for understanding the politics of failed incorporation. The party's electoral participation and the FARC's ceasefire did not mean that every local front abandoned armed operations, nor did attacks against party members make their assassination lawful. Judicial and inter-American proceedings subsequently distinguished specific chains of responsibility and failures of state protection. Survivors' memories also show that political identity had local meanings: a municipal councilor, trade-union organizer, teacher, or peasant candidate could be endangered because of an attributed association rather than proven involvement in guerrilla operations. The killings narrowed the space for legal opposition while allowing armed leaders to insist that their warnings had been vindicated. Both processes reinforced the conflict without removing the need to evaluate the FARC's own subsequent actions independently.

Expansion occurred in a political context of attempted accommodation. In the 1980s President Belisario Betancur negotiated a ceasefire with the FARC, and in 1985 the organization and the Communist Party helped to create a legal party, the Patriotic Union (Unión Patriótica). In the years that followed, thousands of its members, including candidates and elected officials, were murdered, largely by paramilitary groups and state agents according to later judicial and inter-American findings, though the exact number is contested. The killings destroyed the party as a political project and became a central argument in FARC's later claim that peaceful political participation was not safe.

The 1990s saw the FARC reach its greatest military strength. A government attack on its Casa Verde headquarters in 1990, intended to weaken it, was followed by an Eighth Conference in 1993 that endorsed further expansion, and by large offensives in the late 1990s against military bases and police posts, including attacks at Las Delicias (1996) and El Billar (1998), in which soldiers were killed or taken prisoner. Financing from illicit economies, discussed in Section 99.4, made growth possible. By the end of the decade the FARC had a large presence in the southern and eastern lowlands and was active in parts of nearly every department.

The organization's revolutionary rhetoric spoke of agrarian reform, anti-imperialism, and a new government, and it ran some local functions in zones where it was dominant. Historians distinguish these claimed aims from conduct. The FARC recruited minors, killed civilians it accused of collaboration, kidnapped for ransom and political leverage, used indiscriminate weapons, and displaced communities. Relations with civilians varied by region and period, from genuine local acceptance in some colonization zones to coercion elsewhere. A source such as a conference document is therefore evidence of what the leadership wished to project and decided, not a description of how fronts treated communities.

Section 99.3

The ELN: Revolutionary Ideology, Oil, and Regional Insurgency

The ELN differed from the FARC in ideology, structure, and fortune. Its founders drew on Cuban example, Marxism, and, increasingly, liberation theology and revolutionary Christianity. Camilo Torres's brief career gave it moral prestige among students and some clergy, and later leaders included priests, notably the Spanish-born Manuel Pérez, known as "El Cura Pérez," who headed the organization through the 1980s and 1990s. Its stated outlook combined armed revolution with nationalism and demands for social justice, and it valued political debate and consensus more than the FARC's more hierarchical model. Its leadership operated through a collective command rather than a single dominant commander.

The oil economy created contradictions for the ELN's claim to represent workers and communities. Damaging a pipeline could interrupt government revenues and pressure a multinational enterprise, but spilled crude could also contaminate farms and water used by residents whose interests the organization claimed to defend. The payment of extortion demands by some contractors did not make their employees voluntary political supporters. In municipalities where the ELN exerted influence, teachers, union leaders, and local authorities sometimes negotiated with armed representatives to keep schools functioning or protect travel. Such negotiations show civilians exercising constrained agency, not necessarily endorsing an insurgency. They also reveal why a single map showing ELN 'presence' cannot establish how the group governed, financed itself, or treated residents across all regions and years.

The ELN's history includes near-destruction. In 1973 an army offensive at Anorí in Antioquia killed many of its members, and internal purges, including executions of suspected infiltrators, compounded the crisis. Its founder withdrew, and the organization was reduced to a few hundred fighters. Recovery came in the 1980s through new regional bases, particularly in the northeast, in Arauca, Santander, Norte de Santander, and the Catatumbo, and in parts of Antioquia, Bolívar, and Cauca. Oil was decisive. The Caño Limón–Coveñas pipeline, opened in 1986 to carry crude from Arauca to the Caribbean coast, offered a symbolic target and a source of funds. The ELN bombed it repeatedly, extorted contractors and companies, and presented the campaign as resistance to foreign exploitation of national resources. Pipeline attacks caused serious environmental damage, and critics, including some community organizations, objected to the costs borne by local people.

The ELN was smaller than the FARC throughout, with a presence concentrated in specific regions and a looser connection between fronts and leadership. Its relations with unions and social organizations, particularly in the oil sector and in regions such as Arauca, were closer, though not always consensual. It also used kidnapping, including high-profile mass abductions at the end of the 1990s, such as the seizure of worshippers from a church in Cali in 1999 and passengers from an airliner earlier that year. It engaged in peace talks at various moments, including exploratory contacts in the 2000s, but no agreement was reached in this period. The contrast with the FARC became part of later negotiations: peace with one organization did not end the conflict with the other.

Section 99.4

Narcotics Financing, Kidnapping, Extortion, and the Economics of Insurgency

Guerrillas needed money to buy weapons, feed fighters, and pay for communications and training. In the 1960s and 1970s they relied on local contributions, robbery, and kidnapping. Over the following decades they developed more systematic sources, and the growth in their size depended on those sources. Historians group them into several categories, and the differences matter.

Economic organization varied among fronts and across time. A unit operating near an oil installation faced different sources of income and incentives from one near a coca-growing district or a highway. The labels 'taxation,' 'protection,' and 'contribution' also concealed radically different forms of consent. Payments demanded under threat remained coercive even when insurgents portrayed them as revolutionary revenue. Some growers sought stability in areas lacking formal market access; others resisted or were punished for refusing demands. Funding created organizational autonomy, made commanders difficult to restrain, and increased dependence on territorial control. It also exposed peasants to reprisals when a different armed actor entered the area. Students should use front-specific, dated evidence before concluding that the entire FARC or ELN financed itself in one uniform way.

Kidnapping for ransom, known in Spanish as secuestro extorsivo, was used by both the FARC and ELN, and the FARC institutionalized it. In 2000 it announced a so-called Law 002, a levy on people and companies with substantial assets, with refusal to pay punishable by abduction. Mass abductions, known popularly as pescas milagrosas ("miracle fishing"), seized travelers at roadblocks. Extortion, or vacuna ("vaccine"), was demanded from ranchers, farmers, businesses, and companies in extractive sectors, notably oil, mining, and construction. These practices produced revenue while also generating hostility among those who paid.

The relationship to drugs requires care. Many fronts in coca-growing regions did not necessarily run laboratories or export cocaine but taxed the coca economy, charging fees on coca paste (gramaje), on traffickers who bought it, and on landing strips and laboratories, and they provided protection and some regulation of prices and disputes. Some fronts and commanders went further, with direct involvement in processing and trafficking, and United States courts have indicted FARC leaders on trafficking charges. The scholarly question is how much of the organization's income came from each source and how that varied across fronts and years. Estimates differ widely and are rarely based on audited data. Students should therefore avoid statements that the FARC was "just a cartel," which overstates the evidence for central direction of exports, and statements that it merely "taxed" an activity, which understates the involvement of some fronts.

Financing shaped organizational growth and political legitimacy. Money from illicit economies enabled expansion, and it brought recruits, but it also altered relations with the communities on which insurgents depended. Where an organization depended on coca, farmers' interests in the crop could align with the guerrillas against eradication, as in parts of Putumayo and Caquetá; yet coercive taxation, kidnapping, and violence against civilians weakened support elsewhere. The fronts were not economically identical. Those in coca regions differed from those in cattle-ranching, oil, or mining zones, and the ELN's reliance on oil extortion differed from the FARC's mix. The paramilitary groups also drew heavily on drug money, as the next division shows, and the contest for coca regions was a material source of their conflict with the guerrillas.

Section 99.5

The Origins of Paramilitarism: Landowners, Private Armies, and Counterinsurgency

Paramilitarism in Colombia did not begin as a single national organization. It grew from many local arrangements in which ranchers, landowners, traffickers, and security officials responded to guerrilla activity, and it took shape in a changing legal framework. In the 1960s, counterinsurgency doctrine and legislation allowed the armed forces to organize civilians for defense and to supply them with weapons. Law 48 of 1968 gave a legal basis for arming civilians in support of public order. Many such arrangements were modest and local, but they set a precedent for blurring the line between state forces and private armed groups.

The 1980s were decisive. In the Magdalena Medio, a river region of cattle ranches and oil, ranchers organized against guerrilla extortion and kidnapping, and groups around Puerto Boyacá worked with army units and with emerging trafficking figures who were buying land. In 1981, after the M-19 kidnapped a relative of the Ochoa trafficking family, traffickers helped to create a group called Death to Kidnappers (Muerte a Secuestradores, MAS). Such groups combined resistance to guerrilla extortion with the interests of new landowners whose wealth came from cocaine. In Córdoba, the rancher and trafficker Fidel Castaño built a private force known as Los Tangueros. In the late 1980s the Colombian state moved against some of these organizations, after massacres including the 1989 killing of judicial investigators at La Rochela, and a 1989 decree ended the legal basis for civilian armed groups. But the networks persisted, and members and structures reappeared in new forms.

The legal framework moved again in 1994, when a decree authorized private security cooperatives, known as Convivir, as a measure for rural security. Convivir proliferated in regions such as Antioquia and Urabá. Critics, including human-rights organizations, argued that some became cover for paramilitary activity, and a 1997 Constitutional Court ruling upheld regulated private vigilance while limiting practices that encroached on public-force responsibilities. Administrative restrictions, license nonrenewals, and reorganizations followed in 1997–1999. The pattern suggests that legal change repeatedly encouraged civilian armed organization and then attempted to reverse it, with uneven consequences on the ground.

The CONVIVIR controversy also requires a precise legal chronology. Decree 356 of 1994 supplied the framework for private community-security services; the names and authorizations used under that framework were contested. In judgment C-572/97, Colombia's Constitutional Court upheld the general possibility of regulated private security while explaining why public-force functions and restricted weapons could not simply pass to civilians. An October 1997 administrative resolution corrected the use of the CONVIVIR designation. Subsequent investigations led authorities to cancel some licenses and decline others in 1998, while forms of permitted private vigilance and, separately, illegal armed networks persisted. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that every association was dissolved immediately by a single 1997 ruling. The distinction matters because the legality of a regulated service cannot prove that a particular local group committed no abuses, just as allegations against some groups do not establish that all registered services were paramilitary fronts.

Historians therefore emphasize several points. Different groups emerged from different local circumstances: ranchers in Córdoba, traffickers in the Magdalena Medio and Meta, business interests in Urabá. Relationships with members of the military and police varied from open cooperation in particular units, documented in later judicial proceedings, to tolerance, to occasional confrontation. The state was not a single actor. Some officers and officials fought paramilitaries, while others collaborated, and the Colombian courts and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights later made findings about specific cases. Assuming a single, nationally planned paramilitary strategy from the beginning would misdescribe the evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Paramilitarism, forced displacement, and land dispossession

Paramilitarism refers to armed groups organized outside the formal command of a state's armed forces but with some relationship to state institutions, local elites, or counterinsurgency aims. In Colombia the relationship ranged from legal authorization to tolerance to open collaboration. Paramilitaries called themselves autodefensas (self-defense groups), a term that reflects their self-justification and that scholars use with caution.

Forced displacement is the compulsory movement of people from their homes or lands because of threats, violence, or fear, within a country's borders. Land dispossession is the loss of land, whether by violent seizure, forced sale, abandonment, or fraudulent registration. The two often coincided but are distinct: people could be displaced without losing title, and land could be taken without mass displacement.

Section 99.6

Carlos Castaño, the ACCU, and the Formation of the AUC

After Fidel Castaño disappeared in the mid-1990s, his brother Carlos took leading roles in the Peasant Self-Defense Forces of Córdoba and Urabá (Autodefensas Campesinas de Córdoba y Urabá, ACCU), which expanded across northwestern Colombia with the help of Vicente Castaño and commanders such as Salvatore Mancuso. Urabá, a banana-growing region on the Caribbean and Pacific borders with historic labor conflict and guerrilla presence, was a central theater. In February 1997, in an operation near the Atrato River in Chocó, army units and paramilitaries moved against communities accused of supporting guerrillas, displacing many families from the Cacarica and Riosucio areas. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights later found Colombia responsible for aspects of this episode.

The AUC's integration was gradual rather than instantaneous. Colombia's Truth Commission describes the April 1997 meeting as an effort to unify existing organizations, followed by additional affiliations in May 1998. The arrangement offered a common public identity and a means to negotiate political recognition, yet commanders retained territory, revenues, fighters, and rivalries of their own. Subsequent expansion brought distinct blocs into departments where local economic interests and armed conflicts differed substantially. Some leaders invoked protection from guerrilla kidnapping; others invested heavily in narcotics trafficking or used violence to reshape electoral and property relations. Their shared name thus cannot establish shared command responsibility for every operation. Historians must examine a particular bloc, period, and chain of command, and must also investigate allegations about collaboration by local officials without converting those allegations into claims about the entire Colombian state.

On 18 April 1997, according to the reconstruction of the Truth Commission, representatives of the ACCU and other regional organizations met and established the Autodefensas Unidas de Colombia. Its components included groups from Córdoba and Urabá, the Magdalena Medio, the eastern plains, and Santander and Sur del Cesar. The AUC was a confederation, not a unified army. Regional blocs, among them the Northern, Central Bolívar, Catatumbo, and Cacique Nutibara blocs, were commanded by leaders with their own territories, finances, and interests, and tensions among them were serious. Some commanders were former military or police, some were landowners, and some were trafficking figures who had bought paramilitary franchises. Some groups outside the AUC, such as the eastern plains' Casanare force, remained independent and fought the confederation.

The AUC adopted an anticommunist and counterinsurgent discourse. Carlos Castaño became its public face, giving interviews that presented the organization as a self-defense movement forced to act because the state failed. Observers, including Colombian courts, found that the organization also fought for control of economic resources. Commanders eventually acknowledged that drug trafficking financed the organization, and later judicial evidence suggests that the sources of money varied across blocs, from extortion of businesses and landowners to taxation of coca and direct trafficking. Relationships with local elites, including ranchers, business owners, and politicians, were crucial to expansion. The AUC also offered some local services and rules, but evidence shows that its main instruments were terror, selective killing, and massacres, directed far more at civilians than at guerrilla combatants.

Carlos Castaño was killed in April 2004 amid a split within the organization, according to later testimony that his death was ordered by others in the leadership. The case illustrates the fragmentation beneath the confederation's unity. Students should note that the AUC's growth in the late 1990s coincided with the expansion of the guerrillas and with the Pastrana negotiations, and that its stated aim of stopping insurgent expansion contrasted with the pattern of its victims.

Section 99.7

Paramilitary Massacres and the Transformation of Rural Violence

Paramilitary expansion in the late 1990s was marked by massacres, which Colombian data-gatherers define as the intentional killing of several defenseless people in the same event. The CNMH has documented that paramilitary groups were responsible for a large share of massacres in this period, though guerrillas and state forces also committed them. Two cases, both extensively documented, illustrate the pattern and the failures of protection that accompanied it.

Counts of victims in these massacres illustrate the evidentiary problems created by disappearance, intimidation, and flight. For Mapiripán, later human-rights proceedings cited approximately forty-nine people executed or disappeared; that is not the same as forty-nine individually identified bodies. In its case study of El Salado, the CNMH documents sixty deaths in the broader February 2000 massacre, including victims beyond the town's central square. These categories should be reported as defined by each investigation. Survivors' accounts also explain why an event's significance cannot be measured by its death toll alone: relatives searched for people whose remains were missing, land was abandoned, and fear limited testimony long afterward. Judicial findings about specific military failures or complicity are essential evidence, but they require attention to the cases and persons actually adjudicated.

At Mapiripán, a small town in Meta, a force of paramilitaries arrived in July 1997. Judicial and inter-American findings show that groups of armed men were flown from the northwest to the airport at San José del Guaviare and moved to Mapiripán, where over several days, beginning on 15 July, they seized and killed civilians accused of collaborating with the guerrillas. Local officials had warned of the danger, and military units in the region did not intervene effectively. The exact number of victims remains uncertain because many bodies were thrown into the river. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights found Colombia responsible in 2005, and military officers were prosecuted in Colombian courts. The massacre was followed by displacement of many residents and, according to later research, changes in the region's landholding.

At El Salado, in the Montes de María in northern Colombia, paramilitaries entered the hamlet in February 2000 and over several days, from about 16 to 21 February, killed dozens of residents after assembling them in the plaza. The CNMH has documented at least sixty victims, with torture and sexual violence among the abuses. Some state security units operated in the area at the time. Most of the population fled, and for years the settlement was largely abandoned. Survivors later organized to return, and the case became a major example in the national discussion of memory and reparations.

These cases changed the character of rural violence. The massacres were intended to terrorize and to remove populations suspected of sympathy with the opposing side, and a public display of cruelty was part of the intended message. Students should note three points. First, the violence was not simply a response to guerrilla action, since communities were attacked because armed actors claimed they were collaborators, a claim that often rested on little evidence. Second, the violence was not indiscriminate, since it targeted community leaders, teachers, unionists, and others. Third, other armed actors were responsible for other violence in the same regions, including guerrilla massacres and killings, and the historical record must attribute each event to its documented perpetrators. The history of paramilitarism is not an account of guerrilla wrongdoing, nor is it an account of all violence.

Section 99.8

Political Penetration, Land Dispossession, and the Parapolitics Scandal

The strength of the paramilitary groups depended on relations with local elites. In some regions paramilitary commanders influenced municipal and departmental elections by threatening rival candidates, directing votes, and funding campaigns. In some places local officials directed public contracts and services toward allied interests, and ranchers, business owners, and politicians took part, whether willingly or under pressure. A meeting in July 2001 at Santa Fe de Ralito in Córdoba, known as the Pact of Ralito, brought paramilitary commanders together with politicians and others to express a political project, and the document later became evidence in judicial investigations.

Electoral penetration was not always a simple purchase of a vote. Armed commanders could exclude rival candidates from campaigning, influence local contracting, intimidate election workers, or exercise leverage over mayors and legislators after they took office. Such coercion damaged representation even where formal elections still occurred. Dispossession was similarly layered: a household might flee under threat, leave land unused, later discover a disputed transfer, and face years of litigation before returning. Some property transactions had documentary form while lacking meaningful consent. Investigations associated with the parapolítica cases therefore demand careful distinction between a recorded meeting, a criminal agreement, a conviction, and an allegation not sustained in court. These differences protect both historical accuracy and the rights of victims and accused persons.

The scandal known as parapolítica surfaced in the mid-2000s. As demobilization proceeded, paramilitary commanders and former members gave testimony, and in 2006 information from a seized computer and from witnesses led the Supreme Court to open investigations against members of Congress for alleged links to paramilitary groups. Over the following years dozens of members of Congress, together with governors, mayors, and other officials, were investigated, and many were convicted. Investigations also reached some security officials and the national intelligence service. Several politicians were from the governing coalition, and the scandal became a major source of political conflict.

Land is the second major theme. Forced displacement left land abandoned, and abandoned land could be bought cheaply, occupied, or registered by others. Estimates of the area abandoned or taken between the 1980s and 2010 range widely, from around two million hectares to more than six million, depending on definitions and methods, and no figure is settled. The mechanisms varied: violent seizure, forced sales, fraudulent titles, collusion by notaries and registry officials, and legal acquisition by third parties who knew of the circumstances. Studies in regions such as Urabá, Córdoba, and the Montes de María have documented large-scale accumulation, including conversion to cattle ranching and, in some places, oil palm.

Historians urge precision. Parapolitics does not mean that every politician in a paramilitary region, every landowner, or every soldier was complicit. Many were victims of threats, and many resisted, with some killed for doing so. The scandal revealed that links between armed groups and officials were extensive in some regions and cases, as courts established, but judicial findings concerned individuals, not entire institutions or parties. Land accumulation by third parties likewise varied from deliberate dispossession to purchases whose circumstances were obscure. The relation among violence, politics, and land is a central theme of the Truth Commission's report, and it shaped the later land-restitution policies discussed in Section 99.20.

Section 99.9

Forced Displacement, Rural Communities, and the Human Geography of War

Forced displacement was among the most widespread consequences of the conflict. The Truth Commission, using the national victims register, reports more than three million displacement cases registered for 1991–2002, with an annual peak above 800,000 in 2002 under the cited methodology. These are registered cases, and the register's methods, coverage, and periods differ from those of other organizations, so figures must be compared with care. A case is not necessarily a unique person, since families could be displaced more than once.

Displacement data must be read with their reporting dates attached. The CNMH's 2015 study, using the victims register as it stood in December 2014, identified 618,058 displaced persons for the 2002 peak; a later Truth Commission narrative drawing on a subsequent RUV cutoff described more than 800,000 for that year. A separate integrated CEV–JEP–HRDAG project estimated 730,904. The differences do not establish three competing numbers of people who could simply be added; they reflect revisions, coverage, definitions, and statistical reconstruction. For households, the experience was also rarely a single move. Families could flee more than once, settle informally at an urban periphery, lose proof of landholding, or live for years without secure work. Women often carried disproportionate burdens of securing documents, schooling, and household income after displacement.

Displacement was not simply an incidental effect of fighting. In some regions it was used deliberately to remove populations considered hostile, to obtain land, to clear corridors for trafficking and economic projects, or to alter local political control. Threats, massacres, selective killings, forced recruitment, landmines, and confinement all prompted people to flee. In Urabá and Chocó, Afro-Colombian communities with collective titles under Law 70 of 1993 and Indigenous communities with resguardos were displaced, and their displacement raised issues of collective land rights. Campesino communities in the Magdalena Medio, Montes de María, Meta, and Putumayo fled in large numbers. Most displaced people moved within their departments or to cities such as Bogotá, Medellín, and Cartagena, where they settled in peripheral neighborhoods and entered the informal economy.

The effects were uneven. Women headed many displaced households, and women faced sexual violence as a weapon of war, with Colombian institutions later documenting widespread impunity. Children's schooling was interrupted, community institutions such as councils, cooperatives, and churches were disrupted, and access to health care often collapsed. Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities lost not only homes but territory central to their culture and economy, and in some cases their leaders were targeted. Return was difficult, since land might be occupied, threats persisted, and local services had disappeared.

The state's legal response began with Law 387 of 1997, which defined displaced persons, set out their rights, and established a national system of attention. The law was a significant recognition that displacement was a state responsibility, but implementation lagged. In 2004 the Constitutional Court, responding to thousands of petitions, declared an "unconstitutional state of affairs" regarding displaced persons because of the systematic failure of institutions to protect their rights, and it supervised remedial actions for years. The ruling illustrates the role of Colombian courts as institutions through which victims acted.

Section 99.10

Kidnappings, Hostages, Land Mines, and the Civilian Experience

Kidnapping affected civilians across social classes. Guerrilla groups, and to a lesser degree paramilitaries and criminal groups, seized people for ransom and for political leverage. Official counts show kidnappings rising through the 1990s to a peak around 2000–2002 and then falling sharply, but different institutions count differently, and the figures should be used within one series. Victims included farmers and ranchers, business people, children, and public officials. The FARC held hundreds of soldiers and police for years in jungle camps, hoping to exchange them for imprisoned guerrillas. Politicians also were seized: the former presidential candidate Íngrid Betancourt in February 2002, and in April 2002 a group of provincial legislators from Valle del Cauca, most of whom were later killed in captivity, in 2007. Public marches against kidnapping became prominent civic events.

Bojayá also demonstrates why a massacre should be situated within its wider setting. The FARC fired an improvised explosive projectile during combat with paramilitaries, and it struck the church where residents had sought shelter on 2 May 2002. Institutional sources commonly identify seventy-nine direct deaths in the church attack; some broader reconstructions count additional deaths before or after the explosion and later related mortality. Those totals answer different questions. The responsibility of the group that launched the projectile should not be obscured, but neither should the presence of combatants near civilians, the population's warnings, or failures to protect residents be omitted. The community's later return, rituals, efforts to identify the dead, and campaigns for dignity are part of the history of Bojayá—not merely an epilogue to the moment of destruction.

Child recruitment was another form of harm. Both guerrilla and paramilitary groups recruited minors, some through coercion, some through promises or poverty, and some who joined from conditions of rural isolation or abuse. Estimates of the number are uncertain. Former child soldiers later gave testimony describing hardship and abuse, including forced contraception and abortions for girls in some units, according to later accounts. Antipersonnel mines, laid mainly by guerrillas and in smaller numbers by other actors, injured civilians and soldiers, and Colombia became among the most mine-affected countries in the world. Threats and killings of community leaders, teachers, union members, journalists, and local officials were persistent, and many communities lost their leaders.

The Bojayá tragedy of 2 May 2002 is one of the best-documented attacks on civilians. Bojayá is in the Atrato River region of Chocó, home to Afro-Colombian and Indigenous communities. In the town of Bellavista, residents sheltered in the church and a nearby building while the FARC and a paramilitary bloc fought for control of the area. A device launched by the FARC, a crude cylinder bomb, struck the church. The CNMH documents seventy-nine civilian deaths in the attack on the church, with many wounded, and many more fled afterward. Responsibility needs careful statement. The FARC fired the weapon that struck civilians, and later acknowledged responsibility and apologized. Wider responsibility also involves the paramilitary presence in the town, the actions of both sides during the combat, and the failure of state forces to respond to repeated warnings, according to the Ombudsman and later investigations. Distinguishing these levels of responsibility, the weapon, the combat, and the failure to protect, is part of accurate history.

Section 99.11

Andrés Pastrana and the Failed Caguán Peace Process, 1998–2002

Andrés Pastrana, a Conservative, won the presidency in June 1998 after campaigning on negotiation with the FARC. Public weariness with violence and the economic recession of the late 1990s made peace a popular promise, and a symbolic photograph of Pastrana with the FARC's senior leader during the campaign became a political asset. The FARC made it a precondition that government troops be withdrawn from a zone in which talks would take place. In late 1998 the government demilitarized an area of about 42,000 square kilometers, roughly the size of Switzerland, comprising five municipalities in Meta and Caquetá centered on San Vicente del Caguán. The zone, known as the despeje, was to be free of army and police presence while negotiations proceeded.

The Caguán process was neither continuous peace nor a negotiation with no substantive agenda. Parties debated reform, security, and the nature of political participation while military confrontation continued elsewhere. The creation of a demilitarized negotiating zone made the process visible and gave critics a powerful symbol of territorial concession, but it did not suspend all state duties toward civilians inside it. Prolonged talks without a durable ceasefire deepened public skepticism, while violence and kidnapping undermined confidence that armed leaders could be trusted to implement an agreement. On 20 February 2002, President Pastrana terminated the process. That decision affected the presidential election and the appeal of a more forceful security strategy, though it did not prove that negotiations under different conditions were impossible.

Talks opened in January 1999 in a ceremony from which Marulanda was absent, an early sign of difficulty. The sides agreed on a "Common Agenda" covering a range of issues, including economic and social reform, the political system, and the armed conflict itself. Progress was limited. The government did not insist on a ceasefire, so fighting continued across the country while talks proceeded, and violence against civilians continued, including kidnappings and paramilitary massacres. International verification of the zone was weak, and disputes arose over whether the FARC was using the zone to hold hostages, to rest and train, to operate coca-related activity, and to rearm, claims the government and military made, and the FARC denied. The negotiators also lacked agreed procedures for resolving such disputes.

Public confidence declined as events accumulated: kidnappings, FARC attacks, and perceptions that the organization was exploiting the process. The talks ended on 20 February 2002 after the FARC hijacked a commercial aircraft and kidnapped a senator aboard, an event that Pastrana described as a rupture, and he ordered the military to retake the zone. Observers disagree on responsibility for the failure. Some emphasize the FARC's military strategy and unwillingness to commit to a ceasefire; others stress the government's weakness, divided military, and the absence of clear rules; still others point to the paramilitary expansion and the lack of guarantees for political participation after the Patriotic Union killings. It is reasonable to conclude that several factors combined.

The consequences were large. The collapse discredited negotiation for much of the public, and confidence in the FARC's willingness to bargain fell. It prepared the ground for the election in May 2002 of Álvaro Uribe, who campaigned on a firm security policy. Students should note a nuance: the failure of Caguán did not mean negotiation was impossible, since later talks occurred, but that the particular design of a demilitarized zone without ceasefire and verification had produced distrust. The lessons were studied by both sides before 2012.

Section 99.12

The Origins of Plan Colombia: State Reconstruction and U.S. Assistance

Plan Colombia began as a Colombian initiative. Pastrana's government drafted a strategy during 1998–1999, which was presented in September 1999 under a title that translates as the Plan for Peace, Prosperity, and the Strengthening of the State. It sought to combine a negotiated peace with the guerrillas, reconstruction of state institutions including justice, defense, and local government, alternative development for rural areas, counternarcotics, and social investment. Colombia hoped for substantial international funding, particularly from the United States and Europe, to cover costs that it could not pay alone during a severe economic recession. The version that circulated and was debated in Washington gave greater weight to security and counternarcotics than the original Colombian emphasis on social development.

An important financial distinction is between the initial U.S. emergency package and the longer course of assistance. Public Law 106-246 included approximately $1.3 billion for regional programs, with about $860.3 million designated for Colombia, but those totals should not be inserted uncritically into later GAO annual funding series: fiscal years, recipients, and classifications are not identical. GAO-09-71 reports program-objective appropriations from fiscal 2000 through 2008, totaling about $6.13 billion. An appropriation makes funds legally available; it does not establish when equipment was delivered, a program spent its allocation, or benefits reached a municipality. The long-term strategy also depended on much larger Colombian contributions and political decisions made in Bogotá. Comparing the two governments' language is useful, but evaluating actual implementation requires separately measured expenditures and outcomes.

In the United States, concerns about the growth of drug production, a rise in heroin from Colombia, and fears of state weakness drove interest. The Clinton administration proposed an emergency package for the Andean region, and Congress debated it in early 2000, with disagreements over human-rights conditions, the military component, and the risk of deepening U.S. involvement in a counterinsurgency war. Supporters argued that Colombia faced a crisis requiring help; critics argued that the package would strengthen military forces with a record of human-rights problems and ties to paramilitaries, and might escalate the conflict. The outcome was Public Law 106-246, signed in July 2000, which provided about $1.3 billion in emergency assistance for the Andean and Caribbean region, of which approximately $860.3 million was designated for Colombia. These amounts must not be confused. The larger figure covered several countries and programs, and the Colombia portion was the part directed to Colombian institutions.

Most of the Colombian portion was allocated to military and police programs, including the training and equipping of counternarcotics battalions and the supply of helicopters for mobility, with smaller portions for alternative development, judicial reform, human rights, and assistance to displaced people. The law attached human-rights conditions to part of the military aid, with provisions for waivers by the executive. European governments pledged less than Colombia had hoped, and Europe was more skeptical of the military emphasis. The differing priorities, Colombia's wider reconstruction plan and Washington's counternarcotics focus, were evident from the start, and later years saw broadening of the U.S. mandate. After the September 2001 attacks and the designation of the AUC as a terrorist organization, U.S. officials extended permitted uses of aid to counterterrorism, so counterinsurgency and counternarcotics were no longer treated separately.

It is therefore a mistake to treat Plan Colombia as an American program. The Colombian government wrote the plan and shaped its implementation, and Colombian budgets paid for a large part of the costs, particularly after 2002. U.S. assistance, however, was a major factor in what the Colombian military could do, and the American appropriation, in years that followed, was commonly itself called "Plan Colombia." Historians therefore distinguish the Colombian plan from American funding programs, and this chapter does so throughout.

Historical Vocabulary
Plan Colombia, democratic security, demobilization, and transitional accountability

Plan Colombia names the Colombian government's 1999 strategy and, by extension, the stream of U.S. assistance that began in 2000. Democratic Security was the policy of the Uribe government, aiming to reassert state authority over national territory through military and police presence, intelligence, and citizen cooperation. Demobilization is the formal disbanding of armed units and the surrender of weapons, usually associated with reintegration, the return of former combatants to civilian life. Transitional accountability refers to legal mechanisms that seek truth, justice, and reparation for victims while a society moves from conflict toward peace.

Students should note that demobilizing a group does not automatically dismantle its criminal networks, and that security policy and accountability can conflict. These distinctions recur in Divisions IV and V.

Section 99.13

Counternarcotics, Aerial Eradication, and the Limits of Drug-Control Strategy

The counternarcotics component of Plan Colombia centered on the eradication of coca and poppy, interdiction of drugs, and the destruction of laboratories. Its signature method was aerial spraying with the herbicide glyphosate, applied by aircraft operated by contractors, with Colombian police and U.S. support. The strategy aimed to destroy crops before harvest, deter planting, and reduce supply. The program expanded rapidly after 2000, beginning in Putumayo, then Caquetá, Guaviare, and later other regions. Manual eradication and alternative development were also part of the plan, though the aerial program received the largest funding and attention.

The GAO's 2008 assessment offers a good example of how choice of baseline and measurement system changes an evaluation. Its U.S. estimates placed Colombian coca cultivation at about 136,000 hectares in 2000, 157,000 in 2006, and 167,000 in 2007. These observations did not demonstrate achievement of the intended 50 percent decline. GAO also warned that U.S. survey coverage had expanded substantially, complicating trend comparisons; UNODC used different methods and published different cultivation totals. An aircraft's report of hectares sprayed cannot be treated as a measure of hectares permanently removed, since growers may replant or relocate. Nor does a cultivated hectare translate mechanically into an identical quantity of cocaine in every place and year. The policy debate therefore must include measurement uncertainty, farmers' responses, environmental concerns, and demand beyond Colombia.

The results were mixed and contested. Official estimates of coca cultivation depended on the source, since the United States and the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime used different methods and sometimes reached different totals. Within Colombia, estimates of cultivated area fell in some periods and rose in others, and cultivation moved: when spraying intensified in Putumayo, coca spread to Nariño, Guaviare, and the Pacific coast, a pattern often called the balloon effect, while farmers adapted by replanting, planting in smaller plots, and growing varieties better able to survive. Eradication measured in hectares sprayed did not equal a permanent reduction in cultivation or in cocaine supply, since cultivated area, yields, and processing efficiency all affected output. Students must distinguish activity from outcome.

The program had social and environmental costs. Spraying affected legal crops and food plots in some places, and communities complained of damage to crops, water, animals, and health. Studies of the effects of glyphosate were disputed, and Ecuador brought a case against Colombia over spraying near its border. Alternative development projects often reached too few farmers, arrived late, or lacked markets and roads, so farmers had few legal alternatives. In some regions, small farmers who depended on coca were treated as criminals by an eradication policy aimed at traffickers, and these farmers became targets of both state eradication and armed groups' demands. Spraying was gradually reduced and, in the 2010s, suspended in Colombia.

Interdiction produced large seizures and the destruction of laboratories, and extradition of trafficking leaders continued. These measures disrupted organizations, but the cocaine market adjusted, with new routes and new organizations appearing. The experience illustrates a general historical point about prohibition-era drug control: pressure on one part of a market often displaces rather than eliminates activity, since demand in consumer countries remained high. Colombia's cocaine economy did not disappear, and its structure, and the actors in it, changed.

Section 99.14

Military Professionalization and Álvaro Uribe's Democratic Security Policy

Military reform began before Uribe. In the late 1990s, after humiliating defeats by the FARC, the armed forces and the Pastrana government undertook changes, with U.S. training and equipment, to professionalize the army: creating mobile brigades, a counternarcotics brigade, improved intelligence, and greater use of helicopters. These reforms took years to produce results. Colombian military and police were also beginning to shift from static defense of posts toward offensive operations and to increase the proportion of professional, rather than conscript, soldiers.

Military modernization crossed presidential administrations. The Pastrana government had already begun reforms to training, command arrangements, mobility, and cooperation before Uribe took office; the later security strategy intensified and expanded rather than invented them. Improved access to contested municipalities sometimes allowed police, teachers, and health personnel to return, but the arrival of soldiers was not automatically equivalent to functioning courts or trustworthy local government. Civilian oversight mattered because soldiers trained to defeat armed opponents operated among populations where the identification of combatants was often contested. Likewise, a decline in kidnapping statistics offered evidence of improved security without establishing that extortion, sexual violence, or forced displacement had ended. The proper measure of state consolidation is more demanding than a military unit's ability to enter a region.

Álvaro Uribe, an independent former governor of Antioquia whose father had been killed in an attempted guerrilla kidnapping, won the presidency in May 2002 and took office on 7 August. His Democratic Security policy aimed to restore state authority across the national territory. It increased military and police numbers and spending, with a special tax on wealth to help finance it. It expanded the police presence so that every municipality had some police, created units of "peasant soldiers" recruited locally, established networks of informants, and offered rewards and incentives for the surrender of insurgents. Operations such as Plan Patriota (2003 onward) aimed at the FARC's southern strongholds. Intelligence coordination and mobility, partly supported by U.S. assistance, improved the state's reach, and highways, previously unsafe, reopened to travel, a visible effect that many Colombians welcomed.

Public support was high, and security measurably improved by several indicators, including kidnappings, homicides, and attacks on infrastructure. A constitutional amendment allowed Uribe to be re-elected in 2006. But the policy also raised concerns about civil liberties and accountability. Measures extended military powers of arrest and investigation in some areas, and the Constitutional Court struck down parts of an anti-terrorism statute in 2004. The relationship between the state and the media, judges, and critics became contentious, and surveillance by the security agency, the DAS, targeted judges, journalists, and opposition figures, a scandal that came to light later. Critics argued that the policy prioritized military results over human rights and over addressing the causes of conflict, while supporters argued that the state had to recover authority before reform could succeed.

The effect of the military changes needs careful attribution. Improved security reflected a combination of factors: the professionalization begun earlier, resources from Plan Colombia and Colombian budgets, a larger and better-equipped force, high public support, the demobilization of paramilitary groups, and the FARC's own strategic errors. Historians therefore resist attributing improvement to a single policy or leader. Security gains and exceptional measures coexisted, and whether the latter were necessary to achieve the former is itself debated.

Section 99.15

The Changing Military Balance and Operation Jaque

By the mid-2000s the FARC's conventional military capabilities had weakened. Its large-scale attacks on bases became harder as the army gained mobility, air support, and intelligence. The organization lost territory in some southern and central regions and shifted toward smaller units, ambushes, snipers, and mines, a strategic adaptation sometimes described as retreat into guerrilla warfare of an earlier kind. Desertions rose as the government offered individual reintegration incentives, with official figures counting thousands of individual demobilizations. Senior losses followed: in March 2008 the commander Raúl Reyes was killed in a Colombian operation across the border in Ecuador, which provoked a diplomatic crisis with Ecuador and Venezuela; Manuel Marulanda's death from natural causes in March 2008 was announced in May; and later Mono Jojoy (2010) and Alfonso Cano (2011) were killed.

Operation Jaque on 2 July 2008 demonstrated improved Colombian intelligence and coordination and offered an enormous political symbol of state capacity. Its fifteen rescued hostages included Íngrid Betancourt, three U.S. contractors, and eleven Colombian military and police personnel. The event cannot be read in isolation from other developments that year: FARC leaders died or were killed, desertions and internal strains increased, and the organization adjusted its operations after losing capacity to hold large formations in the field. But the rescue did not establish that all prisoners held by armed groups had been freed, or that every rural region was secure. Students should distinguish the symbolic impact of a televised success from the slower institutional work needed to prevent new abuses and protect the rights of civilians.

On 2 July 2008 the army carried out Operation Jaque, a rescue in which Colombian military intelligence deceived guards holding hostages in Guaviare and flew fifteen people to freedom without a shot. The Defense Ministry identified them as Íngrid Betancourt, three United States citizens working as defense contractors, and eleven Colombian soldiers and police officers. The operation had a deceptive element, and Colombian authorities later acknowledged the improper use of the Red Cross emblem by one participant, a matter that raised questions about the protection of humanitarian symbols. The rescue's significance was political and psychological. It showed that military intelligence had penetrated the FARC, restored the army's prestige after years of setbacks, and weakened the FARC's remaining bargaining leverage, since high-profile hostages were among its main assets.

Historians place Jaque within a sequence, not as a decisive turning point by itself. The rescue followed years of improved intelligence and the killing or defection of leaders, and it did not end the FARC, which continued to operate, to hold hostages, and to attack. Some analysts argue that the organization's weakening encouraged its leadership to reconsider negotiation, while others point to the resilience of local fronts and finance. The section's point is to recognize that the balance shifted without assuming that the FARC was defeated.

Section 99.16

Human Rights, Extrajudicial Killings, and State Accountability

Security gains came with documented abuses by state personnel. Two categories are distinct. First, human-rights organizations, judges, and the Colombian and inter-American courts documented cooperation between some members of the military and police and paramilitary groups, including cases such as Mapiripán, discussed in Section 99.7. These involved particular units and officers, not the institution as a whole. Second, a pattern emerged in the 2000s, and became public in 2008, of civilians killed by army units and presented as guerrillas killed in combat. The practice became known as falsos positivos ("false positives"), a euphemism for extrajudicial killings.

The legal distinction between an investigation and an adjudicated individual offense is especially important here. In Case 03, the JEP's preliminary figure of 6,402 covers persons killed between 2002 and 2008 and falsely presented as combat deaths, based on comparison of multiple institutional datasets and testimony. It does not identify 6,402 separate convictions or exhaust the period's total unlawful killings. Judicial investigations have examined particular units, regions, command relationships, incentives, and the ways victims were recruited or misidentified. This method permits more precise responsibility than either dismissing every allegation as propaganda or assuming that every member of the armed forces committed crimes. The existence of real security gains does not reduce the duty to investigate those killings; the duty is part of what distinguishes state authority from extralegal coercion.

The practice was encouraged by pressure for measurable results, including body counts, and by incentives such as promotions, leave, and bonuses. Victims were often young men from poor neighborhoods recruited with false promises of work, taken to remote areas, killed, and dressed as insurgents. The scandal came to national attention in the fall of 2008 when families in the Bogotá suburb of Soacha reported missing young men who turned up as combat casualties in the northeast. The government dismissed dozens of officers, the army commander resigned, and prosecutors and human-rights organizations pursued cases. A United Nations special rapporteur who visited Colombia in 2009 reported that extrajudicial killings were systematic in a number of units, a conclusion the government contested. Families of victims, notably the mothers of Soacha, became leading figures in demanding accountability and faced threats for it.

The Special Jurisdiction for Peace (JEP), created under the 2016 peace agreement, took up the matter in its Case 03. The JEP's investigation has identified a preliminary figure of 6,402 people killed between 2002 and 2008 and falsely presented as combat deaths. This is a later judicially investigated finding, based on prosecutorial and military records, testimony, and victims' accounts, and it should be differentiated from what contemporaries knew in 2008. The figure is preliminary and may be revised; it is not a figure for all killings by the army, nor for all deaths in the conflict. The JEP's findings also name particular units and officers, and some officers have acknowledged responsibility.

Historians therefore hold two ideas together. The military's improved capacity and the reduction in several indicators of violence were real. So were the unlawful killings. The two were not unrelated, since incentives for results helped produce abuses, but the existence of abuses does not prove that the overall campaign against insurgents was illegitimate, any more than security gains prove the actions of all personnel were lawful. Distinguishing lawful military operations from unlawful conduct requires attention to evidence in particular cases and units.

The partial dismantling of paramilitary organizations, the persistence of violence, and the limits of military victory before the 2012 negotiations.

Section 99.17

AUC Negotiations, Demobilization, and the Justice and Peace Law

The Uribe government opened talks with the AUC soon after taking office. In July 2003 the government and AUC commanders signed an agreement at Santa Fe de Ralito committing the organization to a ceasefire and to demobilize by the end of 2005. The decision to negotiate with paramilitaries, while refusing to negotiate with the FARC, was controversial: supporters argued that it would end a major source of violence, while critics argued that it legitimized organizations responsible for massacres and tied to drug trafficking, and that the government had little leverage to enforce disarmament.

The Justice and Peace framework reflected a difficult effort to exchange disarmament for conditional legal treatment, not a blanket pardon. Its benefits required specified forms of demobilization, disclosure, cooperation, and reparative responsibility, although the extent to which these conditions were fulfilled remained contested. The law's consolidated online versions contain later amendments and judicial modifications; the terms applicable in 2005 should therefore be separated from rules enacted afterward. Registered demobilization figures—often summarized as approximately thirty-one thousand participants in the collective AUC process—also demand caution. Administrative registration is not proof that every participant held the same military status, surrendered every weapon, or ceased all illicit activity. Victims asked whether the process produced truthful accounts of disappearances, recovered remains, and restitution of their property, questions not answered by headcounts alone.

Demobilization proceeded in ceremonies between 2003 and 2006. The first major bloc, the Cacique Nutibara Bloc in Medellín, demobilized in November 2003, and other blocs followed, with the last large ceremonies in 2006. Official figures count about 31,000 combatants demobilized in collective ceremonies, though analysts questioned the numbers, since some who demobilized appear not to have been long-standing combatants, and many weapons surrendered were fewer than the combatants counted. Individuals were offered assistance and legal benefits, and a reintegration program was set up. A group of the most senior commanders was concentrated in a special facility for the negotiation of legal terms.

The legal framework came in Law 975 of 25 July 2005, the Justice and Peace Law. The law offered members of illegal armed groups who had committed serious crimes an alternative sentence of five to eight years, in place of ordinary penalties, if they demobilized, surrendered illegally acquired assets for reparation, confessed their crimes fully, and complied with other conditions. Victims were given rights to truth, justice, and reparation. The law was not a blanket unconditional amnesty, and the benefits were conditional and revocable. It was nevertheless criticized by human-rights organizations as too lenient. In 2006 the Constitutional Court reviewed it and strengthened its requirements, including stronger victims' participation and the obligation to make a full confession, so that benefits could be lost for omissions.

The process produced significant disclosures. Commanders gave extended testimony, called versiones libres, which revealed mass graves, massacres, and the involvement of politicians, businesses, and security officials, and led to the parapolitics investigations. But the process also had limits. In May 2008, the government extradited fourteen senior commanders to the United States to face drug-trafficking charges, citing their failure to comply with commitments and their continued criminal activity. Victims and observers argued that the extraditions interrupted their testimony and cut off access to information about crimes and assets. Few convictions were achieved relative to the number of victims, and reparations were slow. The key analytic distinction is between the demobilization of an organization's armed structure and the dismantling of its criminal networks, wealth, and political relationships; the first occurred to a significant degree, the second only partially.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did AUC Demobilization Produce Genuine Peace and Accountability?

The question: Did the 2003–2006 process dismantle paramilitary organizations and deliver accountability to victims, or did it chiefly transform them?

Interpretation A — A real but partial achievement: Supporters point to the demobilization of tens of thousands of combatants, a decline in massacres and some other indicators in the following years, the disclosure of crimes and of political links through versiones libres, and the opening of parapolitics investigations that sent politicians to prison. On this view, the process, while flawed, gave Colombia information and judicial results it would not otherwise have had.
Interpretation B — Demobilization without dismantling: Critics note that criminal and coercive networks, assets, and political relationships persisted, that many former combatants rearmed or joined successor groups, that extraditions cut off disclosure, that convictions were few, and that victims received little reparation or protection. On this view, the process reorganized rather than ended paramilitarism.

Evidence: Both interpretations use official demobilization figures, court judgments, Justice and Peace Unit statistics, reports by the Organization of American States verification mission, and victims' testimony. They disagree about which indicators matter and how to count success.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the later transitional-justice design in 2016 is understood, since it drew lessons from this earlier experience, and how Colombians assess the state's willingness and capacity to deliver accountability. The two interpretations are not exclusive: the process can have yielded real disclosures and also fallen short of dismantling.

Section 99.18

Paramilitary Successor Groups, Parapolitics, and the Limits of Reintegration

Within a few years of the demobilization ceremonies, new armed and criminal groups appeared in regions formerly dominated by the AUC. The government classified them as criminal bands rather than as political or paramilitary organizations and called them bandas criminales emergentes, abbreviated BACRIM. Names included the Black Eagles, the Rastrojos, and the Urabeños, the last of which later became one of Colombia's largest criminal organizations. Observers, including the Organization of American States mission that monitored demobilization, reported rearmament by former paramilitary members and mid-level commanders. Analysts disagree about whether these groups were continuations of paramilitarism or new criminal enterprises, and the label used matters, since it shaped legal treatment and policy. Their members included former AUC fighters, some drawn from those who did not demobilize, and new recruits; their activities centered on drug trafficking, extortion, illegal mining, and territorial control, and they continued to threaten communities and leaders.

The label BACRIM captured an administrative effort to classify criminal organizations operating after demobilization, but it sometimes masked continuities in personnel, territory, revenue, and coercive practice. In some areas former fighters reassembled in different networks; elsewhere groups exploited vacancies created when older commanders withdrew. Communities could experience a formal peace ceremony followed by renewed extortion or threats against claimants seeking restitution. The question of whether a successor group was politically paramilitary, commercially criminal, or both must be answered from its behavior and relationships, not from its chosen name. Judicial investigations and local testimony also show why reintegration was uneven: sustainable livelihoods, credible security guarantees, attention to victims, and prosecutions of continuing offenders were all necessary if organizational disarmament was to become a lasting civilian peace.

Reintegration programs had mixed results. Many demobilized individuals did return to civilian life, and some programs offered job training and support. But economic opportunities were limited, stigma was strong, security for former combatants was uneven, and some commanders retained influence over their former units. Reintegration was complicated by the persistence of illicit economies, which provided employment for armed men, and by the failure of the state to establish its presence in many former paramilitary zones. Where communities had not been able to return and perpetrators lived nearby, tensions were acute.

Judicial processes continued. The parapolitics investigations led to convictions of numerous members of Congress and other officials through the end of the decade, and cases involving businesses and security officials advanced more slowly. Justice and Peace proceedings uncovered thousands of crimes and graves, but they moved slowly relative to the number of victims, and a large number of cases remained unresolved. Land restitution under Justice and Peace was limited, since the process focused on assets offered by commanders, which proved small compared with the land taken. People who sought the return of land faced threats, and community leaders who led claims were killed in a number of cases, as human-rights organizations documented.

These problems formed the background to the reforms of 2011, discussed in Section 99.20. They also shaped debates about how any future settlement with the guerrillas would handle accountability, guarantees of non-repetition, and protection for those who sought to claim rights. The persistence of violence after AUC demobilization became a standard argument for those who warned that disarmament alone does not end the conditions in which armed groups arise.

Section 99.19

Assessing Plan Colombia: Military Gains, Drug Markets, and Social Costs

By 2008 Washington and Bogotá had spent heavily on Plan Colombia and related programs, and evaluators began to assess results against the original objectives. The U.S. Government Accountability Office, in report GAO-09-71, concluded that Colombia's security had improved substantially, with declines in homicides, kidnappings, and attacks, and expanded government presence in much of the national territory, but that the program's principal goal of reducing the cultivation and production of cocaine by half within six years had not been fully met. The report also noted that the Colombian government was assuming a larger share of costs and that sustaining the gains would require continued effort and the transfer of responsibilities from U.S. to Colombian institutions.

GAO-09-71 allows a concrete comparison rather than a broad judgment of success or failure. The report's fiscal 2000–2008 objective-based U.S. appropriations added to approximately $6.13 billion, with most allocated to reducing illicit narcotics and improving security. Using Colombian Ministry of Defense data, it reported national homicides declining from 28,837 in 2002 to 17,198 in 2007, and kidnappings from 1,709 to 226 over the same years. Yet its U.S. coca survey placed cultivation above the 2000 baseline in 2006 and 2007. These are not interchangeable measures: homicide figures include ordinary criminal cases, kidnappings depend on reporting and definitions, and cultivation estimates can change with survey coverage. A sound evaluation should report those gains and failures side by side and should not assign every movement in the data to U.S. financing alone.

A fair assessment compares the program's stated objectives with several kinds of evidence. On territorial presence, the state had extended police and military reach into municipalities it could not previously access. On the FARC, the organization's capabilities were reduced, as Section 99.15 discussed. On kidnapping and homicide, official figures fell substantially. On drug cultivation, results were mixed, since national totals fluctuated, regions shifted, and cocaine supply was not permanently reduced. On forced displacement, registered figures fell from the peak of 2002 but remained high. On development programs, results were smaller than the security component, and many rural areas saw little improvement in services. On human rights, the picture included both institutional reforms and serious abuses, as Section 99.16 detailed.

Attribution is difficult. Plan Colombia's U.S. assistance represented one input among many. Colombian spending, which rose after 2002, the 2002 election and Uribe's policies, the AUC demobilization, FARC strategy, the economy, commodity prices, and public support all contributed, and no analysis can cleanly isolate the effect of any one. Observers also disagree on counterfactuals: whether the state would have collapsed without assistance, as some supporters claimed, or would have adapted anyway. The responsible conclusion is that Plan Colombia contributed to the strengthening of Colombia's security forces, that its drug-control results fell short of targets, and that its social and legal costs were substantial.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Plan Colombia Strengthen the Colombian State?

The question: Did expanded assistance, professionalization, and security reform strengthen the Colombian state's capacity to protect citizens and govern territory, and at what cost?

Interpretation A — A stronger state: Supporters argue that assistance helped professionalize the armed forces, improve mobility and intelligence, extend state presence, reduce kidnapping and homicide, weaken the FARC, and create conditions in which negotiation became possible. They stress that Colombia's institutions survived a period when some observers feared state failure.
Interpretation B — A narrower and costlier gain: Critics point to forced displacement, aerial eradication's harm to rural communities, human-rights violations including false positives, the persistence of narcotics markets, limited development gains, and the continued presence of successor armed groups. They argue that military capacity increased more than civilian institutions, justice, and rural services, so the state became stronger in some respects but not in others.

Evidence: Both views draw on GAO findings, government and UN statistics, human-rights reports, and JEP findings. They differ in what they mean by "state strength": military effectiveness, public security, economic development, or constitutional accountability.

Why it matters: The answer affects how Plan Colombia is used as a model or warning in later debates about security assistance. The debate is not a choice between "success" and "failure," and students should distinguish the four dimensions of state strength above.

Section 99.20

Colombia after a Decade of Military Transformation, 2008–2012

By 2008 the FARC's military power had declined, but it was not eliminated. The organization continued to operate in remote regions, relying on smaller units, mines, and attacks on infrastructure, and it retained funding from extortion and the drug economy. The ELN persisted, mainly in the northeast and the Pacific. Successor criminal groups spread. Colombia had reduced violence in cities and highways, but the conflict continued in many rural regions, where displacement and killings of community leaders persisted. Many analysts described a "stalemate" in which the guerrillas could not win but the state had not ended the insurgency.

Juan Manuel Santos, who as defense minister under Uribe had overseen operations such as Jaque and the killing of senior FARC leaders, won the presidency in 2010 with Uribe's support and took office on 7 August. Santos's decisions surprised many. He maintained pressure on the FARC but also moved toward recognition of the conflict and of victims. His government reached out to neighboring Venezuela and Ecuador, relations with which had been strained, and it pursued reforms to address rural conditions. The shift from Uribe's approach has been explained in different ways: as a belief that military pressure had created conditions for negotiation, as a response to the FARC's own signals, and as Santos's personal assessment of costs. Chapter 100 will examine the process in detail.

The Victims and Land Restitution Law (Law 1448), signed in June 2011, was a major reform. It recognized victims of the armed conflict, including victims of state agents, and set out measures for reparation, including compensation, restitution of dispossessed land, and guarantees of non-repetition. It created institutions, among them a unit for victims and a land-restitution unit, and a framework for returning land to claimants through special courts. Implementation was challenging, since claimants faced threats, land titles were complicated, and the scale of dispossession was large. The law was significant because it acknowledged state responsibility for victims and treated the conflict as a matter of rights and not only of security. It also, in the view of its authors, laid groundwork for a future settlement.

By 2012 the government and the FARC were exploring a political course beyond the battlefield, with assistance from Cuba and Norway. Colombian legislation adopted that year began to lay a constitutional framework for transitional justice, though it could not substitute for the agreements the parties had yet to negotiate. Here this chapter stops. A decade of military transformation had altered the balance between the state and the insurgents, but it had not resolved rural inequality, uneven state presence, illicit economies, impunity, or the rights of victims. The central question was whether changed military circumstances could be converted into a durable political settlement with civilian protection and meaningful accountability.

The transition to negotiation requires three precise dates. Representatives of the government and FARC signed a general framework agreement on 26 August 2012 during the exploratory phase in Havana. News of contacts circulated publicly in late August, but President Santos gave the formal national announcement on 4 September, describing a process intended to begin in Oslo in October. This distinction prevents a press report from being mistaken for the legal instrument or the presidential announcement. The talks ahead would address rural reform, political participation, ending the conflict, illegal drugs, victims, and implementation—subjects left unresolved by military change alone. By 2012 Colombia's institutions were stronger in important respects than a decade earlier, but vulnerable communities still faced armed threats. The opening of talks was thus an important political choice under altered conditions, not proof that peace had already been secured.

What Changed

  • The armed forces professionalized, gained mobility and intelligence, and expanded
  • The 1991 Constitution created new rights and a Constitutional Court that supervised displacement policy
  • The state recognized displaced persons (1997) and victims (2011)
  • The FARC lost military capacity and senior leaders; the AUC formally demobilized

What Stayed the Same

  • Rural land concentration and uneven state presence in many regions
  • Illicit economies financing armed organizations
  • Threats against community and social leaders
  • Impunity for many crimes

Who experienced change most directly: Residents of highways and cities saw security improve most. Greater continuity: Remote rural, Indigenous, and Afro-Colombian communities often saw continued insecurity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that institutional change and security gains were uneven in geography and by social group, which helps explain why negotiations remained necessary.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Rural Inequality, Insurgency, Paramilitary Expansion, Criminal Financing, and Weak Civilian Protection

Long-term causes

Concentrated landholding, uneven state presence on colonization frontiers, political exclusion under the National Front, and the legacy of La Violencia.

Short-term causes

The cocaine boom, FARC and ELN expansion, kidnapping and extortion, ranchers' and traffickers' response, and the legal opening for civilian armed groups.

Event or transformation

The growth of the FARC and ELN and the formation of the AUC in 1997 created overlapping territorial wars among insurgents, paramilitaries, and state forces.

Massacres, mass displacement, kidnappings, mines, land dispossession, and collapse of civilian protection in contested regions.

No single cause explains the conflict. Inequality did not by itself produce insurgency, nor insurgency by itself paramilitarism; economic incentives, state weakness, and choices by leaders and local elites all mattered.
Everyday Life and Social History

Schools, Harvests, and Households under Armed Pressure

In contested regions, rural schools closed when teachers were threatened or killed, or when armed groups occupied buildings, and children lost years of schooling. Farm families planned harvests around the presence of armed actors, paid or avoided taxes, and sometimes depended on coca because it had a buyer. Community leaders, including members of village councils, teachers, and union and Indigenous authorities, received threats, and some fled or were killed. When a family fled, households were often separated: men might hide in the countryside or seek work in another town, while women and children moved to relatives or cities.

Return was a long process. Displaced families faced the loss of tools, animals, and documents, the occupation of their land by others, continued threats, and the absence of schools and clinics. Some communities, such as survivors of El Salado, organized collective returns and rebuilt with support from local and international organizations. The sources record these experiences unevenly: official statistics count cases, testimony records individual stories, and many people, especially women and Indigenous or Afro-Colombian residents, appear in the record chiefly through the reports of others.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Colombia's armed conflict was not one war between two unified sides but a changing system of overlapping conflicts involving guerrilla insurgencies, paramilitary organizations, state forces, narcotics markets, rural landholding, local political power, and civilian communities. Its central claim is that Colombia's military transformation in the 2000s changed the balance between the state and the principal insurgents, but that military gains alone could not resolve the political, economic, territorial, and human-rights issues at the heart of the conflict.

The FARC and ELN emerged in 1964–1965 from different traditions. The FARC grew from peasant self-defense communities and Communist networks, the ELN from student radicalism, Cuban inspiration, and revolutionary Christianity. Both drew on rural exclusion, land inequality, and the closed politics of the National Front, but they differed in structure, geography, and fortune. The FARC's 1982 Seventh Conference turned it toward expansion, while the ELN, nearly destroyed in 1973, recovered in the northeast through oil-related extortion and local roots.

Insurgent financing grew through kidnapping, extortion, and taxation of illicit economies, and some fronts took part directly in drug production and trafficking. These sources enabled organizational growth but undermined political legitimacy where coercion alienated communities. Fronts were not economically identical, and statements that the FARC was merely a cartel or merely a taxing authority both oversimplify.

Paramilitarism emerged from local arrangements among ranchers, traffickers, and security officials, within a legal framework that repeatedly encouraged and then restricted civilian armed groups. The AUC, founded in April 1997, was a confederation of regional organizations with different interests, financed in part by the drug economy, tied to local elites, and responsible for massacres such as Mapiripán and El Salado. Its violence was directed principally at civilians and cannot be understood merely as a response to guerrilla action.

Civilians bore the heaviest costs. Forced displacement, which reached registered peaks above 800,000 cases in 2002 under the Truth Commission's cited methodology, served in some regions to obtain land and control populations. Kidnapping, child recruitment, landmines, threats against leaders, and attacks such as Bojayá afflicted communities, with Indigenous, Afro-Colombian, and campesino communities, and women and children, experiencing particular harm. Law 387 of 1997 and the Constitutional Court's 2004 intervention marked institutional recognition.

The Caguán negotiations of 1998–2002 failed amid continuing war, weak verification, and mutual distrust, and their collapse shaped Colombian politics. Plan Colombia began as a Colombian strategy for peace, institutional strengthening, development, and drug control. U.S. Public Law 106-246 provided about $1.3 billion in regional assistance, about $860.3 million of it for Colombia, with a heavier military and counternarcotics emphasis than the Colombian plan's social components, and the two must be distinguished.

Military reform, begun under Pastrana and intensified under Uribe's Democratic Security policy, professionalized the army and extended state presence. Aerial eradication produced large hectare totals but not a lasting end to cocaine supply. The FARC lost capabilities and senior leaders, and Operation Jaque in July 2008 symbolized the shifting balance. Yet the same period produced the extrajudicial killings that the JEP has preliminarily counted at 6,402 victims between 2002 and 2008, a later judicial finding that must be kept distinct from what contemporaries knew.

The AUC's demobilization between 2003 and 2006 and the Justice and Peace Law of 2005, a conditional framework and not a blanket amnesty, disbanded armed structures and yielded disclosures that fed the parapolitics investigations. But successor groups emerged, reintegration was limited, extraditions interrupted testimony, and victims' reparation and land restitution lagged. Demobilizing an organization was not the same as dismantling its networks, wealth, and political ties.

Assessments of Plan Colombia, including the GAO's 2008 report, found substantial security improvement but unmet drug-reduction goals. Attribution is difficult because Colombian spending, political leadership, paramilitary demobilization, and insurgent errors all mattered. The debates over whether Plan Colombia strengthened the state depend on how one defines state strength: military effectiveness, public security, development, or constitutional accountability.

By 2012, under Juan Manuel Santos, the FARC was weakened but not defeated, the ELN and criminal successor groups persisted, and the Victims and Land Restitution Law of 2011 recognized victims and land dispossession. The government's announcement of negotiations with the FARC opened a new stage. The unresolved tensions among military pressure, political settlement, victims' rights, and lasting peace are the subject of Chapter 100.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Colombia's conflict was a system, not a duel. Guerrillas, paramilitaries, trafficking networks, and state institutions interacted through land, money, and fear, and civilians lived inside that system. Understanding it requires separating actors without losing sight of how they affected one another.

The chapter also shows how states and societies respond to prolonged violence. Colombia combined constitutional reform, courts that listened to victims, a human-rights movement, military reform, and international assistance with abuses by some state personnel and uneven protection of rural communities. Plan Colombia belongs to this mixed record, and neither a verdict of unqualified success nor of total failure fits the evidence.

Chapter 100 takes up the question that this chapter leaves open: whether a changed military balance could be converted into a durable negotiated peace. Students should carry forward the lessons of Caguán, of the Justice and Peace Law, and of the Victims Law, along with the recognition that military pressure, victims' rights, land, and political participation would all be central to any settlement.
