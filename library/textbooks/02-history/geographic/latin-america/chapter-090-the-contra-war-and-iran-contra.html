Chapter Opening: An Election Nobody Expected

On the night of 25 February 1990, a revolutionary government that had survived a decade of armed counterrevolution lost a national election it had invited the world to observe, and then agreed to hand over power.

Violeta Barrios de Chamorro, candidate of the National Opposition Union (UNO), received about 54.7 percent of the presidential vote. Daniel Ortega, the Sandinista president, received about 40.8 percent. Many observers, and many Sandinistas, had expected the opposite result. To understand it, this chapter returns to 1981, when the revolution described in Chapter 89 began to face organized armed opposition.

The chapter addresses three interacting processes: the political divisions inside Nicaragua, the regional and international struggle over the revolution, and the economic and social consequences of prolonged war. It examines the Contras, a heterogeneous movement that included former National Guardsmen, rural farmers, Indigenous activists and civilian dissidents, and it explains how United States covert assistance gave that movement capabilities that few domestic oppositions possess.

Washington's pursuit of that policy produced the Iran-Contra affair, a constitutional crisis in the United States. It is a major subject here, but it is not allowed to displace Nicaragua from the center of its own history. The chapter also gives sustained attention to the Caribbean Coast, civilians, women, conscripts, and the diplomacy of Central American governments, who ultimately shaped the peace process more than is often recognized.

Evidence is uneven and politically charged. Declassified U.S. records, congressional reports, the International Court of Justice record, human-rights investigations, election-observer reports, and Nicaraguan testimony each reveal part of the story. Casualty and displacement figures are contested, and allegations must be distinguished from findings and convictions. The chapter ends in April 1990 and does not narrate later Nicaraguan politics.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how political divisions inside Nicaragua and foreign intervention together produced a prolonged counterrevolutionary war.
  2. Distinguish the FDN, ARDE, and Indigenous armed organizations, and the varied motives of Contra participants.
  3. Analyze the Caribbean Coast conflict without reducing it to either U.S. sponsorship or uniform Indigenous support for the Sandinistas.
  4. Assess evidence of abuses by Contras and Sandinista institutions under consistent evidentiary standards.
  5. Interpret the International Court of Justice judgment of 27 June 1986 and its specific findings.
  6. Evaluate the 1984 election as neither wholly free nor wholly fraudulent.
  7. Trace the changing Boland restrictions and the origins of the Iran-Contra diversion.
  8. Distinguish congressional findings from criminal convictions.
  9. Analyze Nicaragua's economic crisis as the product of war, sanctions, dependence, and domestic policy.
  10. Compare Esquipulas II and Sapoá and explain why neither ended the war at once.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the FSLN's 1990 defeat.
  12. Distinguish the end of Sandinista government from the end of Sandinismo.

Historical Context

Chapter 89 traced the Somoza dynasty, the rise of the Sandinista National Liberation Front (FSLN), and the revolution of July 1979. The new government combined a Junta of National Reconstruction, the FSLN's nine-member National Directorate, an army and security services built from guerrilla forces, and mass organizations for workers, farmers, women, youth, and neighborhoods. It launched a literacy campaign and health programs, expropriated Somoza-family property, and maintained a mixed economy that kept a sizable private sector.

The revolution's allies were broad, and so were its tensions. Business groups, the Catholic hierarchy, newspapers, and non-Sandinista parties disputed the pace and direction of change, while Washington grew alarmed by Nicaragua's ties to Cuba and the Soviet bloc and by alleged arms flows to Salvadoran guerrillas. Chapter 91 treats El Salvador and Guatemala in detail; Chapter 92 treats Panama; Chapter 93 treats the wider debt crisis. Here, those matters appear only as they bear on Nicaragua.

Division I — From Revolution to Counterrevolution
Section 90.1

The Revolution under Pressure: Sandinista Power and the Breakdown of the Coalition

Power in revolutionary Nicaragua was shared unevenly. The Junta of National Reconstruction held formal authority, but the FSLN's National Directorate, the new army and security services, and the Sandinista mass organizations held much of the practical power. In April 1980, Violeta Chamorro and Alfonso Robelo resigned from the Junta, signaling that non-Sandinista members of the founding coalition felt marginalized.

Opposition took several forms. The business federation COSEP objected to economic policy and to the pace of political institutionalization; newspapers and church figures disputed censorship and the role of religion in public life; parties argued over pluralism and timelines for elections. It is important to distinguish such opposition to specific Sandinista policies from any desire to restore the Somoza system, which almost no one in these groups sought.

Tensions also grew over Cuban advisers, the expansion of the armed forces, and the deterioration of relations with Washington. Divisions inside Nicaraguan society were real, but they do not by themselves explain the scale and longevity of the armed conflict that followed. That requires examining foreign intervention.

The uneven balance of power was visible in the very design of the new state. The five-member junta offered continuity with the anti-Somoza alliance, but the FSLN's National Directorate had emerged from the reunification of three guerrilla tendencies and could settle major strategic questions outside the formal executive. Humberto Ortega's command of the new army, the Ministry of the Interior's security responsibilities, and the party's influence over the mass organizations gave the movement resources that independent political groups could not readily match. For supporters, these institutions protected a revolution exposed to attack and guaranteed that illiteracy, landlord power, and old military networks would not return. For critics, they made constitutional offices less consequential than party decisions. Both assessments require examining particular institutions rather than assuming that every Sandinista activist or opponent held identical views.

The political coalition had been assembled to defeat a dynasty, not to govern indefinitely under a settled program. Private entrepreneurs who had opposed Somoza still expected a substantial market economy and protection for independent associations. FSLN leaders announced a mixed economy and political pluralism while arguing that the revolutionary process required leadership able to defend social reforms. A business federation could therefore support the overthrow and subsequently criticize agricultural procurement, foreign exchange regulations, or official labor organizations. The same distinction applies to newspapers: criticism in La Prensa was not equivalent to participation in an armed conspiracy, although authorities sometimes claimed that certain reporting helped the country's external enemies. The politically relevant question is how the state differentiated open opposition from covert military collaboration, and whether restrictions were proportionate to genuine threats.

The resignation of Alfonso Robelo and Violeta Chamorro in April 1980 showed that early constitutional compromises were becoming unstable. Their departures followed disagreements about the Council of State's composition and the widening influence of the FSLN's mass organizations. The departure of visible non-Sandinista figures mattered internationally, but it should not be read as proof that every person outside the FSLN had abandoned the revolution. Some remained in ministries, trade unions, neighborhood committees, and community development programs. Others withdrew gradually as war and restrictions intensified. Political alignments were shifting, not fixed.

Washington's response became a major part of that shifting landscape. The Carter administration had initially pursued a working relationship with the new government while contesting the flow of weapons to Salvadoran insurgents. After Ronald Reagan entered office in January 1981, his advisers read Nicaragua less as an uncertain experiment and more as a security problem within a regional confrontation. The country's growing economic problems, disagreements over religious education and press freedom, and the distinct grievances of the Atlantic Coast provided fertile conditions for opposition. Yet they did not determine that a protracted war would occur. That outcome depended on the political choices of the Nicaraguan state, armed dissidents, neighboring governments, and an American administration willing to develop an externally supported military challenge.

The analytical starting point is therefore neither a story of a wholly popular and unified revolution betrayed from abroad nor one of a uniformly totalitarian regime inevitably producing resistance. It is a struggle over who could legitimately speak for the 1979 victory, what the government's declared pluralism meant, and how war changed both government and opposition. Later chapters can evaluate whether institutions became more inclusive or coercive, but the initial rupture must be understood in its own uncertain setting.

Section 90.2

The Formation of the Contras

The armed opposition drew on former National Guardsmen, exiled civilians, rural people angered by economic and political policy, disaffected former revolutionaries, and Indigenous activists. It would be inaccurate to portray all Contras as former Somoza Guardsmen, although former Guard personnel held important military roles, especially in the Nicaraguan Democratic Force (FDN) in Honduras. Edén Pastora's Democratic Revolutionary Alliance (ARDE) operated from Costa Rica and presented itself as a continuation of the original anti-Somoza revolution.

Motives differed sharply. Guardsmen sought a return to a role they had lost; farmers resented state marketing policy, land arrangements, and conscription; Miskitu activists sought land and local authority; civilian politicians sought a different political order. Foreign training, finance, weapons, and sanctuary allowed the movement to develop military capabilities far beyond those of most domestic oppositions.

The term Contra, derived from counterrevolutionary, became a convenient collective label, but it obscures organizational history. Former guardsmen who had fled to Honduras after the collapse of the Somoza state provided trained personnel for some early units. Other recruits came from farming districts where official procurement prices, compulsory military service, fear of collectivization, or confrontations with government representatives fed discontent. Exiles who had joined the anti-Somoza coalition could oppose the new regime without wanting the old dynasty restored. Separating military commanders from ordinary recruits is essential: a rural recruit's grievance does not establish the leadership's political program, and a leadership statement cannot fully explain every recruit's conduct.

The FDN became the best-resourced northern organization, benefiting from Honduran territory and extensive CIA assistance. Its leadership included politically connected exiles and military officers with different pasts; efforts to improve its public image did not erase the strong role played by former National Guard personnel. Edén Pastora, who had participated prominently in the struggle against Somoza, initially offered a distinct southern opposition under ARDE. His claim that he opposed FSLN monopolization rather than the revolution itself illustrates why anti-Sandinista forces should not be described as one ideological bloc. Competition among command structures, donors, and political representatives repeatedly made coordination difficult. Some organizations pursued negotiations that others resisted.

Caribbean Coast armed organizations require separate treatment. They drew on disputes involving collective land, customary authority, language, religious institutions, and the military presence of the Sandinista government. They were not simply subunits of the FDN or a uniform expression of Miskitu opinion. Refugees in Honduras, activists seeking autonomy, communities wishing to avoid combat, and armed groups could have conflicting priorities. Some communities subsequently negotiated with Managua; others remained aligned with armed resistance. Describing a whole people as pro- or anti-Sandinista would eliminate precisely the political agency the history needs to explain.

Foreign support transformed the scale of warfare. Cross-border camps, communications equipment, training, aircraft, supplies, and funds allowed armed forces to operate even without dependable revenue or secure territory inside Nicaragua. That support created dependencies as well as power. Donors could influence leadership, the timing of operations, and public political messaging; foreign hosts bore diplomatic and humanitarian costs. Conversely, a government fighting insurgents across a recognized border had to consider the possibility of interstate escalation. The map of the war was therefore also a map of the international relationships that sustained it.

The movement never achieved a simple common chain of command or a coherent alternative development program. Its distinct factions could agree that the FSLN should relinquish governing power yet disagree on land reform, religion, political participation, amnesty, and the postwar military. These disagreements became consequential as negotiations gained importance later in the decade. The very diversity that helped the resistance recruit from multiple constituencies also complicated its ability to present itself as a credible government in waiting.

Historical Vocabulary Study I
Proxy war, covert action, and foreign sanctuary

A proxy war is a conflict in which an outside power supports a local force rather than fighting directly. Covert action is government activity intended to conceal the sponsor's role; in the U.S. system it required a presidential finding and notification of congressional committees. Foreign sanctuary, here Honduras and Costa Rica, gave the Contras bases beyond the reach of Nicaraguan forces. These terms are analytical, not insults: they describe the structure of support while leaving open the question of whether the local force had its own motives, which the Contras did.

Section 90.3

Ronald Reagan, Central American Security, and U.S. Covert Assistance

The Reagan administration interpreted the Sandinista revolution through the Cold War. It feared Cuban and Soviet influence, believed Nicaragua was aiding Salvadoran guerrillas, and sought to prevent another consolidated revolutionary government in the region. The congressional Iran-Contra investigation established that U.S. support for the Contras had begun by December 1981, through CIA financing, training, equipment, and intelligence.

Institutional roles differed. President Reagan set policy; CIA Director William Casey drove the program; the National Security Council staff coordinated; the State Department pursued diplomacy with mixed commitment; Congress held appropriating authority. The publicly stated aim, interdicting arms to El Salvador, diverged from the program's likely strategic effect, which was sustained pressure on the Sandinista government. The policy thus turned the Contras into a major Cold War instrument while opening a constitutional dispute over executive authority.

The late Cold War gave the Reagan administration a language for interpreting Central America through global competition. Cuba's military and educational links with Nicaragua, Soviet-bloc equipment, and the separate armed conflicts in El Salvador and Guatemala all fed Washington's fear that a revolutionary government could alter regional balances. Yet these connections should be tested as specific relationships rather than treated as proof that Managua simply followed Moscow's instructions. Sandinista leaders pursued their own national and ideological goals, and Central American states had domestic conflicts predating Reagan's presidency. Their policies cannot be deduced from superpower rivalry alone.

A presidential directive in November 1981 and subsequent authorization documents provided the foundation for the expanding covert program. The public American emphasis was on preventing the transfer of arms to Salvadoran guerrillas, but the development of armed resistance against the Sandinista government quickly raised the question of whether interdiction was an adequate description of the policy. Intelligence activities operated under secrecy; public statements, internal memoranda, operational spending, and congressional testimony did not always present the same objective. Historians therefore reconstruct intent from multiple records rather than accepting the most convenient official formulation.

The CIA's role included organizing contacts with exiles and regional security services, providing training and support, and facilitating communications and logistics. The National Security Council was a policy coordinating institution close to the presidency, not a normal field agency. Its later assumption of operational functions became central to the Iran-Contra affair. The State Department, meanwhile, pursued alliances with regional governments and faced the diplomatic consequences of a policy that could make Honduras and Costa Rica more vulnerable. Congressional authorization and financial oversight formed a separate arena in which the executive's account of covert intervention could be challenged.

The dispute over evidence concerning Salvadoran arms shipments deserves balanced treatment. Washington had reasons to monitor assistance to insurgents in El Salvador, and the Sandinista government had political relationships with regional revolutionary movements. Nevertheless, the existence of those relationships is not proof that every American allegation about a particular shipment was accurate or that a war against Nicaragua would be a lawful response. A security claim must be evaluated separately from the legal authority asserted to pursue it. This distinction later mattered in the International Court of Justice, which rejected the United States' principal collective-self-defense justification.

From Nicaragua's standpoint, a small country had to devote resources to defending borders and infrastructure while still attempting to deliver social services. American policy helped reshape internal debates: officials could identify opponents with foreign sponsorship, while those opponents increasingly relied upon international assistance to maintain military pressure. This feedback loop narrowed space for dissent that was neither aligned with the FSLN nor connected with armed opposition. The central historical question is not whether outside intervention alone created political opposition, but how it altered the scale, incentives, and duration of an already contested revolutionary transition.

Section 90.4

The Caribbean Coast, Miskitu Communities, and Forced Relocation

Nicaragua's Caribbean Coast had a separate history from the Pacific: a British-era connection, Moravian Christianity, English-based Creole speech, and Miskitu, Mayangna, and Rama communities with little experience of rule from Managua. The Sandinistas' centralizing project met Indigenous demands for land, cultural recognition, and local authority. MISURASATA, with leaders such as Brooklyn Rivera, initially cooperated with the government but clashed with it over land and autonomy; Steadman Fagoth and others later led armed opposition.

Fighting in the Río Coco region led the government, in early 1982, to relocate roughly 8,500 Miskitu residents away from the border, claiming military necessity. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights documented the displacement and weighed the security justification against the population's rights. The conflict cannot be reduced to American-sponsored counterrevolution or to uniform Indigenous support for the Sandinistas; it arose from older disputes over power, territory, identity, and security.

The Atlantic Coast had not shared the Pacific region's political history. British influence, Moravian religious institutions, regional trading connections, and different languages shaped political life long before the 1979 revolution. In Miskitu and Mayangna communities, territory was more than property available for individual redistribution; it involved customary use, collective memory, fishing, farming, and local authority. A revolutionary government focused on class redistribution and national integration could interpret demands for communal autonomy as obstacles to unity, even when coastal activists understood those demands as central to equality. That conceptual conflict was made more dangerous by militarization near Honduras.

MISURASATA initially gave some Indigenous leaders a channel for negotiation, education, and organizational activity. Disagreement over land demarcation, representation, and the powers of state officials widened. Brooklyn Rivera and Steadman Fagoth pursued different courses, and neither should be treated as the voice of every coastal village. Local elders, Moravian clergy, teachers, women, refugees, and youth could support different arrangements; political loyalties also shifted as abuses and armed incursions multiplied. It is particularly important not to collapse Miskitu politics into the histories of Rama, Mayangna, and Creole communities, whose political and linguistic traditions differed.

Violent confrontations around San Carlos and Leimus in December 1981 preceded the large relocation from the Río Coco. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights later concluded that the government had illegally killed a number of Miskitu people at Leimus in retaliation, while carefully identifying other incidents where responsibility remained uncertain. Its report described the relocation of about 8,500 people from the river region toward Tasba Pri during January and February 1982. It also documented a separate later relocation involving about 4,000 Miskitu from the Coco and Bocay river areas, a distinction essential to avoiding misleading totals. Many other residents fled into Honduras; these groups should not be counted as though each represented the same event.

The government argued that fighting and raids across the frontier made continued settlement close to the river impossible. Human-rights investigators considered the security problem real while asking whether compulsory removal was a proportionate, temporary response and whether residents could return. The loss of houses, crops, livestock, churches, and familiar local institutions had social consequences beyond movement from one site to another. The Commission also recorded abuses during detention and failures of due process. The report is neither an exhaustive list of every wartime violation nor a declaration that the state's security concerns were fabricated. It provides specific findings that must be read alongside testimony and government replies.

Changes later in the decade show the limits of seeing the coast only through early confrontation. Negotiations, Indigenous political pressure, and changing government policy contributed to the 1987 autonomy framework. That law did not instantly resolve land conflicts or wartime insecurity, but it acknowledged a political question earlier centralizing programs had failed to accommodate. The coast belongs at the heart of the history because it demonstrates that sovereignty could mean different things to the central government and to communities asserting rights within Nicaragua.

Division II — The Contra War, Human Rights, and Electoral Politics
Section 90.5

The Geography and Conduct of the Contra War, 1982–1986

The war was fought mainly in the northern and central highlands (Jinotega, Matagalpa, Nueva Segovia, Madriz), along the Río Coco and Caribbean Coast, and on the southern border. Honduras and Costa Rica provided bases and supply routes, and became diplomatic battlegrounds. Contra forces carried out raids, ambushes, sabotage, and attacks on infrastructure and cooperatives, but they never held sustained territory.

The Sandinista Popular Army, rural militias, border defense, and compulsory military service (introduced in 1983) answered them. A CIA manual on psychological operations, later submitted by Nicaragua to the United Nations, is evidence of assumptions behind the covert program; it is used here as evidence of political assumptions, not as an operational text. The Contras' ability to damage the state did not become an ability to defeat it.

Nicaragua's northern highlands were especially difficult terrain for both insurgency and counterinsurgency. Small farms, forested slopes, coffee-growing districts, roads of varying quality, and a frontier that could be crossed at multiple points allowed armed groups to move without controlling a continuous territory. Some Contra units could raid a cooperative, cut a communications line, attack a road convoy, or threaten a rural official and withdraw before regular troops arrived. Such operations imposed costs on a government trying to supply agricultural markets and keep clinics and schools open. Yet the ability to penetrate a district was not equivalent to maintaining civilian administration there.

Honduras was more than a label on a map. The availability of sanctuary across the northern border enabled rest, reorganization, training, and supply activity. It also created pressures on Honduras, whose leaders had domestic security interests and whose territory became entangled in American policy. The southern front was different: Costa Rica officially rejected militarization, but the political and geographical circumstances of its border permitted some anti-Sandinista organizing and clashes. The existence of such activity must not be mistaken for an assertion that Costa Rica's government supported every armed operation associated with its territory.

The Sandinista Popular Army responded through regular formations, local militias, and efforts to protect settlements and transport routes. Conscription, introduced in 1983 through the military service system, enlarged the state's manpower but created resentment among families losing sons and farm workers. Military service affected the labor supply, household economies, migration decisions, and political legitimacy. An armed force could expand while the civilian economy became less capable of supporting it. This helps explain why the conflict damaged the regime even in years when the Contras could not defeat its regular army.

The American covert campaign also included psychological and political warfare. The notorious CIA-associated manual on guerrilla psychological operations is useful as evidence of planners' assumptions about propaganda, local organization, and the political use of violence. It should not be reproduced as a how-to text or treated as a complete account of what any particular unit actually carried out. Planners' intentions and battlefield events require separate corroboration, as do accusations concerning the targeting of civilians. Reports from churches, human-rights organizations, journalists, combatants, and displaced residents can be compared to identify particular incidents and the limits of each witness's knowledge.

The war was fought simultaneously over territory, resources, and political credibility. A destroyed bridge interrupted transport and created an image of government vulnerability; the killing of a local teacher could undermine a literacy program and terrorize a community; a government sweep that detained innocent residents could push families toward the opposition. The apparent paradox of the mid-1980s is therefore understandable. The Contras generally lacked the territorial and political capacity to replace the Sandinista state, but they could impose enough recurring costs to contribute to exhaustion, sharpen coercive governance, and keep the revolution's future uncertain.

Section 90.6

Civilians, Human-Rights Abuses, and the Social Costs of War

Americas Watch documented sustained Contra attacks on civilians, including killings, kidnapping, and attacks on health and education workers, and separately investigated Sandinista abuses, including arbitrary detention, restrictions on civil liberties, and killings in some rural operations. Documented organizational patterns should be distinguished from allegations against individuals, and the abuses were not identical in frequency, purpose, or institutional responsibility.

Casualty and displacement figures are contested because of polarization and limited access. Whatever the totals, the war reshaped rural life: schools and clinics closed, families were separated, farmers were displaced, and conscription weighed on young men and their relatives.

Human-rights reporting requires the same discipline for every armed actor, but equal standards do not require artificially equal conclusions. Investigators documented recurring Contra attacks on civilians, agricultural cooperatives, teachers, health workers, and local government representatives. Some attacks had an immediate military objective; others targeted individuals because of their presumed political affiliation. A nurse, school volunteer, or cooperative administrator could be seen as an extension of the state despite carrying out primarily civilian work. These attacks harmed public services and communicated that political identification could expose whole families to danger.

Sandinista security and military institutions also committed violations. Reports examined arbitrary arrests, pressure on media and opposition groups, harsh treatment of suspects, abuses during rural operations, and violations on the Caribbean Coast. Responsible historical writing identifies the agency, location, date range, evidence, and legal question where documentation permits. It should not describe all state restrictions as identical to extrajudicial killing, nor present constitutional guarantees as proof that violations did not occur. Conversely, a violation by a state official does not establish the culpability of every Sandinista organization or supporter.

Civilian testimony was difficult to collect. People could fear retaliation from whichever force controlled nearby roads; witnesses might have seen an aftermath but not the perpetrators; exiles could have limited access to events in their former villages. Government statements sometimes minimized abuses, while resistance communiqués had reasons to dramatize them. Independent human-rights organizations and the Inter-American Commission investigated with different access, mandates, and political constraints. A strong case study places their reports beside contemporaneous evidence and explains uncertainties rather than inventing an exact national death toll.

The war produced losses that casualty tables alone cannot convey. A family displaced from a mountain hamlet might lose stored corn, animals, coffee trees, school access, and ties to relatives across a frontier. A conscript's absence could shift farming, childcare, and market work onto older people and women. Rural health campaigns initiated after 1979 faced travel disruption and violence, while urban hospitals received casualties and treated shortages intensified by the wider economic crisis. Refugee camps offered sanctuary but also introduced dependency, disease risk, and contested political representation. Children experienced interrupted schooling and prolonged uncertainty about returning home.

The distinction between military and civilian status is central to interpretation. Some armed groups portrayed cooperative members or teachers as legitimate political targets because they supported the government. International humanitarian law does not convert civilians into combatants merely because they sympathize with a party. Similarly, a state facing raids still bears duties concerning detention and the protection of displaced residents. The conflict can be understood as a struggle over government without abandoning the separate task of evaluating violence under the standards applicable at the time. Its lasting consequence was the destruction of social trust and everyday security in regions where no political resolution was yet available.

Section 90.7

Harbor Mining and Nicaragua's Case before the International Court of Justice

In early 1984, harbors were mined in operations supported by the United States. Nicaragua filed its case at the International Court of Justice on 9 April 1984; the Court issued provisional measures on 10 May 1984; and, after the United States declined to take part in the merits phase, the Court gave judgment on 27 June 1986. It found the United States in breach of obligations including non-intervention, the prohibition on the use of force, respect for sovereignty, and freedom of maritime commerce, and rejected the argument of collective self-defense.

The Court did not hold the United States responsible for every Contra act. It distinguished U.S. support and direct U.S. conduct from attribution of the Contras' specific actions, which required effective control. The judgment became a leading statement of limits on intervention, though enforcement remained politically contested.

Nicaragua's application to the International Court of Justice in April 1984 was a political decision made through an international legal institution. The government alleged that American operations, including support for the Contras and mining of its harbors, violated legal obligations concerning sovereignty and the use of force. Washington contested the Court's jurisdiction and later withdrew from participation in the merits phase; it did not thereby prevent the case from proceeding. Legal jurisdiction, evidence, state responsibility, and the enforcement of judgments were distinct questions throughout the litigation.

Mining coastal waters was an especially concrete episode because it endangered commercial navigation and affected third-state vessels. Its consequences were not confined to Nicaraguan military assets. The 1984 provisional-measures order concerned immediate conduct while the judges considered the merits; it was not a final adjudication of every accusation. The Court's June 1986 judgment then addressed several different forms of American conduct, including the mining, direct operations, and financing and support of armed opponents. Conflating these activities into one legal category would misstate the Court's analysis.

The judgment held that the United States had violated obligations under customary international law not to intervene in another state's affairs or use force against it, not to violate sovereignty, and not to interrupt peaceful maritime commerce. It also addressed obligations under the 1956 bilateral Friendship, Commerce and Navigation Treaty. The judges rejected Washington's reliance on collective self-defense in the circumstances alleged. That conclusion did not require a finding that every Sandinista regional activity was imaginary; it concerned the particular legal conditions necessary to justify American force against Nicaragua.

One particularly important issue was attribution. The Contras received extensive American support, but the Court did not automatically treat every Contra operation as an act of the United States. Responsibility for the conduct of an armed organization depended on a more specific showing of control over the operation. A separate state could still violate international law through direct force or prohibited intervention even without being held legally responsible for all acts of a supported group. Students should separate those legal conclusions from the broader historical question of how assistance affected the war's duration and human costs.

The ruling mattered internationally because it articulated limits on what a powerful state could lawfully do in the territory or waters of a smaller state. Its practical enforcement was another matter: the United States did not accept the judgment's merits or pay an award arising from it, and Nicaragua later discontinued the case in 1991. The document should be read neither as an all-purpose moral verdict on every Nicaraguan actor nor as irrelevant because it lacked immediate coercive enforcement. It is a primary legal source showing how evidence, jurisdiction, treaty obligations, customary law, and political power intersected in the conflict.

Section 90.8

The 1984 Elections, Opposition Boycotts, and the 1987 Constitution

On 4 November 1984, Nicaraguans voted for president and a constituent assembly; Daniel Ortega won. Several opposition parties took part, while others withdrew or boycotted, citing restrictions and unequal conditions. The Sandinistas held large advantages in state organization and communications, and war constrained campaigning. Observers disagreed: some found orderly and credible voting and counting, while others judged the campaign environment insufficiently free.

The elected assembly produced the 1987 Constitution, which declared rights, set up institutions, and recognized a plural political system, but emergency conditions limited how far these commitments operated in practice. The election institutionalized Sandinista rule, while the meaning of pluralism remained contested.

The 1984 election presented several distinct questions. Could voters cast and count ballots in a reasonably orderly process? Did opposition parties have access to the ballot, media, meetings, and campaigning? Did government institutions give the incumbent an unfair organizational advantage? Did the ongoing Contra war make ordinary political competition unsafe? Treating one answer as a verdict on all these questions would turn a complex electoral history into an ideological slogan. The Sandinistas also had a strategic reason to seek recognized electoral legitimacy before the anticipated continuation of Reagan's presidency.

Several parties registered and campaigned. Arturo Cruz and the principal opposition coalition associated with him did not take part, but contemporary observers disagreed about how to interpret his withdrawal and the extent of government responsibility for the political conditions behind it. The Latin American Studies Association's observer delegation concluded that no major political tendency had been denied formal access and described significant concessions by the government to parties that participated. Other critics emphasized intimidation, political censorship, and the advantages flowing from official control of security and mass organizations. Those judgments should be quoted or paraphrased with their authors identified rather than generalized as an undifferentiated verdict of 'observers.'

The electoral results gave the FSLN an institutional mandate within a new constitutional order. Yet a vote in wartime could not by itself settle the legitimacy of armed opposition or the proper limits of presidential power. For many Sandinista supporters, participation was proof that the revolution had not abandoned politics; for opponents excluded from influence or distrustful of the state, elections could appear to ratify a dominant party. These perspectives should be considered as interpretations based on different experiences of coercion, representation, and the promise of pluralism.

The 1987 Constitution codified institutions and rights while the government retained emergency powers and a partisan security establishment. It also provided the framework within which autonomy for the Caribbean Coast was legislated. The constitutional text and actual political practice should be evaluated separately. Formal recognition of rights did not mean that every journalist, party organizer, rural critic, or Indigenous leader could exercise those rights without difficulty. Conversely, wartime restrictions cannot by themselves establish that the election results were fabricated.

The significance of 1984 becomes clearer when compared with 1990. The earlier election built a new legal and institutional framework under Sandinista dominance. The later vote, held after peace negotiations and extensive international observation, resulted in an opposition victory and transfer of presidential power. Those outcomes were different but related: the framework created during the 1980s became part of the route by which the FSLN could relinquish government without relinquishing its political identity.

Division III — Washington, Congressional Restrictions, and Iran-Contra
Section 90.9

The Boland Amendments, Congress, and the Struggle over Covert War

The Constitution gives Congress control of appropriations while the president directs foreign policy. The restrictions known as the Boland Amendments began in December 1982 and became more extensive in October 1984. They were not a single unchanging law: wording, affected agencies, exceptions, and permitted assistance changed over time. Edward Boland and other legislators feared a secret intervention and executive overreach; Contra supporters argued the policy was necessary and lawful.

As restrictions tightened, National Security Council staff explored alternative sources of support. Iran-Contra arose partly from the question of whether executive officials could keep pursuing a covert objective after Congress limited the funds.

Congressional resistance to the covert war developed in stages rather than in one dramatic vote. The first Boland restriction, enacted in December 1982, sought to prevent certain intelligence funds from being used for the purpose of overthrowing Nicaragua's government or provoking a military exchange between Nicaragua and Honduras. Subsequent appropriations provisions used different language and addressed different agencies, funding sources, and purposes. By October 1984 a broader prohibition constrained funds available to the CIA, Department of Defense, and other entities involved in intelligence activities. The precise statutory text matters because an allegation that an official violated a restriction must identify which restriction was in force at the relevant date.

Some members of Congress opposed the Contra project because they judged it an unjustified intervention that would expand regional warfare. Others doubted whether the executive branch had provided accurate information about the program's purposes. Supporters considered the Sandinista government a threat to neighboring states and regarded the resistance as an instrument of pressure. Legislators could oppose covert appropriations while supporting humanitarian assistance or political diplomacy. The dispute therefore did not divide Congress neatly between those 'for' and 'against' Nicaragua: it concerned both policy and the means by which policy would be pursued.

National security officials argued over what entities the statutes reached. Could an NSC staff member coordinate private fundraising if an appropriations provision was directed at specified agencies? Could a foreign government pay for activity the United States could not finance? Were intelligence findings or notification requirements satisfied? Some later defenses turned on narrow textual readings and institutional classifications. Historians must distinguish a legal argument advanced by officials from the conclusions of congressional investigators, courts, or prosecutors. The existence of a loophole theory is evidence of deliberation, not proof that the theory was sound.

Secrecy made oversight difficult. Congress could limit an appropriation only if legislators understood what funds and institutions were involved; executive officials could withhold or misdescribe information, while classified briefings limited public debate. In the Iran-Contra investigation, those problems became visible through surviving notes, memoranda, testimony, destroyed or altered documents, and financial records. Each kind of evidence permits a different inference. Financial records may show a transfer without showing who approved it; a memorandum may show intent without proving that a proposal reached the president.

Boland is important because it locates the crisis within constitutional practice, not simply personal misconduct. A president could regard assisting anti-communist forces as necessary, while Congress could refuse to finance that policy. The resolution of such a conflict had to proceed through lawful authority or a change in legislation. Operating a parallel supply mechanism to evade or reinterpret restrictions raised the possibility that officials could achieve an objective without the accountable approval required for the use of public power. That is why understanding the changing provisions is a prerequisite to understanding the scandal itself.

Historical Vocabulary Study II
Boland Amendment, finding, and diversion

The Boland Amendments were successive funding limits placed in appropriations laws, not one statute. A presidential finding was the formal authorization required for covert action. The National Security Council staff coordinated policy and was not intended to run operations. Diversion refers to the redirection of proceeds from the Iran arms transactions to the Contras. These terms matter because Iran-Contra concerned authority and accountability as much as Nicaragua.

Section 90.10

The Secret Support Network: Oliver North, Private Funding, and the Enterprise

Lieutenant Colonel Oliver North, working under National Security Advisers Robert McFarlane and John Poindexter and with William Casey, helped coordinate private donations, contributions from foreign governments, and logistics managed by private intermediaries such as Richard Secord and Albert Hakim, the network known as the Enterprise. It operated outside the usual channels of CIA budgeting and oversight.

Allegations of narcotics trafficking by some Contra-associated individuals were investigated; these should be distinguished from claims that every Contra group or U.S. institution was involved. The network created a parallel foreign-policy apparatus with limited transparency and accountability.

When direct assistance was restricted, members of the administration and their associates turned to private and foreign sources. Oliver North, serving on the National Security Council staff, coordinated closely with intermediaries Richard Secord and Albert Hakim and with officials involved in policy and intelligence. The resulting network, commonly called the Enterprise, organized resources and logistics for the resistance while operating outside the usual appropriations and reporting channels. Its existence does not mean that everyone who supported the Contras knew of its activities, or that every outside contribution had the same legal status.

The network relied on complex relationships: donors and sympathetic governments could provide money; private intermediaries could manage accounts and transportation; political officials could communicate priorities; and armed recipients could request supplies. Such arrangements made it difficult to identify the proper source of funds and the person responsible for each decision. They also created opportunities for self-dealing, inaccurate accounting, and conflicts of interest. Later investigators followed the movement of money across organizations, banks, and individuals in order to establish what was done and what officials knew. Historians should not confuse this documentary reconstruction with a claim that a single central file recorded the entire enterprise.

The controversy extended to assistance from third countries, but it would be misleading to assume that the provision of any aid by a foreign government automatically violated the same American statute. The relevant questions concerned who solicited assistance, which American officials acted, whether official authority or resources were used, and which restriction applied. The distinction between private action and state action was contested precisely because some of the nominally private efforts were directed or encouraged by public officials. Congressional hearings and the later independent counsel investigation therefore examined both formal titles and actual conduct.

Reports concerning drug trafficking also demand careful source hierarchy. Investigations documented interactions between some Contra-associated people, traffickers, and support networks, but not every allegation has the same evidentiary status. A verified case involving an individual supplier cannot be generalized to all resistance organizations, nor does the existence of traffickers automatically establish an institutional decision by the CIA to organize drug sales. Conversely, concerns documented by investigators must not be dismissed merely because political adversaries used them. The chapter should connect evidence to named actors, dates, and the investigative body that assessed it.

A parallel supply apparatus changed the nature of the political problem. The administration was no longer merely deciding how much American policy should support insurgents abroad; it was deciding whether a covert objective could be sustained through arrangements shielded from normal legislative control. By the time the Iran proceeds became part of the picture, this infrastructure already existed. The scandal arose when covert foreign-policy objectives, personal initiative, and disputed constitutional limits converged within a system whose secrecy had become a source of operational freedom and institutional vulnerability.

Section 90.11

Arms to Iran, Hostages in Lebanon, and the Diversion of Funds

The Iran initiative began separately. Seeking the release of American hostages held by armed groups in Lebanon and contact with Iranian figures, administration officials arranged, beginning in 1985, secret transfers of U.S. weapons to Iran through intermediaries. North's memorandum of 4 April 1986 described a proposed link between the Iran sales and funding the Contras, the point at which the two initiatives joined.

Iran-Contra thus involved two secret policies connected by unauthorized financing. It was not a case of Iran openly financing the Nicaraguan rebels.

The Iran initiative developed from calculations that were separate from Nicaragua. American hostages held by armed groups in Lebanon placed pressure on the Reagan administration, while some advisers believed that contacts with Iranian intermediaries might develop relationships in Iran amid the Iran-Iraq War. Israel played an important intermediary role in parts of the early transactions. These dealings were controversial inside the American government from the beginning because public policy discouraged arms transfers to Iran, because the identities and influence of intermediaries were uncertain, and because negotiations over hostages could encourage additional kidnappings. Public declarations against concessions to hostage-takers contrasted sharply with secret bargaining.

The mechanics of the arms transactions mattered because money paid for weapons could exceed the amounts needed to purchase and move them. Oliver North and others discussed applying the difference to the Nicaraguan resistance. The unsigned and undated memorandum usually cataloged by the National Security Archive as April 4, 1986 was prepared around April 4–7, according to the Tower Commission's reconstruction. It referred to arrangements concerning hostages and to a plan for using residual proceeds. The physical document does not establish on its own that President Reagan read or approved its diversion passage. It does establish that such a proposal existed within the national security apparatus and that the two previously separate covert initiatives had been connected in planning.

The payments were not simply a transparent transfer by the Iranian state to Contra leaders. Transactions involved intermediaries, inflated prices, and accounts controlled by the private supply network. Determining how much money was received, allocated, diverted, or spent required examining financial records and testimony. Later investigators found that some resources were used for purposes beyond the stated policy, including personal expenditures. The existence of an arms transaction does not prove that all proceeds funded Nicaragua; conversely, a record of diverted proceeds does not by itself prove who authorized every element of the Iran initiative.

There were also policy divisions. Some officials argued that contact with Iranian factions might serve a strategic purpose beyond hostage release. Others regarded the arrangements as an unacceptable breach of the administration's public posture. Legal advisers questioned authority and reporting; national security officials disagreed over the credibility of intermediaries. These disagreements became part of the documentary record only after disclosures prompted investigators to recover memoranda, notes, and communications. The scandal illustrates how the secrecy that made a policy possible also weakened normal procedures for evaluating its risks.

For Nicaragua, the direct significance was additional support for an armed movement whose principal purpose was to impose military and political pressure on the revolutionary government. For American constitutional history, the significance was broader. A policy involving Iran was linked to the circumvention or disputed interpretation of restrictions involving Central America. The historian must therefore reconstruct two chains of decisions and the point where money connected them, rather than referring vaguely to an exchange of 'weapons for Contras' or treating hostages, Iran, and Nicaragua as one original project.

Section 90.12

Exposure in 1986, the Tower Commission, and the Rule-of-Law Crisis

On 5 October 1986, a Contra supply aircraft was shot down over Nicaragua and the American crew member Eugene Hasenfus was captured. In November, a Lebanese magazine disclosed the Iran arms dealings; by late November the diversion of proceeds was publicly announced. The Tower Commission reported in February 1987, and joint congressional hearings, including testimony by North and Poindexter, followed that summer.

The joint congressional majority concluded that the affair involved extensive secrecy, deception, and breaches of legal and constitutional controls; minority and supplemental views disagreed on responsibility. A congressional finding differs from a criminal conviction: several prosecutions followed, and some convictions, including those of North and Poindexter, were later vacated on appeal because of problems arising from immunized testimony. Iran-Contra tested the American constitutional system's oversight of covert executive action.

The October 5, 1986 shootdown of an aircraft supplying the Contras made the private support system difficult to deny. Eugene Hasenfus survived and was captured in Nicaragua, creating a public record of what had been concealed through private arrangements and official denials. His capture did not itself reveal the entire Iran initiative. That connection emerged as reports of secret arms dealings with Iran appeared in November and investigators began examining the financial relationships among intermediaries, officials, and accounts. The scandal therefore became public in stages: a supply operation, an arms transaction, and then the diversion of proceeds.

The Tower Commission, created by President Reagan, reviewed the National Security Council's role and presented its findings in February 1987. The congressional investigation examined the constitutional and administrative issues at greater length, holding public hearings that made North's testimony a prominent national event. The two processes had different mandates and access to evidence. Neither should be conflated with the independent counsel's subsequent investigation into possible criminal offenses. Political responsibility, negligence, deception, statutory violation, and a crime proved under applicable rules are distinct conclusions.

The record showed severe weaknesses in supervision and reporting. Officials had pursued ambitious secret initiatives, sometimes bypassing ordinary interagency processes; inaccurate statements complicated congressional oversight; and documents were destroyed or altered as disclosure approached. Yet a conclusion that senior officials failed to supervise adequately should not be converted without evidence into a claim that each had approved every operational step. The crucial historical method is to tie each finding to its author, the evidence examined, and the standard being applied. The majority and minority congressional views differed in interpretation, and the chapter should not erase that disagreement.

The later prosecutions added a further complication. Oliver North was convicted on several counts, but his convictions were vacated on appeal because of problems concerning the use of immunized congressional testimony. John Poindexter's convictions were also vacated. Those outcomes were not judicial declarations that every underlying event had been lawful or that the documented diversion did not occur. They reflected rules designed to protect defendants' constitutional rights in criminal proceedings. The independent counsel's investigation extended beyond the central public hearings and culminated in Lawrence Walsh's 1993 report, which is a later source for evaluating the case, not an event within Nicaragua's 1980s war.

Iran-Contra also affected American political culture. Supporters of Reagan emphasized Cold War aims and the perceived need to rescue hostages; critics warned against an executive apparatus able to operate beyond legislative authorization. The affair did not produce a simple, uncontested answer to all questions about presidential knowledge. It did demonstrate that national security policy could become institutionally hazardous when officials treated secrecy as a substitute for accountable legal process. For this textbook, the scandal belongs within the history of Nicaragua because the controversy began with policy toward its armed conflict, but its consequences extended into American constitutional practice and the governance of covert operations.

Division IV — Economic Crisis, Social Conflict, and Regional Peace
Section 90.13

The Wartime Economy: Sanctions, State Spending, Shortages, and Inflation

War diverted resources to defense, destroyed infrastructure, disrupted harvests, and discouraged investment. Executive Order 12513, issued 1 May 1985 and effective 7 May, prohibited major trade between the United States and Nicaragua, subject to exceptions, closing a major market and supplier. The government also used price controls, rationing, a large state sector, and multiple exchange rates, with mixed results.

Inflation worsened and fiscal imbalances grew, culminating in severe instability in 1988–1989. The crisis reflected war, external pressure, structural dependence on exports, and domestic policy; it cannot be attributed solely to sanctions or solely to Sandinista management. Chapter 93 treats the wider Latin American debt crisis.

The revolutionary economy had inherited deep structural problems. Dependence on coffee, cotton, sugar, beef, and access to imported machinery exposed Nicaragua to changing export prices and foreign exchange shortages. The 1979 war had damaged infrastructure and encouraged capital flight. The Sandinista government attempted redistribution and expanded public services through a mixed economy, retaining private production while increasing state direction. War placed that arrangement under extraordinary pressure: the same government that sought to improve rural health and literacy had to pay for a larger army and replace bridges, vehicles, and facilities damaged by repeated attacks.

The Reagan embargo altered the economic environment but did not operate as an abstract declaration. Executive Order 12513, signed May 1 and effective May 7, 1985, barred imports from Nicaragua, prohibited most American exports destined there except a stated resistance-related exception, and restricted Nicaraguan carriers and vessels. Businesses had to seek alternative markets, suppliers, shipping arrangements, and currencies. The measure increased the costs of dealing with the United States, a historically important trading partner. Historians should still ask which difficulties arose from embargo provisions, which from uncertainty and financing, and which predated the order.

Government policies were another source of pressure. Price controls intended to preserve access to basic goods could create shortages if producers could not cover costs or if transactions moved to informal markets. Multiple exchange rates, subsidies, credit expansion, and rapidly rising fiscal requirements complicated accounting and distorted incentives. Agricultural producers faced a mixture of procurement obligations, market disruptions, scarcity of inputs, and physical insecurity. It would be inaccurate to assert that all private production was eliminated; private farmers and businesses remained significant, even as many disputed state policy or reduced investment.

Inflation accelerated into hyperinflation in the late 1980s. The size of the increase is best conveyed through a properly sourced series rather than an unsourced single number, because different indices and annual measures can differ sharply. Wages could be raised nominally while purchasing power collapsed. Families spent more time obtaining necessities and preserving value through goods or stable foreign currency. Scarcity fostered unequal access, rationing pressures, and political resentment. The government's stabilization measures of 1988 were themselves painful, producing distributional conflicts that cannot be separated from the coming election.

War and policy also interacted. Military spending and security restrictions did not simply compete with economic reform; they changed incentives for investment and made central administration more intrusive. Sanctions weakened export opportunities just as damaged agriculture reduced foreign exchange, while official monetary responses contributed to inflation. The methodological requirement is to reject a one-cause explanation. A serious quantitative study should state the index, date range, currency conventions, and source for each figure. The human significance lies in the combined erosion of household purchasing power, state capacity, and confidence that the revolutionary government could secure a sustainable peace.

Section 90.14

Everyday Life under War: Conscription, Women, Churches, and Community Organizations

Compulsory military service touched families across the country. Teenage conscripts, casualties, displacement, and the loss of agricultural labor shaped household decisions. Women served in government, the army, neighborhood committees, unions, and health services, while others joined opposition groups or organized mothers of conscripts.

Church conflict persisted between the hierarchy under Cardinal Obando y Bravo and clergy sympathetic to the revolution. On the Coast, the government's approach shifted: in 1987 the Autonomy Statute, Law No. 28, recognized regional autonomy and cultural and communal rights, creating two autonomous regions. Civilian participation and demands for rights continued to evolve throughout the war.

Compulsory service reshaped the relationship between the state and the households it claimed to represent. A young man entering the army could leave a farm without its principal worker, interrupt an apprenticeship, or place new responsibilities on parents and siblings. Some volunteers regarded service as defense of the revolution and national sovereignty; others resisted conscription because they rejected the government or feared death and separation. Families' reactions were not determined solely by class or party. A household benefiting from literacy programs might also fear the draft, and one that opposed government economic policies might nonetheless reject Contra violence.

Women were central to survival in wartime communities. They worked in agriculture, markets, schools, clinics, local committees, and military support activities. Some were soldiers or political officials; others joined protests involving the military service of their relatives or challenged restrictions on independent organizations. Organizations associated with the FSLN offered opportunities for leadership while also incorporating women into a ruling-party structure. The test of women's political agency is not whether they always supported the government, but whether their work, demands, and organizational choices are represented on their own terms.

Church institutions became a further site of contestation. The Catholic hierarchy under Miguel Obando y Bravo criticized aspects of the government's direction, while priests and lay Catholics sympathetic to the revolution supported literacy, health services, and community organizing. The Vatican's concerns about clergy holding government office contributed to visible tensions. A church dispute could therefore involve religious authority, education, rights, and the distribution of political power simultaneously. Nor was Catholicism the only significant religious tradition: Moravian churches and their leaders occupied an important place in Caribbean Coast life and disputes over displacement and autonomy.

The autonomy statute of 1987 represented a material change in the coastal legal framework. Law No. 28 established regional institutions and recognized important cultural and communal rights. Its enactment did not undo the suffering of the 1981–1982 confrontations or settle every conflict over land and representation. It did, however, reflect years of Indigenous organizing, military pressure, criticism of centralizing policy, and efforts to negotiate a relationship with the Nicaraguan state. The law should be read as an institutional response to political agency on the coast, not merely an act of government generosity.

War also reorganized time. Market trips became uncertain, clinics struggled to reach dispersed villages, and students lost months of instruction. In cities, people navigated shortages, inflation, political meetings, and news of casualties. Rural families faced difficult decisions about whether to remain near contested roads or flee. To illustrate those effects responsibly, the chapter should distinguish a composite description from a documented individual's testimony. Both can teach; only the latter supplies evidence about what a specific person actually experienced. The result was a society whose revolutionary gains and wartime losses were intertwined in daily practice.

Contadora, Central American Diplomacy, and Esquipulas II

In 1983, Mexico, Colombia, Venezuela, and Panama formed the Contadora Group to seek a negotiated regional settlement. Central American governments had different priorities: Nicaragua sought an end to U.S.-backed attacks, Honduras and El Salvador relied on U.S. security ties, Costa Rica sought to keep the conflict off its territory, and Guatemala wanted to limit external pressure. Costa Rican President Óscar Arias drove a new plan that led to Esquipulas II, signed 7 August 1987 by five Central American presidents.

The agreement addressed national reconciliation, democratization, an end to hostilities, elections, and the ending of aid to irregular forces. It moved regional conflict toward negotiation based on political reforms, mutual security commitments, and verification.

The Central American states were not passive terrain for superpower conflict. Their leaders had reasons to fear an expanding regional war and to resist the assumption that Washington and Moscow could dictate settlement terms. The Contadora initiative, launched by Mexico, Colombia, Venezuela, and Panama in 1983, sought regional arrangements covering security, nonintervention, democratization, and restraint of assistance to armed movements. Its proposals did not immediately resolve the conflict, but they established a diplomatic vocabulary and a negotiation process in which Central American interests could be asserted directly.

Each government approached those talks with different vulnerabilities. Nicaragua demanded an end to armed attacks organized through neighboring territory and sought recognition of its government. Honduras was concerned about security, its alliance with Washington, and the consequences of hosting resistance forces. Costa Rica sought to preserve a civilian political order and prevent its border from becoming a permanent war zone. El Salvador was embroiled in its own civil war, while Guatemala's political and military conditions created additional constraints. A regional text thus had to address multiple conflicts without pretending they were identical or that all governments could accept the same immediate concessions.

Costa Rican President Óscar Arias Sánchez helped turn diplomacy toward a framework emphasizing internal political liberalization alongside an end to support for irregular armed groups. The Esquipulas II agreement of August 7, 1987 was signed by five Central American presidents in Guatemala City. Its provisions addressed national reconciliation, an end to hostilities, democratization, elections, refugee assistance, and the cessation of assistance to irregular forces. These subjects were linked deliberately: the parties could not reasonably demand an end to cross-border armed sponsorship while refusing political procedures that might make negotiations sustainable.

Implementation was much harder than signature. Governments could disagree about who counted as an irregular force, what timetable applied, how political opposition would operate under emergency rule, and whether monitoring bodies had access to reliable information. Public endorsement of peace could coexist with continuing military preparation. International verification required resources and consent; the fact that an accord established obligations did not mean that ceasefires, press freedoms, or repatriation occurred immediately. The evidence must therefore distinguish promised reforms from changes actually documented in each state.

Esquipulas II mattered because it made peace a regional political project rather than simply a consequence of battlefield victory. It also created incentives for Nicaragua to liberalize political life and negotiate with its armed opponents while pressing neighbors and external powers to reduce support for those opponents. The following year's Sapoá talks did not replace the regional framework; they attempted to translate it into arrangements among the Nicaraguan combatants. The chapter should treat Arias and the participating Central American presidents as political agents with distinct calculations, not as supporting figures in a story confined to the Reagan administration and the FSLN.

Ceasefire, demobilization, and regional autonomy

A ceasefire halts fighting but does not settle political disputes. Demobilization is the disarming and reintegration of armed forces. Regional autonomy gives a territory self-government within a state. Each was a separate process in Nicaragua, which is why peace agreements preceded the war's end by years.

Sapoá, the 1988 Ceasefire, and the Difficult Transition from War to Negotiation

On 23 March 1988, the Sandinista government and resistance representatives reached an agreement at Sapoá, with Cardinal Obando y Bravo and the Organization of American States playing roles. It provided for an initial sixty-day suspension of offensive operations beginning 1 April, continuing talks, amnesty, national dialogue, and political freedoms.

Implementation was difficult. Declining external funding, regional diplomacy, and economic exhaustion altered each side's incentives. Sapoá did not immediately end the war, but it established a framework that made a political settlement more plausible.

Sapoá was important because representatives of the government and the armed resistance addressed one another directly in a public process. They had spent years portraying the other side as illegitimate: the Sandinistas emphasized the Contras' outside sponsors and violent attacks, while resistance leaders described a government unwilling to permit genuine opposition. Entering negotiations did not require them to resolve those competing narratives. It required recognition that neither side could achieve its aims quickly by force and that continued combat imposed costs on civilians and regional states.

The parties met March 21–23, 1988 near the Costa Rican frontier. The agreement called for a sixty-day halt to offensive military operations beginning April 1 while negotiations continued toward a definitive ceasefire. It also addressed amnesty, the return of exiles, participation in political dialogue, and matters of civil liberty. Cardinal Obando y Bravo and OAS Secretary General João Clemente Baena Soares served as witnesses and played roles in the wider verification and mediation arrangements. The precise text matters: an initial suspension of offensive operations was not the same thing as disbanding every military unit or settling all political disputes.

Implementation required decisions about where armed groups would assemble, who would observe compliance, which detainees would be released, and how former insurgents could rejoin political life without immediate retaliation. Each side distrusted the other. The government's promise of amnesty depended on questions about timing and eligibility, while the resistance's acceptance of restrictions on fighting depended on its confidence that political participation would be safe. A ceasefire might reduce violence without eliminating armed capacity or resolving the fate of refugees. Negotiating details could therefore be politically as important as announcing agreement.

The international setting had changed. Iran-Contra damaged the credibility of secret American support mechanisms. Congressional disputes constrained some assistance. Central American presidents had invested political authority in Esquipulas, and the Nicaraguan economy was under mounting strain. These conditions encouraged negotiation but did not produce an automatic settlement. For the Sandinistas, talks offered a possible route to reduced military pressure; for resistance leaders, they could offer access to electoral politics and a future not entirely dependent on foreign support. Both faced dissent among their own constituencies.

Sapoá is best understood as a transitional institution rather than an isolated turning point. It linked military restraint, national dialogue, humanitarian concerns, and the regional peace framework. The agreement was followed by further disputes and negotiations; actual demobilization and the final transfer of government came later. Its significance lies in showing that parties which could not defeat one another decisively began searching for enforceable political arrangements. That search opened the way for changes in electoral timing and international observation in 1989 and 1990.

Section 90.17

Nicaragua in 1988–1989: Exhaustion, Liberalization, and the UNO Coalition

By 1988–1989, contraction, inflation, austerity, and frustration with conscription had made an end to war increasingly attractive. The government relaxed some emergency restrictions, and opposition organizations rebuilt. The National Opposition Union (UNO) brought together a broad range of parties with differing economic and ideological views and chose Violeta Barrios de Chamorro, a candidate able to appeal across them.

UNO could unite around peace, recovery, and change without a unified program. For many voters, the question was no longer only whether to defend the 1979 victory but how to end war and crisis.

The late 1980s altered the language of Nicaraguan politics. In the early revolutionary years, many arguments turned on the legacy of the Somoza dictatorship and the defense of national sovereignty. By 1988–1989, inflation, shortages, the draft, and the continuing costs of war had placed household security at the forefront. A voter could respect the overthrow of Somoza and still judge that the ruling party should be replaced. This difference helps explain why the FSLN's wartime resilience did not guarantee electoral popularity.

Economic stabilization measures placed further burdens on workers and producers. State employees faced changes in pay and purchasing power; small traders navigated prices and shortages; agricultural households faced uncertain markets and the continuing possibility of armed activity. Scarcity also strained political solidarity: mass organizations that had distributed social services could be regarded simultaneously as channels of participation and as instruments of official discipline. For many families, a promise to end the war had value independent of its ideological source.

Political opening provided opportunities for organizations that had long criticized the government. Opposition newspapers, civic associations, religious groups, and parties were not interchangeable, but they could cooperate in the expectation that electoral change would reduce military pressure. The National Opposition Union united fourteen parties spanning a wide range of ideological positions. Its breadth was an advantage against an incumbent identified with a powerful political organization. It was also a potential source of difficulty, because disagreements over privatization, land confiscations, military institutions, and the role of the FSLN could be postponed more easily than resolved.

Violeta Barrios de Chamorro's public identity mattered. As the widow of Pedro Joaquín Chamorro, whose 1978 assassination had mobilized opposition to Somoza, and as a former member of the revolutionary junta, she embodied an anti-dictatorial history without remaining aligned with FSLN rule. This biography allowed different voters to see continuity with the struggle against Somoza and the possibility of a different governmental direction. It would be simplistic, however, to describe her campaign as a purely symbolic response to the past. UNO also organized around immediate demands concerning military service, prices, economic recovery, civil liberty, and international relations.

The emergence of UNO did not prove that every voter had abandoned Sandinismo. The FSLN retained extensive networks and committed supporters, while some independents opposed both main coalitions. Voters evaluated not only parties but expectations: whether a Chamorro presidency would help end American military pressure, whether change would threaten gains in education or land access, and whether the armed forces would accept a different civilian government. The election was becoming a decision about how to live after war as much as a judgment on the revolution's origins.

Section 90.18

The 1989 Regional Agreements, Electoral Preparations, and International Observation

A February 1989 Central American presidential summit and subsequent negotiations set arrangements for elections in February 1990, including commitments on demobilization and guarantees for opposition. Nicaragua invited observation by the United Nations, the Organization of American States, and the Carter Center. The George H. W. Bush administration maintained pressure on the Sandinistas and supported anti-Sandinista political forces; diplomatic pressure, electoral support, and direct assistance should be distinguished in the documentary record.

The 1990 elections thus resulted from Nicaraguan political decisions shaped by a regional peace process and unusually extensive international observation.

The decision to hold elections in February 1990 emerged from a sequence of regional commitments and domestic negotiations rather than a spontaneous concession. At the February 1989 summit of Central American presidents, Nicaragua accepted an accelerated electoral timetable within a broader diplomatic process. The surrounding agreements addressed reconciliation and the political conditions for ending cross-border support of irregular forces. Those commitments did not remove the difficulties of administering an election after years of violence; they created a framework in which the government, opposition, and observers could dispute the rules before voting day.

International monitoring was unusually prominent. The Carter Center and delegations linked to the United Nations and Organization of American States observed preparations, registration, campaigning, voting procedures, and counting. Their mandates were not identical, and their presence did not erase domestic power imbalances. Observation provided a mechanism for assessing whether the published result matched the ballots and for communicating concerns about the process. The fact that Sandinista authorities invited such scrutiny is historically significant; it also meant that a disputed result would be judged before international witnesses rather than solely by institutions controlled by the incumbent.

The United States under George H. W. Bush continued to support political forces seeking to replace the FSLN, but different forms of support must be separated. Diplomatic statements, economic pressure, assistance to election-related initiatives, and financing of particular opposition activities were not the same as an armed operation. Some campaign claims implied that a change in government would make a normal relationship with Washington possible; voters could weigh that expectation when evaluating the practical meaning of peace. A historian should cite the evidence for specific channels of assistance rather than converting U.S. preference for UNO into a claim that the United States controlled every decision by the coalition.

Preparations involved a public campaign and the logistics of a nationwide election in a country with large areas of poor infrastructure and recent conflict. Registration and polling had to include displaced citizens and regions with distinct languages and political conditions, including the autonomous Caribbean areas. Opposition access, campaign security, official media, and the role of the armed forces were therefore consequential questions. International observers could document procedures, but no one report could fully capture private intimidation or every voter's motivations.

The 1990 vote was shaped by an exceptional combination: a revolutionary party with strong institutions, an opposition coalition united principally by a wish for change, an unresolved history of externally supported warfare, and an organized international monitoring effort. The earlier peace agreements became meaningful insofar as they provided an electoral opportunity that the main actors were prepared to recognize. The transition's success would ultimately depend not just on credible counting, but on whether defeated incumbents, newly elected leaders, and armed organizations accepted the result and negotiated the practical transfer of authority.

Section 90.19

25 February 1990: Ortega, Chamorro, and the Unexpected Opposition Victory

The FSLN campaigned on defending the revolution's achievements and national sovereignty; UNO campaigned on peace, recovery, and change. War exhaustion, conscription, inflation, expectations about U.S. policy, and campaign organization all mattered. The result gave Chamorro roughly 54.7 percent and Ortega roughly 40.8 percent, with the remainder to other candidates. Observers reported on the vote, and Ortega accepted the outcome.

The Sandinistas lost despite retaining major organizations and surviving the war. The result reflected political preference, war fatigue, hardship, and expectations of peace; it was not a simple referendum on socialism.

The central contrast in the campaign concerned the meaning of peace. The FSLN argued that its government had defended Nicaragua's sovereignty, improved schooling and health services, and resisted an internationally financed insurgency. UNO appealed to people exhausted by war, compulsory service, shortages, and political disputes and promised a chance for recovery. Both could claim elements of the anti-Somoza tradition, though they presented different accounts of how that tradition should guide the future. This was not a choice between an obvious revolutionary constituency and a uniformly conservative opposition.

The election took place on February 25, 1990 with extensive international monitoring. The result surprised many observers and Sandinista officials. The Carter Center's report records the importance of sampled counts and communication with leaders as preliminary figures showed an opposition lead. Daniel Ortega publicly accepted defeat. These moments were historically important because the FSLN commanded substantial state institutions and an organized military, yet chose a political response rather than refusing the declared result. Acceptance did not resolve disputes over the army, property, or appointments; it made negotiation possible.

The headline presidential percentages—roughly 54.7 percent for Violeta Chamorro and 40.8 percent for Ortega—should be presented with their rounding and denominator made clear. Other candidates received the remaining votes. The contemporaneous Inter-Parliamentary Union record for the simultaneous legislative elections reports 1,512,107 voters from 1,752,088 registered electors, turnout of 86.3 percent, and 51 of 92 assembly seats for UNO against 39 for the FSLN. Those are legislative figures, and they must not be confused with the presidential vote totals. Used correctly, the two datasets show both the breadth of participation and the difference between presidential and assembly contests.

Why did UNO win? Wartime hardship, inflation, fear of continued conscription, hope for restored economic ties, concerns about party dominance, and confidence in Chamorro's public identity all contributed. Some voters may have calculated that an FSLN victory would mean continued American pressure; others may have preferred UNO regardless of Washington's posture. A ballot cannot reveal which motive was decisive in each household. Surveys and later interviews are useful but have sampling, recall, and political limitations. The best explanation considers several interacting pressures rather than presenting voters as either coerced or persuaded by one issue alone.

The election also demonstrates the limits of equating military success with legitimacy. The Sandinista army had resisted overthrow, and the FSLN maintained a durable organizational network, yet large numbers of citizens wanted a different government. This judgment did not erase support for the revolution's social achievements. It separated the question of whether Somoza should have been overthrown from the question of who should govern in 1990. The result transformed a bitter ideological and military confrontation into a politically consequential electoral transition whose practical details still had to be settled.

Section 90.20

April 1990: The Transfer of Power and the Meaning of the Sandinista Decade

Violeta Chamorro was inaugurated on 25 April 1990. Negotiations over the transition covered the status of the army, the role of Sandinista institutions, property, and Contra demobilization. The Carter Center and other observers noted the peaceful transfer of authority from a revolutionary government to its electoral opponents.

The revolution's record remained contested: literacy, health care, sovereignty, and participation on one side; militarization, property disputes, and restrictions on opposition on the other. The end of Sandinista rule in 1990 was not the end of Sandinismo, which remained a significant political movement. Later developments lie beyond this chapter.

The weeks between the election and inauguration were not simply an administrative interval. Negotiations dealt with armed forces whose senior officers had emerged from the Sandinista movement, public employees identified with the outgoing government, disputed property transfers, and thousands of men linked to the resistance who needed to disarm and return to civilian life. The incoming administration faced pressure to honor opposition expectations while avoiding the disruption of institutions upon which security and public services still depended. That required bargains across groups that had been enemies during the preceding decade.

Daniel Ortega's acceptance of defeat and Violeta Chamorro's inauguration on April 25, 1990 gave the transfer strong symbolic importance. It showed that the FSLN could relinquish the presidency through an election rather than being defeated militarily or overthrown. Yet the transfer did not mean that every Sandinista institution immediately disappeared or that demobilization had been completed on inauguration day. The continued position of Humberto Ortega in the military became part of debates over continuity, civilian authority, and the meaning of political change. Property and military disputes carried the potential to prolong insecurity even after the election.

Former combatants' reintegration posed another set of problems. People who had lived in camps or as part of irregular forces needed safe routes home, livelihoods, documentation, and assurances against reprisals. Families displaced by combat sought land, housing, and access to fields that might have changed ownership or been damaged. Regional diplomacy and international monitoring could facilitate agreements, but local conflicts over property and retaliation required practical measures on the ground. The distinction between ending open warfare and building a stable peace is therefore essential.

The historical record of the Sandinista decade remains contested because different measures produce different assessments. Literacy and public-health campaigns expanded opportunities for many poorer Nicaraguans. Centralization, press restrictions, war mobilization, and the marginalization of some opposition groups generated genuine conflicts over rights and political participation. The Caribbean Coast experience shows both grave early coercion and later negotiated autonomy. Economic analysis must include the destruction and financing pressures of war, the embargo, and domestic policy mistakes. A textbook should not count improvements in schooling as cancelling abuses, or describe military pressure as proof that every government decision was unavoidable.

The larger lesson concerns agency. The United States influenced the war through covert assistance, sanctions, and diplomacy; Central American governments developed peace initiatives; and Nicaraguan armed organizations fought and negotiated. But in February 1990 Nicaraguan voters made a political choice, and Nicaraguan leaders had to decide whether to accept it. The end of FSLN government did not mean the end of Sandinismo or an end to debate over national sovereignty. It closed a first period of revolutionary rule while leaving the country's political and social future unsettled. Chapter 90 should end here, before turning to the distinct histories of neighboring civil wars and the wider post-Cold-War order.

Chapter Summary

The Nicaraguan revolution entered the 1980s with a divided coalition, a strong ruling front, and a hostile superpower. Domestic opposition was real, but the size and duration of the armed conflict depended on foreign assistance that gave the Contras capabilities beyond most domestic movements.

The Contras were heterogeneous: former Guardsmen, farmers, Indigenous activists, and civilians with different aims. The Caribbean Coast produced a distinct conflict, including the 1982 relocation of roughly 8,500 Miskitu and later negotiated autonomy.

The war damaged civilians, health and education services, and the economy. Both sides committed abuses, though not identically, and the evidence is shaped by polarization.

In Washington, the pursuit of the policy produced a struggle over congressional authority, the Boland restrictions, a secret support network, and the diversion of Iran arms proceeds. Investigations produced findings and prosecutions that must be kept distinct. The ICJ judgment of 1986 set out limits on intervention without attributing every Contra act to the United States.

Regional diplomacy, from Contadora to Esquipulas II, and bilateral talks at Sapoá moved the conflict toward negotiation. Economic exhaustion and shifting external support strengthened that trend.

In 1990, Chamorro defeated Ortega in an internationally observed election, and power passed peacefully. The result reflected peace expectations, hardship, and political preference. The end of Sandinista government did not end Sandinismo.

Nicaragua’s Atlantic Coast illustrates a different line of political conflict: cultural and territorial self-determination, made more urgent by military operations along the Honduran frontier. International investigators reported both security threats and serious abuses, and the later autonomy law reflected an attempt to renegotiate relations between Managua and coastal communities.

The 1984 vote and 1987 Constitution created formal political institutions during war, while opposition parties, journalists, and religious leaders continued to dispute the meaning of pluralism. The Sandinistas gained electoral authority without eliminating the deeper controversy about power, emergency restrictions, and political competition.

The 1985 trade embargo and years of fighting damaged markets and state finances, but central-bank evidence also records domestic monetary and spending choices that contributed to hyperinflation. Economic exhaustion helped make peace and the promise of a different government attractive across previous political alignments.

The February 1990 election was the product of regional commitments, Nicaraguan political choices, and international observation. Its results were accepted and followed by a negotiated transfer of authority, but demobilization, property disputes, and the position of armed institutions remained major challenges for the incoming administration.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a revolution can survive a war and still lose an election. The Contra war was neither purely a domestic uprising nor purely an American creation; its length and capability came from the interaction of Nicaraguan divisions with external support.

Iran-Contra showed that foreign policy by secrecy carries constitutional costs. The Central American diplomacy of Contadora and Esquipulas II showed that regional governments could shape outcomes, not merely react to them.

Chapter 91 turns to the wars of El Salvador and Guatemala, whose links to Nicaragua were part of the regional Cold War, and Chapter 92 to Panama.
