Silver Empires: Potosí, Zacatecas, and Colonial Mining

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Mountain, a Ridge, and the Machinery Between Them

In the middle of the sixteenth century, two discoveries of silver ore, one on a cold Andean mountain and one on a dry ridge in northern New Spain, set in motion a chain of labor, technology, taxation, and trade that reached from Indigenous villages to the courts of Madrid and the markets of Ming China. The silver was real, but so was the machinery that produced it: mercury, mills, mule trains, drafted workers, hired workers, investors, and tax collectors.

Spanish sources date the beginning of large-scale exploitation of the Cerro Rico of Potosí, in the highlands of what is now Bolivia, to 1545. A year later, in 1546, Spaniards and their Indigenous guides registered the first rich claims at Zacatecas, on the northern edge of the territory the Spaniards called New Spain. Within a generation, Potosí had grown into one of the largest cities in the Americas, at an altitude where grain would not ripen and where the cold shaped every part of life. Zacatecas had become a center of Spanish and Indigenous settlement in a region that the Spaniards had not yet subdued, and that Indigenous peoples they lumped together as Chichimecas were still defending. The two places became symbols of the wealth that Spain drew from the Americas, and the phrase "worth a Potosí" entered the Spanish language as a measure of fabulous riches.

This chapter asks how that wealth was actually produced. The answer is more complicated than the image of a mountain full of silver suggests. Mining was not a single activity but a chain of activities: finding and registering veins, digging and hauling ore, draining and timbering workings, crushing ore, refining it by fire or by mercury, assaying and taxing the metal, minting it, and moving it to markets. Each link required people with particular skills, and each depended on infrastructure: dams and mills, fuel and animals, and, above all after the 1550s, a steady supply of mercury. Mercury came from only a few deposits, chiefly at Almadén in Spain and, from the 1560s, at Huancavelica in the Peruvian highlands. The Spanish Crown controlled its sale, which gave the monarchy a lever over the entire industry.

The chapter also asks who did the work and under what conditions. At Potosí, a state-organized system of rotating labor drafts, adapted by the viceroy Francisco de Toledo in the 1570s from an Andean institution called the mit'a, coexisted with a large market of paid and semi-independent labor. At Zacatecas, most workers were Indigenous migrants from central and western Mexico who arrived because the mines offered wages, and the regional economy depended on their willingness to stay. These two systems should not be collapsed into one. Both, however, developed within colonial power relations that gave Spanish authorities and owners great advantages, and both imposed heavy costs on bodies, families, communities, and landscapes.

Silver also connected Latin America to the wider world. Spanish American silver financed the wars and debts of the Habsburg monarchy, circulated in the Atlantic economy, and crossed the Pacific on the Manila galleons to buy goods in a Chinese market that wanted silver. Historians debate how much weight to give silver in explaining global economic change, and this chapter does not claim that it was the only force at work. It does show that colonial mining was a global enterprise from its beginnings, built by Indigenous labor and expertise as much as by Spanish capital, and that its benefits and burdens were distributed very unevenly.

The evidence for this history is rich but partial. Royal treasury accounts, mining ordinances, viceregal decrees, petitions, litigation, notarial contracts, and visit reports record the official and commercial sides of mining, but they were written by officials, owners, and clerics, and they usually describe workers as quantities or categories. Technical treatises, such as the one by Álvaro Alonso Barba examined in this chapter, explain how ore was refined but say little about the people who did the refining. Archaeology, lake-sediment studies, and the analysis of mercury and lead pollution add a physical record that does not depend on written sources. Indigenous voices appear mainly through petitions and legal testimony, where they were shaped by the forms of colonial law.

The chapter belongs to Part IX, which explains how Iberian empires governed, extracted, and connected their American territories. Chapter 40 described the institutions of Spanish rule, and Chapter 41 turned to Portuguese Brazil. This chapter follows the money and the labor, concentrating on the period from about 1545 to 1700. The plantations, estates, ports, and cities that Chapter 43 will examine were tied to mining in many ways, but their detailed treatment lies there. The Portuguese gold boom of the eighteenth century and the Bourbon reforms that reorganized mining after 1700 lie beyond this chapter's boundaries.

Historical Context

Chapters 7 through 9 and 16 through 20 described the long history of Indigenous mining and metalwork in Mesoamerica and the Andes. Andean peoples had worked gold, silver, copper, and bronze for centuries before the Inca, and the Inca state drew on silver-producing districts, including Porco, near Potosí, for objects tied to status and religion. Chapter 20 introduced the Inca mit'a, a system of rotating labor service owed to the state by households and organized through ayllus and local leaders. Metals in these societies were valued mainly for ornament, ritual, and prestige rather than as money in the European sense. That difference matters, because the Spanish demand for silver as currency and tax revenue was a new kind of demand.

Chapters 33 and 36 showed how conquest in Mexico and Peru was followed by decades of incomplete control, and Chapters 40 and 41 described the institutions the Crown built to govern: viceroyalties, audiencias, royal treasuries, and municipal councils. The early colonial economy of both regions rested first on tribute and encomienda, the grants that entitled Spaniards to tribute and labor from Indigenous communities. By the middle of the sixteenth century, the Crown was trying to reduce the power of encomenderos and to assert control over Indigenous labor and tribute. Silver mining entered at precisely this moment and offered a new source of revenue for the monarchy and a new field of opportunity for settlers.

The Spanish monarchy under Charles V and Philip II was also under financial pressure. It fought wars in Europe, borrowed heavily from international bankers, and declared bankruptcy several times in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Revenue from the Americas did not by itself pay for the monarchy's commitments, and silver was only one source among several, including taxes in Castile. But it was a growing and relatively flexible one, and the Crown had strong reasons to encourage production and to capture a share of it. Those reasons shaped royal policy on mercury, taxation, and labor.

The technical background was a transition. Before the 1550s, silver in the Americas was refined mostly by smelting, in which ore was heated with a flux in a furnace, and the metal was separated from the molten mass. Smelting required fuel and worked best on rich ores. The adaptation of the mercury amalgamation method to American ores, beginning in New Spain in the 1550s and spreading to the Andes in the early 1570s, made it possible to refine lower-grade ores profitably. This shift, together with the labor reorganization of the 1570s, helps explain why silver output in the Andes rose so dramatically in the last decades of the sixteenth century.

Finally, the geography of colonial mining was shaped by altitude, climate, water, and distance. Potosí lay at roughly four thousand meters above sea level, far from the coast and from regions that could supply food, fuel, and animals. Zacatecas lay on a semi-arid plateau beyond the densely settled central valleys, on the road north that the Spaniards would extend into the frontier. Historians use "Upper Peru" for the territory of the Audiencia of Charcas, which included Potosí, and "New Spain" for the viceroyalty centered on Mexico City. Neither is identical to a modern country. Students should keep in mind that Bolivia and Mexico did not exist as such in this period.

Division I — The Rise of the Silver-Mining Regions · Section 42.1

Silver and the Making of Colonial Empires

Silver was not the only product of colonial Latin America, and mining was not the whole colonial economy. Most people in Spanish America worked in agriculture, herding, textile production, domestic service, or local trade, and large parts of the continent had little or no mining at all. Brazil's economy rested first on sugar, as Chapter 41 explained. Yet silver occupied a position out of proportion to its share of employment. It generated the commodity that the Crown could tax most easily, it created markets for food, textiles, mules, salt, and tools across enormous distances, and it gave the Spanish Atlantic empire a means of payment acceptable in Europe and Asia. Historians have long seen silver as one of the strongest links among the American, European, and Asian economies of the early modern period.

Why silver and not, for example, gold? Gold had drawn the first Spaniards to the Caribbean, but Caribbean placer deposits were quickly exhausted, and the Indigenous populations that had been set to work washing gold were devastated, as Chapters 27 and 29 described. Mexican and Andean gold was available mostly as accumulated objects, which the conquerors melted down in the first decades after conquest. Silver was different, because it lay in deep veins that could be worked for generations and that, with the right technology, could be refined in large quantities. Colonial silver mining therefore took shape as a sustained industrial undertaking rather than a one-time seizure of treasure.

Mining also reshaped the geography of settlement. Silver ores lay in mountainous and arid places that had little prior Spanish presence, and mining created towns in them almost overnight. These towns needed food, firewood, mules, cloth, salt, and tools, and so they drew in agriculture and trade over wide hinterlands, a process that Chapter 43 will follow. The towns also needed workers, and the competition for labor linked mining to the broader questions of tribute, migration, and Indigenous community life explored later in this chapter.

The relationship among the five elements named in this chapter's title should be kept in view. Mining depended on labor, which came through coercion, wages, and combinations of the two. It depended on technology, including drainage, milling, smelting, and amalgamation, much of which relied on Indigenous knowledge and on innovations made in the Americas. It depended on Indigenous societies, whose communities supplied workers, food, and expertise and whose leaders negotiated, resisted, or exploited the demands made on them. It depended on state power, which regulated claims, taxed output, and controlled mercury. And it depended on global demand, because silver had value only insofar as it could be exchanged. A change in any one of these could change the whole.

A caution about scale is also in order. Figures for silver production are estimates reconstructed from treasury accounts that recorded registered metal, and much silver was never registered or was registered in ways that obscure its origin. Historians therefore use ranges and trends rather than precise totals, and this chapter follows their example. Where the sources allow it, the chapter distinguishes registered production from taxes collected, and both from the wealth that private owners and merchants retained.

Section 42.2

Potosí: Cerro Rico and the Andean Silver Boom

The mountain that Spaniards called the Cerro Rico, the "Rich Hill," rises above the high plateau of the southern Andes in the territory then under the jurisdiction of the Audiencia of Charcas. Spanish tradition holds that the veins were discovered in 1545, and that an Indigenous man, often named in later accounts as Diego Huallpa, found silver while camping on the slopes. Such stories are told with variations, and they serve a particular purpose: they present the mountain as something the Spaniards found rather than something that had a history. The evidence points to a different picture. Indigenous peoples had exploited silver in the region before the Spanish arrival, the Inca drew silver from nearby Porco, and research on sediments around the Cerro Rico indicates metallurgical activity before the Spanish mining boom. What began in 1545 was a Spanish takeover and an enormous expansion of existing practice, not the first human encounter with the mountain's ore.

The early years were dominated by exceptionally rich surface veins. Spanish miners and their Indigenous workers dug into the mountain, and the first refining was done by smelting, with Indigenous specialists operating wind-drafted furnaces called huayras on the hillsides, as Section 42.6 explains. News spread quickly, and people arrived from across the viceroyalty: Spanish miners and merchants, Indigenous migrants and their families, Africans brought by Spaniards, and a large population of artisans, traders, and servants. The settlement of Potosí was formally recognized as a town in the mid-1540s, and it grew without a plan. By around 1600, estimates of its population commonly run to a hundred thousand or more, a figure that made it one of the largest urban places in the Americas, though counts vary and many residents were temporary.

Altitude defined the city. At roughly four thousand meters, Potosí was too cold for most crops, and its food, fuel, cloth, and animals had to be brought from valleys and plains that might be many days away. This produced an economy of supply on a continental scale. Maize, wheat, and wine came from valleys in the surrounding region, coca from the eastern slopes, textiles from other highland regions, and mules from the plains to the south. High prices rewarded those who could provide these goods, and Indigenous traders, including many women who sold food, coca, and cloth in the markets, found openings in the city's commerce. Chapter 43 will treat the wider trade networks.

The social order of Potosí was unstable and competitive. Mine owners and refiners, many of them Spaniards, held the greatest wealth, but their fortunes rose and fell with the veins and with the price of mercury and labor. Disputes over mining claims were frequent, and the rivalries among regional groups of Spaniards, notably in conflicts between those of Basque and of other origins in the early seventeenth century, show how much was at stake. The Crown's officials, the clergy, and a municipal council worked to impose order on a city that also had a reputation for violence, gambling, and display. The Spanish accounts of Potosí's luxury and vice, written to impress or to warn, should be read alongside the evidence for the toil that lay beneath them.

The second great step in Potosí's history came in the 1570s, when amalgamation made it profitable to refine the lower-grade ores that the original surface veins no longer supplied, and when Viceroy Toledo reorganized the labor supply. Those changes, the subjects of Sections 42.7 and 42.10, produced the boom of the following decades. They also illustrate a pattern that recurs through this chapter: the large increases in output did not come from discovery alone but from changes in the technical and institutional apparatus around the ore.

Section 42.3

Zacatecas: Silver and Northern New Spain

Zacatecas lies on a dry plateau north of the Mesoamerican heartland, in a region that had been home to mobile and semi-sedentary peoples whom the Nahuas called Chichimecas, a word that Spaniards adopted for a variety of distinct groups, including Zacatecos, Guachichiles, Guamares, and others. Silver was found there in 1546, according to the traditional account by Spaniards working with Indigenous guides, and the first claims were registered by a small group of Spanish entrepreneurs, among them Juan de Tolosa, Cristóbal de Oñate, and Diego de Ibarra. The new settlement was not a conquest of a densely settled kingdom. It was an intrusion into a region of dispersed communities who defended their lands, and the so-called Chichimeca War, which lasted in different phases from the 1550s until the 1590s, was in part a response.

The war shaped the whole environment of northern mining. Indigenous groups raided the roads along which silver, supplies, and travelers moved, and the Spanish response combined armed escorts, fortified garrisons called presidios, missions, and, eventually, negotiated peace settlements that included gifts of food, clothing, and other goods. The roads connecting Zacatecas with Mexico City, including the route that became known as the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, became corridors of both commerce and violence. The Spanish also arranged for Indigenous colonists from the central valleys to settle in the north: in 1591, families from Tlaxcala, descendants of the allies discussed in Chapter 31, were sent to establish communities in the frontier region. Indigenous settlers were therefore both laborers and instruments of Spanish frontier policy, and their position was shaped by the privileges that Tlaxcalans had negotiated from the Crown.

Mining at Zacatecas required a mobile workforce. The local Indigenous population was sparse and hostile, and the mines drew migrant workers from the more densely populated regions of central and western Mexico, among them Nahuas, Purépecha, Otomí, and others. These workers settled in neighborhoods that took on distinct Indigenous identities, with their own churches, confraternities, and leaders. Some Indigenous and enslaved African workers were brought forcibly in the early years, and captive Chichimecas were enslaved in the context of the frontier war, though as the sixteenth century advanced, wage labor became the main source of workers at Zacatecas. Section 42.12 returns to that question.

The town itself grew into a center of Spanish and Indigenous settlement and one of the principal cities of New Spain's northern interior. Its wealth supported churches, a municipal government, and a merchant community that supplied the mines with mules, salt, mercury, and food from the agricultural zones to the south. It also supported the missions and colonization that extended Spanish authority northward over the following century. The mining town, in other words, was both an economic center and an agent of frontier expansion, and Zacatecas became the model of a pattern that was repeated in later northern settlements such as San Luis Potosí and Parral.

The contrast with Potosí is instructive. Zacatecas lay in a region with milder conditions and closer connections to existing Spanish settlements, and its labor supply rested on migration and wages rather than on a large state-organized draft. Its refining developed around the patio process, which originated in New Spain, and its mercury supply came from across the Atlantic rather than from a nearby mine. These differences shaped everything from labor relations to fiscal accounting, and they illustrate why it is a mistake to speak of a single "Spanish colonial mining system."

Section 42.4

Beyond the Two Great Centers: The Geography of Colonial Mining

Potosí and Zacatecas dominate accounts of colonial silver, but they were two among many mining districts. In central New Spain, Taxco and Sultepec were among the earliest, and Pachuca and the nearby Real del Monte, where the patio process was developed, supplied ore to Mexico City's economy. Guanajuato, where silver was found in the 1550s, grew into a significant center later in the colonial period, with ores of generally lower grade that benefited from amalgamation. San Luis Potosí, founded after the discovery of silver at the Cerro de San Pedro in 1592, extended the northern mining zone. Parral, in the far north of New Spain, began to produce in the 1630s. In the Andes, Oruro began to yield silver from about 1606, and other districts in Charcas and in the central Andes produced silver and, in some regions, gold and tin.

Each district had its own geology and its own constraints. Some ores were rich but shallow and quickly exhausted. Others were poor but abundant and became profitable only with amalgamation. Some mines were far from water, which limited the use of mills, and others lay near streams that could be dammed. Some districts could draw labor from nearby Indigenous communities and others depended on long-distance migration. Access to supplies determined costs, and costs determined which ores could be worked at a profit. The result was a patchwork of regional economies, each with its own timetable of boom and decline.

Mining districts were also linked to one another. Mercury shipped from Spain supplied several New Spanish centers, while Huancavelica's mercury supplied the Andes. Workers moved among districts, merchants carried goods between them, and the experience gained in one place could be applied in another. Spanish and Indigenous specialists took their knowledge of refining and mine management to new districts, and the success of a technique at one center encouraged its adoption elsewhere. The diffusion of mercury amalgamation across the Spanish Americas is the clearest example.

These facts caution against narrating colonial mining as a single rise and fall. In the Andes, registered output grew through the sixteenth century, reached a peak around the 1590s, and then declined in a long and uneven pattern as the richest ores were exhausted and costs rose. In New Spain, output followed a different path, with disruptions in the early seventeenth century, adjustments in the later seventeenth, and a major expansion in the eighteenth, which lies beyond this chapter. Historians continue to debate how deep the seventeenth-century downturn was, in part because evasion of registration makes the evidence ambiguous.

Division II — Mining Technology, Mercury, and Production · Section 42.5

Inside the Mines: Ore, Shafts, Extraction, and Transport

Mining began with the search for veins. Prospectors looked for outcrops, followed traces of ore along the surface, and tested promising ground, and in many districts Indigenous knowledge of local geology guided Spanish claims. A discovery had to be registered with the authorities, who measured out claims under royal mining ordinances. Claim holders were obliged to work their holdings, because abandoned or unworked claims could be denounced and taken by others. The legal framework of mining thus encouraged continuous exploitation and made the speed of development a competitive matter.

Underground work followed the vein. Miners cut shafts and galleries into the rock, using iron bars, picks, and wedges, and in some places fire to crack the stone. Timber was used to support galleries where it was available, though in places such as Potosí it was scarce, and miners often left pillars of ore and rock in place, with consequences for the stability of the workings. Light came from candles, which workers carried or fixed to the walls, and air circulation was poor. At Potosí, the mine entrances lay high on the mountain, and the workings extended down and inward, where heat, dust, and dampness added to the dangers of rockfalls.

Getting the ore out was heavy labor and was often done by carriers. In the Andes, workers climbed notched ladders, often made of braided rawhide, with loads of ore in bags on their backs. In New Spain, carriers called tenateros, after the baskets or bags they used, performed similar work, and animal-powered hoists called malacates, turned by horses or mules, were used to lift ore and to raise water as shafts deepened. Drainage became a growing problem at Zacatecas and in other districts as the workings reached the water table, because flooded galleries had to be pumped or bailed, and the cost of drainage was one of the pressures that pushed mining toward the more marginal ores in the seventeenth century.

The workforce was differentiated. Miners proper, who cut ore at the face, were often the most skilled and, in many districts, the best paid. Carriers moved the ore to the surface, and surface workers sorted, broke, and prepared it. Further processing required still other workers: those who crushed ore in mills, those who prepared and mixed the mercury and the ore, those who operated the furnaces, and the specialists who tested and assayed the final metal. Women worked as sorters and as scavengers of discarded ore, and the Andean term palliri refers to the women who picked through waste rock for pieces that were still worth refining. Distinguishing among these tasks is important, because wages, status, and risk differed widely among them, and because the term "miner" in a colonial source can cover very different kinds of work.

The evidence for underground work is limited. Surviving mining ordinances and owners' accounts describe quantities and costs, and visitors and officials left descriptions of conditions, but workers' own accounts are rare. Petitions and legal testimony by Indigenous workers and their leaders occasionally describe the burdens of the work, and archaeological and physical evidence from the mountains, including the extensive tunnel systems, supplements the documents. Historians therefore reconstruct daily labor largely from indirect sources, and the contours of work are clearer than the experience of the people who performed it.

Section 42.6

Indigenous Metallurgy and the Persistence of Smelting

Spanish writers often described the first years of Potosí as a period of astonishing riches, and the technology behind them was largely Andean. Indigenous metallurgists had long smelted silver and other metals, and in the 1540s and 1550s they continued to do so, using small furnaces that took advantage of the strong winds of the highlands. These furnaces, called huayras (a Quechua word meaning "wind"), were placed on hillsides and exposed to the wind, which supplied the draft needed to reach smelting temperatures. Spanish accounts record thousands of such furnaces dotting the slopes around Potosí at night, and their glow became part of the lore of the city.

Historical Vocabulary
Huayra

A huayra (also spelled guayra) was a small wind-drafted furnace used by Andean metalworkers to smelt silver ore. Spanish writers adopted the Quechua word. The furnaces were often built of clay and placed on exposed slopes where the wind drove the fire. Ore was heated with fuel and, depending on the ore, a flux, and the molten metal was separated from the residue. The word is related to smelting, the refining of metal by melting, as distinct from amalgamation, which uses mercury.

The huayra matters because it shows that the early Spanish silver boom rested on Indigenous technology and Indigenous specialists. It also warns against the idea that Europeans introduced "modern" refining to a primitive region. The fuel the furnaces used, which included wood, the grass called ichu, and llama dung, shaped local economies, and the process worked best on rich ores, which is why it declined in importance as those ores were exhausted.

The people who operated the huayras were Indigenous workers and entrepreneurs, some working under Spaniards and some working for themselves. In the early years of Potosí, many Indigenous workers delivered ore to Spanish owners and shared the proceeds under informal arrangements, and Indigenous smelters sold metal or kept a portion. These arrangements show that Indigenous people took part in the early mining economy as skilled specialists with bargaining power, not solely as drafted laborers. Their knowledge, accumulated over generations, gave them a position that Spanish owners had to respect, at least while the rich surface ores lasted.

Smelting had limits. It worked well on rich ores, but as the best veins were exhausted, the remaining ores were poorer, and smelting them consumed large amounts of fuel for little metal. In the Andes, where wood was scarce, this was a serious constraint. The introduction of amalgamation changed the economics, but did not eliminate smelting. Rich ores continued to be smelted in many places, and some districts used both methods, depending on the ore. Mercury amalgamation also required mercury, which was costly and, in some seasons or regions, scarce. Where mercury was difficult to obtain, smelting remained a necessary alternative. The history of refining, in short, is a history of coexisting methods rather than a clean replacement of one by another.

Indigenous metallurgical practice also left a mark on Spanish terminology and technique. Spanish treatises, including those of the seventeenth century, borrowed Quechua and Aymara terms for ores and processes, and the experience of Indigenous workers was taken for granted in the practices of refiners. Yet such borrowing did not make Indigenous specialists visible in the sources as individuals, and the credit for technical advances went, in the written record, mostly to Spaniards. Students should read treatises with this in mind.

Section 42.7

The Mercury Revolution: The Patio Process and Silver Amalgamation

Mercury amalgamation was not new to the world. Methods for extracting gold and silver with mercury were known in Europe and elsewhere. What was new in the mid-sixteenth century was its adaptation to American ores on an industrial scale. In New Spain, a Sevillian merchant named Bartolomé de Medina, working in the mining district of Pachuca in the mid-1550s, developed a procedure that came to be called the patio process, after the open-air courtyards in which it was carried out. Medina's method is conventionally dated to about 1555, and it spread rapidly through New Spain's mining districts.

The patio process worked in several steps. Ore was first crushed and ground to a fine powder in mills, often powered by animals in New Spain and by water in the Andes. The powder was mixed with water to form a sludge, which was spread in a courtyard. Salt and a copper-bearing material, in the Spanish sources called magistral, were added, along with mercury, and the mixture was worked for days or weeks, often by animals or workers trampling it. The mercury combined with the silver to form an amalgam. The amalgam was then washed out of the sludge, pressed to remove excess mercury, and heated so the mercury evaporated, leaving a spongy mass of silver. Chemically the process was complex, and sixteenth-century practitioners worked by trial and observation rather than from a theory of what was occurring.

The great advantage of amalgamation was that it could recover silver from ores too poor to smelt profitably. It required less fuel than smelting, which mattered in fuel-scarce regions, and it could process large volumes of ore. Its disadvantages were its dependence on mercury, some of which was lost in every operation, its slowness, and the heavy labor of grinding and mixing. In the cold climate of Potosí, chemical reactions proceeded more slowly than in the warmer Mexican courtyards, and refiners experimented with adaptations, including the use of tanks or vats and different timings. Amalgamation was introduced at Potosí in the early 1570s, and Andean refiners adapted the method to local conditions, so the method that became standard there was not an exact copy of Medina's procedure.

The consequences were large. By making low-grade ores profitable, amalgamation expanded the supply of exploitable ore and extended the life of mining districts. It also increased the demand for workers to extract and carry larger volumes of ore and for those to operate mills and refining yards. And it made the mercury supply a central strategic variable. Silver production could now rise or fall depending on how much mercury was available and at what price, and the Crown's control over mercury gave it influence over the whole industry.

Section 42.8

Huancavelica, Almadén, and the Imperial Mercury Supply

Mercury, the only metal that is liquid at ordinary temperatures, occurs in a limited number of deposits, and few of them were accessible to the Spanish monarchy. The most important was Almadén, in the region of La Mancha in Spain, which had been exploited since antiquity. In the sixteenth century, the Crown contracted the administration of Almadén to the German banking house of the Fuggers, an arrangement that lasted, with changes, well into the seventeenth century and linked the financing of Spanish silver production to international finance. Mercury from Almadén was shipped to New Spain, and mercury from other European sources, notably Idrija in the Habsburg lands, was also imported in later years.

In the Andes, a regional supply became available in the 1560s. Mercury was identified at Huancavelica, in the central Peruvian highlands, in 1563 according to the traditional account, although some sources give 1564, which credits a Spaniard, Amador de Cabrera, with the discovery after Indigenous informants showed him cinnabar, the mercury ore, that local people had long used as a pigment. The discrepancy in the year is a reminder that the date records Spanish registration and narrative, while Indigenous knowledge of the deposit was older. The Crown quickly claimed the mines as royal property and organized their exploitation, and mercury production at Huancavelica grew through the 1570s. The availability of local mercury made amalgamation practical at Potosí and across the Andes, as shipping from Spain would have been slower and costlier.

The mercury supply was a royal monopoly. The Crown controlled production at Huancavelica and Almadén and sold mercury to refiners at fixed prices, often on credit. This arrangement served several purposes. It generated revenue, it gave the Crown a record of how much silver was being refined, since mercury consumption was tied to production, and it gave officials leverage over the owners. It also created friction, because refiners accumulated debts to the treasury, and because shortages and price changes affected output directly. When mercury was scarce, refiners could not process ore, and production fell. When the Crown lowered the price, as it sometimes did to encourage output, production could rise.

Huancavelica was dangerous. Workers in the mercury mines faced the toxic effects of mercury vapor and dust, cave-ins, and difficult conditions at high altitude. Indigenous workers drafted for the mercury workings served under a labor draft similar to the one at Potosí, and Spanish officials in the seventeenth century debated whether the human costs justified the workings. The environmental and medical consequences, discussed in Section 42.18, were severe and lasting. The silver of Potosí therefore rested on two mountains, one of silver and one of mercury, and the labor and lives consumed at the second were largely invisible in accounts of the first.

The transportation of mercury linked distant regions. Mercury for New Spain crossed the Atlantic with the fleets, and was distributed by the Crown through its treasury offices. Andean mercury moved from the highlands to the refining centers by mule and llama train. These routes required substantial organization and made the mercury supply vulnerable to disruption by war, shipwreck, or accident. The Crown's dependence on a handful of mines, and the miners' dependence on the Crown, was one of the structural features of Spanish colonial silver.

Section 42.9

Water, Mills, Fuel, Animals, and Mining Infrastructure

Behind the extraction of ore stood an infrastructure of astonishing scale. Ore had to be crushed before refining, and the crushing was done in mills called ingenios. In Potosí, these were powered by water, and the water came from a system of reservoirs and canals constructed in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries along the stream known as the Ribera. A series of dams impounded rainwater and meltwater in artificial lakes high above the city, and the water was released through channels to turn wheels that drove stamps. Dozens of mills lined the Ribera at the height of the boom. The works required engineering knowledge, large investments, and the labor of many workers, including drafted Indigenous laborers, to build and maintain.

The infrastructure was fragile. In 1626, according to the sources, the failure of a dam in the reservoir system released a flood on the mills and neighborhoods below, causing great damage and loss of life. Estimates of casualties from the flood vary, and the account in the Spanish sources reflects the impressions of observers. The event illustrates how heavily the industry depended on structures whose failure could devastate both people and property, and it shows that engineering decisions had social consequences.

In New Spain, where water was often scarcer, many mills were powered by animals, and the courtyards of the patio process were worked by horses or mules. The need for animals was constant: mules and horses turned the hoists, ground the ore, trampled the sludge, and carried ore, fuel, and mercury. Herds of mules were raised and driven from distant regions, and the mule trade became a major commercial activity linked to mining. In the Andes, llamas carried ore, supplies, and silver over the highland trails, as they had before the Spanish, and Spaniards adopted and extended Andean pack-transport systems. Chapter 43 will take up the commercial networks that supplied these animals.

Fuel was another demand. Smelting consumed wood or charcoal, and the processing of mercury required fuel to evaporate it from the amalgam. In regions such as the high Andes, where trees were rare, fuel came from ichu grass, shrubs, and dung, and the search for fuel stripped surrounding landscapes. In the more wooded parts of New Spain, forests near mining centers were cut for fuel and for mine timbers, and the demand for wood extended steadily outward as nearby supplies were exhausted. The environmental consequences are taken up in Section 42.18.

These requirements meant that mining depended on large capital investment. A refining operation required land, mills, water rights, animals, buildings, tools, and the working capital to buy mercury and pay workers while waiting for silver to be produced. Few miners could afford such investments from their own resources, which is why credit, partnerships, and merchant financing, discussed in Section 42.15, were essential. The technology of silver production was therefore inseparable from its economics, and the capacity to organize infrastructure was one of the forms of power in colonial mining societies.

Division III — Labor, Coercion, Wages, and Mining Communities · Section 42.10

Francisco de Toledo and the Colonial Mining Mit'a

When Francisco de Toledo arrived in Peru as viceroy in 1569, the Andean mining economy was in difficulty. The richest surface ores of Potosí were thinning, the Indigenous smelters who had supplied much of the early output were less able to meet demand, and the Crown wanted both greater revenue and greater control over Indigenous labor and tribute. Toledo, who governed until 1581, undertook a sweeping reorganization of the Andean colony. He toured the highlands, ordered a general census of Indigenous communities, resettled many communities into planned towns called reducciones, reorganized tribute, and, in connection with these measures, regularized a system of labor drafts for the mines and mercury workings.

Historical Vocabulary
Mit'a, Mitayo, and Minga

In Quechua, mit'a means a turn, and in the Inca state it referred to rotating labor service owed by households to the state, performed for tasks such as road building, agriculture, military service, and work on state projects, within a system of reciprocity and redistribution organized through the ayllu and its leaders. In the colonial period, Spaniards used the word mita for the rotating labor draft that required designated Indigenous communities to send a share of their adult men to work in mines, mills, and other enterprises. A worker serving under this system was a mitayo. A minga (from Quechua mink'a) referred in colonial mining to hired labor, a worker who agreed to work for a wage, sometimes a mitayo who stayed on after his term, sometimes a migrant.

The distinction matters because the colonial mita borrowed a name and some mechanics from Inca practice but served a different purpose: supplying labor to private Spanish mine owners and refiners under state authority, at wages set by officials and typically below market rates. Treating the two as the same institution would obscure what Toledo changed. The relationship between mitayos and mingas also shows that coerced and paid labor existed side by side in the same mines.

The mining draft was geographically specific. It applied to provinces in the highlands of Upper Peru and southern Peru that were designated to supply workers to Potosí, and a separate draft supplied Huancavelica. In the principal arrangement, a fixed share of eligible adult male tributaries in the designated provinces, commonly described as about one seventh, was to serve in rotation, so that each man worked for a period and then was relieved. The system grew in stages. Research by Carmen Salazar-Soler and others gives nominal annual draft contingents for Potosí of about 9,500 men in 1573, about 11,500 in 1575, and about 14,200 in 1578, so the figure of roughly fourteen thousand belongs to the system's expansion by the end of the 1570s, not to its first arrangement. These were quotas of men owed each year, not the number working underground at any one moment: service was divided into rotating groups, so the number actually present varied. The percentage of tributaries required also differed from province to province, which is why "about one seventh" is best read as a rough average, and the affected provinces, the obligation, and the length and frequency of service changed over time. Other scholars count the labor force differently, and students should treat any single figure as approximate.

The system depended on Indigenous community leaders. Kurakas, the heads of ayllus and provinces, were responsible for assembling the contingent of workers, delivering them to Potosí, and often for ensuring that tribute was paid. This gave kurakas a role that was both an extension of their precolonial authority and a new burden, since failure to deliver workers could bring penalties. Some used the position to protect their communities, some to enrich themselves, and some were punished for resistance. Officials, for their part, were supposed to enforce the draft, regulate wages and hours, and limit abuses, but enforcement was uneven.

Mitayos received wages, which were fixed below the rate for hired workers, and workers faced the costs of long journeys, of food and shelter at Potosí, and of the time away from their fields and families. Journeys from distant provinces could take weeks. Some mitayos traveled with wives and children who helped them survive, and some did not return. The burdens fell on communities as a whole, because the loss of men affected agriculture, herding, and household production, and because tribute continued to be demanded regardless of the number of workers away.

The draft was a state-enforced coercive institution, and Spanish contemporaries debated it. Some clerics and officials denounced it as destructive to Indigenous communities, and Indigenous leaders sent petitions seeking reductions or exemptions. Defenders argued that mining was essential to the monarchy and to the colony and that the draft was lawful and regulated. Toledo's reforms were thus part of a larger project, discussed in Chapter 40, of building a colonial state capable of directing Indigenous labor and tribute. The mita continued, in modified form, into the eighteenth century and beyond the chronological scope of this chapter.

Section 42.11

Compulsory Labor, Paid Workers, and Indigenous Enterprise

It would be a mistake to think of Potosí's labor force as consisting only of mitayos. Historians who have studied the city's records have shown that mingas, or hired workers, made up a large part of the mining workforce and that, at some times and in some tasks, they may have outnumbered mitayos. Estimates differ, and the proportions changed over time, but the central finding is that draft labor and wage labor operated together in the same mines. The mitayos supplied a base of workers, while mingas and others supplied additional labor, particularly skilled labor, for which owners were prepared to pay more.

Mitayos and mingas were not rigidly separate. A mitayo whose term had ended might stay at Potosí as a hired worker, and mitayos sometimes worked extra days at higher wages. Some mitayos paid owners a sum of money to be excused from service, and owners then hired workers with it, a practice that turned a labor obligation into a cash payment. Mine owners had an incentive to prefer such payments, because the cash allowed them to hire experienced workers rather than rely on rotating draftees. This monetization shows how colonial labor institutions were adapted by the people operating them.

Skilled and independent workers occupied important places. Experienced miners, who understood how to follow veins and to work safely, commanded higher wages and sometimes a share of the ore they extracted. Some workers, called in the Andes kajchas (also written capchas), entered the mines on days when owners were not working, particularly on weekends, and extracted ore for themselves, sometimes with the tacit agreement of owners, sometimes against their wishes. They sold the ore to refiners, and the practice shows that some Indigenous workers were able to claim a share of the metal. Indigenous refiners and smelters also worked independently, and some Indigenous people operated small enterprises that supplied the city with food, coca, and textiles.

Paid labor, however, was not automatically free. Indigenous workers entered the market in conditions shaped by tribute demands, by the pressure of the mita, and by debt, and the options available to them were constrained by colonial law and power. A worker who accepted wages to avoid the draft, or who stayed in Potosí because home communities could not support him after the loss of land, was making a choice, but one under pressure. Historians therefore describe the labor system not as a division between slaves and free workers but as a spectrum of arrangements that included legal compulsion, economic pressure, and negotiated agreement.

The coexistence of coerced and paid labor also meant that mining wages operated in a market that employers tried to control. Officials set wage rates for mitayos, owners tried to keep wages low, and workers moved to better opportunities where they could. The result was a labor market in which bargaining power varied sharply by skill, legal category, and local conditions. This helps explain why the picture of Potosí in the sources can appear contradictory: both coercion and opportunity were real, and different people encountered them in different proportions.

Section 42.12

Mining Labor in New Spain: Migration, Wages, and Frontier Society

Labor in the mines of New Spain developed differently. In the early years of mining in central Mexico, mine owners relied on a mix of Indigenous labor drafts, enslaved people, and hired workers. Rotating drafts, called repartimiento, supplied workers to some mines in the central region, though the draft was less extensive and less central than in the Andes and it declined through the seventeenth century. At Zacatecas and the northern mines, a draft system was impractical because there were few Indigenous communities nearby that could be drafted, and in any case the Crown had limited authority over an unsettled frontier. Employers therefore had to attract workers, and they did so principally through wages and the opportunities that the mining town offered.

Migrant workers from central and western Mexico came voluntarily in large numbers, though their reasons varied: escape from tribute demands, the loss of land, the effects of epidemic disease on home communities, and the prospect of earning cash. In the mining town they could earn wages, which were often higher than those available in agriculture, and they could sometimes obtain a share of the ore. Indigenous migrant neighborhoods grew up around Zacatecas, with their own chapels, confraternities, and leaders, and these neighborhoods provided a base from which workers could negotiate with employers. Workers with skills, who could move to other districts, had particular bargaining power, and employers complained of the difficulty of retaining them.

Coercion nonetheless existed. Enslaved Africans and Indigenous captives worked in the northern mines in the early period, free workers were sometimes bound by debt advances, and owners used a variety of means to keep workers from leaving. Frontier violence, too, shaped labor: captives taken in the Chichimeca War could be enslaved, until the Crown restricted the practice. The labor system at Zacatecas was therefore a market in which coercion and compulsion played a part but did not take the form of a state-run draft like the Andean mita. Historians should not import the Potosí model wholesale into northern Mexico, nor should they imagine Zacatecas as a world of free and equal contracts.

Wage labor also influenced social structure. Mining towns in New Spain developed a mixed population of Indigenous people, Spaniards, Africans, and people of mixed ancestry, in which racial categories and legal status mattered but were also fluid. Mining wages enabled some workers to accumulate savings and to participate in local religious and social life, though the work was dangerous and the high mobility of the population meant that communities were fragile. The experience of Zacatecas shows that mining could create new forms of urban Indigenous life, not only extraction and loss.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Coercion, Wage Labor, and the Organization of Colonial Mining

The question: How should historians characterize the labor system of colonial silver mining: as fundamentally a system of coerced Indigenous labor, as a system in which wage labor and market forces were more important than formal compulsion, or as something that cannot be captured by that contrast?

Interpretation A — Coercion as the foundation: Some historians stress that the colonial state organized mining by compelling Indigenous communities to supply workers, and that the profitability of Andean mining depended on drafts at below-market wages. On this view, the mita was central to the mining economy, imposed severe costs on communities, and shaped the structure of Andean society. Even wage labor, in this interpretation, was shaped by tribute demands and pressure.
Interpretation B — The importance of wage and market labor: Other historians, relying on detailed study of mining records, emphasize that paid labor, including skilled and independent workers, made up a large proportion of the workforce at Potosí and was dominant at Zacatecas. On this view, mining was a labor market in which workers could bargain, move, and benefit, and the formal draft, though real, supplied only part of the workforce. Indigenous workers, in this account, were economic actors responding to opportunities.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same kinds of sources: viceregal and royal decrees, census and tribute records, owners' accounts, wage records, legal petitions, and visit reports. The interpretations differ in how they weigh the formal institution against actual practice and in how they count workers, because definitions of the labor force vary and the records are incomplete. Many scholars now combine the two, arguing that coerced and paid labor were interdependent: the draft depressed wages and pushed communities into the cash economy, while the paid labor market allowed owners to adapt.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians understand Indigenous agency, the costs of the mining system, and the character of colonial power. If coercion was central, mining appears as an extractive system built on compulsion. If market labor was central, Indigenous workers appear as participants with choices. A combined view stresses that choices were made within constraints and that neither coercion nor opportunity alone describes the system.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, and then propose a combined account that explains why the two kinds of labor existed side by side. Identify one kind of record that could help determine the proportions.

Section 42.13

Indigenous Communities, Tribute, Migration, and Social Costs

The mining economy reached deep into Indigenous communities that never saw a mine. In the Andes, the mita drew on provinces across the highlands, sometimes hundreds of kilometers from Potosí, and tribute demands applied to those who remained. Communities responded in different ways. Some sent mitayos as required and sought to support them with food and supplies. Others negotiated with kurakas and officials for reductions in the number of men sent, or sought exemption on the basis of earlier service, poverty, or the distance involved. Some paid substitution money so that owners could hire replacements. A significant number of Andean people moved away from their home communities altogether and resettled as outsiders on other lands or in towns, where they were not subject to the draft, a pattern that reduced the population on which the system depended and that contributed to its difficulties.

Households made decisions that shaped community life. A family might send a son to serve in place of a father, or decide that a man would remain at Potosí as a wage worker because earnings there were better than at home. Women took on the work of agriculture and herding in the men's absence, and in some cases accompanied their husbands to Potosí and worked there, as traders, food sellers, ore gatherers, or domestic workers. Children were part of this movement. The consequences included changes in household composition, in the balance of work between men and women, and in the continuity of community institutions, as well as high mortality among those exposed to the cold, hard labor, and disease of the mining centers.

In New Spain, the effects were of a different kind. Indigenous communities in central and western Mexico lost population to the mining north, partly because workers migrated voluntarily, and partly because epidemic disease, discussed in Chapter 29, had already weakened them. Migrants sent money or goods home in some cases, and in others severed ties. Leaders in the receiving towns, who had to organize the settlement of migrants, took on new roles. The tribute demands on Indigenous people in New Spain were generally paid in goods or cash through the community, and the ability to earn wages in the mines could help meet them, which is one reason some people chose to go.

Indigenous leaders and communities used the colonial legal system. Kurakas and communities sent petitions to the viceroy, to the audiencia, and to the Crown, requesting reductions in the mita, protection against abuse, and recognition of their rights, and some pursued litigation for years. These petitions were written within the forms and language of colonial law, and they were shaped by the assumption that the Crown was a potential protector. Their results were mixed. Some won reductions or protections that were not always enforced, and others were rebuffed. The petitions are among the best sources for how Indigenous communities perceived the burdens of mining, and they show that communities understood and tried to use the system rather than only endure it.

The social costs of mining for Indigenous communities cannot be summarized in a single figure. Demographic decline in the sixteenth century was driven overwhelmingly by epidemic disease and the disruptions of conquest, and historians debate how much the mita added to it. What the evidence does show is that mining drafts and mining migration disrupted agricultural cycles, divided families, concentrated disease risk, and placed heavy burdens on community leaders. It also shows that Indigenous people responded with a repertoire of strategies, including negotiation, litigation, flight, substitution, and adaptation, that shaped the system as much as the colonial authorities who designed it.

Section 42.14

Mining Towns: Families, Women, Enslaved Workers, and Everyday Life

Mining towns were crowded, noisy, and unequal places. Potosí in particular grew into a sprawling city in which the wealthy lived in houses of stone and adobe near the central plaza, while workers lived in neighborhoods on the edges, often in small dwellings that gave little protection against the cold. Indigenous workers lived in districts that came to be associated with the communities they came from, and the city's population turned over rapidly as mitayos rotated in and out. Zacatecas, with a more settled workforce, developed neighborhoods around its mines and churches, in which Indigenous, Spanish, African, and mixed populations lived in close proximity and shared markets and religious festivals.

Mining towns depended on a supply economy that employed many people who never entered a mine. Food had to be grown or herded and carried in from the countryside. Markets sold maize, wheat, potatoes, meat, coca, and cloth, and the city's taverns served chicha and wine. Artisans, including tailors, carpenters, blacksmiths, and silversmiths, worked in the town, and servants, porters, and muleteers supplied its daily needs. Many of the traders and food sellers were Indigenous women, who adapted Andean and Mesoamerican market traditions to the demands of a rapidly growing city, and some accumulated property and entered litigation to protect it. Historians have used notarial records, wills, and court cases to trace such women's activities, which official reports of mining output do not capture.

Women's roles in the mining economy were varied. Indigenous women sorted and gathered ore, ground grain, cooked, washed, and carried water for households and for the mining labor force. Some worked as palliris, gleaning ore from discarded rock, and others sold food or produced cloth. Spanish and creole women, too, appeared in mining communities as household heads, as owners of property, and, in some cases, as owners of mines and mills inherited from husbands or fathers. Marriage, inheritance, and widowhood shaped these opportunities, and the law of the Spanish monarchy gave women rights to property that they could and did defend in court.

Enslaved people were part of the picture, though their role varied by region. In Potosí, enslaved Africans worked in the city as domestic servants, artisans, and in some cases in the minting of coins, but the cold altitude and the availability of Indigenous labor meant that they were not the principal mining workforce. In New Spain, enslaved Africans and people of African descent worked in some mines and in the refining yards, and free and enslaved Black and mixed-ancestry workers formed part of the mining labor force, especially in northern districts. Chapter 44 treats slavery and Afro-Latin America in greater depth. Here the point is that mining towns were multiethnic communities in which legal status and ancestry shaped, but did not entirely determine, a person's place.

The experience of the wealthy and the poor in these towns diverged sharply. Successful mine owners and refiners built churches, endowed religious institutions, and sponsored festivals, and some returned to Spain with their fortunes. Skilled workers earned enough to live reasonably, while drafted laborers and their families faced hunger, cold, and disease. Religious life offered some common ground, in the form of confraternities that provided mutual aid, burial services, and sociability, and in festivals in which Indigenous practice and Catholic ritual combined. The town was thus a place of both stark inequality and complex social mixing.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household in a Colonial Mining Community

The sources do not allow historians to follow a single named household in detail, but they allow a composite picture of the kinds of arrangements that existed. A drafted Andean worker arriving at Potosí for a term of service might bring his wife and children, particularly if the distance was great and he expected to be away for months. They would find shelter in a small dwelling in a district settled by people from their province, and the household's survival would depend on pooling resources. His wage was low, so the wife might sell food, prepare chicha, or sort discarded ore, and children might help with carrying or errands.

In a New Spanish mining town, a migrant household might be organized differently. A Purépecha or Nahua worker living in an Indigenous neighborhood of Zacatecas might earn wages sufficient to support a family, and his wife might sell goods in the market or work for a Spanish household. They might participate in a confraternity of their community, which would provide a sense of belonging, support in sickness, and a burial. The household might maintain ties to a village of origin through visits and the exchange of goods.

These households faced common risks. Mercury and mine dust made workers ill, accidents killed or maimed them, and epidemics moved quickly through crowded neighborhoods. The death or disability of a wage-earner could leave a family destitute, and the records of confraternities, wills, and court cases show people trying to protect themselves with mutual aid and by litigation over debts, inheritance, and property. The silences in the sources about the lives of children, the elderly, and the sick are themselves evidence of whose lives were recorded in the mining archive and whose were not.

Division IV — Mining Capital, Imperial Finance, and Global Consequences · Section 42.15

Mine Owners, Refiners, Merchants, and Mining Entrepreneurship

Silver mining was a business as well as an extractive activity, and it was a risky one. A miner who registered a claim might find rich ore or a barren vein, and the cost of reaching the ore could exceed its value. The refiner needed a mill, water, animals, and a steady supply of mercury, and he had to pay workers weeks or months before the silver was sold. Costs rose as veins deepened and as ores became poorer, and the cost of mercury and labor could change with royal policy or with shortages. Success could bring great wealth, but failure was common, and many mine owners died in debt.

Capital came from several sources. Some miners and refiners invested their own resources, accumulated from earlier ventures, from commerce, or from encomienda income. Many relied on credit from merchants, who advanced goods, mercury, or cash against future silver. Partnerships, in which several investors shared the costs and the proceeds of a mine or mill, were common. The Crown also extended credit in the form of mercury sold on account, which created debts that were sometimes forgiven or deferred. These financial relationships connected mining to commerce, and the merchants who supplied and financed mining became among the wealthiest people in colonial society.

The division of labor in the business was also differentiated. Miners worked the veins, and refiners, called azogueros in the Andes, processed the ore, though one person might do both. Some owners lived in the mining town and managed their operations directly, while others lived elsewhere and relied on administrators. The investor class included Spanish and creole families, merchants who had moved into mining, and, increasingly, clerical and religious institutions that held mortgages on mines and mills. The concentration of wealth in a few hands produced the conspicuous display and the political influence that contemporaries noted.

Indigenous people participated as entrepreneurs, though under very different conditions. In the early years at Potosí, Indigenous smelters worked on their own account and sold metal, and later some Indigenous people owned or leased small mills, sold ore, or supplied goods and services. Kajchas and independent ore sellers took a share of the production. In New Spain, Indigenous people took part in mining and commerce on a smaller scale, and some Indigenous leaders and communities held property in mining towns. These opportunities were constrained by legal inequality, limited access to credit, and the preference of officials for Spanish owners, and the scale of Indigenous enterprise was small compared with that of the large Spanish refiners. The evidence for it nonetheless shows that Indigenous participation extended well beyond wage labor.

The mining elite's relation to the Crown was one of mutual dependence and tension. Owners needed royal mercury, legal protection of claims, and enforcement of labor drafts, and the Crown needed owners to produce silver. Yet owners resisted taxation, complained of mercury prices and labor shortages, and evaded registration when they could. Officials, for their part, sought to extract more revenue and to supervise production. This negotiation, characteristic of the imperial system described in Chapter 40, shaped the policies of the Crown toward mining throughout the period.

Section 42.16

The Royal Fifth, Mining Taxes, and Imperial Finance

Historical Vocabulary
Quinto Real, Assay, Mint, and Real Hacienda

The quinto real ("royal fifth") was the Crown's claim to one fifth of the metal mined in its American territories. The nominal rate was frequently adjusted, and in many districts and periods the Crown accepted a lower rate, including a tenth, to encourage production, so the effective rate varied by place and time. To be taxed, silver had to be registered, and to be registered it had to be assayed, tested by officials to determine its purity. It was then stamped and, in many cases, brought to a mint (casa de moneda) to be turned into coins, notably the silver peso or "piece of eight," which circulated widely. The Real Hacienda was the royal treasury administration, which operated treasury offices (cajas reales), collected taxes, and ran the mercury monopoly.

These terms matter because they show that the Crown's share of silver depended on a system of registration, inspection, and collection that could be evaded, negotiated, and adjusted. Treasury figures record what officials registered and taxed, not the total silver mined. The royal revenue from mining came not only from the fifth but from the sale of mercury, from fees for assaying and minting, and from other taxes on mining towns.

The process by which silver became revenue began with registration. Producers brought their metal to royal officials, who assayed it, weighed it, calculated the tax due, and stamped the bars. Mints were established to turn bullion into coin, in Mexico City in 1535, in Lima in the 1560s, and in Potosí in the 1570s, and these institutions earned fees and gave the Crown a share of the coinage. Treasury offices in the mining districts and in the viceregal capitals recorded the receipts and forwarded funds according to the needs of the monarchy.

The Crown's income from silver was therefore the sum of several things: the royal fifth or its reduced equivalent, the profits of the mercury monopoly, minting fees, and other duties. The relative importance of these varied, and the mercury monopoly was in some periods as important to revenue as the direct tax on silver. Much of the revenue was spent in the Americas, on administration, defense, and the support of the clergy, and only a portion was remitted to Spain. Historians who study treasury accounts, among them the scholars who compiled and analyzed the surviving records, have shown that the proportion remitted varied over time and that a large share was retained locally.

Distinctions among different quantities are essential. Gross production is the total amount of silver mined, which can only be estimated. Registered production is what officials recorded, and it understates gross production to the extent that metal was smuggled, unregistered, or consumed locally. Private profit is what owners and merchants retained after costs. Taxes collected is what reached the treasury. And silver that reached the monarchy is the part of the revenue that was remitted to Spain and available to meet its obligations. Confusing these quantities leads to exaggerated claims about the amount of American wealth available to the Spanish crown.

The monarchy's finances remained fragile. The Crown borrowed from bankers, mortgaged future revenues, and suspended payments on its debts several times, and American silver, although important, did not prevent these crises. Some of the silver that arrived in Seville was already committed to creditors, and some was seized or taxed by the monarchy upon arrival. The relationship between silver and imperial finance is therefore one of dependence and strain: the monarchy needed silver, but its expenses outran it. Chapter 40 treated the administrative side of this fiscal system, and Chapter 43 will take up the commercial institutions through which the silver traveled.

Section 42.17

From American Mines to the Atlantic and Pacific Worlds

Silver leaving the mines followed several paths. A large part remained in the Americas, as coin and bullion used in local commerce, payments to workers, church donations, and private wealth, and it supported the markets of the mining regions and their hinterlands. Another part moved to Spain as royal revenue and as private remittances from merchants, officials, and migrants. A third part left the Americas by other channels, including contraband trade, notably the illicit flows from the Andes through Buenos Aires and the Río de la Plata, in which Portuguese and other merchants took part and which Chapters 39 and 43 treat in other contexts. And a fourth part crossed the Pacific.

The Atlantic route carried silver from the mining centers to the coast and onto ships. Silver from Potosí was carried by llama and mule to the port of Arica or to other Pacific ports, shipped to Callao, the port of Lima, and sent northward to Panama, from which it crossed the isthmus and was loaded onto fleets for Spain. Mexican silver went to Mexico City and then to Veracruz. In Spain, silver entered the economy of Castile and passed to creditors in Genoa, the Netherlands, and elsewhere, and it circulated through European commerce, financing wars, trade, and the purchase of goods from Asia.

The Pacific route linked New Spain to Asia. After the Spanish established Manila in 1571 and the route across the Pacific was opened in 1565, galleons sailed annually between Acapulco and Manila, carrying American silver westward and Asian goods, notably Chinese silks and porcelain, eastward. Manila served as an entrepôt where Chinese merchants exchanged goods for silver. The silver came principally from Mexico, though Andean silver also reached Acapulco by way of Pacific shipping. Chinese demand for silver was strong, in part because the Ming state had moved toward taxation in silver, and the metal commanded a higher value in China than in Europe, an arbitrage that sustained the trade. Historians such as Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez have emphasized the importance of this connection to early modern globalization, though the scale of the flows and their effects remain debated.

The people who carried out these exchanges included merchants in Seville, Mexico City, Lima, and Manila, Chinese traders who sailed to the Philippines, ship owners and crews, and the officials who regulated the fleets. Latin American actors were not mere suppliers. Merchant guilds in Mexico City and Lima organized commerce, mining entrepreneurs responded to opportunities, and colonial elites used silver to build cities and fund institutions. The detailed commercial structures of fleets, monopolies, and merchant networks belong to Chapter 43, and this section outlines only the paths silver took.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why American Silver Entered Global Exchange Networks

Long-term causes

European and Asian demand for silver as money; the monetization of tax systems, notably in Ming China; the Spanish monarchy's need for revenue; and Indigenous mining and metallurgical knowledge in the Americas.

Short-term causes

The discoveries at Potosí and Zacatecas in the 1540s; the invention and spread of amalgamation; the mercury monopoly; the reorganization of Andean labor in the 1570s; and the opening of the Pacific route.

Event or transformation

Silver production expanded to a scale that supplied currency to Spain, the Atlantic economy, and the Asian trade.

Immediate effects

Rapid growth of mining towns; new markets for food, mules, and goods; royal revenues and credit; and annual flows of silver toward Seville and Manila.

Long-term consequences

Lasting links among the Americas, Europe, and Asia; fiscal dependence of the Spanish monarchy on mining and credit; and environmental and demographic costs borne in mining regions.

Historical complexity

Silver did not by itself cause European prosperity or Spanish decline, and historians debate its effects on prices and trade. It entered global networks because several conditions coincided, and its consequences varied by region.
Section 42.18

The Environmental and Human Costs of Silver Production

Silver production changed landscapes and damaged bodies. The most serious and best-documented hazard was mercury. Every amalgamation operation used mercury, and a considerable fraction was lost to the environment, through spillage, evaporation during the heating of amalgam, and discharge in waste. The mercury mines at Huancavelica released vapor and dust, and refining yards at Potosí and in New Spain released more. Studies of lake sediments, ice cores, and soils in the Andes have detected mercury pollution beginning in the colonial period and have linked it to the operations at Huancavelica and Potosí, and research in the mining regions of Mexico has found comparable signals. The contamination did not end with the colonial period, and it remains detectable centuries later.

The effects on human health were severe, though the surviving documents describe them in the language of the time and do not allow precise statistics. Workers in the mercury mines and refineries suffered tremors, loss of teeth, and mental disturbance, symptoms later recognized as mercury poisoning, and contemporaries described them. Miners underground faced silicosis and other respiratory diseases from dust, along with accidents, cave-ins, and the effects of cold and altitude. Communities downstream from refining yards were exposed through contaminated water. Spanish officials in the seventeenth century debated whether the toll at Huancavelica justified continued operation, and some proposed abolishing the draft there, but the monarchy's need for mercury prevailed.

The environmental effects extended beyond mercury. Fuel demands stripped vegetation from the areas around mining towns, and in the high Andes the harvesting of ichu grass and shrubs altered the surrounding landscape. In New Spain, forests were cut for timber and fuel, and the demand extended farther from the mines over time. Water was diverted to turn mills and to supply towns, and the dams and reservoirs at Potosí changed the hydrology of the area. Mills discharged ground ore and waste into streams. The accumulation of tailings and slag left physical residues that remained in the landscape long afterward. Animals, too, were affected: herds of mules and llamas grazed and exhausted pastures near the towns.

The evidence for these costs comes from different kinds of sources. Colonial documents describe illness and deaths among workers and complaints about fuel and water, but they were not designed to measure environmental damage. Modern research uses lake and ice-core records, soil and sediment analysis, and archaeological investigation to reconstruct pollution histories, and medical research has examined the long-term consequences of exposure. These sources complement each other, but they also leave uncertainties, such as the exact amounts of mercury released and the extent of human mortality attributable to it. Historians therefore rely on ranges and qualified statements.

The unequal distribution of costs deserves emphasis. The wealth that silver generated accrued largely to owners, merchants, the Crown, and the recipients of silver in Europe and Asia, while the burdens of toxic exposure, dangerous labor, community disruption, and landscape damage fell mostly on Indigenous workers, their families, and the communities of the mining regions. This is not to say that Indigenous people gained nothing from mining, since some earned wages and traded profitably, but the balance was heavily tilted. The environmental history of silver is thus inseparable from its social history, and the legacy of contamination is one of the most durable consequences of the silver age.

Section 42.19

Silver Empires by 1700: Wealth, Dependence, and Unequal Consequences

By 1700, the silver economy had been in operation for a century and a half, and its character had changed. In the Andes, production had peaked in the late sixteenth century and declined unevenly afterward. Potosí remained a large and important city, but its richest ores were less abundant, mercury supply and labor drafts were chronic problems, and new districts such as Oruro had risen to share the output. The mit'a continued, although many communities had been depleted by migration and mortality. In New Spain, production had adjusted to the hardships of the early seventeenth century and recovered in some districts in the later seventeenth, laying the basis for the larger expansion that Chapter 48 and later chapters will address.

The contrast between Potosí and Zacatecas remains the key to understanding these developments. Potosí's economy was shaped by altitude, the mit'a, Huancavelica's mercury, and the engineering of the Ribera. Zacatecas depended on migrant labor, wages, the patio process, and imported mercury, and it grew within a frontier society defined by war and settlement. Both produced vast quantities of silver, but they did so under distinct conditions that produced different societies. The Spanish monarchy depended on both, and its dependence shaped its relations with the mining elites, with Indigenous communities, and with the creditors who financed its wars.

Silver also changed the regions around the mines. The need for food, mules, cloth, salt, and tools created commercial networks that linked agricultural regions to mining centers, a process taken up in Chapter 43. It stimulated urban growth, built churches and institutions, and created fortunes. It also created dependence, because regional economies became tied to the fortunes of mining. When veins declined or mercury became scarce, the effects spread across the region.

The global significance of silver is clear in outline, though debated in detail. American silver supplied money to Spain and the Atlantic economy and, through Manila, to the Chinese market, and it helped connect the Americas, Europe, and Asia in a single exchange system. Historians disagree about how much weight to give silver in explaining price changes in Europe, the fortunes of Spain, and Asian economic developments, and this chapter has avoided broad claims about a single cause. What can be said with confidence is that silver was a major component of global exchange from the late sixteenth century, and that it was produced by a system of labor, technology, and state power centered in Latin America.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Colonial Mining from the Sixteenth Century to 1700

What Changed

  • Refining shifted from Indigenous smelting in huayras toward mercury amalgamation, and the mercury supply became a royal monopoly
  • Andean labor moved from a largely market-based early economy toward the Toledo mining draft, then toward a mixed system with substantial wage labor
  • Mining spread from the first centers to many districts, and the paths of Andean and Mexican production diverged
  • Infrastructure of dams, mills, and refining yards grew, and its environmental effects accumulated

What Stayed the Same

  • Indigenous knowledge, labor, and community institutions remained essential to production
  • The Crown depended on mining for revenue and negotiated with owners over taxes, mercury, and labor
  • Benefits flowed disproportionately to owners, merchants, and the monarchy, and costs fell on workers and communities
  • Smelting persisted alongside amalgamation, and mining remained dependent on distant supplies of food, fuel, and animals

Who experienced change most directly: Andean communities subject to the mita and Indigenous migrants to the northern mines experienced the sharpest changes in labor and residence. Greater continuity: Mine owners and royal officials retained a structure of privilege and dependence that changed more slowly. Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that mining was a dynamic system rather than a fixed institution, and that its technical, legal, and social elements changed at different speeds.

The chapter's central question, who benefited, who bore the costs, and how they were distributed across continents, has no single answer. Owners, merchants, and officials profited, as did the monarchy's creditors and the traders of the Pacific and Atlantic worlds. Some Indigenous workers and traders earned wages and profits. But the people who dug the ore, carried it, ground it, handled the mercury, and lived with its consequences were overwhelmingly Indigenous and African-descended workers and their families, and the landscapes of the mining regions bore lasting damage. Chapter 43 now widens the lens from mines to estates, plantations, ports, and cities, examining the agricultural and commercial economies that fed the mining centers and tied Latin America to the early modern global economy.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined colonial silver mining as a system of labor, technology, finance, and environmental change centered on Potosí and Zacatecas. Its argument is that silver production transformed the Spanish monarchy and the early modern world, but that its success depended on Indigenous knowledge, coerced and paid labor, technical innovation, environmental exploitation, private investment, and the Crown's ability to organize extraction and taxation.

Silver mining did not begin with Europeans. Indigenous peoples had mined and smelted metals for centuries, and the Inca had drawn silver from the region around Potosí. Spanish exploitation of the Cerro Rico beginning in 1545 was an expansion and takeover of existing practice rather than a first discovery. Zacatecas, where rich silver was found in 1546, grew in a different setting: a frontier region of dispersed Indigenous communities, the scene of the Chichimeca War, and a destination for migrants from central and western Mexico.

Mining produced distinct regional economies rather than a single colonial mining system. Potosí's altitude, Andean labor traditions, and nearby mercury shaped its development. Zacatecas relied on migration, wages, and imported mercury. Other districts, including Guanajuato, Pachuca, San Luis Potosí, Parral, and Oruro, had their own timetables, and the paths of Andean and Mexican production diverged in the seventeenth century.

Production was a chain of tasks and technologies. Miners cut ore underground, carriers hauled it to the surface, mills crushed it, and refiners separated the silver by smelting or by amalgamation. Indigenous huayras supported the early Andean boom, and smelting persisted alongside mercury processing. The patio process, developed in New Spain around 1555 and adapted to the Andes in the 1570s, made it possible to refine lower-grade ores and expanded the supply of exploitable silver.

Mercury was the strategic variable. Almadén in Spain and Huancavelica in Peru supplied it, and the Crown controlled its sale, which gave the monarchy leverage over the industry and a measure of production. Mercury mining was among the most dangerous work in the colonial economy, and the vapor and dust it generated damaged workers and landscapes.

Labor combined coercion, wages, and independent work. Under Viceroy Toledo, the Andean mit'a, adapted from an Inca institution, became a state-enforced draft supplying Potosí and Huancavelica, with kurakas responsible for assembling contingents. Alongside it, mingas, skilled workers, kajchas, and Indigenous entrepreneurs formed a large paid and semi-independent labor force. At Zacatecas, wage labor and migration predominated, though coercion and enslavement also played a part. Historians debate the proportions, and many now stress the interdependence of coerced and paid labor.

Indigenous communities responded to mining in varied ways. They negotiated, petitioned, litigated, paid substitutes, migrated, and adapted, and women contributed as traders, ore gatherers, household managers, and market sellers. Enslaved Africans and free people of color formed part of the mining towns' populations, with roles that varied by region. The social costs included disrupted agricultural cycles, divided families, disease, and heavy burdens on leaders, even as some people gained wages and opportunities.

Mining depended on capital. Owners, refiners, and merchants invested in mills, water systems, animals, and mercury, relied on credit and partnerships, and negotiated with the Crown over taxes and mercury prices. Indigenous people took part as smelters, ore sellers, and traders on a smaller scale. Royal revenue came from the royal fifth, mercury sales, minting fees, and other duties, and it must be distinguished from gross production, registered production, private profit, and silver remitted to Spain.

Silver reached Spain, the Atlantic economy, and, through Manila, the Chinese market, and it remained in large part in the Americas, circulating in local economies. Historians debate its effects on prices and global trade, but its role in connecting the Americas, Europe, and Asia is well established. The monarchy remained fiscally fragile despite the silver, and its dependence on mining shaped its policies toward owners, Indigenous labor, and mercury.

The environmental costs were substantial and long-lasting. Mercury contamination, fuel demand, water diversion, and landscape alteration left physical traces that remain detectable, and the human costs of toxic exposure and dangerous labor fell mainly on Indigenous and African-descended workers and their communities. By 1700, the silver economy had changed in structure and geography but retained its essential features: dependence on Indigenous knowledge and labor, negotiation between the Crown and mining elites, and the unequal distribution of benefits and burdens. The wealth of the silver empires was real, and so was the price paid by those who produced it.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the silver of Potosí and Zacatecas was the product of a system rather than a discovery. Ore became coin through the interaction of Indigenous knowledge, drafted and hired labor, mercury, mills and dams, merchant credit, and royal taxation. The outcomes were not predetermined: the same mountain, under different technology or labor arrangements, would have produced very different results, and the contrast between Potosí and Zacatecas shows how much local conditions shaped the colonial economy.

The chapter also reveals the structure of colonial power. The Crown depended on silver but could not simply command it, and so it bargained with mine owners, relied on Indigenous leaders to deliver workers, and adapted institutions such as the mit'a to new purposes. Indigenous communities used petitions, litigation, migration, and negotiation to shape the burdens placed on them. These patterns recur through Part IX and through the larger history of colonial governance: authority was real but negotiated, and the people subject to it were active participants.

The consequences extended far beyond the mines. Silver connected the Americas to Europe and Asia, helped finance the Spanish monarchy, and stimulated markets and cities across Spanish America. It also left lasting costs in contaminated landscapes, damaged communities, and unequal distributions of wealth that persisted into the modern period. Later chapters will return to mining in the eighteenth century, in the independence era, and in the export economies of the nineteenth and twentieth, when the question of who benefits from extraction would recur in new forms.

Chapter 43 broadens the picture from mines to the agricultural estates, plantations, ports, and cities that fed mining centers and tied Latin America to the early modern global economy. Students should carry forward the view that colonial economies were interconnected systems in which mining, agriculture, commerce, and labor shaped one another, and that each depended on Indigenous and African-descended workers whose contributions the written record often obscures.
