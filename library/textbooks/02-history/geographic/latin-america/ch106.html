Hugo Chávez: From the 1992 Coup Attempt to the Bolivarian Revolution

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Failed Rebel and the Question of Legitimacy

Between 1992 and 2002 a lieutenant colonel who tried to overthrow an elected president was himself elected president, rewrote the constitution by popular vote, and was briefly removed from office by an unconstitutional seizure of power. Understanding that decade means holding together facts that partisans usually separate: the 1992 rebellion was illegal, the 1998 victory was democratic, the 1999 constitution-making process was popular and contested, and the 2002 coup was unconstitutional.

In the late morning of 4 February 1992, Venezuelan television carried a brief address by a paratrooper lieutenant colonel whose military rebellion had failed within hours. Hugo Chávez Frías asked his remaining comrades to lay down their arms, accepted responsibility for the rising, and said that the objectives had not been achieved “por ahora”—for now. The speech lasted about a minute. By the standards of a failed coup it should have ended a career. Instead it introduced him to a national audience, in a country whose two dominant parties, Acción Democrática and COPEI, had lost much of the public’s confidence.

This chapter asks how that happened and what followed. It traces Chávez from his formation in the Venezuelan army and the clandestine movement he helped build, through two military uprisings in 1992, his imprisonment and release in 1994, and his conversion of a conspiratorial organization into an electoral movement. It follows his victory in the presidential election of 6 December 1998, the referendums and elections of 1999 that produced a new constitution, the political and institutional struggles of 2000 and 2001, and the crisis of 11–14 April 2002, when he was removed from office, replaced by a short-lived interim regime, and restored by a combination of popular mobilization and military realignment.

The chapter’s argument is that Chávez’s rise was produced by several things working together: the economic and political exhaustion of the petroleum-based democracy examined in Chapter 105; a widespread popular repudiation of established parties; a tradition of military nationalism that drew on the image of Simón Bolívar; and a political leader who proved unusually skilled at television, rallies, and the language of national redemption. His government drew real democratic legitimacy from repeated electoral victories and from referendums that changed the country’s legal order. It also concentrated authority in new ways, and it provoked an opposition that grew increasingly suspicious of its intentions and, in part, increasingly willing to abandon constitutional methods.

Two simple stories are rejected here. One treats Chávez as a military autocrat who seized a democracy; the evidence shows that he won competitive elections, whatever one concludes about what followed. The other treats every institutional change after 1998 as an uncomplicated expansion of democracy; the evidence shows that the new institutions also increased presidential power and were created under conditions in which the opposition was weak and divided. A third simplification should also be avoided: the idea that Venezuelan society divided neatly between the poor, who supported Chávez, and the rich, who opposed him. Support and opposition crossed class lines, even though class, race, and neighborhood shaped how people experienced the conflict.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. Chávez’s own speeches, interviews, and the Bolivarian movement’s retrospective history present the origins of the movement in heroic terms and cannot be taken as neutral. Journalists’ biographies, press coverage, television footage, and later memoirs by participants offer rival accounts. Electoral results, published by Venezuela’s electoral authority and verified by outside observers such as the Carter Center, are firmer ground, as are the constitution itself, the Organization of American States’ reports, and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights’ later findings on April 2002. Even these sources have purposes and limits, and the chapter identifies them as they appear.

The chapter’s boundaries matter. It begins where Chapter 105 ended, with the economic crisis and the Caracazo of 1989, and it closes with Chávez’s restoration on 14 April 2002. Petroleum policy, the restructuring of PDVSA, the 2002–2003 oil strike, the social missions, and oil diplomacy belong to Chapter 107, and the wider regional left to Chapter 108. Where those subjects appear here, they appear only to explain why political conflict intensified.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Chávez’s military formation and the origins of the MBR-200, distinguishing documented events from the movement’s retrospective self-history.
  2. Reconstruct the rebellions of 4 February and 27 November 1992, and distinguish the two operations and their leaders.
  3. Explain why a failed coup produced a nationally known political figure without altering the unconstitutional character of the attempt.
  4. Analyze the decline of Acción Democrática and COPEI, using electoral and economic evidence from 1988 to 1998.
  5. Assess the 1998 election as a repudiation of the established parties and not as the completion of the 1992 rebellion.
  6. Trace the 1999 referendum, Constituent Assembly election, and constitutional referendum, distinguishing voter approval from approval among all registered voters.
  7. Compare the 1961 and 1999 constitutions, evaluating both participatory provisions and the concentration of executive authority.
  8. Evaluate concerns about judicial, military, and electoral institutions without treating every new institution as illegitimate or every concern as partisan.
  9. Explain why the 2001 decree-laws and the December 2001 strike deepened political polarization.
  10. Interpret competing evidence about the shootings of 11 April 2002, distinguishing footage, testimony, judicial findings, and political reconstruction.
  11. Distinguish lawful opposition, strikes, military insubordination, and unconstitutional regime change in the events leading to the Carmona decree.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XXIV’s argument about oil, state power, and political conflict in Venezuela.

Historical Context

Chapter 105 described how Venezuela, after the fall of the dictator Marcos Pérez Jiménez in 1958, built a competitive democracy in which Acción Democrática (AD) and the Christian-democratic COPEI alternated in power under the arrangements known as Punto Fijo, financed by petroleum revenue. For three decades the system distributed oil income through state spending, subsidies, patronage, and labor and business organizations tied to the parties. It also brought the armed left’s insurgency of the 1960s to a negotiated end and kept the military out of politics. By Latin American standards, Venezuela’s democracy looked unusually stable.

That stability eroded in the 1980s. Oil prices fell, debt rose, the currency was devalued in 1983, and living standards declined. In February 1989, soon after beginning his second term, President Carlos Andrés Pérez announced an adjustment program negotiated with the International Monetary Fund. Fare increases and price rises set off the riots of 27–28 February 1989, the Caracazo, and the army and police restored order with force. Official figures reported hundreds of deaths and many observers believe the true number was higher. The episode did not only damage the government. It shocked military officers, who had been used against civilians, and it gave critics of the party system a powerful example.

The armed forces had their own history. Since 1958 they had been officially professional and nonpolitical, and the academy’s curriculum, reformed in the early 1970s under the Plan Andrés Bello, included civilian university subjects. Younger officers of the 1970s and 1980s often came from modest provincial families, studied history and social science, and watched civilian politicians with some disdain. Venezuela had experienced military uprisings in the early 1960s, the Carupanazo and Porteñazo, mostly associated with the left, but a decades-long period without a successful coup had encouraged the belief that the military was safely subordinate.

The international setting also mattered. The Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, the market-oriented policies of the so-called Washington Consensus dominated Latin American economics, and Peru’s president Alberto Fujimori suspended the constitution in April 1992, two months after Venezuela’s first uprising. Venezuelans debated their crisis in a region where elected governments were widely accused of corruption and where military and civilian actors were rethinking what democracy meant. Finally, the Venezuelan public carried a strong inherited reverence for Simón Bolívar, whose image appeared in schools, plazas, and currency. A movement that spoke in his name could be understood by nearly everyone, and each side could claim him.

Section 106.1

From Barinas to the Armed Forces: Hugo Chávez and the Origins of the Bolivarian Movement

Hugo Rafael Chávez Frías was born on 28 July 1954 in Sabaneta, a small town in Barinas state, in the western plains (llanos) of Venezuela. His parents were schoolteachers, and he and his older brother Adán spent part of their childhood with their paternal grandmother, Rosa Inés Chávez, in a modest household. Barinas had been a cattle and landowning region, and it was also the setting of nineteenth-century struggles over land and equality that later mattered to him. Chávez later described a childhood of limited means and ambition for baseball. Such accounts come largely from his own recollection and from interviews, and biographers treat them as a mixture of memory and self-presentation. They are best used as evidence of how he wished to be understood.

In 1971 he entered the Venezuelan Military Academy in Caracas. For a young man from the provinces without political connections, the army offered education and upward mobility. The academy’s curriculum, reformed under the Plan Andrés Bello, let cadets study civilian subjects alongside military ones, and Chávez graduated in 1975 as a sub-lieutenant. In subsequent years he served in several units, taught at the academy, and developed contacts with officers who shared his dissatisfaction with corruption and with the way the parties treated the armed forces. He also read widely in Venezuelan history, and encountered Marxist and nationalist thinkers through informal study circles and conversations with civilians on the left, including veterans of the 1960s guerrilla movements.

The ideology that later became known as Bolivarianism drew on three figures whom the movement called the “tree of three roots.” The first was Simón Bolívar, the Liberator, treated here less as a historical person than as a national symbol of unity, independence, and social justice (Chapter 53). The second was Simón Rodríguez, Bolívar’s teacher, a radical educator who argued that republics needed to educate ordinary people. The third was Ezequiel Zamora, a nineteenth-century general of the Federal War (1859–1863) who led peasant forces under the slogan of land and free men. Together these figures allowed officers to present a critique of Venezuela’s existing democracy as a return to national traditions rather than as an import from abroad. The ideas were flexible. Critics of the movement later argued that they were imprecise; supporters argued that the flexibility was an advantage.

The clandestine organization that grew from these circles was first associated with the name Ejército Bolivariano Revolucionario-200 and later the Movimiento Bolivariano Revolucionario-200 (MBR-200), where “200” referred to the bicentennial of Bolívar’s birth in 1983. According to the movement’s own history, Chávez and several fellow officers, among them Felipe Acosta Carles, Jesús Urdaneta Hernández, and Raúl Isaías Baduel, swore an oath under the Samán de Güere, a tree in Aragua state associated with Bolívar, on 17 December 1982. This story became foundational for the movement. Historians note, however, that its details rest chiefly on the participants’ later testimony, and that the group’s membership, size, and organization remained fluid through the 1980s. What is better documented is that, by the late 1980s, a network of officers, mostly captains and majors with a few lieutenant colonels, was discussing a conspiracy against the government.

The grievances they discussed were not obscure. Officers complained of corruption, of the inequality visible in Venezuelan cities, and of what they regarded as their own misuse. The Caracazo of February 1989 intensified these feelings, since many soldiers had been ordered to fire on crowds in their own neighborhoods. At the same time, the opening for military dissent was created by civilians. When citizens, newspapers, and political commentators openly said that the democratic system had failed, officers could believe that a rebellion would be understood as a response to a national crisis. This did not make a coup legal or inevitable. It made one imaginable.

Section 106.2

The February 4, 1992 Coup Attempt against Carlos Andrés Pérez

In the night of 3–4 February 1992, units loyal to the MBR-200 moved against the government of President Carlos Andrés Pérez, who was returning from an international economic forum in Switzerland. The operation, reportedly named for Ezequiel Zamora, involved army units in several cities. Chávez commanded paratroopers from Maracay and directed the effort in Caracas from the Military Museum on a hill overlooking the palace. Other officers, including Francisco Arias Cárdenas in Zulia, Yoel Acosta Chirinos, and Miguel Ortiz Contreras, led units in Maracaibo, Caracas, and elsewhere. The plan was to seize strategic installations and the president, to take control of broadcasting, and to rely on the army’s units and on public support to carry the country.

It failed for several reasons. Pérez escaped an attack on the presidential residence and reached television studios, from which he broadcast a message to the nation that he remained in command. The rebels did not secure control of broadcasting, which meant that their message did not reach the public in the first critical hours. Units that had been expected to join did not, and Defense Minister Fernando Ochoa Antich and loyal commanders mobilized the rest of the army. Casualties were substantial. Official counts reported around a dozen or more soldiers killed, along with civilians, and the totals reported by different sources run to several dozen. In Zulia, Arias Cárdenas’s forces held positions longer, but he too ultimately surrendered.

The legal character of the operation is not ambiguous. It was an armed attempt by serving officers to overthrow a constitutionally elected president. Its subsequent popularity, and the later judgment of many Venezuelans that the rebels’ grievances were legitimate, do not change that. Students should keep two questions apart: whether the attempt was lawful, and whether the political conditions that produced it were serious. The answer to the first is no; the answer to the second is yes. Historians who write about the rebellion sympathetically and those who write about it critically both have to account for both facts.

What transformed a military failure into a political event was the surrender. Negotiating with Ochoa Antich, Chávez agreed to appear on television to urge the remaining rebel units to stand down. He asked for little time. In his address he accepted responsibility and said that the objectives had not been achieved in the capital “por ahora.” The brevity, the acceptance of responsibility, and the open-ended phrase were all striking in a political culture accustomed to evasion. For viewers who were disillusioned with the existing parties, the officer who accepted blame appeared unusually direct. Others, particularly those who had experienced the night’s violence, saw a conspirator who had endangered the constitutional order. Both reactions were real.

Public and political reactions that day were mixed and instructive. Former President Rafael Caldera, speaking to Congress, condemned the use of force but also said that it was difficult to ask people to defend a democracy that did not appear to feed them or protect their dignity. The speech was widely noted at the time, since it acknowledged the legitimacy of grievances while rejecting the method. It also positioned Caldera for his later political comeback. Pérez’s government, for its part, treated the rebellion as a criminal act and arrested the conspirators, including Chávez. Across the country, however, there were expressions of sympathy, from graffiti to casual conversation, that surprised the political establishment.

Historical Vocabulary: Golpe de Estado
Golpe de Estado

A golpe de Estado (coup d’état) is the sudden, unconstitutional removal or displacement of a government by a small group, typically using or threatening force. In Latin American usage the word is often applied to military seizures, but it can also describe civilian-military combinations or irregular removals by legislatures or courts. A rebelión militar is the legal charge for armed rising by military personnel, and Venezuelan courts used it for the 1992 officers.

The term matters here because 1992 and 2002 are both described as coups, and because the movement and its opponents used the words selectively. This chapter applies the term to an armed attempt to overthrow a government, or to an irregular displacement of an elected government by officers and civilians who then abolish its institutions. Using the term for both events does not make them identical in scale, cause, or consequence. Nor does it require assigning the same moral weight to the grievances behind them.

Primary Source Window

Chávez’s Televised Surrender Statement, 4 February 1992

Creator: Hugo Chávez · Date: 4 February 1992 · Audience: rebel units still in the field, the military command, and the national television audience · Type: broadcast address (video and audio recording)

Context: After the failure of the operation in Caracas and Maracay, Defense Minister Ochoa Antich negotiated Chávez’s surrender, and Chávez was permitted to speak briefly on television to ask the remaining rebels to stand down.

Editorial paraphrase (not a transcript): Chávez addresses his comrades, tells them that the objectives set by the movement were not achieved in the capital, asks them to lay down their arms to avoid further bloodshed, and accepts personal responsibility for the rebellion. The one short phrase widely remembered and verifiable from the recording is “por ahora” (“for now”), which he applied to the failure of the objectives.

Analysis: The address is among the most closely studied moments of recent Venezuelan history, and the recording itself is the best evidence for its wording. It shows a defeated rebel who spoke briefly and without elaborate justification. It also shows him presenting the failure as provisional. Historians note that the statement cannot establish what Chávez privately intended, how large his support was, or how viewers across social groups reacted. Opinion polls of the period are limited, and later recollections of what “everyone felt” are shaped by what happened afterward. The statement does not include voices of soldiers and civilians who died or were wounded that night.

Section 106.3

The November 1992 Rebellion, Military Divisions, and the Collapse of Presidential Authority

The second uprising occurred on 27 November 1992, with Chávez already in prison. It should be distinguished from the first, since it was organized by different officers, drew more heavily on the air force and navy, and involved civilian groups. Among its leaders were Rear Admiral Hernán Grüber Odreman and Air Force General Francisco Visconti, as well as junior officers from the MBR-200 and civilians associated with leftist groups. Rebel aircraft attacked targets in Caracas, and rebel units seized a television station in order to broadcast a message that included a recorded video from Chávez. The government regained control after heavy fighting, and the death toll was far higher than in February. Estimates run well over a hundred, including many civilians and many soldiers, and no single figure is universally accepted.

The second rising shows that the first had not been an isolated act. Within the armed forces there were divisions that went beyond a small circle of conspirators, reaching into several branches and ranks. These divisions were not identical to the beliefs of the MBR-200. Some officers were motivated by Bolivarian ideals, others by anger at corruption, institutional rivalries, or a conviction that the government could not survive. After the failure of the second rising many participants fled the country, some to Peru, which gave them asylum. The government was temporarily stronger but politically weaker.

That weakness was demonstrated by the corruption case that ended Pérez’s presidency. The attorney general, Ramón Escovar Salom, brought charges that the president had misused a secret presidential fund. In May 1993 the Supreme Court found that there was a basis for trial and the Senate suspended Pérez from office. In August 1993 Congress declared his absence permanent, and the veteran politician Ramón J. Velásquez served as interim president. Pérez was later convicted of misappropriation, though the details of his sentence and the fairness of the process remained the subject of debate. The sequence is important because it was constitutional. The institutions of the old order removed a president who had survived two coups, even as public trust in them declined.

The consequences for the meaning of 1992 were considerable. A president who survived armed rebellion lost his office by legal process because of a perception of corruption, which seemed to many Venezuelans to confirm one of the rebels’ central claims. This did not make the rebellions lawful. It did change how they would be remembered. In the election of December 1993 the two parties that had governed since 1958 together won less than half the vote, and the winner was Rafael Caldera, a former COPEI founder who ran on a new coalition. The fragmentation persisted through the mid-1990s. The old party system continued to exist formally but no longer commanded the same loyalty.

Section 106.4

Imprisonment, Political Reconsideration, and Chávez’s Release in 1994

Chávez spent about two years in military detention, first in Caracas and then at the prison of San Francisco de Yare, in Miranda state. The conditions were not those of an ordinary prison. Visits were allowed, and he received relatives, civilian activists, writers, and politicians. Participants and supporters later described Yare as a school of political thought. Chávez read and corresponded, and discussed strategy with fellow prisoners and visitors, including people from small left-wing parties and veterans of the 1960s guerrilla movements. The claim that he formulated a complete political program in prison is a later simplification. The evidence suggests an evolving set of ideas, shaped by visitors, by the changing political situation outside, and by his own growing sense that the movement needed civilian allies.

Outside the prison, the movement gained visibility. Relatives, particularly the families of imprisoned officers, and supporters organized demonstrations and distributed materials. Women played an important role in this early support network, as organizers, correspondents, and public advocates for the prisoners, though their names appear less often than those of the officers in the sources. The movement also gained sympathy among sections of the left that had previously been skeptical of the military, and among intellectuals who regarded the rebels as symptoms of a crisis in the political system. At the same time, many Venezuelans, including many on the left, remained wary of a movement led by officers who had attempted a coup.

The political context changed during 1993. The election of Rafael Caldera in December of that year brought to power a politician who had long been associated with democracy and who had publicly distanced himself from his own party. During the campaign Caldera had suggested that the rebels might be treated with leniency. After taking office on 2 February 1994 he moved to resolve the matter. On 26 March 1994, a government decision dismissed the pending proceedings against the leaders of the 1992 uprisings, on condition that they leave the armed forces. Chávez was released from Yare and discharged from the army, after nearly two years of detention, and he walked out as a civilian.

The decision needs careful analysis. Pardoning or releasing a rebel is not the same as endorsing his rebellion, and neither is the same as permitting his participation in electoral politics. Caldera’s government treated the release as a measure of political pacification: it ended a source of tension in the military, recognized the political significance of the rebels, and removed the question from the courts. Critics said it rewarded those who had used force and set a precedent. Supporters said that it reflected a mature democracy. Caldera himself could not have predicted the consequences, and later he is sometimes criticized for making Chávez’s career possible. That criticism is an example of hindsight. At the time, many civilian politicians believed that a former rebel without a party would be a marginal figure.

What the release created was a new political situation. Chávez was a free civilian with a recognizable name, a small organization, and no party structure. His movement had a choice between continuing to prepare for another rising or entering civilian politics, with its elections, coalitions, and compromises. The way he resolved that choice, and the debates it provoked among his supporters, are the subject of the next division.

Section 106.5

The MBR-200, Popular Organizing, and the Turn toward Electoral Politics

After his release, Chávez traveled through Venezuela, speaking in union halls, universities, neighborhood meetings, and small towns, and meeting with local activists, retired officers, and leaders of left-wing parties. He also traveled abroad. In December 1994 he visited Havana, where he gave a speech at the University of Havana and met Fidel Castro. The visit is often described as a turning point, but it should not be overstated: it showed Chávez’s interest in Cuba’s symbolism and its resistance to the United States, and it began a relationship that mattered much more after 1999. In 1994, though, he did not yet govern anything, and his political economy remained vague.

The movement faced a basic strategic question. One position, associated for a time with Chávez himself, held that elections under the existing rules were a trap. Voting would legitimize a system that the Bolivarians held corrupt, and a new order would come from a constituent process, not through the existing parties. This was called abstención activa, active abstention, and the movement used it in the regional elections of 1995, when it urged supporters not to vote. Another position, argued by civilian allies and some officers, held that the 1992 rebellion had created a political audience that would be wasted if the movement refused to compete. These debates were serious, and they divided people who agreed on the diagnosis of the crisis.

The movement’s program in these years was an evolving collection of ideas. A document often called the Agenda Alternativa Bolivariana, published in 1996, criticized the economic reforms of the Caldera government, then adopting an IMF-backed program, and called for a different development model with an active state, a more nationalist economic policy, and a constituent assembly. Economists and other civilians such as Jorge Giordani contributed to such documents. The program should be read as a statement of orientation, not as a blueprint, and historians note that its economic content was less specific than its political critique.

By 1997 the arguments for electoral participation had prevailed. The decisive factors were the evidence that Chávez was personally popular in opinion polls, the approach of the 1998 elections, and the recognition that a party was necessary to register candidates and campaign across the country. The organization created for this purpose was the Movimiento Quinta República, or MVR, whose name evoked the idea of a Fifth Republic after four earlier republics in Venezuelan history. The MVR was formally registered during 1997 and became the chief vehicle of the movement, though the MBR-200 continued to exist as an organization alongside it. Veteran politician Luis Miquilena, a former labor lawyer with long experience in Venezuelan politics, became an important organizer and a bridge to civilian groups.

The new strategy was not a simple abandonment of revolutionary goals. Chávez argued that the elections could be used to convene a constituent assembly that would rewrite the rules, and so, in his view, the electoral road led to the same destination as the insurrectionary one. This fusion of electoral and constitutional ambition is one of the distinctive features of his movement. It allowed supporters who distrusted elections to participate, and it allowed skeptical voters to view the movement as committed to democratic procedure. It also left unanswered the question of what would happen if the constitutional process met resistance from existing institutions, a question that became pressing in 1999.

Section 106.6

The Collapse of Party Loyalty: Caldera, Economic Crisis, and Public Disillusionment

Caldera took office in February 1994, and almost at once Venezuela’s financial system suffered a major crisis. In January 1994 Banco Latino, one of the country’s largest banks, failed, and a wave of failures followed. The government spent very large sums to rescue depositors and the banking system, and the crisis exposed fraud, insider lending, and weak supervision. It damaged the public’s trust in financial and political elites alike. Caldera introduced exchange and price controls in 1994 and then, in 1996, under an IMF-backed program known as Agenda Venezuela, reversed course and adopted market-oriented reforms that included devaluation, price liberalization, and fuel price increases. The economic record of these years was poor. Inflation was extremely high in 1996, real incomes fell, and poverty remained widespread.

The fall in oil prices compounded the problem. By 1998 the price of Venezuelan crude oil had fallen to levels near ten dollars a barrel, the lowest in years, which cut government revenue just as political and social demands were increasing. A reform of the severance-pay system in 1997, negotiated with the labor confederation, was another source of resentment among workers, who saw it as a reduction of protections. Venezuelans who expected the state to share oil wealth saw instead budget cuts, unemployment, and an informal sector growing in the cities. The state’s capacity to meet its promises seemed to have collapsed.

Political loyalty collapsed with it. Acción Democrática and COPEI had been the dominant organizations of Venezuelan life for decades, with branches in neighborhoods, unions, universities, and professional associations. By the 1990s their reputations were damaged by corruption scandals and by a perception that leaders served themselves. Surveys showed declining identification with parties, and voter abstention rose. The 1993 election, in which neither party’s candidate won, was a sign of this change. Yet the change was not uniform. Some voters, especially older ones and those in rural areas, remained loyal to AD or COPEI, and the parties kept control of governorships and local organizations in many regions.

Different social groups experienced the crisis differently. Unionized workers saw the loss of benefits and the weakening of a confederation, the CTV, that had been closely tied to AD. Residents of poor urban neighborhoods, many of whom worked informally, experienced the crisis through inflation, crime, and poor public services. Middle-class voters were hurt by the banking collapse and by stagnating salaries. Business groups were divided: some benefited from market reforms and others from state contracts and protection. Many former military personnel also felt aggrieved. Rejecting the old parties therefore did not mean that Venezuelans agreed on what should replace them. Some wanted a stronger state, others a more market-oriented one, others honest versions of the old system, and others a radical break.

The crisis was experienced very differently by savers, wage earners, and business owners. Bank interventions and public guarantees were meant to restore confidence but required large transfers of resources at a time of fiscal weakness. Inflation eroded salaries and the value of savings; recurring devaluations made imported goods more expensive; and workers in the informal economy often lacked the bargaining power to defend their incomes. Yet no single economic grievance translated automatically into support for Chávez. Some voters wanted stronger social provision, others administrative competence or anti-corruption enforcement, and others simply a different civilian leadership. The loss of confidence in AD and COPEI therefore opened a contest among alternative projects rather than a single popular demand for military nationalism.

Section 106.7

The 1998 Presidential Campaign: Chávez, Salas Römer, and the Promise of Political Refoundation

Chávez began the 1998 campaign with significant name recognition and little institutional support. The Polo Patriótico, the electoral alliance backing him, included the MVR and several smaller parties of the left, among them the Movimiento al Socialismo (MAS), which had earlier participated in Caldera’s government, Patria Para Todos (PPT), and the Communist Party. The alliance gave him the organization of established left-wing parties, local candidates, and some access to funding, while the MVR provided the personalist center. Chávez toured the country, often traveling by road, appearing in plazas and market towns, and speaking at length. His rallies drew large crowds, and he used the radio and television coverage they attracted.

The campaign’s central themes were moral and institutional. Chávez attacked corruption, called the existing political class a failed establishment, and promised to convene a constituent assembly to rewrite the constitution and create a new republic. On economics, he emphasized an active state and a development model that was neither the market orthodoxy of the 1990s nor state socialism, avoiding the language of socialism that he later adopted. Students should be careful here: the platform of 1998 was not identical to the policies of later presidential terms. Chávez said little about what he would do with the oil industry, and he did not describe the political order that eventually emerged. Treating the campaign as proof of a hidden plan ignores the way that his positions developed under the pressures of government.

The opposing field changed rapidly. At first the leading candidate in many polls was Irene Sáez, a former beauty queen and the popular mayor of the wealthy Caracas municipality of Chacao, who was supported by COPEI. Her support collapsed in the course of 1998 as voters concluded that her political experience was limited and as COPEI’s endorsement tied her to the old order. AD nominated Luis Alfaro Ucero, a party veteran, but, as Chávez’s lead grew, AD and later COPEI withdrew support from their own candidates and backed Henrique Salas Römer, a business-oriented governor of Carabobo who headed his own party, Proyecto Venezuela. This late consolidation turned the contest into a two-person race, but it also meant that the traditional parties’ endorsement may have cost Salas Römer as many voters as it gained him.

The timing of elections mattered. The congressional and regional elections were held in November 1998, a month before the presidential vote, in an effort by the existing parties to prevent a Chávez victory from carrying over to Congress. Chávez’s MVR made a strong showing, though AD retained the largest delegation. That sequencing produced a divided legislature and set the terms for the struggles of 1999. Television, radio, and opinion polling shaped the campaign. Chávez’s command of television attracted viewers, while his opponents and their allies in much of the private press portrayed him as dangerous, with predictions of authoritarianism that would be debated for decades.

Campaign documents require close chronological reading. The 1996 Agenda Alternativa Bolivariana criticized the prevailing adjustment model, while the 1998 electoral program, Una revolución democrática: la propuesta de Hugo Chávez para transformar Venezuela, presented constitutional refounding and administrative change as essential instruments of national renewal. These were political commitments, not neutral forecasts or a full blueprint for the later presidency. The broad coalition supporting Chávez included organizations with different priorities on markets, redistribution, decentralization, and foreign policy. Electoral language temporarily linked them through the promise that the people would replace a discredited political order; it did not resolve their disagreements about how the successor order would function.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Chávez Won in 1998

Long-term causes

The decline of the oil-financed distribution system, the corruption scandals of the Punto Fijo parties, the shock of the Caracazo, and a long fall in living standards from the early 1980s.

Short-term causes

The banking crisis of 1994, the unpopular adjustment of 1996, low oil prices in 1998, the collapse of Sáez’s candidacy, and Chávez’s campaign skill and television presence.

Event or transformation

The election of 6 December 1998, in which Chávez won 56.20 percent of valid votes against Salas Römer’s 39.97 percent.

Immediate effects

A peaceful transfer of authority, the discrediting of AD and COPEI as governing forces, and a mandate to convene a constituent assembly.

Long-term consequences

A new constitution, a new party system dominated by the president’s movement, and deepening polarization.

Historical complexity

Voters had many motives. Some voted for Chávez as a protest, some for his program, some as a rejection of the old parties, and some out of enthusiasm for the man. No single cause explains the result.
Section 106.8

December 6, 1998: Chávez’s Electoral Victory and the End of Punto Fijo Dominance

The presidential election of 6 December 1998 was held under the supervision of Venezuela’s electoral council and was observed by international monitors, including the Carter Center and the Organization of American States. Chávez won 56.20 percent of valid votes, and Salas Römer won 39.97 percent. IFES reports about 63.76 percent turnout using its published ballot and registration totals (other CNE-derived compilations use a slightly different registry), a higher level than in 1993 but still showing that a substantial portion of the electorate did not vote. Salas Römer conceded quickly, and the outgoing president Caldera and the president-elect conducted a cordial transition. These facts are the foundation of any discussion of Chávez’s legitimacy: he won a competitive election whose result was accepted by his opponents.

It is important to state precisely what the election was. It was a democratic repudiation of the established parties, not the completion of the 1992 coup. The voters who supported Chávez in 1998 were not endorsing an armed overthrow, though some of them admired his willingness to challenge the system. Many had not supported him in 1992. His victory also did not reflect an unambiguous rejection of the earlier democratic order: voters chose through an election run under the 1961 constitution, and AD and COPEI continued to hold seats in Congress and many state governments. What ended in 1998 was the ability of the Punto Fijo parties to define the options for the presidency.

Chávez was inaugurated on 2 February 1999. In his oath he referred to the 1961 constitution as moribund, signaling that his government would seek to replace it. Caldera, whose own election had been a sign of the old order’s decline, handed over the presidential sash. The orderly transfer of authority from an elected president to a former coup leader is one of the notable facts of Venezuelan history, and it was not inevitable. It reflected the commitment of the outgoing government and military to the election result. From the first day, however, Chávez stated that his government would pursue constitutional transformation, and the question of how to do so would shape the next year.

The published count was more informative than a simple winning percentage. The Carter Center reproduced 3,673,685 votes for Chávez and 2,613,161 for Salas Römer, or 56.20 and 39.97 percent of valid votes respectively. IFES lists 6,988,291 ballots cast and 10,959,530 registered voters, equivalent to approximately 63.76 percent participation on that particular registration denominator. Other electoral compilations use a slightly different registry and consequently report a somewhat different turnout. For classroom analysis, numerator and denominator must travel with every percentage. A majority of valid votes was a strong electoral mandate; it did not mean that 56 percent of every adult Venezuelan had voted for the winner.

The observation record also helps qualify the campaign's outcome. Election monitors described a generally orderly count and an accepted result, not a military transfer of government. Salas Römer's defeat did not eliminate opposition to constitutional change, and the voters who supported Chávez did not all belong to the clandestine military movement or share a single ideology. One practical measure of the old parties' collapse was their inability to field viable standard-bearers of their own by the closing weeks of the campaign. The outcome therefore changed the personnel and legitimacy of national leadership while preserving elections as the instrument through which authority passed.

Section 106.9

The April 25, 1999 Referendum and the Decision to Convene a Constituent Assembly

The 1961 constitution contained procedures for amendment and reform, but it did not provide for a constituent assembly. Chávez argued that the sovereign people could always convene one, and that a popular vote could do so even without a constitutional clause. His opponents, including many jurists, argued that the president could not bypass the amendment procedures, and that a constituent assembly outside the constitution would be an irregular act. The dispute reached the Supreme Court in early 1999. The court, in rulings that were themselves open to differing interpretations, concluded that a consultative referendum on the question could proceed. Chávez’s opponents later claimed that the rulings failed to settle the limits of a constituent assembly’s powers, while his supporters claimed they confirmed popular sovereignty.

The referendum was held on 25 April 1999 and asked two questions: whether voters wished to convene a National Constituent Assembly to transform the state and create a new legal order, and whether they approved the electoral rules for choosing its members, as proposed by the president. Published official tabulations report 87.75 and 81.74 percent affirmative respectively, using the stated referendum conventions. Participation was low, however. About 38 percent of registered voters took part, with abstention at roughly 62 percent. The Organization of American States and other observers reported on the process and noted the legal questions surrounding it.

Interpreting these figures requires care. The vote showed that among those who voted, support for convening an assembly was overwhelming, and the opposition parties did not mount a major campaign against it. It also meant that only about a third of all registered voters had voted yes. Both statements are accurate. The first is the basis for the government’s claim to a popular mandate; the second is the basis for the opposition’s reminder that a large part of the electorate had not voted. The mandate was clear enough that the political class largely accepted the assembly. It was not unanimous, and the details of the electoral rules, which the president had proposed, would turn out to matter greatly.

The referendum's legal history was more intricate than a presidential appeal to popular sovereignty alone. Courts addressed the possibility of consulting voters, while the electoral authority revised the second question and the accompanying electoral rules after judicial scrutiny. The National Electoral Council's March 24 resolution, published at the end of that month, is therefore an especially useful primary source: it records the final ballot wording rather than an early proposal. The distinction matters because approval of the principle of a constituent assembly was not identical to approval of the procedures by which delegates would be chosen. Constitutional disagreement centered partly on whether the electorate could exercise original constituent power outside the written amendment machinery and partly on who would control the transition while the new document was drafted.

Official tabulations reported 87.75 percent affirmative for the first question and 81.74 percent affirmative for the second under the conventional published percentage series, with participation about 37.65 percent. The affirmative shares should not be multiplied casually by turnout without checking whether the published percentages use total ballots, valid ballots, or another denominator. A large majority of participating voters approved the process, but most registered electors did not vote. Neither fact makes the other disappear. The referendum authorized a constitution-making experiment; it did not supply a detailed public endorsement of every later intervention in the courts and legislature.

Historical Vocabulary: Poder Constituyente
Poder constituyente (constituent power)

The poder constituyente is the power to create or rewrite a constitution. In democratic theory it is attributed to the people, as distinct from the poderes constituidos (constituted powers) such as presidents, legislatures, and courts, which exist under a constitution and are limited by it. A constituent assembly is a body elected to exercise this power.

The concept was at the center of the 1999 conflict. Supporters said that a constituent assembly expressed original popular sovereignty and could therefore reorganize existing institutions. Opponents said that the assembly had been created under the existing legal order and could not override it. The 1999 constitution itself later recognized the people as holder of constituent power and set rules for calling an assembly (Article 347). Compare Colombia’s Constituent Assembly of 1991, where similar questions arose.

Section 106.10

The July 1999 Constituent Assembly Elections and the Struggle over Institutional Authority

The Constituent Assembly election of 25 July 1999 chose 131 members: 104 elected from the states, 24 from a national list, and 3 from Indigenous communities. The system allowed voters in each state to cast multiple votes for individual candidates. Chávez’s allies, running as the Polo Patriótico with the president’s personal endorsement, dominated the seats: a contemporary U.S. State Department report counted 119 MVR members of 131, whereas an OAS summary counted 122 pro-government winners among 128 non-Indigenous seats; these are different classifications, not interchangeable party totals. Meanwhile the opposition, which was divided and had run with little coordination, won only a handful. Participation was under half the electorate, with abstention a little over half. Analysts have noted that the system, in which winners in each district took most of the seats, gave the governing coalition a much larger share of seats than of votes. Opposition candidates who won seats included Alfredo Peña and the jurist Allan Brewer-Carías.

The Assembly’s members came from a range of backgrounds, including veteran politicians such as Luis Miquilena, who became its president, academics, and activists. Three seats were reserved for Indigenous delegates, and Noelí Pocaterra, a Wayuu leader from Zulia, became one of the Assembly’s vice presidents and a central figure in the writing of the Indigenous provisions. Women were a minority among the delegates, but their role in debates on social rights and gender-inclusive language was significant. The Assembly’s deliberations were broadcast and its sessions open to the public, with the consequence that constitutional debates became a form of national political theater.

The key conflict concerned the Assembly’s powers. When it was installed in early August 1999, it declared itself to be an original, sovereign body, and in the following weeks it issued decrees reorganizing the judiciary, restricting the functions of Congress, and declaring a legislative emergency. Congress, many of whose members belonged to AD, saw this as an attack, and there were tense confrontations. The Supreme Court’s president, Cecilia Sosa, resigned in August, saying in effect that the court had committed suicide by failing to defend its independence against the Assembly. The court’s rulings on the Assembly’s authority were ambiguous and, in the view of critics, yielding.

The conflict showed a basic tension. On one side was the claim that constituent sovereignty belongs to the people and that an Assembly elected for the purpose can restructure existing institutions. On the other was the principle that even a constituent body arises within a legal framework and that its authority should be limited by the commitments made when it was called, including the referendum’s terms. Electoral victory did not settle this. Supporters said that the Assembly merely expressed the popular will and that Congress and the court were part of the discredited order. Critics said that the Assembly set a precedent for overriding institutions through majority rule, and that the practices of 1999 shaped those of later years. Both claims drew on real evidence.

The electoral design amplified the political consequences of the vote. With separate national and regional contests and the ability to cast more than one candidate vote, a disciplined bloc presenting recognizable names could turn a sizable voting advantage into a much larger share of seats. The contemporary U.S. Department of State human-rights report described the MVR as winning 119 of the 131 seats, while some academic accounts count a broader pro-Chávez alliance or classify affiliated independents differently. The number 119 is thus an attributed party count in a contemporary official report, not a timeless, unqualified measurement of every delegate's political loyalty. Analysts should distinguish the party label on the ballot, an electoral alliance, and the individual choices of delegates once the assembly convened.

The composition of the assembly also showed the distance between descriptive representation and effective influence. Three Indigenous-designated delegates created a route for proposals on language, land, and cultural rights; nevertheless, their participation did not erase the wider imbalance between government supporters and opponents. Women, professional associations, universities, and regional movements sought to influence the drafting through petitions and public discussion, but opportunities varied with access and organization. At the same time, the judicial-emergency decrees shifted the terrain on which critics could contest assembly decisions. The October 1999 OAS-archived decree on judicial reorganization is direct evidence of the assembly claiming authority over an institution not originally elected by it. Reading its legal justification alongside critiques helps students assess institutional change without assuming that the word 'reorganization' establishes either reform or capture by itself.

Section 106.11

The 1999 Constitution: Participatory Democracy, Rights, and Presidential Authority

The Assembly completed its draft in November 1999. The resulting constitution is a long document with 350 articles, and it differs from the 1961 text in many ways. Several of its features were widely praised. It declared Venezuela a democratic and participatory state, not simply a representative one, and it created mechanisms for citizen involvement: consultative, recall, approving, and repealing referendums (Articles 71–74), and provisions for citizen assemblies. Its social-rights chapters guaranteed health care and education, and the document used gender-inclusive language throughout. Article 88 recognized housework as productive economic activity entitled to social security, a measure of interest to historians of women’s labor, though its practical effect depended on later policy.

The constitution’s treatment of Indigenous peoples was a significant change. Chapter VIII recognized Indigenous peoples’ social, political, and economic organization, cultures, languages, and rights to their ancestral lands, and guaranteed Indigenous representation in the National Assembly through three reserved seats. The text extended recognition well beyond the 1961 constitution. Its implementation, especially the demarcation of lands, was slower and more contested in later years, and Indigenous communities continued to experience conflicts with mining, ranching, and oil interests. Afro-Venezuelan communities, by contrast, did not receive comparable constitutional recognition in 1999, and activists later campaigned for it.

The structure of government was reorganized. Instead of three branches, the constitution established five: executive, legislative, judicial, electoral, and “citizen” power, the last composed of the attorney general, comptroller general, and human rights ombudsman. A unicameral National Assembly replaced the bicameral Congress, eliminating the Senate. The Supreme Court became the Tribunal Supremo de Justicia, organized into chambers, including a constitutional chamber, with magistrates serving a single twelve-year term chosen by the Assembly from candidates presented through a nominating committee. The presidential term was lengthened from five to six years and immediate reelection for one additional term was allowed (Article 230). The president could appoint and remove the vice president, and could dissolve the Assembly under limited conditions after repeated censure of the vice president. On the military, the constitution allowed active-duty personnel to vote while prohibiting them from political activity, and specified that the armed forces served the nation and not any person or party.

These provisions produced both hope and concern, and the two reactions were not simply partisan. Supporters argued that participatory mechanisms, especially the recall referendum, gave citizens tools that the 1961 constitution lacked, and that the document expanded social and Indigenous rights. Critics, including jurists who were not hostile to reform, argued that lengthening the presidential term, allowing reelection, abolishing the Senate, and increasing the executive’s influence over appointments weakened checks and balances. Some also noted that constitutional design is not the same as practice: a well-written independence clause for the judiciary means little if appointments are politicized. Subsequent chapters and the next sections consider how the system worked.

Article 72 illustrates the difference between the announcement of a participatory right and the rules that govern its use. The text describes elected positions as revocable and conditions the request for a referendum on the passage of half the official's term and signatures representing at least one fifth of registered electors in the relevant constituency. Other requirements concern turnout and the votes needed for revocation. These thresholds matter: without them, the bare promise of recall could be mistaken for unrestricted popular removal at any moment. They also show that participation was mediated by electoral administration and by the interpretation of constitutional authorities. A referendum provision can enhance accountability, but much depends on impartial implementation, registration, signature verification, and access to review.

The constitution's other checks were similarly double-edged. The newly established electoral and citizen-power branches were meant to separate oversight functions from ordinary party control; the Assembly received responsibility for selecting key officials through procedures meant to screen candidates. Yet replacing the Senate with a single legislative chamber removed a potential institutional veto point, and transitional appointments allowed the constituent majority to shape the bodies that would initially enforce the new rules. The issue was not simply whether the text contained rights and independent bodies—it did—but whether institutions administering those rights possessed sufficient autonomy when the executive and legislative majorities aligned. The contrast became a recurring subject of Venezuelan legal scholarship.

Article 72 of the 1999 Constitution: The Recall Referendum

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did the 1999 Constitution Deepen Democracy or Concentrate Power?

The question: Was the 1999 constitutional process and its product primarily an expansion of democratic participation and rights, or primarily a concentration of executive power under democratic forms?

Interpretation A — Deepening democracy: Scholars sympathetic to the project emphasize the referendums that preceded the constitution, the recall mechanism, the social and Indigenous rights, and the openness of the Assembly’s debates. They argue that the 1961 order had become exclusionary and that participatory provisions responded to real demands.
Interpretation B — Concentrating power: Other scholars emphasize the lopsided Assembly, its claim to original authority over existing institutions, the abolition of the Senate, the longer term with reelection, and the politicization of appointments. They argue that these features weakened accountability even though the process was popular.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the constitutional text, the referendum and election results, the Assembly’s debates, and the later record of how institutions operated. The two views are not strictly opposed: many scholars hold that the constitution did both things, and the debate concerns weighting and causation.

Why it matters: The question affects how later events, including the strikes and the 2002 crisis, are understood. If the process mainly broadened democracy, opposition fears of authoritarianism look exaggerated; if it mainly concentrated power, they look prescient. A careful reading finds that the opposition’s fears were partly justified and partly overstated, and that the governing coalition’s claims to be deepening democracy were also partly accurate.

Student task: Write a short assessment of each interpretation and identify what evidence, such as comparisons of judicial rulings or the use of recall mechanisms, would best test them.

Section 106.12

The December 15, 1999 Constitutional Referendum and the Establishment of the Bolivarian Republic

The draft constitution was submitted to a referendum on 15 December 1999. According to official results, 71.78 percent of valid votes approved and 28.22 percent rejected the text. IFES records approximately 44.38 percent turnout and hence about 55.62 percent abstention on its registration figures. The campaign against the draft was weak and fragmented, though prominent critics, including some church leaders, business figures, and politicians, argued against provisions such as the additional presidential power and the change of the country’s name. Yet the majority of those who voted accepted it.

The referendum took place in the middle of a national disaster. In the days around the vote, extremely heavy rains caused floods and mudslides, especially in Vargas state on the Caribbean coast north of Caracas. Entire neighborhoods were buried or swept away. The number of dead is still uncertain, with estimates ranging from several thousand to tens of thousands. The disaster made the voting more difficult in some areas and became an enduring political memory. The government’s handling of the response, including the decision to decline some foreign offers of engineering assistance, became a subject of debate. The tragedy also displaced many people, with long-term effects on the region.

The constitution came into force with its publication on 30 December 1999. It renamed the country the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela, a change that opponents regarded as partisan and supporters as an expression of national tradition. The Assembly also approved a transitional regime that, until new institutions could be elected, named interim officeholders, including magistrates of the new Supreme Tribunal, the attorney general, and the comptroller, and created a temporary legislative commission. Critics said these appointments were made without the nominating procedures the new constitution prescribed, and that the transitional arrangements gave the governing coalition early control. Supporters said that transitions require temporary measures and that new elections would follow in 2000.

The referendum established a legal framework but did not end political disagreement. The result gave the government a clear legal basis and demonstrated that the process had broad if not universal support, while disagreement continued about how the new framework should operate and whether its safeguards would work. Chapter 107 follows the economic and oil policy dimensions, while the sections that follow trace the institutional and political ones.

The polling record makes the mandate more precise. IFES records 3,301,475 affirmative votes and 1,298,105 negative votes, corresponding to 71.78 and 28.22 percent of valid votes, respectively. It reports 4,819,786 ballots cast from 10,860,265 registered electors, a turnout of approximately 44.38 percent. Accordingly, the affirmative side represented about three tenths of the electorate on that registry, not three quarters of all Venezuelans. The result was decisive among those voting and legally operative, while abstention remained substantial. Comparisons with the April referendum should preserve each event's specific ballot and registry denominator rather than treating all published percentages as interchangeable.

Section 106.13

The 2000 Elections and the Consolidation of the New Constitutional Order

The new constitution required that all elected offices be renewed, a process known as “relegitimation.” The so-called mega-elections, covering the presidency, the National Assembly, governors, mayors, and local councils, were originally scheduled for May 2000 but were postponed after technical failures in the electoral system, and were held on 30 July 2000. Chávez ran for a new six-year term against Francisco Arias Cárdenas, his former comrade in the 1992 rebellion, who ran as a reformist alternative and presented himself as a critic of Chávez’s style and of the growing role of the military in government. Chávez won 59.76 percent of valid votes to Francisco Arias Cárdenas’s 37.52 percent (IFES/CNE tabulation), and with about 60 percent of the vote, while Arias Cárdenas received about 37 to 38 percent. Participation was around 56 percent.

The governing coalition also won a majority in the new unicameral National Assembly, enough to control legislation and, together with allies, to reach the three-fifths majority needed to delegate lawmaking powers to the president. Opposition parties, including AD, COPEI, and Proyecto Venezuela, kept seats and several governorships, and the opposition remained a visible presence in the regions. The party landscape was changing. AD and COPEI continued but diminished, MAS and other former allies divided over whether to support the government, and new groupings appeared. The opposition was weakened by fragmentation and by its association with a discredited past.

The elections gave the government a fresh democratic mandate under the new rules, and international observers did not question the overall result, though technical problems and the postponement drew criticism. They did not resolve disputes about fairness. Critics pointed to the government’s advantages, including its use of public resources and its dominance of state broadcasting, and to the transitional appointments to electoral and judicial bodies. Supporters replied that all governments use their resources and that the opposition had access to private media. The general problem, which is not unique to Venezuela, is that repeated victories at the polls establish legitimacy without guaranteeing the institutional conditions, such as independent courts and electoral bodies, that make accountability effective.

The precise results reveal both continuity and electoral change. CNE-derived tabulations reproduced by IFES report 3,757,773 votes for Chávez, conventionally listed as 59.76 percent, compared with 2,359,459 votes, or 37.52 percent, for Arias Cárdenas. IFES lists 6,600,196 ballots cast and 11,720,660 registered voters, yielding about 56.31 percent participation on that registry. The renewed presidential mandate was thus clear among valid votes but was accompanied by abstention exceeding two fifths. It also followed the inauguration of a new constitutional order whose design had been approved in a distinct referendum. Combining the several events into a single aggregate claim about unanimous support would obscure their different questions, candidates, and constituencies.

The contest between Chávez and Arias Cárdenas also complicates the image of a unified original military movement. The challenger had joined the 1992 uprising and later broke politically with Chávez, giving voters a choice between former participants in the rebellion who drew different conclusions from it. The election therefore revealed that sharing the experience of military dissent did not imply permanent agreement about constitutional government. At the legislative level, the governing side's majority offered the president substantial capacity to enact his program, while opposition members could still challenge bills and mobilize constituencies outside the Assembly. The existence of elected opponents is compatible with concern about the practical consequences of a dominant governing majority.

Section 106.14

Reorganizing the State: The Judiciary, Armed Forces, and New Constitutional Institutions

Under the new constitution, the Supreme Tribunal of Justice, the electoral council, and the citizen-power institutions were formally independent. Their members were to be chosen through nominating committees and legislative votes. In practice, the first officeholders had been designated through the transitional regime, and the later selection of magistrates and officials was shaped by the governing majority’s control of the Assembly. Critics, including legal scholars and human rights organizations, argued that these appointments reduced judicial independence. They also pointed to the large number of provisional judges whose tenure depended on administrative bodies. Supporters argued that the old judiciary had been corrupt and slow, and that renewal was necessary. Both claims have evidence behind them, and this chapter distinguishes constitutional design from actual institutional independence without describing the new institutions as automatically illegitimate or as inevitably captured.

The armed forces’ role changed in several ways. Chávez was himself an officer, and many of his early collaborators were retired officers. Soldiers gained the vote, and officers were given roles in government. In February 2000 the government launched Plan Bolívar 2000, a program in which troops repaired schools and hospitals, ran food-distribution operations, and carried out public works, mostly in poor neighborhoods. Supporters praised the program for reaching communities the civil bureaucracy had neglected. Critics argued that it blurred the line between civilian and military roles and raised the risk of corruption, and subsequent investigations did find irregularities in some projects. The constitution said that the armed forces served the nation, not any person or party, but in practice the political loyalties of officers became a question of national debate.

The government’s relations with the legislature and with regional authorities were also reshaped. The president’s coalition controlled the Assembly, and the Assembly could delegate lawmaking powers to the executive. State governors and mayors, many of whom came from opposition parties, retained local authority, but the central government’s fiscal power gave it influence. The Assembly’s new human rights ombudsman and the other citizen-power offices were intended as independent watchdogs, but their independence from the executive was disputed.

Evaluating these changes requires attention to what evidence is available. Rulings of the Supreme Tribunal, reports by the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights and by nongovernmental organizations, press investigations, and later scholarship provide material, but all come from observers with perspectives. A balanced account notes that the new institutions did produce legitimate decisions and real reforms, that opposition accusations were sometimes exaggerated, and that the trend in appointments increasingly favored the government’s supporters.

The 1999 judicial-emergency and reorganization decrees had a legacy beyond the drafting of the constitution. They placed professional status, disciplinary review, and appointments within bodies created or reshaped by constituent authority, producing a disputed transition from the old courts to the new Supreme Tribunal. Judicial independence is not measured only by the formal wording of a constitutional article. Judges require secure tenure, transparent selection, rules for recusal, and procedures through which judgments are not converted into grounds for political retaliation. Temporary and provisional appointments can create genuine administrative flexibility during reform, but they may also make judges more vulnerable to pressure from those able to renew or terminate their service.

A similar distinction applies to the armed forces. Sending military engineers or medical teams to underserved neighborhoods could fill urgent gaps in a strained civil bureaucracy, and beneficiaries need not have experienced the work as coercive. Yet extending uniforms into food distribution, infrastructure management, and senior administration altered the boundary between public-service provision and political organization. The issue was not resolved by quoting the constitution's command that the armed forces serve the nation. The historian must ask how officers were promoted, whether political disagreement affected careers, which civilian institutions supervised expenditures, and how abuses or errors could be investigated. Evidence from oversight agencies and human-rights organizations can answer parts of those questions, while slogans on either side cannot.

Section 106.15

Popular Mobilization, Organized Labor, Media, and the Emergence of Political Polarization

By 2001 Venezuelan society was dividing into camps whose boundaries were visible in neighborhoods, workplaces, and media. Supporters of Chávez included many residents of poor urban barrios and rural communities, retired and active military personnel, left-wing parties, and a growing network of local groups. In late 2001 the government encouraged the formation of “Bolivarian Circles,” neighborhood groups of supporters, which the opposition denounced as partisan militias, though their members described them as community organizations. Indigenous organizations had mixed relations with the government: they had gained constitutional recognition but continued to push for land demarcation.

Opposition groups included the established parties, the business federation Fedecámaras, the labor confederation CTV, universities and student groups, professional associations, much of the Catholic hierarchy, and most private media owners. The CTV, long tied to Acción Democrática, was threatened by a government-called referendum in December 2000 that asked voters whether union leaders should be reelected through new elections. Most voters who took part approved, but abstention was very high, and the CTV and international labor organizations saw the measure as interference. Business groups feared the government’s interventionist rhetoric. Many middle-class voters, who had initially been willing to give Chávez a chance, became alarmed by his confrontations with critics.

Media became a battlefield. Chávez used television and radio extensively; since May 1999 he had hosted a weekly program, Aló Presidente, in which he spoke for hours about policy, history, and personalities. He also used the legal power to require all stations to carry his addresses simultaneously, known as a cadena. The five major private television channels and most large newspapers were critical of the government and increasingly overt in their opposition. Both sides accused the other of distortion. The government accused private media of lying and conspiring; media owners and press-freedom organizations accused the government of intimidation. Journalists were harassed or attacked in some cases, and the language of political life grew harsher on both sides.

The social divide did not follow a simple rich-versus-poor line. Many poor Venezuelans opposed Chávez, and many middle-class and professional Venezuelans supported him. Race and class nonetheless shaped how conflict was perceived. Chávez, who spoke of his Indigenous, African, and Spanish ancestry, was the target of racist insults from some opponents, while his supporters sometimes used class-based labels for opponents. Geography mattered too, as Caracas neighborhoods tended to align, with wealthier eastern districts more often opposed and many western and hillside barrios more often supportive, though with many exceptions. Women were active on both sides as community organizers, professionals, and public voices.

Political identity was also being negotiated within households and neighborhoods rather than merely imposed by party headquarters. Some residents welcomed a president who spoke in familiar idioms and visited areas long excluded from elite political life; others feared that his public confrontations were narrowing room for dissent. Established unions had their own internal electoral conflicts, so a union federation's stance did not necessarily represent every worker. Media institutions ranged from strongly oppositional private broadcasters to outlets supporting the government; their editorial choices, access to advertising, and decisions about what to broadcast became political questions. A historian should distinguish criticism of a government's rhetoric, documented violations of journalists' rights, and the legitimate partisan positions of independent publications.

Everyday Life and Social History

Barrio Meetings, Pots and Pans, and Living with Polarization

For many Venezuelans the political conflict was not an abstract matter. In poor neighborhoods, residents’ committees, many organized by women, met to discuss water supply, land titles, schools, and clinics, and some of these local groups aligned with the government. The new Bolivarian schools, introduced from 1999–2000, extended school days and meals in some districts. Informal workers, who made up a large part of the urban workforce, experienced the economy through unstable income and rising prices.

In middle-class neighborhoods, residents followed political news on television and radio, signed petitions, and later protested by banging pots and pans, a practice that became common in 2002. Families and workplaces divided, and by 2001 many Venezuelans described having stopped discussing politics with relatives or colleagues. Official statistics and news coverage tell much of the story, but oral histories, neighborhood studies, and local archives are needed to recover how ordinary people experienced these years. Scholars of this period often note that the sources overrepresent Caracas and underrepresent small towns, rural areas, and Afro-Venezuelan and Indigenous communities.

Section 106.16

The Enabling Law, Forty-Nine Decrees, and the Escalation of Political Conflict, 2001

In November 2000 the National Assembly approved an enabling law (ley habilitante) authorizing the president to issue decrees with the force of law for one year in specified areas. The constitution permits such delegation by a three-fifths vote. Governments in Venezuela had used enabling laws before, but the opposition protested that the delegation was broad and that the government would use it to avoid debate. On 13 November 2001, as the authority was about to expire, the government published a package of 49 decree-laws. They covered a wide range of subjects, and three drew the sharpest controversy: a land law, a fisheries law, and a hydrocarbons law.

The land law (Ley de Tierras y Desarrollo Agrario) set rules for the use of agricultural land, taxed idle large estates, and allowed the state to redistribute underused land under certain conditions. Supporters presented it as a response to extreme concentration of land ownership, a historic grievance in Venezuela. Landowners and agricultural associations argued that it threatened property rights and was adopted without consultation. The fisheries law restricted industrial trawling in coastal waters in favor of small-scale fishers, and the hydrocarbons law raised the state’s share of oil revenue and strengthened state control of the industry. This chapter explains why the laws provoked conflict, since their economic consequences and the restructuring of PDVSA are the subject of Chapter 107.

The opposition’s objections were procedural and substantive. Procedurally, they said that the number of decrees, issued with little public discussion, violated the spirit of participatory democracy that the constitution proclaimed. Substantively, different groups objected to different decrees. Employers saw threats to property and investment; others feared a drift toward state control; many opposed the government’s style as much as its laws. The government answered that the enabling law was constitutional, that the decrees fulfilled campaign promises, and that opposition to redistribution came from privileged interests.

Fedecámaras, headed by Pedro Carmona Estanga, and the CTV, headed by Carlos Ortega, an unusual partnership of employers and unionists, called a national strike for 10 December 2001. The one-day strike was widely observed in some sectors and cities, and it marked the first time that the business and labor leadership acted together against the government. Human Rights Watch and other organizations reported contemporaneously on the growing confrontation and on concerns about both press freedom and the government’s rhetoric. Internationally, Chávez’s criticism of the American bombing campaign in Afghanistan in late 2001 added tension with Washington. By the end of 2001 the opposition had a national leadership, a program of resistance, and a growing willingness to consider tactics beyond elections.

The enabling-law debate illustrates how questions of procedure and policy became mutually reinforcing. Delegated legislation was expressly contemplated by the 1999 Constitution, so its use was not automatically unconstitutional; but the scope and timing of forty-nine measures reduced ordinary opportunities for committee deliberation and detailed public scrutiny. The land and fisheries measures had distinct potential beneficiaries, including small farmers and artisanal fishers, whose views were not reducible to those of the established business federations. Conversely, critics concerned about property security or the absence of consultation were not necessarily opponents of every redistribution measure. Evaluating the episode requires reading the authorizing law, the actual decree-laws, and organized reactions, not only a retrospective account of the December strike.

The April 2002 Constitutional Crisis: Protest, Coup, Restoration, and Historical Consequences

Sections 106.17–106.20
Section 106.17

Strikes, Military Dissent, and the Escalation toward April 2002

The first months of 2002 saw growing confrontation. In January, Luis Miquilena, who had been one of Chávez’s closest civilian allies and president of the Constituent Assembly, broke with the government, a significant sign of division among supporters. In February, several officers publicly called on the president to resign. The first, in early February, was an air force colonel, Pedro Soto, followed by others, including a navy rear admiral, Carlos Molina Tamayo. These public statements were unusual in a military that had been outwardly disciplined, and they showed that dissent now included serving officers. The government treated them as insubordination.

The conflict over the state oil company PDVSA became the immediate trigger. In February the government replaced the company’s president, and in April it appointed new board members and moved against managers who objected. On 7 April, in a televised broadcast of Aló Presidente, Chávez read out the names of several PDVSA managers and dismissed them, blowing a referee’s whistle as he did so. Employees and managers protested, and Fedecámaras and the CTV called a general strike, which began on 9 April and was extended. The details of PDVSA’s governance and the broader oil confrontation are the subject of Chapter 107; what matters here is that the dispute merged labor, business, managerial, and political grievances in a single crisis.

Students should distinguish among several types of action. A strike, a demonstration, and a campaign for the president’s resignation or for a recall referendum are forms of lawful political opposition. The employers’ role was contested, since critics described the stoppage as a lockout. Public calls by officers for a president’s resignation were military insubordination, whatever one thought of the government. Preparations to remove the president by force, which some officers and civilians were discussing, were preparations for unconstitutional regime change. Historians have shown that these categories overlapped in practice, and that participants did not share identical aims: some wanted early elections, some wanted resignation, and some wanted to dismantle the government altogether.

Why did many different actors believe the country had entered an exceptional crisis? Opposition leaders believed that the government was becoming authoritarian and that elections alone could not remove it. Government supporters believed that business, media, and conservative forces were preparing to overturn the results of elections. Both beliefs were sincere, and both were shaped by the way each side read the other’s statements. The result was a situation in which each side expected the worst of the other, and a confrontation that could be resolved only by institutions that no longer commanded shared trust.

One immediate source of conflict was the disagreement over who had authority to speak for the nation. Opposition organizers asserted a right to protest and strike in defense of constitutional democracy; government supporters saw increasingly coordinated elite pressure as an effort to overturn the electoral outcome of 2000. Neither claim resolves whether particular actions were lawful. Marches, work stoppages, speeches by retired officers, and public criticism are different from a serving officer's disobedience of orders or a plan to replace the president by force. The historical challenge is to place each actor's words and deeds on that scale rather than referring to all opposition as coup plotters or all government measures as democratic by definition.

Historical Vocabulary: Paro
Paro

In Spanish, a paro is a work stoppage. The word is used both for a labor strike called by workers (huelga) and for a stoppage in which employers close businesses, which English speakers often call a lockout. In Venezuela in 2001–2003 the opposition used paro cívico (civic stoppage) to present the action as a national movement of citizens, while the government called it a lockout or sabotage.

The word matters because naming shaped argument. A strike is protected as a labor right in democracies; a lockout by employers is treated differently; and a stoppage with the aim of removing a president raises further questions. Historians use neutral terms such as “general stoppage” when describing the event, and then analyze who participated and for what aims.

Section 106.18

April 11, 2002: The Opposition March, Shootings, and the Breakdown of Constitutional Authority

On 11 April 2002 a very large opposition march, estimated by most sources at several hundred thousand people, set out from the Parque del Este in eastern Caracas toward the PDVSA headquarters in Chuao, where the dismissed managers had their base. During the march, leaders including Carlos Ortega announced that it would be redirected to the Miraflores presidential palace in the city center to demand Chávez’s resignation. Meanwhile, supporters of the president had gathered near the palace, and some groups were on the Puente Llaguno overpass and nearby streets. The two crowds were separated by a short distance and by police lines of the National Guard and the Metropolitan Police, the force of Caracas under the control of a mayor aligned with the opposition.

In the afternoon, shooting broke out in the streets near the palace, around Baralt Avenue and the surrounding blocks. At least nineteen people were killed and many more, well over a hundred in most counts, were wounded. The dead included both opposition marchers and government supporters. Chávez ordered a mandatory simultaneous broadcast (cadena) and private television channels split their screens, showing both his address and images from the streets. Footage, in particular images of armed men on the Puente Llaguno overpass firing toward the street below, became central to the dispute. The private channels and the opposition presented the images as proof that government supporters were shooting at unarmed marchers. Government supporters said the men were responding to shots from police or from snipers and that the footage was edited or presented without context.

The competing interpretations have not been fully reconciled. Later investigations, including Venezuelan court proceedings, found some officials of the Metropolitan Police guilty in the deaths and also examined the roles of armed pro-government civilians, but those proceedings were criticized by some observers as one-sided and by others as incomplete, and the full sequence of who shot whom has remained disputed. Historians therefore distinguish several kinds of evidence: video and photographs, which show fragments and depend on framing and editing; witness testimony, which varies; contemporary reporting, which was often partisan; court findings, which are shaped by procedure and political context; and politically interested reconstructions, including documentary films made by each side. A 2003 Irish documentary on the crisis, widely screened abroad, was criticized for omissions and chronology, and the opposition produced its own accounts. This chapter does not assign responsibility for each death.

What can be said with confidence is that by the evening of 11 April the constitutional order was breaking down. Senior military commanders, some of whom had previously been loyal, declared that they no longer supported the president, citing the deaths and his refusal to withdraw the military from civilian crowds. Pro-government officers contested this, and the high command’s position was not unanimous. The decisive point is what followed: instead of seeking a constitutional solution, such as the procedures for removal or the recall mechanism, which the constitution allowed only after the midpoint of the term, the officers and civilians who opposed Chávez moved to remove him outside the constitution.

The chronology of the shootings requires a higher standard of source criticism than the political slogans that followed. Television footage contains a frame, not a complete view of the surrounding streets; the camera's location, the timing of cuts, and whether the soundtrack was contemporaneous all affect interpretation. Witness statements can be valuable but may contradict one another because observers occupied different positions and were exposed to fear, noise, and partial information. Later judicial verdicts address the responsibility of particular defendants under legal evidentiary standards; they do not automatically settle every contested shot fired by every participant. A responsible textbook therefore describes confirmed deaths and injuries and the institutional sequence without manufacturing a universally accepted map of individual shooters.

Section 106.19

April 12, 2002: The Removal of Chávez and the Carmona Decree

In the early hours of 12 April, officers of the high command informed the public that the president had been asked to resign and had accepted. Chávez had in fact surrendered himself to the military rather than resign, and he and his supporters have always denied that he resigned. He was taken to Fuerte Tiuna and later to naval installations on the coast and an island. No verified voluntary resignation was produced. Article 233 lists resignation as an absolute presidential vacancy but does not explicitly require submission of a formal resignation before the National Assembly; it separately requires an Assembly declaration for abandonment of office and prescribes succession procedures. The attorney general, Isaías Rodríguez, was among the officials who stated publicly that no valid resignation had been presented. The vice president and cabinet were removed or went into hiding.

The opposition coalition chose Pedro Carmona Estanga, the president of Fedecámaras, to head an interim government. In a ceremony at Miraflores on 12 April, attended by business leaders, politicians, military officers, and clergy, including Cardinal Ignacio Velasco, Carmona was sworn in and a decree was read. The decree established a “government of democratic transition and national unity,” restored the country’s former name, the Republic of Venezuela, and dissolved or removed the main institutions of the constitutional order. The National Assembly was dissolved, the magistrates of the Supreme Tribunal were removed, and the attorney general, comptroller, and human rights ombudsman were dismissed. The decree suspended the enabling-law measures (its own Article 9 refers to 48 decrees, although accounts of the November 2001 package commonly speak of 49) and promised new elections.

The decree’s content is the key to its legal character. Even if Chávez had resigned, the constitution prescribed that the vice president succeed him and that elections follow. Instead, a private citizen who had not been elected, chosen by a group of opposition figures and officers, assumed power and abolished the legislature, the courts, and other independent bodies. Political disagreement with Chávez, however deep, did not confer authority to abolish constitutional institutions. This was an unconstitutional interruption of Venezuela’s elected government. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights subsequently condemned the rupture of constitutional order and the dismantling of public institutions.

Responses were swift and divided. Within Venezuela, many officers who had broken with the president were uneasy about the scope of the decree. The Organization of American States’ Permanent Council, meeting on 13 April, condemned the alteration of constitutional order, and the Rio Group of Latin American governments, then meeting in Costa Rica, condemned the interruption of the democratic process. The United States government’s first statement placed responsibility for the crisis on the Chávez government and did not condemn the removal, a reaction widely criticized in Latin America. Chávez’s supporters have alleged a U.S. role in planning; official reviews and journalists’ reports have documented contacts between American officials and opposition figures and American-funded democracy programs, but the extent of any involvement is disputed, and the key decisions were taken by Venezuelans. Some governments, such as Spain’s, were also criticized for early acceptance of the new situation.

Article 233 of the 1999 Constitution lists presidential resignation among the circumstances producing an absolute vacancy and specifies succession through the executive vice president during the relevant part of a presidential term. It separately requires an Assembly declaration when abandonment of office is the ground asserted. The text does not itself prescribe that a resignation be formally lodged before the Assembly as a universal condition of resignation; what matters here is the absence of a verified voluntary resignation and the failure to follow the constitution's succession mechanisms. Even if an agreed resignation had occurred, it would not have authorized the selection of an unelected business leader to dissolve the legislature and remove judicial officials.

The Carmona document is unusually revealing because it makes a claim to restore democracy while immediately displacing the institutions through which that democracy was constituted. Its own articles appoint Carmona to the presidency, change the republic's name, suspend elected legislators, and reorganize the other branches. This is more precise evidence than a retrospective description of the interim government as merely caretaker or transitional. The OAS Permanent Council's April 13 Resolution 811 condemned an alteration of the constitutional regime while also condemning violence and calling for a fact-finding mission; it should not be flattened into an endorsement of every government claim about the April 11 shootings.

Section 106.20

April 13–14, 2002: Chávez’s Restoration and the Meaning of the Bolivarian Revolution

The interim government collapsed within about two days. Supporters of Chávez, many from the barrios of western Caracas and other cities, gathered at the palace and at military installations, demanding his return. Private television stations were criticized for not reporting these mobilizations and for broadcasting entertainment programming, while state television, which had been taken off the air, resumed broadcasting later. Within the armed forces, units that had not accepted the removal, notably the paratroop brigade in Maracay under General Raúl Isaías Baduel, an old associate from the early movement, declared loyalty to the constitutional president, and the presidential guard moved to retake the palace. Senior officers who had broken with Chávez on 11 April became divided over the decree’s scope.

On the afternoon and evening of 13 April, Carmona lost the support he needed and left office, and Vice President Diosdado Cabello assumed the presidency as the constitutional successor. In the early hours of 14 April Chávez returned to Miraflores by helicopter, and resumed his office. The restoration of an elected president was accomplished through a combination of popular mobilization, realignment of military units, and the collapse of the interim government’s legitimacy. On 14 August 2002, a chamber of the Supreme Tribunal declined to initiate criminal proceedings against four officers; the associated “vacuum of power” reasoning became controversial and was not a conclusive judicial validation of Carmona’s unconstitutional takeover. A later judicial reversal in 2005 further complicated that interpretation. Many of the leaders involved went into exile or faced legal proceedings that were later disputed.

The crisis demonstrated several things. It showed that Venezuelan institutions were weak enough to be dissolved by a decree but that popular and military loyalty to constitutional legality could be mobilized to restore them. It showed the importance of the armed forces’ divisions. It showed the power of mass mobilization on both sides of the conflict, and the role of television in shaping perceptions. And it showed the cost of polarization: neither side trusted the other to follow rules, and each used the other’s actions to justify its own.

The restoration did not end the disputes. Chávez made conciliatory gestures, called for dialogue, and mediators including the Carter Center, the OAS, and the United Nations Development Programme tried to broker negotiations. Yet suspicion intensified. Opposition leaders who had endorsed or accepted the coup lost credibility with some voters, while Chávez’s supporters felt vindicated and his opponents felt that he used the coup to justify tighter control. The Bolivarian project claimed achievements: new participatory institutions, expanded constitutional rights for Indigenous peoples, a revival of national symbols, and efforts to extend services to the poor. It also faced documented problems: concentration of presidential authority, politicization of institutions, harsh public rhetoric, and a society split into camps. Both lists are true, and a historian’s task is to weigh them without collapsing one into the other.

The unresolved issues point to Chapter 107, which examines the conflict over PDVSA, the 2002–2003 oil strike, the redistribution of petroleum revenue through social missions, and the expansion of Venezuelan oil diplomacy. Those subjects show how the political struggles of 1999–2002 became struggles over the central economic institution of the country.

The courts later became another arena in which the meaning of the crisis was contested. On 14 August 2002, a chamber of the Supreme Tribunal declined to initiate criminal proceedings against four officers, a decision publicly associated with the assertion that a vacuum of power had existed. This was a specific judicial disposition under particular legal procedures, not an independent historical proof that Carmona's decree or the removal of an elected president had followed the constitution. The ruling provoked further conflict over judicial accountability and was later superseded in the legal debate, including by a 2005 judicial reversal. Readers should distinguish what the tribunal decided at that moment from the wider findings of the Inter-American human-rights system and the OAS.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Planned Was April 2002, and Who Bears Responsibility?

The question: This debate does not concern whether the removal of Chávez and the Carmona decree were unconstitutional; the IACHR and the OAS found that they were. It concerns how much the events were planned in advance, who shared responsibility, and what role domestic and foreign actors played.

Interpretation A — A premeditated conspiracy: Supporters of the government and some scholars argue that civilian and military opponents, with support from business, media owners, and perhaps foreign actors, had planned the removal in advance and used the shootings as a pretext.
Interpretation B — An improvised rupture: Other scholars argue that while some conspirators had planned for a removal, the events of 11–12 April unfolded unpredictably from the strike, the march’s redirection, the shootings, and the officers’ decisions, and that many participants did not intend to abolish institutions. They also point to the disputed evidence about the shootings.

Evidence: Both views rely on participants’ memoirs, press reports, military and court records, and foreign documents. The evidence indicates that some officers and civilians had been discussing removal for weeks, and also that the events escalated quickly. The two interpretations overlap, and many accounts combine them.

Why it matters: The answer affects how responsibility is assigned for the violence and for the breakdown of constitutional order, and it shaped how both sides interpreted later events, including the oil strike of 2002–2003. Giving equal weight to every claim would be false balance: that the removal was unconstitutional is established, while the degree of planning and the identity of the shooters remain uncertain.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From the Punto Fijo Order to the Bolivarian Republic

What Changed

  • The party system dominated by AD and COPEI gave way to a system centered on the president’s movement and its opponents
  • A new constitution replaced that of 1961, with new participatory mechanisms and Indigenous rights
  • The armed forces’ public role expanded, and the president himself was a former officer
  • Political language and social relations became sharply polarized

What Stayed the Same

  • The state’s dependence on oil revenue and the expectation that the state would distribute it
  • A strong presidency, a tradition of personalist leadership, and the appeal of Bolívar’s image
  • Deep inequalities in income, land, and access to services
  • Doubts about the independence of courts and electoral bodies

Who experienced change most directly: Party activists, public officials, military officers, and residents of poor neighborhoods drawn into new organizations experienced the most visible change. Greater continuity: Rural communities and the informal workforce often experienced continuing precarity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that Venezuela’s transformation was one of political institutions and coalitions built on top of an unchanged economic base, which helps explain why oil policy became the next arena of conflict.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the political transformation of Venezuela between 1989 and April 2002 and argued that Hugo Chávez’s rise cannot be explained by his personality alone. It was produced by the exhaustion of the oil-financed party democracy, by a widespread repudiation of the established parties, by a tradition of military nationalism, and by a leader who combined television skill with the language of Bolívar.

Chávez grew up in Barinas, entered the Military Academy in 1971, and helped build a clandestine officers’ movement that took the name MBR-200. Its Bolivarian ideology drew on Bolívar, Simón Rodríguez, and Ezequiel Zamora. The movement’s own foundation story is evidence of how it wished to be remembered, while independently documented events begin chiefly in the late 1980s.

The Caracazo of 1989 and the economic crisis opened the way for dissent. On 4 February 1992 Chávez led an armed attempt to overthrow the elected president Carlos Andrés Pérez. The attempt was unconstitutional and it failed, but his brief televised surrender, with the phrase “por ahora,” made him known across the country. A second uprising in November, led by other officers, showed the depth of military dissent.

Pérez was removed in 1993 through constitutional channels on corruption charges, and Rafael Caldera won the presidency with a plurality in an election that showed the decline of AD and COPEI. In 1994 Caldera’s government dismissed the proceedings against the rebels on condition that they leave the army, and Chávez left Yare as a civilian.

Between 1994 and 1998 Chávez moved from conspiracy to elections. After debate within his movement, including a period of active abstention, he founded the MVR in 1997. The banking crisis of 1994, the adjustment of 1996, and low oil prices deepened disillusionment, though voters rejected the old parties for different reasons and without agreeing on an alternative.

On 6 December 1998 Chávez won 56.20 percent of valid votes against 39.97 percent for Salas Römer, in an election observed by the Carter Center. The result was a democratic repudiation of the established parties, not the completion of the 1992 coup, and the transfer of power on 2 February 1999 was peaceful.

In 1999 the government used referendums and an elected Constituent Assembly to replace the 1961 constitution. Approval among those who voted was large each time, while participation was often low, so that approval among all registered voters was roughly a third. The Assembly’s lopsided composition and its claim to original authority led to conflict with Congress and the courts, and the constitution it produced was approved on 15 December 1999 and took effect on 30 December.

The constitution expanded participation, through referendums and the recall mechanism, and recognized Indigenous rights and social rights. It also lengthened the presidential term, allowed reelection, abolished the Senate, and created institutions whose independence depended on appointments. Historians weigh these features differently, and many conclude that the document did both things.

After the 2000 elections renewed his mandate, Chávez’s government reorganized the judiciary and other institutions, expanded the armed forces’ public role, and clashed with business, labor, and media. Polarization crossed class lines though it was shaped by class, race, and neighborhood. The 49 decree-laws of November 2001 and the December strike showed how disputes over executive authority and national development had hardened.

In early 2002 military officers called for Chávez’s resignation, the PDVSA dispute escalated, and the opposition marched on 11 April. Shootings near the palace killed at least nineteen people and wounded many more, and responsibility remains disputed. On 12 April officers detained Chávez, and Pedro Carmona decreed the dissolution of the National Assembly, the Supreme Tribunal, and other institutions. This was an unconstitutional interruption of an elected government.

The interim regime collapsed after popular mobilization and the refusal of military units to accept it, and Chávez returned on 14 April. The restoration did not end the disputes. It left a society more polarized, an opposition damaged by its association with the coup, and a government that claimed vindication. Chapter 107 follows how conflict over PDVSA, oil revenue, and social programs defined the next phase.

A body elected to write or rewrite a constitution; in 1999 the Asamblea Nacional Constituyente of 131 members.
Poder constituyente
Constituent power, the power to create a constitution, attributed in democratic theory to the people.
Poderes constituidos
Constituted powers, such as presidents, legislatures, and courts, which exist under and are limited by a constitution.
Consultative referendum
A popular vote asking citizens their opinion, used in April 1999 to authorize the Assembly.
Recall referendum
A vote on whether to remove an elected official before the end of the term, provided in Article 72 of the 1999 constitution.
Participatory democracy
The idea, stressed in the 1999 constitution, that citizens should take part directly in decisions beyond voting.
Bolivarian Republic
The name given to Venezuela by the 1999 constitution, replacing Republic of Venezuela.
Citizen Power
The fourth branch created by the 1999 constitution, comprising the attorney general, comptroller general, and ombudsman.
Electoral Power
The fifth branch of government, represented by the National Electoral Council (CNE).
Tribunal Supremo de Justicia
The Supreme Tribunal of Justice, created in 1999 to replace the Supreme Court.
Relegitimation
The process of renewing all elected offices under the new constitution, carried out in the elections of July 2000.
Plan Bolívar 2000
A 2000 program that used soldiers for public works and food distribution in poor communities.
Ley habilitante
An enabling law delegating to the president power to issue decrees with the force of law for a limited time.
Decree-law
A norm issued by the executive under delegated authority, with the force of law.
Fedecámaras
Venezuela’s main business federation, headed in 2002 by Pedro Carmona.
CTV
The Confederación de Trabajadores de Venezuela, the country’s largest labor confederation, historically tied to AD.
Bolivarian Circles
Neighborhood groups of government supporters organized from 2001, described as community organizations by members and as partisan groups by critics.
Cadena
A mandatory simultaneous broadcast of an official address on all television and radio stations.
Paro
A work stoppage; in 2001–2003 a contested term for the opposition’s general stoppages.
Interim government
A temporary administration; here, the short-lived regime of Pedro Carmona on 12–13 April 2002.
Constitutional order
The system of institutions and procedures established by a constitution, including rules for succession and removal.
Polarization
The division of a society into opposed camps with declining trust and communication across them.
Vacuum of power
The interpretation adopted by Venezuela’s Supreme Tribunal in 2002 that the presidency had been vacant on 12 April, which critics contested.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a political order that appeared stable could lose legitimacy within a decade, and how the people who replaced it came from inside the institutions that had failed. The armed forces, once a pillar of the Punto Fijo settlement, produced the rebel who became its most effective challenger. The chapter also shows that legitimacy has several sources, including elections, legality, participation, and institutional restraint, and that a government can hold some while contesting others.

The decade’s lessons are not simple. Chávez’s victories were democratic, his rebellion was not, the constitution he sponsored expanded rights and concentrated power, and the coup that removed him was unconstitutional whatever the failures of his government. Holding these statements together is the historian’s task, and refusing any one of them distorts the record.

The chapter also connects Venezuela to the wider region. The crisis of party systems, the use of constituent assemblies, and the tension between participation and executive power appeared in Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and elsewhere, and Chapter 108 compares these paths. The oil economy that underlay Venezuelan politics meant that the next stage of conflict would be fought over PDVSA and the distribution of petroleum revenue, the subject of Chapter 107, and the polarization that hardened in 2002 would shape Venezuelan politics through the collapse examined in Chapter 109.
