CJNG, Fentanyl, Synthetic Drugs, and Transnational Organized Crime

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: From Fields to Chemical Markets

On February 22, 2026, Mexican soldiers killed the founder of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel. The organization he led had been built on a different economy from the one that made the cartels of the 1980s.

Earlier chapters followed Mexican trafficking from the Guadalajara organization to Sinaloa, Juárez, the Gulf Cartel, and Los Zetas, and then examined the militarized state response and its human costs. This chapter studies a change in the underlying economy. Organizations once tied to opium poppy, cannabis, and the transit of Andean cocaine became increasingly involved in synthetic drugs: methamphetamine and, above all, illicitly manufactured fentanyl. These products depend less on land and harvests and more on commercially traded chemicals, small production sites, and financial channels that cross several continents.

The Jalisco New Generation Cartel (Cártel de Jalisco Nueva Generación, CJNG) is the central case. It emerged around 2010–2011 from the wreckage of older Sinaloa-linked networks and expanded across much of Mexico in the following decade. It is also easy to misdescribe. It was not a single perfectly centralized company, it did not produce all illicit fentanyl, and its rise cannot by itself explain U.S. overdose mortality. Treating it as a franchise-like network with a powerful founder, a family core, and many semi-autonomous regional operators is closer to what the evidence supports.

The evidence is uneven. Indictments, sanctions notices, and government designations state allegations or legal conclusions for specific purposes. Journalism and think-tank work reconstruct structure but often rely on officials. Public-health data measure deaths, not trafficking. Seizures reflect enforcement as well as supply. This chapter therefore returns repeatedly to a method question: what can each kind of source establish?

The chapter stops at the evidence cutoff of November 15, 2026. Events of 2025–2026, including the February 2025 terrorism designations and the death of Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, are recent and incompletely documented; the chapter marks what is established and what remains uncertain.

Editorial cutoff: November 15, 2026. Retrospective government descriptions, sanctions decisions, intelligence assessments, criminal charges and statistical estimates have different evidentiary weight; each is identified in the source studies.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace CJNG's emergence from Milenio-linked networks and its expansion after 2010.
  2. Distinguish centralized organization, family enterprise, and loose network as models of CJNG.
  3. Compare agricultural and synthetic drug supply chains.
  4. Explain the separate histories of methamphetamine and fentanyl markets.
  5. Analyze the role of chemical commerce and financial networks without reducing them to a single country's responsibility.
  6. Assess what CDC overdose statistics can and cannot show about trafficking.
  7. Distinguish allegations, indictments, designations, and convictions.
  8. Interpret the 2025 FTO and SDGT designations as legal instruments.
  9. Reconstruct the established facts of the February 22, 2026 operation and the uncertainties after it.
  10. Evaluate whether synthetic drugs transformed organized crime or accelerated existing change.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a new organization emerge in Jalisco rather than simply a reshuffling of older Sinaloa-linked groups?
  2. What does it mean to call CJNG one organization?
  3. How did the shift from harvested to synthetic drugs change who could participate in the market?
  4. Why does a fall in overdose deaths not prove a fall in criminal revenue?
  5. How should historians weigh sanctions notices, indictments, and journalism?
  6. What did the 2025 designations change legally, and what did they leave unchanged?
  7. What can the death of a founder-leader tell us about the organization he led?
  8. In what ways do Mexico, the United States, China, and other states share responsibility for the synthetic-drug economy?

Historical Context

Mexican organized crime grew out of a century of prohibition. Cannabis and opium poppy were grown in Sinaloa, Durango, Chihuahua, and Guerrero; Chapter 101 traced how political protection structured those markets and how the Guadalajara organization linked them to Colombian cocaine. Chapter 102 followed the fragmentation of that system after the 1980s into rival organizations with territorial corridors. Chapter 103 examined the militarized offensive launched in 2006, which coincided with leadership decapitations, splintering, and rising violence and disappearances.

Three developments set the stage for the present chapter. First, the removal of leaders repeatedly produced new organizations rather than simply ending old ones. Second, demand in the United States shifted: prescription-opioid misuse, then heroin, then illicit fentanyl reshaped the market; methamphetamine remained a durable stimulant market. Third, global chemical trade expanded, creating legitimate industries whose products could be diverted. These conditions, not any single organization, define the synthetic-drug era.

Division I — The Rise and Expansion of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel, c. 2010–2020
Section 104.1

From the Milenio Organization to CJNG

The Milenio organization, led by the Valencia family and associates such as Óscar Orlando Nava Valencia, operated in Michoacán and Jalisco and worked with Sinaloa-linked networks. Ignacio Coronel, a major Sinaloa figure in Jalisco, was killed by Mexican forces in July 2010. Agency histories, including the DEA's, place CJNG's emergence in the following period, among operators who had belonged to Milenio-linked networks. Some press accounts give earlier dates and describe a group of defectors from Sinaloa; sources differ, and the chapter treats the founding as a process of reorganization over several years rather than a single event.

Attributing CJNG to one man oversimplifies. Leadership losses opened opportunities, but regional alliances, local police and political relationships, and access to ports and roads shaped who could build a lasting organization in Jalisco.

The organizational genealogy begins before the name CJNG. The Valencia family's trafficking relationships in Michoacán and neighboring states crossed into the markets controlled or influenced by the Sinaloa federation. Connections with Ignacio Coronel Villarreal made Jalisco an important area of cooperation, but the word federation can mislead: criminal alliances were informal and vulnerable to arrests, killings, changing prices, and personal quarrels. The Mexican state did not confront a fixed chessboard. An investigation or leadership death could redistribute authority among rivals, while local police and political patrons remained highly consequential. This history helps explain why an organization could be both new and composed of experienced participants.

In the aftermath of Coronel's death in July 2010, different local actors sought to retain access to established trafficking relationships and regional markets. Public reporting sometimes traces CJNG to an earlier group labeled the Mata Zetas and sometimes describes the organization as emerging from a Milenio split. These terms refer to overlapping phenomena rather than a documented corporate founding ceremony. Retrospective official assessments often turn a messy sequence into a clear timeline because they need to identify a target for sanctions or investigations. A historical reconstruction instead asks when the name appeared, whose interests it represented, and how much operational coordination can actually be demonstrated at each point.

Jalisco offered advantages beyond its symbolic association with a future leader. Guadalajara was a major commercial and financial city with transport connections, migrant networks, and legitimate businesses. Other places in the west combined coastal access, agricultural production, and connections to central Mexico. Those opportunities did not by themselves create CJNG; many firms and communities used the same legitimate infrastructure. Organized crime depended on repeated choices by a small number of people, access to corrupt intermediaries, and the willingness or capacity to use coercion. The historical question is how particular networks captured commercial opportunities while competing against older criminal organizations and state institutions.

The name New Generation advertised discontinuity with earlier networks, although that claim itself was strategic. Branding could attract recruits, communicate threats, and help disparate operators present a united front; it did not establish that all people using the name answered to a single central headquarters. This distinction guards against writing the organization's subsequent influence backward into the year 2010. Later documents that refer to CJNG as a powerful national organization are evidence for the period they describe, not automatic proof of identical capabilities during its emergence.

This section therefore treats the early history as a transition from interconnected Milenio and Sinaloa-linked relationships toward a recognizably separate organization. The process unfolded amid Mexico's post-2006 security offensive discussed in Chapter 103, yet CJNG was not simply a government-created consequence or a spontaneous rebellion against an old cartel. It developed through the interaction of enforcement pressure, organizational knowledge, market demand, and territorial contests. Historians must compare agency genealogies, court materials, local reporting, and scholarship rather than accept any one institutional account as exhaustive.

Historical Vocabulary
Decapitation strategy

Targeting the leaders of criminal organizations through arrest or killing. In Mexico, successive removals were often followed by splintering and by new organizations such as CJNG. Whether removal weakens or merely reorganizes an enterprise depends on its structure, which is why this chapter treats the 2026 death of El Mencho cautiously.

Section 104.2

Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, Los Cuinis, and Organizational Structure

Oseguera Cervantes, a former police officer in Jalisco by most accounts, became CJNG's best-known leader. His family, and the González Valencia family associated with the financial network Los Cuinis, appear in U.S. sanctions documents as central to the organization's money management. Those documents are administrative findings made for sanctions purposes; they are not criminal verdicts.

Three models help students think about structure. A centrally coordinated organization has a command hierarchy. A family enterprise rests on kinship trust, especially in finance. A network of local actors operates under a shared brand, paying for protection, supply, or weapons. Analysts commonly describe CJNG as combining a family-linked financial core with regional commanders of considerable autonomy, which helps explain both its rapid expansion and its resilience. The evidence does not allow a definitive organizational chart.

Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes became widely known as El Mencho, but public recognition of a leader is not the same as direct observation of how orders moved through an organization. Accounts of his biography include varying details of early employment, residence, and service in policing; an academic chapter should identify the issuing authority when repeating them. Court records and sanctions releases can reveal alleged associations, communications, and financial interests, but they are usually assembled to support a particular legal action. Even a large body of prosecutions cannot yield a complete map of a clandestine enterprise whose participants have incentives to mislead officials and competitors.

The González Valencia family is prominent in U.S. Treasury documentation concerning Los Cuinis, an organization associated with CJNG's financing. Kinship can reduce some problems of trust in illicit business, but it does not eliminate competing interests, nor does it prove that every family member performed an operational role. Claims of a single unified criminal family should be tested against dated sanctions measures and individual legal proceedings. The administrative act of placing a person or company on a sanctions list is a government determination under specified legal criteria; it is not a substitute for a criminal trial or proof of every publicly alleged relationship.

Three organizational levels are useful for teaching. Senior figures may set broad priorities, maintain alliances, or control portions of capital. Regional commanders and business partners may exercise considerable discretion about local opportunities and conflicts. Peripheral associates may conduct transactions or use a recognized name without sharing the leadership's full agenda. These levels can overlap, and particular relationships may change in months rather than years. Describing all three as a single organization is convenient for law enforcement and public discussion but can obscure the internal boundaries of responsibility.

The organizational form also helps explain persistence under pressure. Removing a public leader may interrupt personal alliances or financial coordination, while commercial relationships and regional coercive capacity survive. Conversely, the existence of apparently autonomous regional groups can create dangerous competition after a leadership shock. Neither automatic collapse nor seamless succession follows from decentralization. Evidence about leadership should therefore be dated and attributed, especially after the February 2026 death that provides a later case study in Section 104.19. It is analytically possible for an organization to remain economically significant while its command structure is contested.

Students should also distinguish rhetorical centralization from demonstrated authority. Governments sometimes speak of a cartel as one enemy; groups themselves may promote a unified brand to intimidate rivals. Scholars such as those examining networked organized crime ask instead which relationships withstand arrests, where financing is allocated, and which participants can actually compel behavior elsewhere. This approach makes the history less cinematic but more accurate. It preserves the agency of Mexican institutions, regional communities, and rival organizations instead of treating a single famous individual as the only motor of change.

Section 104.3

Territorial Expansion, Rival Organizations, and Regional Criminal Markets

During the 2010s CJNG expanded from Jalisco into Colima, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Veracruz, and other states, confronting Sinaloa-linked groups, the Knights Templar, and local organizations. Alliances shifted: groups that fought one another in one period cooperated in another. Contemporary maps of influence compress these changes into a single image; students should date their evidence.

Expansion often followed the economic assets of a region: ports, highways, fuel pipelines, avocado and agricultural production, and mining areas. Control was frequently contested rather than complete, and local police and officials could be allies, victims, or both.

Territorial influence is best reconstructed through a chronological sequence, not a map shaded uniformly in one color. A criminal group may maintain a presence in a municipality, negotiate with another organization, intimidate a few officials, or dominate a particular business; these are different kinds of influence. Official assessments sometimes group them under the phrase areas of operation. Students comparing documents from 2012, 2015, 2020, and 2025 should ask whether the map records trafficking incidents, prosecutions, violent disputes, or intelligence judgments. The criteria often remain partly confidential and should not be equated with public authority over every resident.

Western Mexico was especially significant because the fortunes of CJNG intersected with those of local organizations in Michoacán, Colima, Jalisco, and Guanajuato. The collapse or fragmentation of a regional rival could open a short-lived opportunity but also generate new armed factions. An apparent alliance might be narrower than it looked, applying only to a particular locality or enterprise. This is why detailed histories of the Knights Templar and other Michoacán movements matter as context without becoming another chapter of organizational biographies. Local disputes were shaped by their own political histories, grievances, and patterns of security intervention.

Regional economies offered several sources of leverage. Commercial ports connected Mexico to international trade, highways connected ports and population centers, and farming or mining districts supported legitimate employment and investment. Criminal organizations seeking rents from these activities often imposed costs on people outside the drug trade. The economic value of a place does not prove that it was controlled by CJNG, and statements of control must be tied to evidence and time. A port may be a target of illegal commerce and an institution in which thousands of honest workers operate under laws designed to prevent corruption and violence.

The geography of competition also changed as synthetic drugs became important. Industrial and commercial supply networks could matter alongside the rural territories associated with earlier poppy and marijuana economies. Yet describing synthetic drugs as placeless would be inaccurate. Production still required people, access to material resources, transport, finance, and opportunities to hide illicit activity. Those requirements connected transnational markets to particular Mexican municipalities where residents faced threats, displacement, and the risks of contamination. A product's physical compactness does not erase its social geography.

A defensible map study should therefore distinguish four layers: historical sites of organizational emergence, documented contemporary centers of activity, major legitimate transport and commercial geography, and areas of conflict whose organizational attribution remains contested. Comparing these layers over time allows students to ask why the organization expanded while avoiding the false inference that every person in a region consented to its presence. The exercise should also include the role of local security institutions and civilian organizations responding to violence, restoring the social landscape that a map of alleged criminal territory usually omits.

Section 104.4

CJNG's Confrontation with the Mexican State and the Limits of Centralized Control

In 2015 CJNG gunmen shot down a military helicopter in Jalisco, an attack in which nine people were killed according to Associated Press accounts, and blockades and arson spread across the state. The episode showed willingness to confront federal forces. It also illustrated the organization's use of coordinated disruption, a pattern seen again in 2026.

On June 26, 2020, gunmen attacked Mexico City police chief Omar García Harfuch, who survived. Mexican and U.S. authorities attributed the attack to CJNG; specific charges and attributions are allegations pending or resolved in particular proceedings. The incident is evidence that Mexican officials considered CJNG capable of operating in the capital, but it does not by itself establish the organization's structure or command.

Corruption and intimidation of local authorities remained part of the picture; Chapter 103 treats the wider militarization and its human costs.

The 2015 Jalisco confrontation marked a change in the organization's public profile. Reports of barricades, vehicle fires, and an attack on a Mexican military helicopter suggested that CJNG-associated gunmen could carry out coordinated actions against heavily armed state personnel. A dramatic event, however, does not establish the group's capabilities in every region or the precise internal chain of command responsible. Casualty totals also depend on whether they count those killed in a specific attack, fatalities across several incidents, or later deaths. The chapter should preserve the distinction between contemporary reports and retrospective official summaries.

Mexico's armed forces, federal police, prosecutors, and state security agencies did not share identical functions or powers. A military encounter might produce immediate tactical results without yielding admissible criminal evidence or sustained civilian protection. Where officials were corrupt or threatened, even reliable national enforcement efforts could have uneven local effects. Chapter 103 examined how recurring deployments reshaped democratic accountability. Here the question is narrower: what did the state's response to CJNG reveal about the organization's tactics, political ambitions, and ability to survive successive interventions?

The June 2020 attack against Mexico City security official Omar García Harfuch placed the organization's name into a national debate. Authorities attributed responsibility to CJNG and pursued particular suspects. A historian must still differentiate attribution by investigators, proceedings involving individual defendants, and the broader claim that a leader personally ordered every component of an operation. Public statements about responsibility may carry institutional incentives, while prosecutorial evidence is constrained by rules of admissibility and the stage of proceedings. Treating these levels carefully is not skepticism for its own sake; it prevents an allegation from silently becoming an established fact.

Violence may function as a message to competitors and to the state, but the message rarely has only one audience. Residents experience disrupted mobility and threatened livelihoods; government agencies face pressure to appear effective; rival groups may interpret attacks as signs of both strength and vulnerability. News coverage often foregrounds spectacular scenes because quieter forms of coercion are harder to document. Local business owners may face extortion long before national audiences learn a criminal organization's name. The historian's focus should include those everyday constraints, although the detailed history of disappearances belongs to Chapter 103.

The limits of centralized control emerge from these episodes. Coordinated actions can coexist with local autonomy, and continued violence after an operation may reflect planned retaliation, improvisation, or multiple actors borrowing a common identity. It follows that official claims about the success of leadership-targeting measures must be assessed over time, against changes in violence, financial activity, investigations, and the security of affected communities. The 2015 and 2020 episodes also establish useful comparisons for February 2026, when the removal of the most prominent leader prompted intense immediate retaliation but did not settle the organization's longer-term prospects.

Division II — The Synthetic-Drug Transformation
Section 104.5

From Agricultural Drug Commodities to Synthetic Production

Cannabis and opium poppy require land, seasonal labor, and exposure to eradication; cocaine passed through Mexico as a transit product. Synthetic drugs can be made in small facilities from commercial chemicals, are less visible from the air, and are compact relative to their value. They also depend on access to chemical suppliers and on technical knowledge. These differences altered who could enter the market and how enforcement might respond. They did not eliminate older markets: cocaine and heroin trafficking continued, and methamphetamine was produced in Mexico long before CJNG existed. This chapter deliberately omits chemical and production details.

For much of the twentieth century, Mexican narcotics history was rooted in cultivation, cross-border movement, and the transit of substances grown elsewhere. Poppy farmers, cannabis producers, wholesalers, and transport intermediaries occupied different positions within chains that linked rural poverty and international demand. Cocaine came largely from Andean production regions, while Mexico developed into a major transit territory. The rise of synthetics did not replace this history at once. Instead, existing organizations adapted established relationships, legal vulnerabilities, and financing practices to markets in which the commodity need not be harvested from fields.

The key difference is the role of globally traded industrial materials. A conventional harvest is affected by seasons, climate, land access, and eradication. Synthetic-drug enterprises are also constrained, but more by access to commercial chemical markets, technical personnel, financial services, and enforcement of chemical regulation. This distinction must not be overstated: illegal synthetic production has a physical footprint, and chemical commerce is heavily intertwined with lawful industries. Nor was there a single historical moment when all traffickers switched products. Cannabis, heroin, methamphetamine, cocaine, and fentanyl coexisted in changing proportions.

The older story also corrects a common misconception that Mexico entered synthetic-drug commerce because CJNG invented it. Methamphetamine trafficking and production expanded long before CJNG's appearance, and the history of prescription-opioid consumption in the United States also predated the rapid growth of illicit fentanyl markets. Later expansion depended on these earlier processes. A comparative chronology should examine regulatory changes, changes in consuming populations, and the evolution of supply relationships rather than attributing every shift to a cartel leadership decision. Political economy explains more than a list of prominent names.

The social consequences of production differed by region. In communities reliant on agricultural commodities, changing markets could undermine local incomes or leave cultivation systems under pressure. Elsewhere the risks associated with clandestine industrial activity affected workers and neighborhoods. No single livelihood pattern describes Mexico as a whole. Employment in legitimate chemical manufacture, transportation, or international trade does not itself imply criminal involvement. The historian must avoid language that criminalizes ordinary industrial sectors simply because materials circulating in them can also be misused.

A historical comparison therefore needs more than a list of commodities. It should track land dependence, workforce organization, regulatory exposure, the visibility of supply chains, the relationship to international prices, and the ways police and health agencies measured each market. A seizure of one quantity of a substance cannot be compared directly with another without considering purity, market significance, and reporting practices. This framework allows students to see why synthetic drugs altered incentives while refusing the false conclusion that criminal violence, corruption, and demand suddenly began with chemical commerce.

Section 104.6

Methamphetamine, Mexican Criminal Organizations, and Changing Consumer Markets

Methamphetamine has its own history. Mexican organizations expanded large-scale production as U.S. regulation constrained domestic small-scale manufacture of earlier decades; CJNG later became a major participant. The drug harms individuals and communities independently of fentanyl, and CDC data show psychostimulant-involved deaths remaining large even as opioid deaths fell: about 29,852 in 2024 and 26,181 in 2025 by provisional estimate. Seizures and consumption estimates are not the same thing, a point developed in 104.16.

Methamphetamine's place in this history is distinct from that of fentanyl. It is a stimulant, not an opioid, and its medical, regulatory, and consumer histories followed different paths. Concern over stimulant misuse in the United States preceded the synthetic-opioid crisis; production and distribution shifted as enforcement altered domestic and international markets. Mexican criminal organizations participated in that evolution through arrangements developed well before CJNG became a household name. Failing to distinguish the two substances obscures how historical regulatory choices shaped separate markets and how public-health responses must address different forms of harm.

In the 1990s and 2000s, restrictions on particular commercial materials and enforcement against domestic production altered the landscape in the United States. Larger cross-border supply relationships became increasingly significant. This historical development should be analyzed without giving technical manufacturing details: the relevant questions are who controlled financing, how legitimate regulatory changes influenced criminal opportunity, and whether demand followed or preceded changes in availability. Documents from different agencies emphasize different causal mechanisms, so a textbook should not treat a single regulatory intervention as the entire explanation.

CJNG's participation developed alongside networks associated with Sinaloa and other groups. Government threat assessments describe the importance of methamphetamine alongside fentanyl and cocaine, but they do not provide a transparent accounting of each organization's complete revenues. Even a large number of seizures attributed to a particular organization may reflect concentrated law-enforcement operations or investigative priorities. In addition, regional wholesalers and independent distributors can move products linked to more than one upstream supplier. An organization-centered narrative must make room for the commercial relationships that extend beyond its membership.

The human consequences are significant and do not become less important because opioid deaths attract more attention. Stimulant-related emergencies, chronic health problems, and deaths involving several substances put pressure on hospitals, outreach services, and families. The CDC's provisional 2025 estimates placed psychostimulant-involved overdose deaths at 26,181, compared with 29,852 in 2024; these were drug-category counts, not the number of people who consumed methamphetamine in either year. They were also not exclusive categories, because toxicology investigations can identify several drugs in the same death. The difference between a death certificate and a measure of prevalence is fundamental.

A useful comparison is between the public discussion of methamphetamine around the turn of the century and the later debate over illicit fentanyl. Both involved cross-border criminal markets, but each generated different medical risks and forms of political attention. Neither is adequately understood through stereotypes about users or by treating every person with substance-use disorder as a criminal. Historians can study legal, economic, and institutional change while recognizing the agency and circumstances of people seeking treatment. The synthetic-drug transformation was a change in market structure, not evidence that one drug replaced all others.

Section 104.7

Fentanyl: From Pharmaceutical Opioid to Major Illicit Market

Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid used legitimately in medicine. Illicitly manufactured fentanyl is a different supply chain, appearing in U.S. markets first as an adulterant in heroin and then in counterfeit pills made to resemble prescription medications. Its potency means small quantities can be lethal, and consumers often could not know what a pill contained. The Congressional Research Service identifies approximately 2019 as a turning point after which Mexico became a major source of U.S.-bound illicit fentanyl. This does not mean all fentanyl was made by one organization; several Mexican groups, including Sinaloa-linked factions and CJNG, have been implicated, and the allocation of market share is uncertain.

Fentanyl has long had legitimate applications in anesthesia and pain management, under regulated medical supervision. The existence of a medical product is not itself an explanation of the illicit market that grew much later. Illicitly manufactured fentanyl developed through different commercial relationships and appeared in products sold without reliable disclosure of contents or strength. It entered an existing U.S. landscape of prescription-opioid dependence, changing prescribing practices, and heroin use. These histories overlap without becoming identical. Distinguishing medical and illicit supply prevents both needless stigmatization of patients and serious misunderstanding of the public-health emergency.

The illicit market evolved in stages rather than beginning with a single cartel decree. Different sources and regions experienced different changes, including the substitution of illicit fentanyl for heroin in some markets and its presence in counterfeit tablets or mixtures of substances. Many consumers were not able to determine the contents of what they bought. Public-health agencies increasingly used toxicology data, drug-checking reports, and mortality statistics to observe these changes. Yet none of these sources provides a complete history of how many producers, wholesalers, and distributors participated at any moment.

Congressional Research Service analysis identifies approximately 2019 as an important transition in Mexico's prominence as a source of U.S.-bound illicit fentanyl. The qualification matters: it is an interpretation of changing trafficking patterns, not an exact date at which a supply chain switched on. Mexican organizations had older connections to U.S. markets and Colombia's cocaine trade, while upstream commercial networks involved suppliers, brokers, and entities in multiple countries. It is therefore more accurate to describe growing Mexican participation in an international market than to claim that an entire industry began in one locality.

The economic significance of a potent product cannot be translated directly into a historical claim about cartel profit. Wholesale and retail markets have different prices; enforcement and purity affect estimates; and participants may sell several substances. Officials who describe CJNG and Sinaloa-linked groups as major suppliers are providing important investigative assessments, but their reports do not necessarily establish precise market shares. The continued presence of independent distributors and other organizations also means that a decrease in overdose mortality cannot be used as an uncomplicated proxy for the financial condition of a particular Mexican network.

The human consequences require particular care. Risk derives partly from unknowable composition, variations between products, polysubstance exposure, and uneven access to treatment and emergency response. A public-health analysis should distinguish drug-use prevalence, overdose incidents, fatal overdoses, and deaths involving specific substances. The increase in fentanyl deaths during the late 2010s and early 2020s was a major historical development; the subsequent fall in 2024–2025 counts was another. Neither alone resolves the causes of changing markets or the effectiveness of competing security policies. Those questions return in the chapter's quantitative studies.

Section 104.8

Precursor Chemicals, International Commerce, and the Global Supply Chain

Synthetic drugs depend on industrial chemicals traded legitimately in large volumes. U.S. government investigations, including a December 2025 GAO report, document links among suppliers in several countries, Mexican producers, and money-laundering channels. China and India appear in official accounts as significant sources of relevant chemicals, and diversion within Mexico and the United States has also been documented. International scheduling of chemicals and bilateral cooperation changed incentives over time, but suppliers adapted. The chapter does not describe operational methods; its subject is the governance of a dual-use trade.

Synthetic-drug production connects illicit enterprises to a vast legitimate chemical economy. Manufacturers supply industries ranging from pharmaceuticals to agriculture, sanitation, and industrial processing. Criminal procurement and diversion can exploit this scale, but legitimate trade should not be described as an illicit system by association. The central governance problem is distinguishing lawful commerce from transactions intended to support prohibited production, without imposing unrealistic or indiscriminate burdens on honest firms. That challenge crosses national borders because chemical manufacturing, international finance, and transport are organized through different legal jurisdictions.

The December 2025 Government Accountability Office report identified China as the primary source country for many relevant chemicals in U.S. investigative accounts, with India also appearing in official assessments. These are statements about documented supply patterns, not claims that every Chinese or Indian supplier is complicit, nor that the governments of those countries direct illicit commerce. The report combined law-enforcement documentation with interviews and acknowledged limits to what investigators could confirm. Students should ask how agency selection of cases shapes the picture and whether alternative channels remain less visible.

Regulators face the additional problem that commercial chemicals have multiple legitimate uses. International drug-control conventions and national scheduling decisions can restrict specified substances, but regulatory systems must adapt as markets and supply relationships change. Policy effectiveness requires cooperation among customs, financial regulators, commercial firms, chemical-industry bodies, police, prosecutors, and health agencies. A legally restricted item is not the same as a legally prohibited commercial sector. Treating chemical regulation as a straightforward police operation would overlook the importance of oversight capacity, reliable documentation, and international coordination.

The GAO report also describes payment and financial relationships associated with procurement, but this chapter's emphasis is institutional rather than operational. Investigators use financial records, court processes, and cooperation with regulated firms to identify suspicious activity; the existence of a payment in an ordinary financial system is not evidence of wrongdoing by the financial provider. Cases summarized from indictments should retain the presumption of innocence. A documentary source window should distinguish what GAO independently found, what it reported from other agencies, and what remained an allegation about identified defendants.

A visual study using the GAO report can compare its public diagrams with its written methodological notes. The figures illustrate broad relationships among legally traded inputs, Mexican illicit markets, and U.S. consumers, but simplified arrows can make distinct intermediaries look like a fixed chain. Students should interpret the image as a government analytical representation, not a photograph of actual movement or a complete census of actors. They should ask which people and businesses are shown, which disappear from view, and how the figure would change if drawn from Mexican public-health or community perspectives.

Section 104.9

Cross-Border Drug Markets and the Structure of Transnational Distribution

Mexican organizations typically sell to wholesalers and distribution groups rather than controlling retail sale in the United States. Most seizures at the border occur at official crossings, where commercial traffic is heavy, according to U.S. government reporting, which complicates the image of a sealed border penetrated only by tunnels or desert routes. U.S. distributors, gangs, and consumers are participants in the market, not bystanders.

The international movement of illicit drugs depends on multiple kinds of commercial relationships, which should not be collapsed into a single smuggling organization. Upstream suppliers, Mexican wholesalers, cross-border intermediaries, and distributors operating inside the United States may cooperate without sharing one managerial hierarchy. DEA and GAO assessments describe Mexican transnational organizations as major sources of fentanyl and methamphetamine in the United States while also emphasizing that U.S.-based groups commonly undertake local distribution. This division of functions matters when discussing responsibility, prosecution, and the geographical reach of any named organization.

Legal international trade creates the setting in which regulatory agencies work, but most commercial activity is lawful. Mexico and the United States exchange goods and services through enormous regulated systems, and millions of travelers cross their border for work, family, and business. Official assessments indicate that much fentanyl detected at the southwest border is seized at legal ports of entry. Such statistics describe where interdiction occurred; they do not establish the precise proportion of all illicit movement that used one location. Neither a port-of-entry count nor an image of a distant border crossing provides a complete estimate of trafficking routes or market volume.

National discussions often depict the border as a barrier between a producer country and a consuming country. Historically it has been a shared economic space, with participants and institutions on both sides. U.S. demand, domestic wholesale relationships, distribution organizations, financial services, and firearms markets are part of that history. Mexico's institutional choices and criminal violence are equally important. A balanced account rejects the idea that one country merely exports harm while the other merely receives it. It also rejects the assumption that responsibility is uniform among governments, companies, and individual consumers.

Distribution is best studied as a relationship rather than a map of fixed corridors. Court cases identify particular contacts, organizations, and transactions, but their existence does not establish that an accused network controlled a whole city. The relationship between a Mexican supplier and an American distributor may be durable in one case and temporary in another. Marketing claims made by criminal actors are especially unreliable. Differences among a cartel affiliate, independent purchaser, and local gang are important for explaining why leadership changes in Mexico do not instantly terminate U.S. retail markets.

For the geographic study, students should compare dated government assessments of Mexican regional activity with a general map of major trading centers, transport networks, and the U.S. population. The purpose is to identify economic scale and political jurisdiction, not to reconstruct concealed routes. Students should ask which movements officials can observe, which figures are derived from seizures, and how geographical emphasis changes across agencies. The outcome should be an understanding of transnational commerce as a multi-actor system rather than a diagram implying a direct command line from Guadalajara to every U.S. neighborhood.

Section 104.10

CJNG beyond Mexico: International Connections and Regional Variation

Prosecutions and reports connect CJNG-linked traffickers or business partners to Latin America, Europe, and Asia-Pacific. Students should distinguish branches that answer to the organization, independent traffickers who buy from it, and individual criminal cases. Claims of global control are not supported by that distinction; the better-evidenced picture is a network of commercial relationships varying by region.

International investigations have associated CJNG with people and businesses outside Mexico, yet the meaning of such connections varies. A foreign distributor may purchase products from an intermediary, a financial actor may assist several unrelated criminal groups, and a person facing charges abroad may have only a limited relationship with a Mexican organization. An indictment can show an alleged agreement among specified defendants; it does not necessarily identify a permanent institutional branch. Scholarly writing must preserve these distinctions if it is to avoid exaggerating the global reach of an organization whose very structure is disputed.

North America remains the principal setting for the synthetic-opioid market discussed here, but documents also identify connections involving Central and South America, parts of Europe, and the Asia-Pacific region. Different criminal commodities produce different international relationships: cocaine sourcing connects to older Andean markets, chemical commerce connects to several industrial economies, and money laundering connects to financial services located in multiple jurisdictions. A claim of presence in a country could therefore refer to procurement, finance, transit, distribution, or a particular investigation. It should never be treated as equivalent to territorial control.

The global dimension also exposes differences in law and evidence. One government may designate a person under sanctions authority while another seeks extradition or conducts a criminal prosecution. Legal cooperation can make otherwise hidden relationships visible, but records are shaped by jurisdictional priorities. Cross-border cases often emphasize elements that fall within a prosecutor's authority, leaving social and political conditions in another country underexplored. A historically accurate comparison should track who issued each document, what power it exercised, and whether the facts it asserted were tested in court.

Trade and travel are not themselves suspicious. The same ports and commercial cities that appear in drug-control records are centers of lawful exchange and employment. An analytical map can locate the general regions described in public government assessments without assigning guilt to their inhabitants. It should distinguish an alleged criminal associate living abroad from an international subsidiary and distinguish a commercial connection from a chain of command. This matters especially for historical writing that will remain online after investigations change and new evidence appears.

Finally, global criminal relationships are not merely Mexican exports. Suppliers, customers, professional enablers, and financial actors elsewhere can contribute to illicit markets, while foreign authorities may differ in investigative resources and policy goals. The transnational perspective thus broadens, rather than diminishes, Mexico's place in Latin American history. It explains why efforts concentrated exclusively on arrests in one Mexican state may have limited effect on demand, finance, and production incentives elsewhere. The chapter's institutional comparison should return repeatedly to the difference between a network's geographic reach and the actual authority of its leadership.

Section 104.11

Money Laundering, Financial Networks, and Legitimate Businesses

Illicit revenue must be moved and invested. U.S. Treasury actions against the González Valencia/Los Cuinis network describe interests in real estate, hospitality, and other businesses; these are administrative allegations supporting sanctions. Trade-based laundering and digital assets have drawn regulatory attention. Financial organization can sustain an enterprise even when armed leaders are removed, which is one reason sanctions and financial investigations accompany arrests.

Financial organization is less visible than armed enforcement, but it is central to the persistence of transnational illicit markets. Money obtained through illegal drug sales has to support participants, expenses, investments, and relationships with intermediaries. U.S. Treasury sanctions measures against Los Cuinis and other CJNG-linked actors provide an unusually detailed administrative record of alleged financial connections. They also reveal a methodological challenge: an asset freeze identifies a government determination under sanctions law, while a criminal conviction establishes findings under a different legal process. A source window should teach that distinction rather than present all named firms as judicially proven criminal enterprises.

Mexican commercial life offers opportunities for legitimate investment in property, travel, agriculture, and manufacturing. Sanctions allegations regarding hotels, property companies, and other businesses must not be generalized to those sectors. The historian's question is how the boundary between lawful commerce and organized criminal proceeds was investigated and regulated, not whether a class of ordinary businesses was inherently suspect. Communities whose jobs depend on a particular company may experience uncertainty when government action closes it or restricts its transactions. This is another way financial enforcement can have social consequences separate from criminal prosecutions.

GAO's December 2025 report discusses mechanisms through which illicit proceeds were alleged or observed to pass across borders and through financial systems. Its analysis draws on banking records, regulatory reporting, interviews, and cases selected for review. The chapter can describe the institutional vulnerabilities and oversight challenges without reproducing procedural instructions for hiding funds. Financial institutions have compliance responsibilities and can provide evidence to investigators, while individual financial transfers require context. A suspicious-activity report is a signal for possible inquiry, not proof that its subject committed a crime.

The expansion of digital payments made some relationships easier to move across jurisdictions while generating new investigative records. It would be misleading, however, to present all digital assets as criminal or to claim that technology alone explains money laundering. Traditional commercial transactions and professional intermediaries remained important. Regulatory capacity, staffing, international information sharing, and differing legal standards influence what can be detected. A count of reported suspicious transactions also depends on reporting requirements and institutional awareness; rising counts can mean increased monitoring rather than a proportional increase in illicit finance.

Family-linked financial networks matter because they can remain significant when an armed leader dies or an operational cell is disrupted. Yet a financial organization does not necessarily control the violence of every CJNG affiliate, and the relationship may change with new participants or state action. For historical comparison, ask how the same broad market was financed before and after the synthetic-drug expansion, what changed in its international relationships, and why investigators increasingly focused on companies and financial intermediaries alongside individuals. The result should be a study of institutions and accountability, not an instructional catalogue of concealment practices.

Section 104.12

Criminal Diversification: Fuel Theft, Extortion, Fraud, and Local Economies

U.S. Treasury documentation links CJNG-associated actors to fuel theft (huachicol), illicit oil commerce, fraud, and extortion alongside narcotics. International commodity sales differ from extortion of local businesses: the first depends on distant demand, the second on coercion of communities. Diversification can make an organization less dependent on any one drug market, which matters for judging the effect of lower overdose deaths on criminal revenue.

Focusing exclusively on illicit drug exports obscures the ways criminal organizations operate inside local economies. U.S. Treasury announcements have identified CJNG-associated actors with fuel theft and illicit oil commerce, while other actions describe extortion, fraud, and additional illicit businesses. The term diversification is therefore more than a metaphor. Some activities depend chiefly on international consumer demand, others on the ability to intimidate a shopkeeper, occupy an area, or exploit a weak administrative system. Revenue from one activity may support another, but the extent of cross-subsidy cannot be assumed without financial evidence.

Fuel theft is a useful historical comparison because it involves infrastructure and state revenue rather than consumer narcotics. Mexico's petroleum sector has its own political history, and problems involving stolen or fraudulently traded fuel predate the most recent Treasury actions. Criminal involvement can intersect with regulatory loopholes and corruption, but it should not be assumed that every irregular transaction involves CJNG. The same caution applies to extortion affecting agricultural enterprises. A community can suffer criminal coercion without its legitimate local industry becoming an extension of a cartel.

Extortion has different effects from wholesale drug trafficking. Its victims may be small merchants, transport workers, farmers, or other residents whose income comes from ordinary economic activity. It can alter business hours, investment decisions, school attendance, and migration. Some local groups may invoke a major organization's name to strengthen threats even where its direct control is uncertain. These distinctions are important when comparing the criminal geography of a region with patterns of formal employment and public administration. A single national estimate of cartel revenues would conceal this unequal distribution of harm.

Fraud provides another example of transnational vulnerability. U.S. agencies have identified schemes linked to actors associated with CJNG, including allegations involving timeshare transactions and other consumer-facing activity. Such allegations show how criminal entrepreneurs may exploit trust, contracts, and distant jurisdictions. They also show why victims are not limited to drug users or the residents of areas experiencing armed violence. The documentary evidence should specify which schemes were alleged, which defendants were charged, and what outcomes courts actually established. Claims of multibillion-dollar turnover are often estimates, not audited accounts.

Diversification complicates the evaluation of security policy. A decline in the sale of one drug or a fall in overdose deaths may change market incentives without ending extortion or illicit fuel commerce. Conversely, a successful financial investigation may affect networks whose activity does not appear in drug-seizure tables. The chapter should not infer precise organizational revenue from isolated actions or assume that every local market is centrally directed. Comparing criminal commodities reveals a recurring historical pattern: organizations survive through relationships with political institutions, legitimate economies, and vulnerable populations, not solely through control of one forbidden product.

Division IV — Criminal Competition, Public Health, and Social Consequences
Section 104.13

Communities, Labor, Environmental Damage, and Criminal Economies

Criminal economies affect rural and urban communities through coerced recruitment, extortion, contamination from clandestine production, and pressure on municipal governments. Cases of communities organizing self-defense or seeking protection exist, but evidence is local and should not be generalized to entire regions. Chapter 103 treats disappearances and victims' movements.

National descriptions of organized crime risk erasing the different ways people experience it locally. A family living in a rural municipality with coercive recruitment pressures does not experience the same risks as an urban resident who encounters counterfeit medicines, a worker in a legitimate chemical factory, or a business owner threatened with extortion. These settings should be studied through local investigations, interviews, public-health records, and contemporary reporting. General statements about Mexican communities can be misleading because the forms of coercion vary widely by region and because the overwhelming majority of residents are neither traffickers nor willing collaborators.

Illegal chemical production can harm surrounding environments, but the degree and type of contamination require case-specific evidence. Claims about damaged soil, water, or worker health should identify observed sites, the nature of available tests, and what investigators did not measure. Without such evidence, the language of environmental devastation can become a dramatic but empty substitute for analysis. The same applies to stories of forced recruitment: some documented cases involve abduction, threats, or coercive labor, while the experience of other workers may differ. Historians should not silently turn allegations concerning a particular place into statements about an entire industry or province.

Criminal demands can also reshape local politics. Municipal officials may face threats, bribery, or pressure to favor certain interests, while communities develop strategies to avoid confrontation or seek protection. Some residents organize collectively, others leave, and many continue ordinary life under constraints that outsiders may not see. Reporting is often incomplete because public testimony creates danger and because frightened residents may not trust investigators. This problem is central to historical source criticism: silence in official records does not necessarily mean an absence of harm, but it also does not authorize invention of testimony.

Families of people who use drugs face a different geography of crisis. In the United States, illicit markets interact with treatment systems, insurance arrangements, emergency medical services, policing, and public information. Some overdose deaths involve drugs consumed without a person's knowledge of their composition; others occur in settings of long-standing dependence and barriers to medical care. Victims and family organizations have advocated for naloxone access, treatment, and prevention. Their experiences deserve attention as social history and not only as evidence in an argument about the effectiveness of border enforcement.

A multiple-perspectives feature can make these contrasts visible. It should distinguish an ordinary rural resident, a municipal official, a health worker, an overdose victim's family, an honest chemical supplier, and a government investigator, while acknowledging unequal power and unequal access to public records. A composite everyday-life account may be useful pedagogically if explicitly labeled as an illustrative synthesis without invented quotations. Chapter 103 covers the full history of disappearances and search collectives; the present chapter instead emphasizes how changing commodities and commercial relationships created new vulnerabilities while preserving older forms of extortion and intimidation.

Fentanyl, Methamphetamine, Overdose Deaths, and the Public-Health Crisis

Overdose deaths in the United States peaked in 2022–2023 at more than 100,000 per year by CDC provisional counts. CDC's provisional estimate for 2025, released May 13, 2026, was 69,973 deaths, about 14 percent below the estimated 81,313 for 2024 and roughly the 2019 level; synthetic-opioid deaths fell from about 48,913 to 38,084. Figures are provisional and counts overlap because a death can involve several drugs. Researchers offer several explanations for the decline, including changes in the drug supply, naloxone access, treatment, and shifts in who uses drugs; the data do not isolate causes. New synthetic substances continue to appear, and some Western states saw rises.

A public-health history of illicit synthetic drugs begins with the people who consume substances, seek treatment, and face overdose risk. It should not assume that every exposure was voluntary, that users reliably knew what they purchased, or that the same drug drove mortality trends in every U.S. region. Fentanyl-related deaths grew dramatically as the illicit market changed, but the national curve is an aggregation of different local epidemics and institutional responses. Hospital systems, emergency medical services, public-health departments, and family networks all mediated outcomes. These factors matter alongside the supply of illicit substances.

The CDC's May 13, 2026 provisional release estimated 69,973 U.S. drug-overdose deaths during 2025, compared with 81,313 in 2024. Deaths involving synthetic opioids declined from 48,913 to 38,084, while deaths involving psychostimulants decreased from 29,852 to 26,181. These figures are provisional statistical estimates and may be revised as more death certificates are processed. The categories are not mutually exclusive: one death can be counted under several substances. Adding the rows together would therefore create a false total, as would treating every synthetic-opioid death as evidence of one organization's distribution.

Why did mortality decline? Potential explanations include changing use patterns, the characteristics of circulating drugs, expanded access to naloxone, treatment, and other interventions. Analysts disagree about the relative importance of these factors, and the aggregate figures cannot test a specific theory on their own. A reduction in deaths can coexist with continued trafficking and widespread substance-use disorder. Conversely, enforcement announcements cannot be taken as proof of falling deaths without a method that compares regions, timing, and alternative explanations. This is a case in which responsible interpretation requires a narrower claim than public political rhetoric often permits.

Geographic differences reinforce the point. CDC reported that some states experienced especially large declines while others saw increases. Overdose trends also have demographic dimensions and may diverge between substances, age groups, and local health systems. A single national figure should not erase these variations. The statistical study should identify the release date, whether the counts are provisional or final, and whether the comparison refers to calendar-year deaths or rolling twelve-month totals. Readers should recognize that statistical systems measure a phenomenon rather than supplying a direct causal explanation.

The chapter's broader historical question is how a changing illicit market became a transnational public-health challenge. U.S. policymakers could portray fentanyl chiefly as a border-security threat, while health professionals emphasized prevention and treatment. Mexican authorities faced pressure to suppress illicit trade and violence while insisting on sovereignty; families sought survival and accountability rather than a competition among official metrics. These positions need not be portrayed as mutually exclusive. History can show how enforcement, healthcare, social conditions, and international commerce interacted without pretending that one administrative dataset settled every contested political question.

That does not mean either controls every stage; production, transport, and finance involve many independent actors. Their rivalry shaped regional violence, and the two organizations differed in structure, with Sinaloa historically more federated and CJNG more tied to a family core, though both rely on alliances. Detailed Sinaloa chronology belongs to Chapter 102.

Public discussion frequently portrays the CJNG and Sinaloa organizations as two coherent rivals. The comparison has value because both figured prominently in U.S. and Mexican law-enforcement assessments, but it also obscures the varied actors operating under each name. The historical Sinaloa federation included relationships among distinct leadership groups, while CJNG combined a prominent founding figure and family-linked financial associations with important regional networks. Neither should be depicted as a conventional company with a publicly observable hierarchy. The organizational categories are analytical models whose usefulness varies across time and place.

The imprisonment of Joaquín Guzmán and the July 2024 detention of Ismael Zambada became major turning points in public accounts of Sinaloa's internal disputes. Yet the chronology of an arrest does not alone demonstrate why a particular local conflict escalated or which factions benefited. Regional interests, existing grievances, changes in protection arrangements, and outside enforcement may matter. An accurate section should avoid folding every act of violence into a rivalry between two names. It should also avoid assuming that a weakened rival automatically yields unchallenged territorial control to CJNG.

The synthetic-drug economy intensified comparisons because both organizations were identified by U.S. authorities as principal participants in markets for fentanyl and methamphetamine. Government assessments can identify patterns and priorities, but organization-level market shares are difficult to establish. Distribution inside the United States often involves multiple independent actors, and upstream chemical suppliers may have diverse customers. Describing two organizations as major sources is therefore different from claiming a binary monopoly. Students should distinguish a national threat assessment from the narrower findings of a court case concerning named defendants and a specific period.

The organizations' relationship to local populations is also not reducible to their product portfolios. Violence, corruption, extortion, and intimidation appeared in regions associated with both, although the precise forms differed. Section 104.16

Measuring Illicit Markets: Seizures, Estimates, and Historical Uncertainty

Seizures can rise because enforcement expands or because supply grows; arrests measure policing; laboratory discoveries reveal individual sites; revenue estimates vary widely by method; indictments state prosecutorial claims. Historians combine these with caution and avoid presenting any single figure as the size of the market.

Historical measurement of an illicit economy is intrinsically difficult because the activities under study are concealed, agencies gather data for different purposes, and public records usually concern the cases most likely to be discovered. Drug seizures are counts of detected material, not a census of production or consumption. A rise in seizures might reflect better detection, more inspections, a larger market, or some combination of these. A decline might represent reduced traffic, changed enforcement, or displacement to activities officials observed less effectively. Students should not apply a single mechanical interpretation to an official trend line.

Court records and financial sanctions supply richer detail, but their populations are also selected. An indictment identifies alleged conduct that prosecutors believe they can charge; it does not describe everyone engaged in a market. A sanctions list may include individuals and firms on the basis of intelligence that is not public, making some claims difficult for outside historians to evaluate. Likewise, suspicious-activity reports produced by financial institutions are instruments of monitoring rather than determinations of guilt. Their volume can increase after new regulations, alerts, or training even without a corresponding change in underlying criminal behavior.

The 2025 GAO study presents valuable evidence about synthetic-drug sourcing and finance, but it also states its own limitations. Its investigators relied on official documents, interviews, cases, and analyses of regulatory reports. Some communications with putative suppliers did not establish whether real illicit products were sold. The report can support a description of known patterns and oversight arrangements; it cannot establish the total number of criminal participants or a definitive net revenue figure for CJNG. A quantitative study should make these boundaries explicit before calculating percentages from selected government datasets.

For public-health measurements, compare the CDC's 2024 and 2025 provisional overdose estimates and calculate changes using the exact same categories and reference periods. The overall decrease was 11,340 estimated deaths, approximately 13.9 percent of the 2024 baseline. The synthetic-opioid category decreased by 10,829, approximately 22.1 percent; the figures should not be added because categories overlap. This exercise shows both the utility and the limits of accurate arithmetic. It cannot determine whether the change resulted from drug enforcement, treatment, drug composition, or shifts in exposure without further independent evidence.

A second quantitative exercise can compare the types of evidence produced by agencies rather than inventing a single market-size measure. Students can classify counts of court cases, seizures, financial alerts, fatal overdoses, and survey responses, indicating the population and activity each represents. They should then identify what the table omits. This approach makes measurement itself part of the historical argument. Instead of claiming to know the exact size of the illicit synthetic economy, the chapter explains how governments and scholars have constructed partial but consequential pictures of a changing international market.

Section 104.17

Sanctions, Prosecutions, and the February 2025 Terrorism Designations

U.S. sanctions against CJNG-linked persons began in 2015. On February 20, 2025, the U.S. designated CJNG and other Mexican organizations as Foreign Terrorist Organizations under section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act and as Specially Designated Global Terrorists under Executive Order 13224. The FTO designation is a U.S. legal instrument that makes material support unlawful and triggers financial and immigration consequences; the SDGT designation operates through sanctions authority. Neither is a conviction of any individual and neither is a universal scholarly classification of the organizations.

The legal response to CJNG did not begin with its designation as a foreign terrorist organization. U.S. Treasury authorities had sanctioned CJNG in April 2015 under the Foreign Narcotics Kingpin Designation Act, and subsequent actions targeted specified individuals, firms, and alleged financial relationships. Criminal prosecutions proceeded through a separate system with higher evidentiary burdens and judicial safeguards. This chronology matters because the February 2025 actions did not create the government's interest in the organization; they changed the legal framework used to describe and pursue some of its activity.

The U.S. State Department's February 20, 2025 Federal Register notices addressed the designation of CJNG under two distinct authorities. The Foreign Terrorist Organization determination followed section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, while the Specially Designated Global Terrorist designation relied on Executive Order 13224 as amended. These instruments have different legal histories and consequences for financial transactions, immigration, and support to designated entities. A textbook should not collapse them into a vague statement that the organization was convicted of terrorism. Administrative designation and conviction of an individual are categorically different acts.

Such classifications are also political and diplomatic events. Mexican authorities must address violent organized crime while maintaining constitutional responsibility for domestic security and control over the deployment of foreign armed forces. U.S. officials emphasize the capacity of designation tools to disrupt finance and prosecute support, while critics may question unintended effects, the breadth of legal categories, and the danger of treating a criminal enterprise as equivalent to every group studied under terrorism law. These perspectives should be evaluated in relation to the legal documents and contemporaneous policy debates, not portrayed as mere slogans.

The Federal Register is particularly useful as a primary source because it identifies the issuing authority, date, named organizations, and formal statutory basis. But the notice does not publish a complete historical account of each organization's origins, command structure, or responsibility for particular violence. Students should read it beside scholarly histories, Mexican statements, and the public records of prosecutions. The designation's significance lies in what it legally did and how it affected bilateral relations; it is not an independent confirmation of every claim made in subsequent political debate.

By 2026 Treasury actions under both narcotics and terrorism-related authorities illustrated an increasingly layered enforcement regime. Yet sanctions cannot independently prove a reduction in criminal revenues, just as the existence of an indictment cannot demonstrate that an accused person was guilty. The most careful interpretation distinguishes enforcement activity, judicial outcome, and demonstrated social effect. This distinction will be essential for assessing whether intensified pressure following February 2026 substantially altered CJNG's operations or simply changed the leadership and public visibility of certain networks.

Section 104.18

U.S.–Mexico Cooperation, Chemical Regulation, and Security Policy, 2024–2026

Cooperation included extraditions, intelligence sharing, financial sanctions, and diplomacy with China over chemical controls. Tensions centered on sovereignty and on U.S. pressure, including public threats of unilateral action. Mexico's government under Claudia Sheinbaum insisted on cooperation without foreign military intervention. Announced results, court outcomes, and policy effectiveness are separate questions; this chapter reports only what is documented.

Security cooperation between the United States and Mexico reflects both shared interests and persistent asymmetries. Mexican authorities confront violence, territorial coercion, and corruption within their own jurisdiction. U.S. institutions respond to overdose mortality, financial crimes, firearms trafficking, and international drug distribution. The governments can agree on particular investigations while disagreeing on rhetoric, the scope of intelligence sharing, and the sovereignty implications of public pressure. The period from 2024 to 2026 saw intensified official attention to synthetic drugs, yet it did not erase the long history of disagreement over who should determine priorities inside Mexico.

Under President Claudia Sheinbaum, Mexican officials emphasized arrests, intelligence, financial investigations, and coordination while repeatedly rejecting the notion that cooperation required unilateral foreign military operations. U.S. governments pressed for more action against illicit fentanyl and designated criminal organizations under new legal authorities. Historians should separate official policy announcements from their implementation and measured effects. An announced strategy may be evidence of political intent; a filed criminal case demonstrates a legal action; and a decline in overdose deaths is a public-health result requiring its own causal analysis. These evidentiary categories should remain distinct even when governments present them as one success story.

International chemical controls add a third dimension. Officials in China, India, Mexico, and the United States have faced questions about illicit diversion from otherwise lawful markets. Effective governance requires information sharing among regulators and legitimate manufacturers as well as criminal enforcement. Different legal systems can define controlled materials differently and update lists on different schedules. A bilateral agreement may influence procedures without immediately affecting markets; its historical significance must be determined through subsequent evidence. The GAO report documented coordination efforts and described limits on agencies' ability to evaluate some newer policies by the end of 2025.

Financial investigations are equally transnational. Money suspected of originating in illicit drug markets may pass through banks or businesses in several countries, and mutual legal assistance determines which records can be obtained. U.S. Treasury and Justice announcements identify public cases and sanctions, but they do not disclose the full universe of alleged criminal capital. Mexico's agencies confront their own limits in policing local protection networks and ensuring witnesses can safely cooperate. No single actor can solve these institutional problems through a press statement, and no single U.S. seizure figure can measure their resolution.

The chapter should also recognize the political implications of framing illicit fentanyl as a foreign threat. The supply chain involves Mexican criminal organizations and international chemical commerce, but consumer markets, distribution networks, financial providers, and demand inside the United States are part of the same system. Assigning responsibility requires evidence rather than national stereotypes. Students should evaluate whether cooperation targets production, financing, public-health prevention, or all three, and whether published outcomes show progress on the particular objective promised. This framework prepares the reader for the high-profile operation of February 2026 without reducing it to a binary story of bilateral triumph or conflict.

February 22, 2026: The Death of El Mencho and CJNG's Uncertain Future

On Sunday, February 22, 2026, Mexico's Defense Ministry announced that Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes had died after an army operation in Tapalpa, Jalisco. According to the ministry, troops came under fire, four people were killed at the scene, and Oseguera Cervantes and others were wounded and died while being flown to Mexico City. The U.S. Embassy said Mexican special forces carried out the operation with complementary U.S. intelligence. Vehicles were burned and roads blocked in Jalisco and other states; the Associated Press reported at least 14 dead in Jalisco, Michoacán, and Guanajuato, including seven National Guard members. Many accounts of the operation come from government statements.

Afterward, Mexican officials said highways returned to normal within days. A U.S. security-advisory analysis in the months after concluded that CJNG had not undergone large-scale fragmentation, though it noted some possible weakening. U.S. sources identified Juan Carlos Valencia González as leader; Mexican authorities named several possible successors, and Audias Flores Silva was arrested in late April. Leadership remains contested in public reporting, and the chapter does not treat any successor as established.

The operation in Tapalpa on February 22, 2026 was a major symbolic and operational event, but the evidence must be presented in layers. Mexico's Defense Ministry reported that special forces planned and executed an effort to detain the person it described as El Mencho, drawing on Mexican military intelligence and contributions from other agencies. U.S. authorities described providing complementary intelligence. Contemporary press coverage reported that Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes died following injuries sustained during the operation. These accounts establish the outline of the event while leaving many tactical and command questions dependent on participant statements rather than independent investigation.

The Defense Ministry's initial communiqué described four members of the criminal group killed at the scene and three seriously wounded people dying during aerial transport, including the target. It also reported two arrests. Those figures are the ministry's original operational account, not the final total of casualties throughout the country. Subsequent road blockades, vehicle fires, and violent incidents disrupted travel and public life in several states. Early press tallies were soon superseded by larger reports encompassing the operation and retaliatory violence, sometimes combining civilians, suspected criminals, and security personnel. It would be historically careless to repeat a preliminary fourteen-person count as the definitive death toll.

The use of public disruption after the raid revealed the organization's continued ability to influence events beyond one individual. It did not prove that every incident was centrally ordered or that the group's hierarchy remained intact. Local actors may have followed prior plans, responded independently, or acted in the expectation that a familiar organizational name would increase intimidation. Officials also had incentives to emphasize restored mobility and the success of the operation. A responsible account compares government statements with subsequent reporting while allowing uncertainty where the chain of responsibility has not been demonstrated.

Succession became a distinct historical question. In July 2026 the U.S. Treasury publicly identified Juan Carlos González, also known as Pelón and elsewhere named Juan Carlos Valencia González, as the organization's new leader, attributing a family relationship to its former head. The release itself used a different given name for El Mencho than widely established biographical accounts, which is a reminder that government documents can contain errors even when authoritative about sanctions decisions. Treasury's attribution should be reported as a dated U.S. government assessment, not treated as conclusive proof that every CJNG regional actor recognized one successor. Other officials and analysts discussed alternative power centers.

The April 27, 2026 arrest of Audias Flores Silva removed another figure described by U.S. prosecutors as a high-ranking member and possible successor. The Justice Department's May announcement set out allegations in an indictment; those accusations were not equivalent to convictions. After the shock of the leader's death, CJNG's international business relationships and financial structures were not automatically dismantled. This episode provides a test case for the theory of leadership decapitation: the historical outcome depends on longer-term evidence about organizational adaptation, civilian security, and criminal markets, not only on the immediate prestige of a successful arrest operation.

Section 104.20

From Regional Cartels to Transnational Criminal Economies: Mexico in 2026

By November 2026 four changes were visible: CJNG's rise, synthetic-drug markets, global chemical and financial linkages, and expanded legal and security responses. Older features persisted: demand, corruption, territorial competition, violence, and weak institutions. Open questions include CJNG's future, causes of the overdose decline, the effect of chemical regulation, and the durability of financial networks. Chapters 101–104 together show prohibition, state formation, and international relations shaping a changing criminal capitalism.

The history of CJNG through November 2026 illuminates changes that began before the organization itself existed. Mexico's illicit markets grew out of prohibition, cross-border demand, local political protection, and networks that linked agricultural producers to international consumers. The fragmentation of earlier trafficking arrangements created opportunities for new groups, while commercial and technological changes enabled a greater role for synthetic drugs. CJNG became a major participant, but neither its rise nor its leader's death can explain every transformation. The historical record shows overlapping organizational, market, state, and public-health histories rather than a single uninterrupted biography.

Some aspects of the new economy were genuinely different. Industrial materials and international commercial regulation became central to synthetic supply, illicitly manufactured fentanyl changed the risks faced by many consumers, and financial investigations connected criminal activity to institutions across several countries. Yet older features persisted. Violence against communities, local corruption, informal protection, political pressure, and interorganizational rivalry were not invented in the 2010s. The central debate is whether synthetics transformed the foundations of organized crime or accelerated patterns already established by earlier trafficking systems. A persuasive answer must make room for both discontinuity and continuity.

The evidence also sets limits on what can be concluded about policy. A designation expands legal tools but is not a conviction; a seizure records enforcement but not total market size; an overdose death certificate records a fatal event but not a complete supply chain; and a government statement about a criminal leader establishes an official account rather than an independently verified organizational chart. These distinctions are not academic obstacles to policy. They are prerequisites for judging which interventions appear effective, which costs remain unmeasured, and how public institutions should be held accountable for their claims.

As of November 15, 2026 the organization's leadership after El Mencho's death remained an issue of ongoing assessment even as U.S. Treasury publicly named a successor. The extent of any later fragmentation, the effects of chemical controls, and the relationship between falling U.S. overdose mortality and illicit market demand could not be settled definitively with the documents then available. Future historians may have access to trials, financial records, internal documents, and longer statistical series unavailable to contemporary writers. An intellectually honest textbook marks those uncertainties rather than filling them with the certainty expected of a dramatic narrative.

Part XXIII therefore closes with a regional history of political economy. Chapter 101 traced the creation of older illicit markets; Chapter 102 examined regional trafficking organizations; Chapter 103 followed militarized public security and its civilian consequences; and this chapter explained synthetic drugs, finance, and transnational criminal relationships. Taken together, the chapters portray Mexican people and institutions as historical agents within a system shaped by decisions in many countries. The lesson is neither that the state was powerless nor that eliminating one organization could end the underlying economy, but that durable outcomes depend on institutions, social conditions, international cooperation, and evidence about what actually changed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Mexican Organized Crime

What changed? Products, supply chains, financial reach, and legal framing. What remained continuous? Corruption, territorial competition, and demand-driven markets. Who experienced change most directly? Overdose victims and communities under extortion. Greater continuity: Regions where political protection persisted. Why it matters: Policy built on one side of the pattern may miss the other.

Historical Debate II

Which Interventions Can Change a Transnational Criminal Economy?

Interpretation A: Arrests, sanctions, and cross-border intelligence can impose immediate costs on leadership and financing, and the 2026 operation demonstrated the state’s ability to reach a prominent figure.

Interpretation B: So long as demand, local protection arrangements, weak judicial institutions, and independent distribution relationships persist, leaders can be replaced and markets can adapt.

Evidence and limits: The 2015 and 2026 Treasury actions, the 2025 GAO audit, CDC mortality trends, and the Mexican Defense Ministry statement document different aspects of the problem. None by itself measures the ultimate effect of all interventions.

Chapter Summary

CJNG emerged around 2010–2011 from Milenio-linked networks after Sinaloa leadership losses and expanded through the 2010s, combining a family-linked financial core with autonomous regional commanders.

Synthetic drugs changed the economics of trafficking by reducing dependence on land and increasing dependence on chemical commerce, while older markets persisted.

Illicit fentanyl became a major U.S. market around 2019, with several Mexican organizations implicated; methamphetamine has a separate and continuing history.

Financial networks and diversification into fuel theft, extortion, and fraud helped sustain the enterprise. Sanctions and prosecutions targeted these networks, but administrative findings and allegations differ from convictions.

CDC data show a third annual decline in U.S. overdose deaths in 2025, but these data do not measure criminal revenue or establish causes.

The February 2025 FTO and SDGT designations expanded U.S. legal tools; they are U.S. legal categories.

On February 22, 2026, El Mencho was killed in an operation with U.S. intelligence support, followed by widespread disruption. As of November 2026 the organization's future remained uncertain.

Older agricultural and transit markets did not disappear: cocaine, heroin and cannabis continued to coexist with methamphetamine and illicit fentanyl, and their supply histories must be analyzed separately.

Chemical regulation involved legitimate manufacturers, multiple national jurisdictions and international cooperation; evidence of diversion does not implicate all businesses or entire source countries.

The recorded fall in 2025 overdose deaths was a major public-health development, but the provisional statistics do not independently explain causation or reveal the revenues of named organizations.

Mexican communities and U.S. consumers experienced very different vulnerabilities. Extortion, political pressure, medical harm, and failures of accountability call for different forms of evidence and response.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that Mexican organized crime is a historical system shaped by prohibition, demand, global trade, and state action. The synthetic-drug era changed products and linkages without ending older patterns of corruption and territorial competition.

It also teaches a method: separate allegation from finding, statistic from cause, and official account from independent verification. These habits matter for Chapter 105, which turns to Venezuela's oil state.
