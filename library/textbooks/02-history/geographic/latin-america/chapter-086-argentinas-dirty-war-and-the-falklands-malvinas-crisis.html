Chapter Opening: A Coup Announced on the Radio, and a Search That Lasted Decades

Argentina's last military dictatorship ended in two ways at once. Abroad it ended in a lost war over islands in the South Atlantic; at home it left behind a system of secret detention whose victims are still being identified, and whose survivors, relatives, and lawyers helped make "human rights" a central language of Argentine politics.

In the early hours of 24 March 1976, the armed forces of Argentina removed the constitutional president, María Estela Martínez de Perón, known as Isabel Perón, and took over the government. By that morning the coup was no surprise. Rumors had circulated for months, military commanders had effectively warned the government that its time was short, and large parts of the political class, the press, and the public expected the army to intervene. What surprised many observers over the following years was not that the military took power but what it did with it. Alongside open measures such as the closing of Congress and the banning of political activity, the new rulers organized a clandestine apparatus that seized people from streets, workplaces, and homes, held them in secret detention centers, and in a very large number of cases killed them and concealed the bodies.

The people who were taken were called, in a term that entered world vocabulary from Argentina, desaparecidos: the disappeared. They included members of armed organizations, but also trade unionists, students, teachers, lawyers, journalists, priests and nuns, neighborhood organizers, and relatives or friends of people the security forces sought. The state denied holding them. Relatives who asked at police stations, barracks, courts, and church offices were told, in effect, that no record existed. The chapter therefore asks a problem of evidence as well as a problem of politics: how do historians reconstruct a system of violence that was designed to leave no official trace?

The answer involves the testimony of survivors, the persistence of relatives, forensic work on graves, the records of courts and truth commissions, and documents from abroad. It also involves the Mothers and Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo, who began as small groups of women who could find no one willing to say where their children were and who turned the main square of the capital into the most recognizable site of protest in Latin America. They appear in this chapter, as they should, as historical actors with a specific strategy and a specific history, and not as symbols alone.

The dictatorship was more than a machine of repression. It also carried an economic project, associated with Economy Minister José Alfredo Martínez de Hoz, that tried to dismantle parts of the protectionist order built since the 1930s and the Peronist period. It censored culture and education, hosted and won the 1978 World Cup, quarreled with neighboring Chile to the edge of war, and managed relations with Washington under three different presidents. It was also not a single unchanged government. Four army generals held the presidency, the navy and air force kept their own interests, and the system that seemed so confident in 1976 was visibly decaying by 1981.

On 2 April 1982, the junta led by General Leopoldo Galtieri ordered Argentine forces to land on the Falkland Islands, which Argentina calls the Malvinas. The sovereignty dispute was real and old, the islands' roughly eighteen hundred residents were British in allegiance and identity, and Britain chose to fight. Seventy-four days later, Argentine forces surrendered at Puerto Argentino/Port Stanley. The defeat discredited the armed forces in a way that economic failure and international criticism had not. The chapter treats the war as a genuine armed conflict, with its own diplomacy and its own human cost, and not merely as a diversion from domestic problems.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. Official documents of the junta, such as its founding acts, show how the military described its own authority. Clandestine and exile publications, including the letter by the journalist Rodolfo Walsh, show how opposition spoke when speech was dangerous. The report of the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights shows what outside investigators found in 1979. The report of the National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons (CONADEP), published in 1984 as Nunca Más, was produced after the dictatorship and must be treated as a retrospective investigation. Declassified American diplomatic records show what Washington knew and said, and they must not be read as evidence that Argentine officers took orders from the United States.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It builds on Chapter 77's account of Peronism and uses Chapter 85's account of Chile for comparison. It leaves the full regional apparatus of Operation Condor to Chapter 87, the wider comparison of human-rights movements to Chapter 88, continent-wide debt to Chapter 93, and the trials and transitional justice that followed 1983 to Chapter 94. It ends in December 1983, when an elected president took office and created CONADEP. The aim is to show how a state built an apparatus of clandestine terror and then lost its authority, and how those two stories connect without being reduced to one.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political, economic, and security crisis of 1973–1976 without treating military rule as inevitable or legitimate.
  2. Describe the formal institutions of the Proceso de Reorganización Nacional and the military command structure that organized repression by zones.
  3. Analyze the national-security doctrine used to justify repression and trace the continuity between counterinsurgency before and after March 1976.
  4. Explain how clandestine detention, torture, forced disappearance, killing, and the appropriation of children operated as a system.
  5. Distinguish categories of victims and the status of figures such as CONADEP's documented cases and the commemorative figure of 30,000.
  6. Distinguish armed organizations such as the Montoneros and ERP from the wider civilian population targeted, and evaluate why state and nonstate violence are not institutionally equivalent.
  7. Assess the economic program of Martínez de Hoz and the sources of the financial crisis and foreign debt of 1980–1982.
  8. Interpret how everyday life, culture, and labor changed under military rule, including the 1978 World Cup.
  9. Evaluate the strategies and impact of the Mothers and Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo and other human-rights organizations.
  10. Contextualize American and other international responses, the Beagle Channel crisis, and the role of exile networks.
  11. Reconstruct the origins, course, and outcome of the 1982 Falklands/Malvinas War, including the experiences of conscripts and islanders.
  12. Evaluate competing explanations for the dictatorship's collapse and connect the chapter to the transition of 1983.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How can historians explain the severity of Argentina's crisis before 1976 without suggesting that the coup, or the system of clandestine repression that followed, was a necessary response?
  2. What made the disappearance system an organized state practice and not a series of excesses, and what evidence supports that conclusion?
  3. How should historians treat numbers of victims when the state concealed its records and different figures answer different questions?

Historical Context

By the 1970s Argentina was one of the most urbanized, literate, and industrialized societies in Latin America, with a large organized working class, a politically active middle class, and an exceptionally interventionist military. Chapter 77 described how Juan Perón built a movement on organized labor and social welfare between 1946 and 1955, and how the armed forces overthrew him in 1955 and then for eighteen years banned or constrained Peronism. Military governments ruled in 1966–1973 under a self-described "Argentine Revolution," and their authoritarian rule helped radicalize a generation of students, workers, and Catholic activists. The Cordobazo of 1969, a mass uprising of workers and students in the city of Córdoba, signaled that the regime had lost control of important social sectors.

Armed organizations emerged in this setting. The Montoneros, a left-wing Peronist group, announced themselves in 1970 by kidnapping and killing the former de facto president Pedro Eugenio Aramburu. The Ejército Revolucionario del Pueblo (ERP), the armed wing of a Marxist party, pursued a different revolutionary strategy and fought in the rural province of Tucumán. Some of their members came from the middle class and from the Catholic and student movements; they also drew on genuine working-class and neighborhood support in some places. Military governments met them with repression, including the killings at Trelew in 1972, in which captured prisoners were shot in a navy base. Illegal repression therefore preceded the coup of 1976.

The Cold War shaped the language and the networks of military thinking. Argentine officers had studied counterinsurgency doctrine, partly through French theorists of the Algerian war and partly through American training and conferences, and they saw Argentina as a battlefield in an ideological struggle. Domestic causes were equally important: inequality, the long exclusion of Peronism, the decline of civilian institutions, and the military's habit of treating itself as a guardian of the nation. Chapter 84 on Brazil and Chapter 85 on Chile show other forms of the same regional pattern, and Chapter 87 will trace how the regimes cooperated.

Students should keep three cautions in mind. Political violence in 1973–1976 was real and serious, and this chapter describes it. It nevertheless provides no basis for equating the actions of armed groups with the actions of a state that controlled the police, courts, prisons, and army and chose to suspend legal safeguards. Second, the sources are uneven. The state's perpetrators left few honest records, survivors' testimonies are indispensable but vary, and numbers are contested. Third, the chapter's terminology, including "Dirty War," is itself a matter of historical analysis. The term was used by the military to describe its campaign and by some later writers; many scholars and human-rights organizations prefer terms such as state terrorism or state terror, because "war" implies two armies, and the Argentine experience was a campaign by the state against a civilian population with armed opposition at its margins.

Division I · Section 86.1

The Return of Peronism, Political Violence, and the Crisis of 1973–1976

Peronism returned to office in 1973 after eighteen years of proscription. Héctor Cámpora, Perón's delegate, won the presidency in March and took office on 25 May, and Perón himself returned from exile in June. The homecoming was marked by tragedy: on 20 June, at Ezeiza airport near Buenos Aires, armed groups of the Peronist right fired on a crowd that included left-wing Peronists, and numerous people were killed. The episode revealed that the movement Perón led was a coalition of incompatible forces, from the labor bureaucracy and nationalist right to the Montoneros and Peronist Youth. Cámpora resigned in July, and in September Perón won a new election with roughly three-fifths of the vote, with his wife Isabel as vice president.

Perón's government moved against the left within his own movement. In May 1974 he expelled the Montoneros from the Plaza de Mayo rally, and the organization soon returned to clandestine armed activity. Perón died on 1 July 1974, and Isabel Perón, with little political experience and no independent base, became president. Her closest adviser was José López Rega, the minister of social welfare, who was associated with the Argentine Anticommunist Alliance, known as the Triple A. This paramilitary group, drawing on police and other state personnel, assassinated lawyers, politicians, journalists, and activists, beginning in 1973–1974. Historians generally treat the Triple A as an important precedent: killing opponents outside the law had become a tool linked to officials of a constitutional government.

Armed insurgency intensified in the same months. The Montoneros carried out kidnappings for ransom, assassinations, and attacks on military installations, and they went underground in September 1974. The ERP concentrated on the rural northwest and on urban operations, and in December 1975 it attacked the army arsenal at Monte Chingolo near Buenos Aires in an operation that failed with heavy losses. Both organizations killed police, soldiers, executives, and civilians. Their strategy rested on the belief that violence would expose the repressive character of the state and mobilize the masses, a belief that most Argentines did not share and that left the groups increasingly isolated.

The government also faced economic collapse. In June 1975, Economy Minister Celestino Rodrigo introduced a drastic devaluation and price increases, known as the Rodrigazo, that sent inflation soaring and cut real wages. Trade unions, normally close to the Peronist government, responded with a general strike in July that forced the cancellation of the wage guidelines and the resignation of Rodrigo and, soon afterward, López Rega. Isabel Perón's remaining authority collapsed. Inflation reached triple digits, and Congress and the parties were divided and ineffective.

Meanwhile the army had already begun counterinsurgency. In February 1975, under a decree of the Peronist government, the army launched Operativo Independencia in Tucumán, authorizing it to "neutralize or annihilate" insurgent activity there. Under General Acdel Vilas, and then other commanders, troops occupied towns, set up the first documented clandestine detention center at the so-called Escuelita de Famaillá, and disappeared suspects. In October 1975 further decrees extended the military's role in counterinsurgency across the country. The army, in other words, had been given legal cover for combat operations while it developed illegal methods.

The weakness of the constitutional government did not make military dictatorship inevitable or legitimate. Alternatives existed, including elections scheduled for 1977 and political negotiation. Many leading military officers had decided that they would take power, and had prepared for months. Historians such as Marina Franco have shown that the language of an internal enemy was already shaping policy before the coup, and that many Argentines accepted the idea of an extraordinary solution. Acceptance of a coup is different from consent to the system that followed, which was concealed.

Division I · Section 86.2

March 24, 1976: The Military Coup and the Destruction of Constitutional Government

On the night of 23–24 March 1976, military units took control of key locations in Buenos Aires and the provinces. Isabel Perón was arrested after leaving the Casa Rosada by helicopter, and she was held in detention for years. The three commanders, General Jorge Rafael Videla of the army, Admiral Emilio Eduardo Massera of the navy, and Brigadier Orlando Ramón Agosti of the air force, formed a ruling junta. A communiqué broadcast in the early hours declared that the armed forces had assumed control of the state, and a series of orders published over the following days announced the structure of the new regime.

The legal measures were swift. Congress was dissolved, the Supreme Court and provincial high courts were removed and replaced, and provincial governors and legislatures were replaced by military appointees or interveners. Political parties were suspended and then banned from activity, and the junta intervened in the General Confederation of Labor (CGT) and many unions, suspended collective bargaining and the right to strike, and removed union leaders. University administrations were taken over. The state of siege that had existed formally since 1974 remained in force and gave the new government a framework of emergency law. Within days, the junta began arresting people under its authority, and the first of the clandestine abductions occurred.

Civilian reactions were mixed and should be described individually. Many political leaders of the main parties were silent or accommodating, and some saw the coup as inevitable. Business organizations, including leading associations of landowners and industrialists, generally welcomed the new government, though not all firms and sectors benefited equally. Most large newspapers, among them La Nación and Clarín, accepted the new authorities at first, and some cooperated with the government's propaganda; others, such as La Opinión under Jacobo Timerman, were later repressed. Catholic leaders were divided. Some bishops and chaplains publicly supported the armed forces; others, including bishops such as Enrique Angelelli of La Rioja, were later killed or threatened. The Communist Party offered a notably accommodating line at first, a decision that did not protect its members from later repression.

Opposition existed from the first days. Workers in some factories slowed production, and individual lawyers and activists protested. Armed organizations planned attacks. Very soon the repressive system showed its character. In the first weeks, the junta announced the arrests of thousands of people, and many of those never reappeared. The distinction between the coup, which replaced the government, and the repression, which reorganized society through terror, is essential. The coup was the precondition for a system of organized state violence that the junta did not announce.

Division I · Section 86.3

The Proceso de Reorganización Nacional, Military Institutions, and Authoritarian Authority

The military government named itself the Proceso de Reorganización Nacional, or National Reorganization Process. The name was deliberate. It claimed a mission, not a transition: the armed forces would reorganize the state, the economy, and society, and no date was set for a return to elections. The junta issued a set of founding documents on 24 March 1976, including a statement of its objectives and an act declaring its powers, which were later published in the official gazette. These documents asserted that the junta held the supreme organ of the nation and placed the junta above the constitution, which remained formally in force only insofar as it did not conflict with the junta's own acts.

Power was organized through a formal structure that students should distinguish from its practice. The junta, made up of the three commanders, held ultimate authority, including the power to appoint the president. The president, initially Videla, held executive power but was in theory subordinate to the junta. A Legislative Advisory Commission, with representatives of the three services, performed some legislative functions. Behind this structure, each branch of the armed forces held its own territory and its own areas of responsibility, which meant that the three services shared power but also competed. This division of the state among the services was a major structural feature of the regime and a source of its later instability.

The territorial organization of repression followed the army's command structure. The country was divided into five military zones corresponding to army corps areas, centered on Buenos Aires, Rosario, Córdoba, Campo de Mayo, and Bahía Blanca, and these were subdivided into subzones and local areas. In each zone the army commander directed the security forces in his area, including police, and ran or coordinated clandestine operations. The navy held its own major facility in the capital, the Escuela de Mecánica de la Armada (ESMA), and the air force maintained facilities of its own. Historians use this territorial structure to demonstrate that disappearances were not independent acts of rogue units: operations moved through a chain of command, and zone commanders held responsibility for what occurred in their areas.

Successive military presidents ruled under this structure. Videla governed from 1976 to March 1981; General Roberto Viola replaced him briefly in 1981; General Leopoldo Galtieri took office in December 1981 and fell after the war in June 1982; and General Reynaldo Bignone presided over the final transition from July 1982 to December 1983. Each government faced different problems, and later sections treat them separately. The presidency rotated within the army, but the principal commanders of the services retained control of the junta, and the 1982 war would reveal how much each service's institutional interests shaped decisions.

Historical Vocabulary · Study I
Military Government and Counterinsurgency

Military junta: A governing committee of senior commanders, here the heads of the army, navy, and air force, that holds supreme authority. Unlike a personal dictatorship, the Argentine junta distributed power among the services.

Proceso de Reorganización Nacional: The official name of the regime, usually shortened to "the Proceso." The name asserted a long-term program of reorganization.

National-security doctrine: A set of ideas that treated internal political opposition as part of an ideological war and the armed forces as the guardian of national survival. Historians distinguish its varied sources, including French counterinsurgency theory, Cold War anticommunism, and Argentine military nationalism.

Counterinsurgency: Military and police operations aimed at defeating an insurgency by targeting its organization, intelligence, and civilian support. In Argentina it came to include illegal detention and killing.

Authoritarianism: Rule that suspends or sharply limits competitive elections, independent courts, a free press, and organized opposition, while claiming a national mission. It is not a synonym for any single leader's rule.

Division I · Section 86.4

National-Security Doctrine, Counterinsurgency, and the Militarization of Argentine Politics

The generals who took power did not regard themselves as the heads of a repressive regime. They described themselves as defenders of Western Christian civilization in a global war against communism, and they treated Argentine politics as a theater of that war. The central idea was that of an internal enemy, which they called subversion. Subversion was defined not only as armed insurgency but as a broad ideological and organizational phenomenon that could be present in schools, factories, churches, newspapers, and neighborhoods. A teacher who discussed Marx, a priest who worked in a slum, or a student activist could be classified as part of the enemy's infrastructure. The definition's elasticity was its most dangerous feature.

The doctrine drew on several sources. French officers who had fought in Algeria taught that revolutionary war was fought for control of the population, and that intelligence gathered through interrogation was the decisive weapon. American training and Cold War anticommunism shaped how many Latin American officers understood their role, though historians caution against treating Argentine doctrine as a simple import. Argentine officers also drew on their own tradition of military nationalism and on the Catholic traditionalism of parts of the officer corps. The result was a doctrine that justified extralegal methods as a necessary response to an enemy who did not respect the rules.

Continuity with the period before March 1976 was substantial. Operativo Independencia in Tucumán had tested methods of secret detention. The Triple A had shown that killing opponents outside the law could be tolerated by officials. The October 1975 decrees had expanded the army's mandate. After the coup the armed forces extended these practices into a national system, with centralized planning, specialized units, and a division of the country into zones. The coup greatly expanded and institutionalized the repression, but it did not begin it, and historians who place the beginning of state violence in March 1976 understate the role of the constitutional government and of the earlier military governments.

The security system relied on cooperation among institutions. Army intelligence units, naval intelligence, air force intelligence, the federal and provincial police, the prison service, and the intelligence agency known as the SIDE shared information and personnel. Task forces drawn from several institutions carried out abductions. Local police, who knew neighborhoods, often provided lists and cleared areas, and in many cases they recorded no arrests. Army battalion 601, a major intelligence unit, collected information across the country. Rivalry among the services sometimes produced competition over prisoners and credit, but the broad system was coordinated.

Official claims must be distinguished from practice. The junta repeatedly said it was restoring order, and that it respected the law and human dignity. It also issued decrees limiting habeas corpus and expanded the powers of military courts. Yet the clandestine detentions and killings took place outside any legal procedure, and the government denied holding detainees. This combination of public legality and secret terror was not accidental. It allowed the regime to present a respectable face to foreign governments and domestic audiences while using fear to dismantle political organization. The next division examines how this apparatus worked.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The 1976 Coup, State Terror, Political Exclusion, and Social Resistance

Long-term causes

Chronic political instability since 1930 and the proscription of Peronism after 1955; the military's self-image as guardian of the nation; Cold War doctrine; polarization within the left and Peronism; economic stagnation and inflation.

Short-term causes

The collapse of Isabel Perón's authority; the Rodrigazo and general strike; armed insurgency and paramilitary violence; Operativo Independencia; the preparations of military leaders and a climate in which many expected a coup.

Event or transformation

On 24 March 1976 the armed forces replaced the constitutional government with a junta and expanded existing illegal repression into a national clandestine system.

Immediate effects

Dissolution of Congress and political parties; control of unions; thousands of abductions; disappearance of armed militants and of many civilians; censorship and fear.

Long-term consequences

Destruction of much of the organized left; economic restructuring; the rise of the human-rights movement; delegitimation of the armed forces; a durable political culture of "never again."

Historical complexity

The crisis was real but did not require a coup. The repression went far beyond the armed organizations, and it produced resistance that the regime had not anticipated.
Division II · Section 86.5

Clandestine Detention Centers, Abduction Networks, and the Organization of Repression

The disappearance system rested on a network of secret detention centers. CONADEP, in its 1984 investigation, identified about 340 such sites in the country, and later research by courts, archives, and organizations such as Memoria Abierta has identified more. They ranged from large, permanent complexes to improvised rooms in police stations, army regiments, and private houses. Some were located in the middle of cities. The Escuela de Mecánica de la Armada (ESMA), a navy training school, stood on a prominent avenue in Buenos Aires near a major football stadium. La Perla, near Córdoba, operated under the Third Army Corps. Campo de Mayo, a large army base north of the capital, held a number of facilities. In greater Buenos Aires, a circuit of centers known as Club Atlético, Banco, and Olimpo was run by the army and federal police, and the Pozo de Banfield and other police stations served the same purpose.

The sequence of events followed a pattern that survivors, relatives, and court cases have documented repeatedly. Intelligence services identified targets through surveillance, informants, and information extracted from earlier detainees. A task force, usually in civilian clothes and using unmarked vehicles, abducted the person from home, work, or street, often at night and often with relatives present. Neighbors sometimes saw the vehicles, and in many cases the area had been "freed" in advance, meaning that local police had been told not to intervene. The victim was hooded or blindfolded and taken to a detention center, where interrogation under torture began. Property was frequently taken from homes, and some officers appropriated houses and goods.

Secrecy was central. Detainees were held without names, often identified by numbers, and their existence was denied to relatives and courts. Writs of habeas corpus filed on behalf of the disappeared were routinely rejected or answered with statements that no record of the detention existed. Most judges accepted these answers, and few challenged the military. Records were kept internally in some centers, and some were later destroyed. In 1983, as the dictatorship was ending, the military ordered the destruction of documents, a fact that complicates later reconstruction.

The system was possible because it operated within otherwise functioning institutions. The army, navy, and police continued their ordinary work. Officers who ran torture centers lived in ordinary neighborhoods. Staff included doctors, who sometimes attended births and treated prisoners, as well as chaplains, guards, clerks, and drivers. Courts, newspapers, and hospitals functioned. The result was a society in which many people suspected, some knew, and most could not safely say what was happening. The scale of the system suggests that it was organized and sustained by state institutions, and the pattern across regions suggests central planning, rather than isolated abuses.

Division II · Section 86.6

Torture, Forced Disappearance, Extrajudicial Killing, and Appropriation of Children

Torture was the central practice inside the centers. Survivors consistently describe beatings, near-drowning, electric shocks applied with the picana, prolonged hooding, deprivation of food and sleep, and threats against relatives. Interrogators sought names, addresses, and meeting places, and the information produced further abductions in chains. Sexual violence was widespread, and later courts have recognized it as a distinct crime and not as an incidental part of torture. Detainees were held for periods ranging from days to years in conditions that included overcrowding, shackling, and humiliation. A small number were released, some after being forced to collaborate. Most of those who were not released were killed.

The killing of detainees was extrajudicial and concealed. Prisoners were shot and buried in unmarked graves, sometimes in public cemeteries as unidentified bodies, called NN for ningún nombre. Others were burned. The navy, and to some extent other services, used what came to be known as death flights: drugged prisoners were thrown alive from aircraft into the Río de la Plata or the Atlantic. The practice was reported by survivors and later confirmed by the testimony of a former naval officer, Adolfo Scilingo, in 1995, and by judicial findings. Bodies washed ashore on coastal beaches in late 1977 and were buried as NN in a local cemetery, and decades later forensic anthropologists identified some of them, including founders of the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo.

Pregnant women in detention faced a particular fate. Some were kept alive until they gave birth, in clandestine maternity wards at sites including ESMA, Campo de Mayo, and police facilities such as Pozo de Banfield. After birth the mothers were generally killed, and the babies were given to military or police families, sold, or left in institutions, with falsified birth records and false identities. The Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo estimate that roughly five hundred children were appropriated; this is an organizational estimate based on their investigations, and the number confirmed by courts and DNA identification is lower. More than one hundred thirty people, now adults, have recovered their identities through the work of the Grandmothers and state genetic databases, and that number continues to change as cases are resolved.

Students must keep categories distinct. A desaparecido is a person who was seized by agents of the state and whose fate was concealed. Some disappeared persons were later confirmed to have been killed, when remains were identified or when judicial evidence established the death; others remain missing. Torture survivors, many of whom were held in clandestine centers and later released or "legalized" as political prisoners, are a separate group, as are the thousands who were imprisoned legally under the state of siege through executive power, many for years. Children whose identities were appropriated form another category: some were born in captivity, some were taken with their parents, and their situation involves the crime of identity falsification as well as the killing of their parents. These categories overlap but cannot be merged into a single total. CONADEP's investigation found that the largest portion of disappearances occurred in 1976 and 1977, that roughly thirty percent of the disappeared were women, and that young adults were overwhelmingly the victims; by its own tabulation, workers and students were the largest occupational groups.

Historical Vocabulary · Study II
State Terror and Human Rights

Enforced disappearance: Arrest or abduction by agents of the state, or with their acquiescence, followed by a refusal to acknowledge the deprivation of liberty or to reveal the person's fate. International law later defined it as a distinct crime.

Clandestine detention center: A secret facility, often in a military or police building, where prisoners were held without legal process.

Death flights: The practice of killing detainees by throwing them from aircraft over water.

Appropriation of children: The seizure of children born in captivity or taken from detained parents and their placement with new families under false identities.

Habeas corpus: A legal writ requiring authorities to produce a detained person before a judge. Under the dictatorship it was routinely ineffective.

Crimes against humanity: Serious acts, including murder, torture, and forced disappearance, committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population. Argentine courts have applied this category to repression of the 1970s.

Division II · Section 86.7

ESMA, Survivor Testimony, Documentary Evidence, and the Scale of Disappearance

ESMA is the best-documented case. The navy school operated a task group, under the direction of naval officers, that held prisoners in specific areas of the main building, including an attic known as Capucha, where detainees were hooded and shackled. Estimates commonly cited suggest that about five thousand people passed through, and that only a few hundred survived; those figures are approximations based on testimony and trials. Some prisoners were forced to work, producing documents, translating, or doing forgery for the task group, in a system that survivors call a perverse form of labor. In 1977 an officer, Alfredo Astiz, infiltrated the group of relatives that met at a Buenos Aires church, and in December several members, including the Mothers' founder Azucena Villaflor and two French nuns, were abducted. Astiz was convicted by an Argentine court in 2011.

La Perla, in Córdoba, provides a second case outside the capital. Operating under the army's Third Corps, whose commander was General Luciano Benjamín Menéndez, it held an estimated two thousand or more prisoners, most of whom were killed. Survivors and court findings describe its operation within a military complex near a main highway, with the participation of army and provincial police personnel. Menéndez was later convicted of crimes against humanity. The comparison of ESMA and La Perla shows that the system had common features, including hoods, torture, and secret killing, and also regional and institutional differences: the navy used flights, while army sites more often relied on burial.

Historians reconstruct this system from multiple, partial sources. Survivors gave testimony in exile, to CONADEP, and in later trials, and their accounts converge on the layout, routines, and personnel of centers. Relatives submitted habeas corpus petitions and complaints that became documentary evidence. The Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team, founded in 1984, exhumed unmarked graves and identified remains. Courts held trials after the amnesty laws were annulled by Congress in 2003 and declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 2005, and their rulings include findings on facts. Archives, including those of the National Memory Archive and Memoria Abierta, collected documents, photographs, and oral histories. International sources, including declassified American cables and reports of the Inter-American Commission, added outside evidence.

The scale of disappearance requires care. CONADEP, which worked from September 1983 to September 1984 under novelist Ernesto Sabato, documented about 8,960 cases of disappearance, a figure later revised to 8,961 by the state's archive after additions. It described this as a partial number, since many families had not reported cases, feared to do so, or were unknown to the commission. The figure of thirty thousand, used by human-rights organizations since the dictatorship and widely adopted in public memory, originated in estimates by activists and exiles during the military period and became a commemorative and political symbol of the scale of the crime. It is not a verified, itemized register. Later official efforts, including a unified registry of victims maintained by the state, continue to add and verify names. Students should understand each number as the answer to a different question: how many cases were documented by a given deadline, how many have been verified since, and how large the movement believes the total to have been.

Testimony also requires methodological care. It is indispensable, since the perpetrators concealed records, but memory is shaped by trauma, by time, and by the conditions of detention, in which prisoners were hooded and disoriented. Historians compare testimonies across many witnesses, test them against physical evidence and documents, and recognize that not every experience can be established with equal precision. Scholars such as Emilio Crenzel and Elizabeth Jelin have shown that testimony was also shaped by the political circumstances in which it was given, including the form of questions asked by commissions and trials. That does not weaken its value; it requires students to read it critically.

Division II · Section 86.8

Armed Organizations, Civilian Opposition, and the Destruction of Political Networks

The Montoneros and the ERP were different organizations with different histories. The Montoneros were an urban, Peronist guerrilla group that grew out of Catholic nationalist and student circles, and they claimed to represent the Peronist working class. Their leaders included Mario Firmenich. The ERP, founded as the armed wing of a Marxist party led by Mario Roberto Santucho, rejected Peronism and sought a socialist revolution through prolonged war, with a rural front in Tucumán. Both organizations used kidnapping, bank robbery, and assassination, and both attacked military targets. Both also killed civilians, and their actions were criticized at the time and since by many on the left, including some former members. By 1976 both were heavily infiltrated and weakened. Santucho was killed in July 1976, and the Montoneros' leadership went abroad, launching a failed "counteroffensive" in 1979 that cost the lives of many militants.

The military's campaign went far beyond armed groups. Evidence from CONADEP, trials, and archives shows that many victims had no connection to armed organizations. Union delegates in factories such as those of the automobile and metallurgical sectors were targeted, as were students, teachers, psychologists, lawyers who defended political prisoners, journalists, and priests and nuns involved in social work. Relatives and friends of suspected militants were taken to extract information or to pressure them. The aim was to dismantle political and social networks that the regime saw as the soil of subversion, and the broader the definition, the broader the repression.

Violence by armed groups and violence by the state differed institutionally. The Montoneros and ERP were clandestine organizations with limited resources whose acts were crimes under Argentine law and are historically documented as such. The state held a monopoly of legitimate force, was bound by law and by international commitments, and controlled the police, courts, prisons, and intelligence services. It chose to suspend legal procedure, torture and kill prisoners, and conceal the bodies. Moral and legal evaluation therefore differs, and the later trial of junta members rested on this difference. A theory of "two demons," which presented the violence of left and right as symmetrical and society as an innocent bystander, shaped the 1984 introduction to Nunca Más and has since been criticized by many historians for blurring the structural difference between a state's and a group's violence.

Rodolfo Walsh is the most documented example of clandestine opposition. A prominent writer and investigative journalist, author of Operación Masacre, he had joined the Montoneros and later distanced himself from the leadership's strategy. After the coup he founded a clandestine news agency, ANCLA, and distributed information about the repression. His daughter was killed in a confrontation with the army in 1976. On 24 March 1977, the first anniversary of the coup, he sent a letter to the junta and to the press. The next day he was ambushed in Buenos Aires by a navy task force and killed; his body was never found.

Division III · Section 86.9

José Alfredo Martínez de Hoz, Economic Liberalization, and the Military Government's Economic Program

On 2 April 1976, nine days after the coup, Economy Minister José Alfredo Martínez de Hoz announced an economic program. A member of a wealthy landowning and business family and a former minister and corporate leader, he held the post until March 1981, the longest tenure of any economy minister in the period, and his policies defined the regime's economic project. He presented them as a break with decades of stagnation and inflation and as a restoration of the market and of private initiative. His aims included lowering inflation, reducing the fiscal deficit, restoring growth through investment, opening the economy to foreign trade and capital, and reducing the bargaining power of organized labor.

The program had an immediate component that rested on repression. Wages were frozen at the outset while prices were freed, and real wages fell sharply in 1976, by estimates of roughly a third, and did not recover their pre-coup levels during the dictatorship. Collective bargaining was suspended, strikes were banned, and union leaders were removed, jailed, or killed. Historians such as Eduardo Basualdo and Jorge Schvarzer argue that the repression of labor and the economic program were intertwined: a policy that cut wages and exposed industry to competition could not easily have been carried out in a democracy with powerful unions. Others emphasize that the military's own security goals and the economic team's aims were distinct, though complementary.

The program differed from the import-substitution and Peronist models. Those models protected domestic industry with high tariffs, expanded the state's role in industry and welfare, and gave unions political influence. Martínez de Hoz sought to lower tariffs gradually, abolish price and rent controls, liberalize finance, and encourage foreign borrowing and investment. Argentina's industrial base was to become more competitive, or shrink. The program was not a simple withdrawal of the state, however. The government kept control of major state enterprises, including the oil company YPF, the railways, and the military-linked firms of Fabricaciones Militares. It increased military spending, financed large public works such as highways and hydroelectric dams, and spent heavily on the 1978 World Cup. State companies borrowed abroad. The state remained a powerful economic actor, and its expenditure helped fuel the debt.

Results were mixed in the early years. Inflation, which exceeded four hundred percent in 1976, dropped but remained at triple digits through 1981, far above the minister's targets. Output recovered somewhat in 1977–1978, and exports grew in some agricultural sectors. Unemployment was officially low, at about three to four percent, a figure that reflected exile, informal work, and withdrawal from the labor force as well as limited job creation. The economy's structure changed, with finance gaining and manufacturing losing ground. These changes, and the debts that accompanied them, are the subject of the next section.

Division III · Section 86.10

Financial Liberalization, Inflation, Foreign Debt, and Industrial Restructuring

The financial reform of 1977 transformed Argentine banking. A new law on financial institutions freed interest rates, allowed the expansion of banks and finance companies, and, crucially, kept a state guarantee on deposits, administered through the Central Bank. Savers could therefore place funds in high-interest institutions with little risk, and institutions could lend aggressively. The result was a boom in speculative finance, sometimes called "financial wealth" or the "sweet money" era, in which short-term profits outpaced productive investment. Many firms found it more profitable to borrow and place funds in the financial market than to invest in production.

Exchange-rate policy deepened these dynamics. In December 1978, the government announced a pre-set schedule of monthly devaluations, the tablita, intended to reduce inflation by guiding expectations. Because domestic inflation continued to exceed the rate of devaluation, the peso became increasingly overvalued. Imported goods became cheap, foreign travel became affordable for the middle class, and exporters and import-competing industry suffered. Together with a program of tariff reductions announced in 1979, the policy exposed manufacturers to foreign competition. Many firms closed or cut employment, and industry's share of output and employment declined during the period. Some large conglomerates, however, grew by combining finance, state contracts, and privileged access to credit.

Foreign borrowing exploded. Using a published Argentine government historical series, external debt was about US$7.7 billion in 1975 and US$45.9 billion in 1983. The comparison uses the same national debt category and identifies 1975, rather than 1976, as the starting observation; alternative institutional series may yield different estimates. Some of the debt was incurred by the public sector, including state enterprises and the military, which bought equipment and financed public works. Part was private, borrowed by firms that took advantage of high domestic rates and the expected stability of the exchange rate. The debt later changed character. In 1982, under Central Bank president Domingo Cavallo, the state assumed responsibility for a large portion of private foreign debt through exchange-rate insurance schemes, converting private liabilities into public ones. Historians continue to debate how far this shift reflected policy design and how far it reflected crisis management. (Source: Argentine government historical debt study).

The model collapsed between 1980 and 1982. In March 1980, a major private bank, the Banco de Intercambio Regional, failed, and a wave of failures followed among banks and finance companies. Capital flight accelerated as confidence dropped. Under Viola's economy minister Lorenzo Sigaut, a large devaluation in early 1981 ended the tablita, and the peso lost much of its value. Inflation rose again, interest rates in the United States rose, and international creditors tightened. Argentina entered a deep recession in 1981–1982. By the time Galtieri took power in December 1981, the economic model that had defined the regime was in ruins, and political support from business was fraying.

Students should consult the original statistical series for inflation, real wages, industrial production, and external debt when using these figures. National and international sources sometimes differ in method, and some data from the period were politically manipulated. The overall pattern is not in doubt: inflation remained high, real wages fell, manufacturing contracted, and debt increased dramatically, leaving the country in a severe crisis and burdened with obligations that the next government would inherit. Chapter 93 treats the continent-wide debt crisis.

Division III · Section 86.11

Labor Repression, the 1978 World Cup, Censorship, and Everyday Life

Organized labor was a main target. The CGT was intervened, collective bargaining was banned, and a labor law passed in 1976 curtailed union rights. Factory floor representatives, known as delegates, were frequent victims of abduction, and in some enterprises the company played a role in identifying them. In 2018, an Argentine court convicted two former executives of Ford Argentina for aiding the kidnapping and torture of workers at its Pacheco plant, and other proceedings concerning companies, including in the sugar-producing province of Jujuy, remain subjects of judicial inquiry. Not all firms behaved alike, and responsibility must be established case by case. Workers who remained faced wage erosion, intensified discipline, and fear. Some union leaders negotiated with the military, others organized quiet resistance, and a minority of unions took more open action, including a general strike in April 1979.

Culture and education were closely supervised. The government banned books, closed publishers, blacklisted artists, and purged universities and schools of staff deemed subversive. In Córdoba, the army burned books in 1976. A program of school and university "cleansing," sometimes called Operativo Claridad, sought to remove suspect teachers and materials. Newspapers practiced self-censorship; journalists who reported disappearances were threatened, and scores were themselves disappeared. Writers and artists went into exile, among them the singer Mercedes Sosa, who left in 1979. Others stayed. Resistance took forms such as coded lyrics in rock and folk music, and in 1981 the Teatro Abierto festival brought playwrights and actors together to stage short plays, until a fire destroyed the theater where it was held.

One documented industrial case illustrates how repression crossed the factory gate. At the Astarsa shipyard in Tigre, on the outskirts of Buenos Aires, an army operation on the day of the coup detained approximately sixty workers; a later Argentine decree declaring part of the site a national historic place records testimony that workplace personnel and union figures supplied lists and information. Military presence within the workplace changed the practical meaning of organization: employees knew that names assembled for employment and union administration could also be used for coercion. Further disappearances and killings among naval workers followed in the Tigre area. These facts do not establish that all employers or all unions collaborated with the dictatorship; they identify a specific documented case. Nor do they tell us what every worker’s family endured. For historical interpretation, the case links security repression with labor relations and shows why economic reform cannot be studied independently of the coercive circumstances in which workers negotiated, protested, or remained silent.

The 1978 FIFA World Cup, held in Argentina from 1 to 25 June, was a central propaganda event. The regime built or renovated stadiums, spent heavily, and promoted the tournament as a showcase of order and national unity. Argentina won the final against the Netherlands, 3–1 after extra time, at the River Plate stadium, a short distance from ESMA. Large crowds celebrated in the streets, and many Argentines, including some who opposed the regime, shared in the joy. Historians stress that the national celebration and the repression coexisted, and that the event allowed the government to claim legitimacy. Opponents abroad, including a committee in France that campaigned for a boycott, tried to draw attention to the disappearances, and visiting journalists encountered the Mothers in Plaza de Mayo. The regime responded with a campaign to rebut "anti-Argentine" criticism, one slogan of which played on the words for "right" and "human."

Everyday life under the Proceso varied by class, region, and political position. Many middle-class Argentines enjoyed cheap imports and foreign travel in the period of the overvalued peso, while industrial workers and the poor lost income. Women bore the burdens of household economies under falling wages, and women were also among the most active resisters, as the next section shows. Young people were suspects: in public places they could be stopped and searched, and rumors circulated about what happened to those taken. Children were affected directly when parents were disappeared and, in some cases, taken into new families. Outside Buenos Aires, repression took local forms: in Tucumán, Córdoba, Jujuy, and the Chaco, it affected rural workers and in some cases Indigenous communities, though the evidence for specific cases is uneven and some remain under judicial investigation.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Astarsa Shipyard Workers, Tigre: A Documented Industrial Community, 1975–1976

Evidence base: This is a documented workplace case, not an invented household. It draws on Argentina’s official historical-site designation of the former Astarsa shipyard area and on testimony and judicial findings summarized by the national human-rights authorities. The records concern identifiable worker groups and documented events; they do not establish what every family individually experienced.

At Astilleros Argentinos Río de la Plata (Astarsa) in Tigre, northern Greater Buenos Aires, shipbuilding and industrial repair provided employment for a substantial workforce. Workers organized over employment conditions and industrial safety in the years before the coup. The shipyard’s history also demonstrates that factory repression did not suddenly begin in March 1976: official memory records describe the seizure and torture of workers by paramilitary groups in 1975, followed by their release under public pressure. Worker activism, union conflict, management decisions, and political violence intersected in a single industrial community.

On 24 March 1976, the day of the military takeover, army personnel entered the shipyard and detained workers. Argentina’s 2014 national historical-site decree records the detention of sixty workers at the plant entrance and cites witness accounts describing cooperation through information and employee lists supplied by elements of management and the union. Further disappearances and killings of naval workers occurred in the Tigre area during the following months. The record documents how the state’s security operation could use workplace knowledge to select targets, and how the loss of delegates weakened workers’ capacity to organize or protest.

For local households, an arrest at the workplace could remove income and force relatives to confront institutions that denied knowledge of the missing. Yet historians should not ascribe the same experience to all families: some workers were detained and released, others disappeared or were killed, and surviving colleagues had different relationships to management, unions, and the armed forces. The case supplies a concrete basis for studying how authoritarian economic and security policies entered industrial neighborhoods. Division III · Section 86.12

Mothers and Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo, Religious Organizations, and Human-Rights Mobilization

The Mothers of Plaza de Mayo began on 30 April 1977, when a small group of women gathered in the main square in front of the presidential palace to demand information about their missing children. Among the founders was Azucena Villaflor. Because gatherings were banned under the state of siege, police told them to keep moving, and they began to walk in pairs in circles, a form that became their trademark. They met every Thursday afternoon. They wore white headscarves, which became their symbol after the pilgrimage to the basilica of Luján in October 1977, and carried photographs of their relatives. Their strategy relied on motherhood, a role the regime claimed to honor, and on the visibility of a public square at the center of the capital. The government harassed them, called them "the crazy women," and in December 1977 abducted several of the group's founders and supporters after an infiltrator gained their confidence.

The Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo organized in October 1977, and their mission was distinct: to find grandchildren who had been born in captivity or taken with their parents and whose identities had been concealed. Beginning as a small group of women, including later leaders such as Estela de Carlotto, they gathered information quietly, followed leads, and developed with scientists the genetic methods that allow a person's biological family to be identified through grandparents' blood. Their work, more than that of any other organization, produced a body of evidence for the systematic nature of the appropriation of children, and they continue to search.

Other organizations worked in parallel. The Permanent Assembly for Human Rights, formed in 1975, and the Ecumenical Movement for Human Rights, formed in 1976, gathered testimony and provided legal and spiritual support. Associations of relatives of the disappeared and of political prisoners, and in 1979 the Center for Legal and Social Studies (CELS), documented cases and filed habeas corpus petitions. Lawyers who took these cases risked their lives, and many were themselves abducted or killed. Journalists documented cases and sent information abroad.

Religious institutions responded in diverse ways. Catholic leadership was divided. Some bishops and military chaplains publicly supported or accommodated the regime, and papal representatives and the episcopal conference were criticized for insufficient protest. Other Catholics risked their lives. In July 1976, five members of the Pallottine order were murdered at a Buenos Aires church; in August 1976, Bishop Enrique Angelelli of La Rioja died in a car crash that a court later ruled was a murder; and priests and nuns working in poor neighborhoods were abducted. Protestant, Jewish, and other religious leaders also took part in human-rights organizations. Historians therefore distinguish defenders of victims from those who supported or accommodated the dictatorship, and caution against treating "the Church" as a single actor.

International attention grew. In September 1979, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, an organ of the Organization of American States, visited Argentina at the government's invitation, after years of requests. Thousands of people queued to submit complaints, and the sight of long lines at the commission's temporary office in Buenos Aires was itself evidence that the scale of disappearance could not be denied. The commission's report, published in 1980, concluded that a large number of persons had been disappeared through the actions of government authorities. The regime rejected the report, but its findings gave human-rights organizations an authoritative outside document and helped shift American and European policy.

Division IV · Section 86.13

Argentina, the United States, Cold War Diplomacy, and International Responses to Repression

The United States recognized and worked with the new government, and American diplomats and officials were aware early of the scale of repression. Under President Gerald Ford and Secretary of State Henry Kissinger, American policy treated the coup as a defeat for Argentine leftist armed groups and as a development that could stabilize a key South American country. Kissinger's conversations with Argentine officials, including a meeting in October 1976, combined encouragement with warnings that human-rights criticism, especially from Congress, would complicate relations and that the junta should act quickly and return to normal procedures. Historians use these records to show that Washington knew about, and did not forcefully oppose, the early repression.

That conclusion has limits. The documents show American knowledge, diplomatic tolerance, and verbal encouragement. They do not show that American officials ordered or directed the disappearance system, which the Argentine services planned and ran, and which had roots in Argentine counterinsurgency before the coup. Historians such as Kathryn Sikkink and Lars Schoultz emphasize that American policy was divided between officials who protested abuses and those who prioritized anticommunism. Reading American documents as evidence of command would erase Argentine responsibility, while ignoring them would understate Washington's role in shaping the regime's international environment.

The administration of Jimmy Carter, from 1977, made human rights an explicit part of foreign policy. Assistant Secretary of State Patricia Derian and others pressed the junta on political prisoners and disappearances, Congress restricted military assistance and some credits, and American officials met with human-rights groups, including the Mothers. Argentine officials responded with irritation and, at times, by turning to other partners. After the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, Argentina refused to join the American grain embargo and continued to sell to the Soviet Union, an irony of an anticommunist regime trading with Moscow that shows how national interests shaped foreign policy. Pressure from Washington nevertheless helped open space for the Inter-American Commission's visit and for the release of some prisoners.

The administration of Ronald Reagan, from January 1981, reversed course. Reagan and his advisers criticized Carter's approach, and officials such as Ambassador to the United Nations Jeane Kirkpatrick argued that authoritarian anticommunist governments were less dangerous than communist ones and should be treated as allies. General Roberto Viola visited Washington in March 1981, and General Galtieri was received warmly in August. Argentina cooperated with the United States in Central America, where Argentine military advisers helped train Nicaraguan counterrevolutionaries, a subject taken up in Chapter 90. The relationship was a basis for Galtieri's later expectation that Washington would stand aside in a South Atlantic dispute, an expectation that proved mistaken.

Division IV · Section 86.14

The Beagle Channel Crisis, Regional Military Relations, Exile, and International Human-Rights Pressure

The dictatorship's relations with neighboring Chile show that military governments did not automatically cooperate. The Beagle Channel dispute concerned three small islands, Picton, Lennox, and Nueva, and the maritime rights they implied at the southern tip of the continent. An arbitration court awarded the islands to Chile in 1977, and Argentina declared the award null in January 1978. Both countries were ruled by military governments, and in December 1978 the two came close to war. Argentine forces were prepared to carry out an operation, scheduled for 22 December, and the armed forces on both sides mobilized. The conflict was averted when Pope John Paul II, newly elected, offered mediation and sent a personal envoy. In January 1979 the two governments agreed to accept papal mediation, and a treaty was signed in 1984, after the return of democracy, and endorsed by an Argentine referendum.

The Beagle crisis must be distinguished from Operation Condor, the intelligence cooperation among Southern Cone security forces examined in Chapter 87. Argentine and Chilean services collaborated in tracking and eliminating exiles, while their governments simultaneously prepared for war over territory. The coexistence of cooperation and conflict indicates that the ideological solidarity of anticommunist military governments was limited by nationalism and strategic rivalry. For Argentina, the near-war also fueled confidence among military planners that force could be used to settle territorial questions, a lesson that affected the later decision on the Falkland Islands, though historians disagree about how much weight to give it.

Exile was a major experience. Tens of thousands of Argentines left, some fleeing direct threats, others the economic and cultural climate. Communities formed in Mexico, Spain, France, Italy, Sweden, Venezuela, and elsewhere, and many hosts welcomed them. Exiles organized committees that gathered testimony, published bulletins, and lobbied governments and international organizations. In Paris, Madrid, and Mexico City they spoke to journalists and parliamentarians, and they helped create the evidence that outside bodies later used. Exile was not safe: Argentine agents operated abroad in some cases, and the regime tried to discredit the exiles as part of an "anti-Argentine campaign." The exile networks were an essential link between the domestic movement and international audiences.

International recognition of nonviolent opposition came in October 1980, when the Nobel Peace Prize was awarded to Adolfo Pérez Esquivel, an Argentine sculptor and coordinator of a Latin American network promoting nonviolent action. He had been arrested in April 1977 and held for about fourteen months without trial, and his imprisonment had been the subject of international appeals. The award was a significant moral victory for Argentine human-rights organizations and an embarrassment for the junta, which had tried to portray itself as a defender of Western values. In his lecture in December, Pérez Esquivel spoke of the rights of peoples in Latin America and of nonviolence as a response to injustice.

Division IV · Section 86.15

The Succession from Videla to Viola and Galtieri, 1981, and Divisions within the Armed Forces

The junta's founding leaders gradually lost influence. Massera, the navy commander and a political rival of Videla, left the junta in 1978 and had political ambitions of his own, which came to little. Agosti left in 1979. Videla's term as president ended on 29 March 1981, when he was succeeded by General Roberto Viola, the army commander designate. The sequence showed that the military held the presidency within the army and rotated it by internal agreement, and not through any outside process.

Viola's brief government faced a worsening economy and disputes about the future. He replaced Martínez de Hoz with Lorenzo Sigaut, whose large devaluation in early 1981 ended the tablita and set off inflation and capital flight. Viola also leaned toward a limited political opening, speaking of dialogue with parties and unions, and in July 1981 the main political parties, from Peronists to Radicals and others, formed a coalition called the Multipartidaria to press for elections. Hard-liners in the army and navy opposed this approach, regarding concessions as a betrayal of the Proceso's mission. Viola's health and authority declined, and by late 1981 the junta was ready to replace him.

General Leopoldo Galtieri, the army commander, took the presidency in December 1981. Viola was first removed temporarily and then formally replaced, and Galtieri was sworn in on 22 December. He was supported by Admiral Jorge Isaac Anaya, who had become navy commander in September, and by Brigadier Basilio Lami Dozo of the air force. Galtieri appointed Roberto Alemann as economy minister, who pursued orthodox policies, including budget cuts and plans to privatize state companies. Galtieri's political ambitions were reportedly broad, including the formation of a civilian political movement under military leadership, and he cultivated a style of confident anticommunism and good relations with Washington.

The armed forces were never monolithic. The army, the largest service, dominated the presidency and the zone system. The navy under Massera and later Anaya maintained its own repressive apparatus at ESMA, had its own political projects, and sought a larger role in government. The air force was smaller and sometimes distanced itself from the others. The services cooperated against the left and competed for budgets, positions, and policy influence, and disputes about political opening and economic policy cut across them. By late 1981 the regime had lost the unity of 1976 and was increasingly dependent on decisions by a few commanders. The instability preceded the Falklands/Malvinas conflict, which many of its leaders hoped to use to restore cohesion and authority.

Division IV · Section 86.16

Economic Crisis, Social Protest, Military Unpopularity, and the Road to War

In 1981–1982, Argentina faced recession, high inflation, bank failures, unemployment in some sectors, and falling wages. Businesses that had borrowed in dollars saw their debts balloon after the devaluation. The regime's earlier base of support among business and the middle class eroded, and opposition became more open. The Multipartidaria demanded elections, the human-rights organizations grew bolder as the regime weakened, and public criticism of the military appeared in the press. The disappearances of 1976–1978 had declined by 1980, but the regime had not accounted for the missing, and families continued to march.

Labor mobilization became important. The CGT, split into factions, included a more combative sector known as CGT-Brasil, after its address, led by Saúl Ubaldini. On 30 March 1982 it called a march to the Plaza de Mayo under slogans calling for peace, bread, and work. Tens of thousands marched in Buenos Aires, and demonstrations took place in other cities. Police and security forces attacked with force, there were large numbers of arrests, and at least one demonstrator was killed in Mendoza. The day showed that the working class, which the dictatorship had repressed for six years, was again able to challenge the government openly, only three days before the landing in the Falkland Islands.

The decision to use force over the Falklands/Malvinas must be understood in this context but not reduced to it. The sovereignty claim was long-standing, and Argentine governments of all kinds had pursued it. Negotiations with Britain, which had seen proposals for sharing sovereignty or leasing the islands back, had stalled, in part because the islanders opposed any transfer and because the British Parliament was unwilling to impose one. In early 1982, talks in New York in February produced little, and the Argentine government signaled that it might choose other means. British decisions also mattered: a plan to withdraw the patrol vessel HMS Endurance, and a nationality law that denied full citizenship to many islanders, were read in Buenos Aires as signs of declining British commitment. On 19 March 1982, a group of Argentine scrap-metal workers landed on South Georgia and raised the Argentine flag, creating a crisis that Britain treated seriously.

Historians offer different interpretations of the junta's intentions. One emphasizes domestic need: a military government facing economic failure and protest sought a national cause that would restore its legitimacy. A second stresses the navy's long-standing plan, developed under Anaya, to recover the islands before the 150th anniversary of the British takeover in January 1983, an aim that existed independently of the crisis. A third emphasizes strategic miscalculation: the junta expected that Britain, facing budget cuts and a distant theater, would negotiate after a fait accompli, and that the United States, its recent partner, would at least remain neutral. Federico Lorenz and other Argentine historians warn that reducing the decision to a publicity exercise ignores the broad national consensus on the claim. Evidence from the later military review of the war, the Rattenbach report, supports the view that planning was poor and assumptions about British and American responses were wrong.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that domestic weakness made risk more attractive, and that the decision reflected a mixture of sovereignty goals, service rivalries, and misjudgment. War was a plausible choice for leaders convinced of a diplomatic window; it was neither inevitable nor strategically sound. That the junta did not anticipate a British military response is one of the best-documented facts of the episode.

Division V · Section 86.17

The Sovereignty Dispute, the April 2, 1982 Landing, and the Outbreak of War

The Falkland Islands, known in Argentina as the Islas Malvinas, lie about 450 kilometers off the Patagonian coast in the South Atlantic. Several European powers contested them in the eighteenth century. Argentina claims that it inherited Spanish rights when it became independent, and that it governed the islands in the 1820s and 1830s, including a settlement under Luis Vernet. Britain maintains that it held the islands before Argentina's independence and reasserted control on 3 January 1833, when a British warship removed the small Argentine garrison. Argentina has never accepted that act, and the claim became part of national identity, taught in schools and shared across the political spectrum. Britain has relied on continuous administration since 1833 and on the principle of self-determination for the people who live there.

The islanders, about eighteen hundred people in 1982, were largely of British descent, many from families that had lived on the islands for generations, and their economy centered on sheep farming and the Falkland Islands Company. They were British subjects, and their preferences mattered in the dispute. Starting in the 1960s, Britain and Argentina held talks under United Nations encouragement, and a communications agreement in 1971 brought Argentine flights and supplies to the islands. Proposals for transferring sovereignty combined with a lease-back to Britain were rejected by the islanders and by Parliament. Argentine governments, military and civilian, saw the long diplomacy as a means of delay.

On the morning of 2 April 1982, Argentine naval and marine forces carried out Operation Rosario, landing near Stanley, which they called Puerto Argentino. A small garrison of Royal Marines resisted briefly, with a few Argentine casualties, and Governor Rex Hunt, after consulting with the marine commander, ordered a surrender. No British personnel were killed. General Mario Benjamín Menéndez was named military governor. In Buenos Aires, a large crowd gathered in the Plaza de Mayo, the same square where the Mothers marched, and cheered General Galtieri. The opposition, including many critics of the regime, widely supported the recovery of the islands, a fact that surprised some of the junta's critics and encouraged its leaders.

The junta expected a limited conflict. Its leaders believed that Britain would protest and negotiate, and that a stable occupation would force Britain to accept a transfer of sovereignty in negotiations. They misjudged. Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher addressed an emergency session of the House of Commons on Saturday, 3 April, and the government immediately began assembling a naval task force. The United Nations Security Council on 3 April adopted Resolution 502, by ten votes to one with four abstentions, demanding an immediate cessation of hostilities, the immediate withdrawal of all Argentine forces, and a diplomatic solution. The resolution did not decide sovereignty; it condemned the use of force. The legitimacy of Argentina's long-standing claim and the legality of its use of force were separate questions, and the resolution's judgment on the latter shaped international opinion.

Division V · Section 86.18

British Mobilization, Failed Diplomacy, the South Atlantic Campaign, and International Alignments

Britain dispatched a naval task force that sailed in the first days of April, built around the aircraft carriers HMS Hermes and HMS Invincible and supported by a large fleet of auxiliary and requisitioned civilian ships. The operation faced extraordinary challenges. The islands lie about 12,000 kilometers from Britain, and the nearest friendly base, Ascension Island, was about 6,000 kilometers away. The force had to cross the Atlantic, land troops in winter conditions, and fight without the land-based air cover that Argentina enjoyed from mainland bases at the edge of its aircraft's range. The weather, the treeless terrain, and the cold shaped every operation. Britain declared a maritime exclusion zone around the islands in April and a wider total exclusion zone effective at the end of the month.

Diplomacy ran alongside the military buildup. American Secretary of State Alexander Haig made a shuttle between London and Buenos Aires in April, seeking a formula that would combine Argentine withdrawal with negotiation over the islands' future. Both governments held positions that left little room: Britain insisted on withdrawal first, and Argentina would not withdraw without a commitment on sovereignty. When the mediation failed, the Reagan administration chose to side with Britain. On 30 April the United States announced economic measures against Argentina and an offer of material support to Britain, and it supplied intelligence, fuel, and weapons such as advanced missiles. Reagan's government had faced a conflict between its two allies, and its decision was shaped by NATO ties and by the view that Argentina had committed aggression.

Latin American governments divided. Many sympathized with Argentina's claim and with the principle of opposing colonial possessions in the hemisphere. The Organization of American States passed a resolution supporting Argentina's sovereignty claim and condemning sanctions, with the United States and a few others abstaining. Peru's president offered a mediation plan in early May that nearly produced a settlement. Chile, still in dispute with Argentina over the Beagle Channel, was widely reported to have assisted Britain with intelligence, and its stance showed that regional military governments did not form a bloc. Brazil represented Argentine interests in London. Cuba and others condemned Britain, while European governments generally backed it. The United Nations Secretary-General pursued diplomacy in May and June without success.

The Battles of 1982: Naval Warfare, Air Attacks, Ground Combat, and Argentine Defeat

A major British operation culminated on 25 April, when British forces recaptured South Georgia, an operation that included damage to an Argentine submarine. The war's central naval incident came on 2 May, when the British nuclear submarine HMS Conqueror sank the Argentine cruiser ARA General Belgrano, which was outside the declared exclusion zone but was considered a threat. About 323 of its crew died, roughly half of all Argentine deaths in the war. The incident remains controversial in Britain and Argentina, with debate about the rules of engagement and the ship's course. Two days later, an Argentine Super Étendard aircraft hit HMS Sheffield with an Exocet missile, killing twenty. Argentina's navy withdrew its fleet to port, and the war at sea was fought largely by aircraft and submarines.

The air war was the Argentine forces' most effective effort. Pilots from the air force and naval aviation, flying at extreme range and very low altitude, attacked British ships, sinking or damaging several, including HMS Coventry and the container ship Atlantic Conveyor, which carried helicopters and supplies. Many Argentine aircraft were lost, and some bombs failed to explode because of the low-altitude release. The British landed at San Carlos on 21 May, establishing a beachhead, and the surrounding waters became known as Bomb Alley. Dozens of ships were hit. On 8 June, aircraft struck the landing ship Sir Galahad at Bluff Cove, killing 48 men, many of them Welsh Guardsmen.

On land, British troops marched across East Falkland. On 28–29 May they fought the Battle of Goose Green, in which paratroopers defeated a larger Argentine garrison, after their commander, Lieutenant Colonel H. Jones, was killed. More than a thousand Argentine soldiers surrendered. In the last days, from 11 to 14 June, British forces took the ridges ringing Stanley, including Mount Longdon, Two Sisters, Mount Harriet, Tumbledown, and Wireless Ridge, in night attacks. General Menéndez surrendered on 14 June. The British took more than eleven thousand prisoners. South Georgia had already surrendered on 25 April. The remaining Argentine detachment on Southern Thule in the South Sandwich Islands surrendered on 20 June, six days after the main Argentine capitulation at Stanley.

The war's human history is not limited to commanders. Many Argentine soldiers were conscripts, young men of eighteen or nineteen with only a few months' training, sent to the islands in thin clothing and boots. British forces, in contrast, were professional soldiers and marines, better trained and equipped, though they also suffered from the cold. Argentine units faced shortages of food and supplies, and conscripts suffered hunger and exposure. There is documented evidence that some officers mistreated their own conscripts, including punishments such as staking them out in the cold and withholding food, and Argentine courts and the veterans' own testimony have addressed these cases. British service personnel, naval and air crews, and the crews of merchant ships also died. Falkland Islanders endured occupation, curfews, and the displacement of some from their homes; three civilian women were killed by British shellfire in the last days. Commonly cited deaths are 649 Argentine military personnel, 255 British military personnel, and 3 islanders.

Military geography helps explain why the campaign unfolded in stages. The recapture of South Georgia was a separate operation from the later landing on East Falkland; the land battle for the main islands depended on building a bridgehead at San Carlos and moving men, ammunition, and supplies across difficult terrain toward Stanley. The Argentine surrender on 14 June therefore did not mean that every isolated South Atlantic outpost changed hands that same day. These distinctions matter because a war’s chronology includes local capitulations, naval actions, aerial attacks, civilian exposure, and diplomatic decisions rather than one undifferentiated moment of defeat.

Division V · Section 86.20

June 1982–December 1983: Military Collapse, the Return of Elections, and the Democratic Inheritance

The surrender produced an immediate political crisis. Galtieri's speech on 15 June, which still suggested resistance, enraged crowds in Buenos Aires, and he resigned on 17 June. The air force and navy withdrew from the junta, leaving the army to select a successor, and on 1 July General Reynaldo Bignone took office with the declared mission of organizing a transition. The armed forces, long presented as guardians of the nation, had lost a war, and revelations about the poor planning of the campaign and the treatment of conscripts deepened their discredit. The military commissioned an internal review, the Rattenbach report, which criticized the conduct of the war, though it was not made public for years.

Civil society mobilized. The Multipartidaria pressed for an election date, labor unions revived, and human-rights organizations found larger audiences. In October 1982, the discovery of unmarked graves of unidentified bodies in a cemetery near Buenos Aires brought the disappearances into the press, as newspapers that had previously been silent now published reports. On 16 December 1982, a march for democracy drew a very large crowd to the Plaza de Mayo. The economy remained in crisis, with inflation accelerating again and debt unresolved. Bignone's government announced that elections would be held in October 1983.

The military tried to control the terms of its exit. On 28 April 1983 it issued a final document, a statement that described its campaign as a war against subversion, defended its methods as necessary, and declared that the disappeared should be considered dead without explaining what had happened to them. In September 1983, it enacted a law of national pacification that granted amnesty for crimes committed in the repression. Both measures provoked outrage. The press, parties, and human-rights groups rejected them, and the Radical Party candidate Raúl Alfonsín promised that he would not accept the self-amnesty. The Peronist candidate, Ítalo Luder, was criticized for suggesting it might stand, and a charge that a pact existed between some union leaders and the military figured in the campaign.

The election was held on 30 October 1983. Alfonsín won roughly 52 percent of the vote to about 40 percent for Luder, the first defeat of the Peronists in a free national election. He took office on 10 December. In his first days, he ordered the prosecution of the leaders of the juntas and of armed organizations, and the new Congress annulled the self-amnesty. On 15 December he signed Decree No. 187, creating CONADEP to investigate the fate of the disappeared. That date closes the narrative of this chapter. It marks the return of elected government and the beginning of a process of investigation that Chapter 94 examines, including the Trial of the Juntas of 1985; it is not the completion of accountability.

The dictatorship's end resulted from several forces. Defeat in the South Atlantic was decisive, since it destroyed the armed forces' prestige and cohesion at the moment of greatest vulnerability. But the regime was already weakened by economic failure, by the opposition of unions, parties, and human-rights groups, by international criticism, and by divisions among the services. Argentina's transition was therefore a collapse more than a negotiated departure, a feature that contrasts with the cases of Brazil and Chile and that shaped the possibility of prosecuting the former rulers.

Historical Vocabulary · Study III
Economic Crisis, War, and Democracy

Financial liberalization: Policies that free interest rates, expand private finance, and reduce controls on capital movements. In Argentina after 1977 it combined with a deposit guarantee that encouraged speculation.

Tablita: A pre-announced schedule of devaluations (from December 1978) meant to guide inflation expectations, which instead left the peso overvalued.

External debt: Obligations owed to foreign creditors, including public and private borrowers; the 1982 transfer of private liabilities to the state is an important distinction.

Sovereignty: Supreme authority over a territory. In the Falklands/Malvinas case, Argentina and Britain advanced competing claims, and the islanders' self-determination was central to Britain's position.

Military surrender: The formal end of fighting by the defeated command, here by General Menéndez on 14 June 1982.

Democratic transition: The period of change from authoritarian to elected government, which can occur by negotiation or collapse and does not by itself ensure accountability.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did the Military Dictatorship Collapse?

The question: Was the dictatorship brought down mainly by economic crisis, social resistance, defeat in the war, or divisions within the armed forces?

Interpretation A — War defeat as the decisive cause: Many historians stress that the regime had survived economic crisis and criticism for years and fell only when it lost the Falklands/Malvinas War. Defeat shattered the armed forces' prestige and their ability to dictate the terms of withdrawal.
Interpretation B — A cumulative crisis accelerated by war: Others emphasize that the regime was already in crisis by 1981: the economic model had failed, the opposition was mobilizing, the Multipartidaria had formed, and the services were divided. On this view, the war was a catalyst that accelerated a decline already under way, and the decision for war was itself a product of the crisis.

Evidence: Both views rely on economic series, accounts of the March 1982 protest, party and union records, military internal documents such as the Rattenbach report, and the chronology of events after June 1982. Counterfactual judgments about what would have happened without the war cannot be tested.

Why it matters: If defeat alone brought the regime down, the transition looks like an accident of war. If the collapse was cumulative, it highlights the role of Argentine society and of the human-rights movement, and it affects how historians assess the credit due to different actors.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation and identify what evidence would help test the claim that the regime would have survived without the war.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Argentine Institutions and Civil Society, 1973–1983

What Changed

  • A constitutional government gave way to a junta and then, in 1983, to an elected president
  • The armed forces went from enjoying national authority to deep discredit
  • The economy shifted from protection and state direction toward finance and external debt
  • Human-rights organizations emerged as influential political actors

What Stayed the Same

  • Inflation, debt, and fiscal strain remained central problems
  • The Peronist movement and the Radical Party survived as the main political forces
  • The sovereignty claim on the Malvinas remained a national cause
  • The judiciary and many state institutions persisted, with consequences for later accountability

Who experienced change most directly: The disappeared and their families, the unionized working class, and the conscripts of 1982. Greater continuity: Many business groups, courts, and wide sectors of daily life. Why the pattern matters: It explains why the return of democracy in 1983 was a beginning, not a resolution.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Argentina's armed forces turned a deep political and economic crisis into an authoritarian state built on clandestine repression, how that state lost its authority, and how defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War accelerated the return of elected government in 1983. Its central argument is that these two stories are connected but not reducible to one another.

The crisis of 1973–1976 was real. Peronism returned in a divided movement, Juan Perón's death left Isabel Perón without authority, and armed organizations on the left and paramilitary groups on the right killed and kidnapped. The Rodrigazo and the general strike of 1975 destroyed the government's economic and political footing. Yet the constitutional government's weakness did not make a coup inevitable or legitimate, and illegal repression, including the Triple A and Operativo Independencia, preceded March 1976.

On 24 March 1976, the junta of Videla, Massera, and Agosti replaced constitutional government with the Proceso de Reorganización Nacional. It dissolved Congress, replaced courts, suspended parties, and intervened in unions, and it governed through a hierarchy of command that divided the country into military zones. Authority operated through formal institutions and through military chains of command, and the services shared and contested power.

The regime justified itself through a national-security doctrine that defined opposition broadly as subversion. This doctrine drew on French counterinsurgency theory, Cold War anticommunism, and Argentine military nationalism, and it supported a national system of clandestine repression. The coup institutionalized and enlarged practices that had begun earlier.

That system operated through hundreds of secret detention centers, including ESMA, La Perla, and Campo de Mayo. Abduction, torture, forced disappearance, extrajudicial killing, death flights, and the appropriation of children formed an organized sequence, sustained by secrecy and by the cooperation of otherwise ordinary institutions. Victims included members of armed groups and a much larger number of workers, students, teachers, lawyers, journalists, and others. Categories of victims must be kept distinct, and numbers such as CONADEP's documented cases and the commemorative figure of thirty thousand answer different questions.

Historians reconstruct this concealed system from survivor testimony, relatives' petitions, forensic work, trials, archives, and foreign documents. The Montoneros and ERP were armed organizations whose actions are historically documented, but the violence of a state controlling police, courts, and prisons is institutionally different from that of clandestine groups, and the "two demons" framing obscures that difference.

Economic policy under Martínez de Hoz combined repression of labor with financial liberalization, tariff reductions, and foreign borrowing. Real wages fell, industry contracted, finance expanded, and external debt multiplied. The model collapsed between 1980 and 1982 through bank failures and devaluation. Culture and education were censored, and the 1978 World Cup was used as propaganda, though ordinary Argentines experienced these years differently according to class, region, and political position.

Resistance came first from families. The Mothers of Plaza de Mayo, the Grandmothers, and organizations such as the Permanent Assembly for Human Rights turned private searches into public pressure, aided by exile networks, religious figures, the Inter-American Commission's 1979 visit, and Pérez Esquivel's 1980 Nobel Prize. International responses varied: the Ford and Kissinger era showed knowledge and tolerance, the Carter administration applied pressure, and the Reagan administration restored warm relations, though Argentine officers were responsible for their own system.

By 1981 the junta's unity had eroded. Videla gave way to Viola and then Galtieri, the services competed, the economy collapsed, and unions and parties mobilized, as the 30 March 1982 march showed. In this setting the junta landed on the Falkland Islands on 2 April 1982, motivated by a long-standing sovereignty claim, navy planning, domestic weakness, and mistaken expectations about British and American responses.

Britain, with United Nations backing and, after April, American support, retook the islands in a campaign defined by distance, weather, air and naval combat, and the experience of conscripts, professionals, and islanders. Argentina surrendered on 14 June 1982, with 649 Argentine, 255 British, and three island civilian deaths commonly cited. Defeat discredited the military, but economic crisis, social opposition, and divisions also mattered. Alfonsín's election on 30 October 1983, his inauguration on 10 December, and the creation of CONADEP on 15 December marked a beginning of investigation and not a completion of justice.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a modern state can build a hidden apparatus of terror inside functioning institutions, and how that apparatus can be exposed by people with little formal power. Argentina became the book's principal national case of enforced disappearance, and the evidence for it came not from the perpetrators but from relatives, survivors, forensic scientists, and later courts.

It also shows how authoritarian projects can fail. The Proceso combined repression, economic liberalization, and nationalism, and lost authority through debt, protest, military division, and a war its leaders misjudged. The Falklands/Malvinas War deserves its own place in the history of the South Atlantic, with its own sovereignty dispute, diplomacy, conscripts, and islanders, even as it shaped Argentina's transition.

The inheritance was ambiguous. Elected government returned in December 1983 with an economy burdened by debt, a discredited military, and a commission to investigate the disappeared.
