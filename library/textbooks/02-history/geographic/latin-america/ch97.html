The Cocaine Economy and the Rise of the Medellín and Cali Cartels

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Commodity Made by Many Hands

Cocaine did not begin as a Colombian invention, and Colombia did not begin as a country of cocaine. The cocaine economy that shook Colombian institutions in the 1980s was assembled over decades from Andean leaf, European chemistry, North American demand, international law, and the commercial skills of entrepreneurs who learned to profit from the gap between legality and desire.

In the highlands of Peru and Bolivia, people had chewed coca leaf and offered it in ritual for many centuries before any laboratory isolated an alkaloid from it. Communities in the Colombian Andes and the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta used the plant too. The leaf was a mild stimulant, a social bond, a medicine, and a sacred offering. In the 1980s the same plant became the raw material for a global industry whose product, cocaine hydrochloride, was chemically and socially a different thing. This chapter begins by insisting on that difference, because confusing coca with cocaine has distorted both Andean history and the history of drug policy.

The chapter's central subject is how cocaine became a major international illicit commodity, why Colombian organizations came to hold such a prominent place in the trade, and how the profits transformed Colombia's economy, politics, and society. The period of principal focus runs from about 1960 to 1990. The chapter reaches back to the nineteenth century, when cocaine was a scientific and pharmaceutical novelty, and it ends at the threshold of the 1990s, when Colombia faced a confrontation between traffickers and the state that Chapter 98 narrates in detail.

The chapter treats the cocaine trade as an economic, institutional, and social system rather than a gallery of famous criminals. Pablo Escobar appears, and so do the Ochoa family, Carlos Lehder, the Rodríguez Orejuela brothers, and others, but they appear inside a structure of coca growers, chemical suppliers, pilots, shippers, wholesalers, bankers, politicians, police officers, and consumers. A history that begins with Escobar cannot explain why there was a market for him. A history that ends with Escobar cannot explain why the trade survived him. The sequence of this chapter therefore moves from origins, to demand and markets, to Medellín, to Cali and illicit capital, and finally to the state and international responses.

A comparison of the three Andean producers sets up an essential distinction. During most of the 1980s Peru and Bolivia were the principal coca-growing countries. Colombia was central to processing the coca base they supplied into cocaine hydrochloride and to organizing its export. Colombian coca cultivation expanded later, and the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime dates the point at which it first surpassed the older growing countries in the late 1990s. Colombia's position was thus a product of commerce, organization, and geography as much as of agriculture.

The evidence for this history is uneven and often produced by interested parties. Enforcement agencies such as the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) wrote intelligence assessments to support budgets and policy. International bodies compiled estimates from governments that sometimes lacked the capacity to measure what they reported. Survey researchers asked people about illegal behavior. Journalists and Colombian truth-telling bodies documented violence, while traffickers' own records seldom survive. Seizures, hectares, estimated retail values, and reported use are four different measurements, and none of them equals production, profit, or addiction. Learning to separate them is one of the chapter's main skills.

This chapter belongs to Part XXII, which examines Colombia's cocaine economy, cartels, armed conflict, and peace. It opens a four-chapter sequence. It follows Chapter 83, which traced La Violencia, the National Front, and the origins of guerrilla war, and it precedes Chapter 98 on Escobar, narco-terrorism, extradition, and the Colombian state. It also draws on earlier chapters: Chapter 20 on the Inca state, which controlled and ritualized coca, and Chapter 42 on colonial Potosí, where coca leaf sustained mining labor. The Colombian cases examined here were never isolated from Mexico, whose later trafficking organizations are the subject of Part XXIII.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish traditional coca-leaf use from the manufacture and trade of cocaine, and explain why the distinction matters historically and politically.
  2. Trace the development of national and international drug control from the early twentieth century through the 1961 Single Convention and the creation of the DEA in 1973.
  3. Compare the positions of Peru, Bolivia, and Colombia in the cocaine economy of the 1980s and explain how rural livelihoods differed from criminal enterprise.
  4. Explain how Colombia's marijuana boom of the 1960s and 1970s created networks, skills, and expectations that shaped the move into cocaine.
  5. Analyze the expansion of U.S. cocaine demand, including the appearance of crack, while interpreting survey, seizure, and public-health data with appropriate caution.
  6. Describe the economic structure of the transnational cocaine trade, including unequal bargaining power, the concentration of profit, and the effect of prohibition on prices and risk.
  7. Evaluate why the label "Medellín Cartel" is both useful and misleading, and situate Pablo Escobar among other Medellín traffickers without reducing the trade to him.
  8. Compare the Medellín and Cali organizations in leadership, cooperation, violence, commercial organization, and public presentation.
  9. Assess how illicit capital entered Colombia's legal economy and how criminal wealth tested elections, police, courts, and local governments, distinguishing proof from allegation.
  10. Analyze the Colombian state's early responses, including Law 30 of 1986, and the international framework created by the 1988 UN Convention.
  11. Interpret primary sources produced by enforcement and treaty institutions, identifying their purposes, perspectives, and limits.
  12. Connect the economic history of the 1980s to the confrontation between traffickers and the state examined in Chapter 98.

Historical Context

Earlier chapters supply three background elements. First, coca belongs to Andean history. The Inca state treated coca as a prestigious, regulated good (Chapter 20), and under Spanish rule coca leaf became a staple of the labor system at Potosí and other mines (Chapter 42). Coca chewing, known as acullico in Quechua and as mambeo in parts of Colombia, persisted through colonial and republican rule as a marker of Indigenous and campesino life, and it was often disparaged by elites who saw it as a mark of backwardness.

Second, Colombia in the mid-twentieth century was a country of regional economies and weak national integration. The violence of the 1940s and 1950s (Chapter 83) displaced rural families, and the National Front arrangement of 1958 to 1974 shared power between the two traditional parties while leaving many rural zones with few public services. Antioquia, whose capital is Medellín, had a strong commercial and industrial tradition and a long history of contraband. Cali and the Valle del Cauca had sugar estates, commerce, and a Pacific port at nearby Buenaventura. The Caribbean coast, including the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta and La Guajira, had long been a smuggling zone.

Third, the United States was becoming the world's largest market for illicit drugs, while its government pressed other states to treat drug supply as a security problem. Washington promoted international treaties, dispatched enforcement agencies abroad, and tied foreign assistance to cooperation. Colombian governments responded with a mixture of cooperation, hesitation, and domestic calculation. They were not simply instruments of U.S. policy, and the dispute over extradition in the late 1980s shows how seriously Colombian institutions weighed sovereignty and security.

Two cautions run through the chapter. Poverty alone does not explain drug trafficking: most poor rural communities did not build international criminal enterprises, and many of the Colombian traffickers who became wealthy came from middle-class or commercial backgrounds. Nor were all people who grew coca members of criminal organizations. Many were farmers responding to prices, credit, road access, and the lack of alternatives, and they usually earned a small fraction of the final value of the drug.

Finally, "cartel" is a term of art with a contested history. In economics it means a group of producers who agree to fix prices or output. The Colombian trafficking groups did cooperate, but they also competed, and observers disagree about how tightly they were organized. This chapter uses "cartel" where sources do and where convention requires, but it asks students to treat the word as a problem to be examined rather than a fact to be assumed.

Section 97.1

Coca, Cocaine, and the Historical Transformation of an Andean Commodity

Coca (Erythroxylum species, chiefly E. coca in the Andean highlands and eastern slopes and E. novogranatense in parts of Colombia and Venezuela) is a shrub whose leaves contain small quantities of alkaloids, including cocaine. Archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence shows that Andean peoples had used coca for a very long time. Chewing a wad of leaf with an alkaline substance releases the alkaloids gradually. The effect is mild, comparable to strong tea or coffee in many accounts, and it eases fatigue, hunger, and the effects of altitude. Coca also functioned as an offering, as a gift that sealed reciprocity, and as a divinatory tool. Under the Inca state it was a controlled, high-status good (Chapter 20). After the Spanish conquest, planters and mine owners expanded cultivation and supplied it to laborers at Potosí, and some clerics condemned it while Crown and Church officials taxed it (Chapter 42).

Cocaine is a different thing. In 1859 and 1860 the German chemist Albert Niemann isolated the alkaloid from coca leaf. The extraction required chemical processing and yielded a concentrated white powder whose effects, whether swallowed, sniffed, or injected, were far stronger and more rapid than those of chewed leaf. A scientific and commercial boom followed. Physicians, among them Sigmund Freud, studied and promoted it, and ophthalmologists adopted it as a local anesthetic in the 1880s. Firms such as Merck in Germany manufactured it, and tonics and wines containing coca extract, among them the French Vin Mariani, were widely sold. American beverages used coca extract in their early formulations before it was removed.

This nineteenth-century market had Andean consequences. Peru developed a legal export trade, first in leaf and then in crude cocaine processed near the growing areas for sale to European and American refiners, and Java and other colonial regions grew coca for the same market. Paul Gootenberg, whose Andean Cocaine: The Making of a Global Drug (2008) is the foundation of modern scholarship on the subject, shows that Peruvian elites and Andean farmers took part in a legal cocaine economy from the late nineteenth century. The later illicit trade, in his account, was neither an ancient Andean inheritance nor a purely modern invention. It grew from a legal commodity whose status changed.

That change came as medical opinion shifted. By the early twentieth century, evidence of dependence, toxicity, and social harm had accumulated, and reformers in the United States and Europe pressed for restrictions. The Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 in the United States treated cocaine alongside opiates, and other countries followed with their own controls. International conferences gradually extended the idea that the supply of such drugs should be limited to medical and scientific needs. A UN Commission of Enquiry on the Coca Leaf, which visited Peru and Bolivia in 1949 and 1950, concluded that chewing coca was harmful. Critics then and later argued that the commission rested on prejudiced assumptions about Indigenous people and ignored Andean knowledge.

By mid-century, legal demand for cocaine had collapsed, and cocaine moved to the margins of consumption. Peru retained a legal coca trade through a state enterprise, and Bolivian and Peruvian highlanders continued to chew leaf. The conditions for the later illicit boom were not yet in place, but the building blocks existed: a known chemical process, Andean knowledge of cultivation and processing, trade networks that had once served legal markets, and a legal regime that would make future supply risky and therefore profitable.

Historical Vocabulary
Coca versus Cocaine

Coca is the plant and, by extension, its leaf, used in the Andes for chewing, infusion, ritual, and medicine for centuries. Coca paste or base is a crude intermediate made by processing large quantities of leaf with solvents and chemicals. Cocaine hydrochloride is the refined powder that entered international illicit markets. Crack is cocaine prepared in a smokable form, sold in small, cheap units.

The distinction is both chemical and historical. Chewing leaf delivers a small, slow dose, and the leaf is bulky and perishable. Cocaine hydrochloride is concentrated and easy to transport. Treating the two as one thing makes traditional Andean consumers appear to be drug users, and it makes coca farmers appear to be traffickers. Policy debates about eradicating coca have repeatedly turned on this confusion.

Section 97.2

Prohibition, International Drug Control, and the Expansion of Illicit Markets

International drug control developed in stages: the Hague Opium Convention of 1912, League of Nations agreements in the 1920s and 1930s, and, after the Second World War, United Nations instruments. In 1961 a conference in New York adopted the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, which consolidated earlier treaties, placed cultivation and trade of designated plants and substances under national licensing, and created a monitoring system. It entered into force in December 1964. It gave coca leaf its own treatment, and its approach set the framework within which Andean states, and later Colombia, were expected to act.

The U.S. role in building this framework was substantial, but the framework also reflected the interests of many governments. In the United States, the Controlled Substances Act of 1970 organized federal regulation, and President Richard Nixon declared drug abuse a national priority in 1971. In July 1973 the Drug Enforcement Administration was established by consolidating earlier agencies, and the new agency stationed agents abroad to work with foreign police. This history matters for Colombia because enforcement against cocaine was never purely domestic. It involved bilateral agreements, intelligence sharing, extradition, funding for equipment, and annual political reviews of foreign cooperation, particularly after the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 introduced a certification process for aid recipients.

The economics of prohibition shaped the trade. When a drug is illegal, producers and sellers cannot use courts to enforce contracts, advertise openly, or borrow from banks. They face risks of seizure, arrest, and violence. Those risks raise costs, and costs raise prices. Where demand is strong, high prices produce high margins for those who can manage risk, and the illegal margin attracts entrepreneurs willing to accept it. Economists such as Peter Reuter and Mark Kleiman, writing in the 1980s, argued that enforcement raised prices mainly by increasing risks to sellers and that it affected different parts of the supply chain very differently: it was cheap for traffickers to replace a farm's crop and expensive to replace an experienced smuggler or a trusted financial contact.

Prohibition did not create demand, and it is too simple to say that it created the cartels. It is fair to say that prohibition made trafficking an activity in which violence, corruption, and secrecy had commercial value. Whether stricter or looser regimes would have produced a different history is a contested counterfactual, taken up in Historical Debate I below.

For Andean peoples the 1961 Convention had a distinct meaning. By classifying coca leaf as a controlled substance and calling for the end of chewing within a transitional period, it treated an Indigenous practice as a form of drug abuse. Bolivia, Peru, and later Colombia, where Indigenous organizations defended traditional use, negotiated this tension for decades. The 1961 text therefore illustrates a general feature of drug control: international rules written largely from the perspective of consumer and manufacturing countries could place heavy obligations on producing countries and their rural populations.

Primary Source Window

Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, 1961: The Coca Bush and Coca Leaves

Creators: Plenipotentiary conference convened by the United Nations, New York · Dates: adopted 30 March 1961; in force 13 December 1964 · Type: International treaty

Context: The convention replaced a patchwork of earlier agreements. Delegations from drug-manufacturing and consuming states, and from producing states, negotiated its terms, including a provision on coca that drew on the 1950 report of the UN Commission of Enquiry on the Coca Leaf.

Editorial paraphrase (original treaty, Arts. 26, 27, 49): Article 26 establishes special controls on permitted coca-bush cultivation and leaf production, Article 27 allows limited use of coca leaf for preparing a flavoring agent without alkaloids, and Article 49 sets transitional conditions for certain traditional practices, including a twenty-five-year phase-out for coca chewing following entry into force. These provisions describe legal obligations and policy assumptions, not a direct observation of Andean communities.

Analysis: The treaty reveals the assumptions of its drafters: that coca chewing was a harm to be phased out and that the leaf could be regulated like opium. It shows international law treating an Indigenous practice as a transitional anomaly. Its limits are equally important. It does not describe how coca was actually used in Andean communities, it does not record Indigenous voices, and it says nothing about how states would enforce it. Later history shows that the abolition deadline passed without ending traditional use, and that Bolivia and Peru kept legal domestic markets. Students should read the treaty's text directly and not rely on summaries.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the treaty say about coca leaf, and how does that differ from its treatment of manufactured cocaine?
  2. Who wrote the convention, and what interests and assumptions may have shaped it?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the logic of international drug control?
  4. What can they not learn about Andean coca use or the effects of the treaty from the text alone?

Section 97.3

Peru, Bolivia, and the Andean Coca-Growing Economy

In the early 1980s the world's illicit coca came overwhelmingly from Peru and Bolivia. In Peru, the traditional growing areas lay in valleys on the eastern slope of the Andes, around Cusco and La Convención, and a newer zone of settlement and cultivation grew in the Upper Huallaga valley near Tingo María. In Bolivia, long-established growers in the Yungas of La Paz supplied the traditional market, and settlers colonized the Chapare in the department of Cochabamba. Colombia, in contrast, grew little coca in 1980 and imported most of the base it processed. A United Nations bulletin estimated that in 1981 Peru had some 50,000 hectares of coca, of which a portion was for local traditional consumption, and Bolivia roughly 55,000 hectares, again with a share for traditional use. Such estimates were rough, but they show that traditional and illicit uses overlapped.

Why did coca expand in these regions? Several forces converged. Migrants from the highlands moved to eastern lowlands in search of land, driven by rural poverty, fragmented holdings, and, in Peru, by economic crisis and violence. The Peruvian economy suffered severe inflation and debt problems in the 1980s. Bolivia experienced hyperinflation in the mid-1980s, and the stabilization program of 1985 led to the closure or downsizing of state mines, after which many unemployed miners and their families moved to colonization zones, including the Chapare. State services were scarce in these frontier areas. Roads, credit, and marketing support for alternative crops such as bananas, citrus, and pineapples were weak or unreliable, while coca leaf had a ready buyer who came to the farm, paid cash, and required no road to market.

The economics for households were therefore comparatively rational but not lucrative. Growers earned the farm-gate price of leaf or, in some zones, of coca paste prepared in pits near the field. This income was far above what many alternative crops yielded, and far below the value added later in the chain. Farmers were also exposed. They depended on buyers, faced eradication, and suffered when armed groups, notably Sendero Luminoso in the Upper Huallaga, taxed or controlled areas where coca was grown. In Peru, state forces, insurgents, and traffickers competed over the valley. To treat growers as "narcotraffickers" is to conflate an agricultural livelihood with international organized crime.

Legal frameworks differed. Peru's state enterprise, ENACO, had a monopoly over the legal trade in leaf for traditional use, and Bolivia's Law 1008 of 1988 divided territory into zones of traditional, transitional (surplus), and illicit production, setting limits on eradication and aid. These laws recognized, however imperfectly, that coca had licit uses. They also created difficult enforcement problems, because leaf could be diverted between legal and illegal channels.

The coca-growing economy also reveals women's and children's roles. Households cultivated and harvested leaf and dried it; women and children were often involved in harvesting and in processing tasks, and families shared the risks of eradication and violence. Indigenous organizations, particularly in Bolivia, built political movements around the defense of coca as a cultural and economic resource, a theme that would shape national politics in later decades (Chapter 108). In the 1980s, however, they faced a United States policy that emphasized crop reduction.

Section 97.4

Colombia's Transition from Marijuana Trafficking to Cocaine

Colombia's entry into the international drug trade began with marijuana. During the late 1960s and the 1970s, cultivation in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta and La Guajira, and smuggling by sea and air to the United States, brought sudden income to parts of the Caribbean coast. Residents called the boom the bonanza marimbera. Lina Britto's Marijuana Boom (2020) shows how this first drug economy remade a peripheral region: it financed new towns, lifestyles, and local power, and it involved peasants, Wayuu and other Indigenous communities, merchants, and officials. The U.S. demand for marijuana had grown in the 1960s counterculture, and enforcement against Mexican marijuana, including herbicide spraying in the mid-1970s, increased the attractiveness of Colombian supply.

The marijuana boom did several things that mattered for cocaine. It built routes and relationships, from boats and airstrips to purchasers in Florida and connections with corrupt officials. It created a class of smugglers who knew how to move bulky goods and manage risk. It introduced Colombian migrants and entrepreneurs to the U.S. market, helped by Colombian communities that had settled in New York, Miami, and other cities. And it showed that large profits could be made with limited interference. Government campaigns in the late 1970s, notably a military-led offensive in La Guajira, disrupted parts of the marijuana trade and encouraged the shift of capital and expertise toward a more compact and valuable product.

Cocaine was that product. It had a far higher value per unit of weight than marijuana. It was easier to conceal and transport. And by the late 1970s, demand in the United States, particularly among higher-income consumers, had begun to rise. Earlier cocaine channels had involved Chilean processors, Cuban-exile networks in Miami, and Bolivian and Peruvian suppliers. Colombian entrepreneurs, some already in contraband and marijuana, moved into buying base from Peru and Bolivia, processing it in Colombian laboratories, and arranging shipment northward. The Colombian state's weakness in remote lowlands, the prevalence of small aircraft and airstrips, and the long coastlines of the Caribbean and Pacific aided this expansion.

Geography and commerce explain much of Colombia's role, but not all of it. Colombia sat between the coca-growing countries to the south and the markets to the north, with ports on two oceans. Its commercial class included people experienced in contraband and in dealing with U.S. counterparts. Some regions, notably Antioquia and the Valle del Cauca, had business traditions that valued entrepreneurship and informal exchange. Institutional weakness mattered as well: police and courts were underfunded, and customs control was limited. But these conditions existed elsewhere in Latin America without producing the same result. The Colombian case required, in addition, entrepreneurs who could organize multiple stages of the trade.

An early DEA assessment captures how contemporary enforcement officials saw the shift. In 1985 the agency reported that cocaine trafficking in 1984 and early 1985 remained widespread, that enforcement pressure in Colombia and an oversupply of coca products contributed to further expansion of the international traffic, that conversion of base to cocaine was spreading beyond Colombia to Panama, Venezuela, Mexico, Canada, and the United States, and that distribution was extending to Canada, Western Europe, and Asia.

Section 97.5

The Expansion of U.S. Cocaine Demand, 1970–1990

A trade needs buyers. In the 1970s cocaine regained a place in U.S. consumption after decades of marginality. Several developments contributed. Public concern had focused on heroin and on marijuana, and cocaine acquired an image, in part encouraged by the entertainment industry and by some medical commentary, as a glamorous, expensive, and relatively safe stimulant. It circulated among musicians, entertainers, professionals, and wealthier young adults. The high price, a gram costing a substantial fraction of a week's earnings for many workers, made it a status good. The belief that it was not addictive, now known to be false, lowered perceived risks.

The figures from national surveys show a broad rise and fall that is easy to misread. The National Household Survey on Drug Abuse (NHSDA) found that past-month cocaine use among adults aged 18 to 25 stood at 9.9 percent in 1979, fell to 7.0 percent in 1982, rose to 8.1 percent in 1985, and fell to 4.8 percent in 1988. Among those aged 26 to 34, the rate rose from 3.0 percent in 1979 to 6.3 percent in 1985, then dropped to 2.8 percent in 1988. In other words, the peak of casual use came around 1979 to 1985 and receded by the end of the decade, while measures of harm such as emergency room visits and deaths rose and peaked later.

That paradox is explained by the emergence of crack. In the mid-1980s dealers began selling cocaine in smokable form in small, inexpensive units. Crack appeared in several U.S. cities in 1984 and 1985 and spread widely in 1986. It delivered a rapid and intense effect and was affordable to people with little money. It was concentrated in particular urban neighborhoods, where the retail trade also created high levels of violence among sellers and with police. The death of the basketball player Len Bias from cocaine in June 1986 produced intense public alarm, and Congress passed the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 and a further act in 1988. The 1986 law established mandatory minimum sentences with a far harsher quantity threshold for crack than for powder cocaine, a disparity that critics later argued fell heavily on Black communities.

Students must distinguish different consumers and different data. Occasional powder users, often employed and middle-class, rarely appeared in hospital records. Heavy users, including many crack users, were more likely to be arrested or hospitalized. Lifetime use, past-year use, and past-month use measure different things. Surveys of households missed homeless people, prisoners, and others, and respondents may underreport illegal behavior. Emergency room mentions count episodes in which cocaine was named, not individuals, and they depend on which hospitals reported. Seizures reflect enforcement effort as well as supply. Reported use is not the same as a substance-use disorder, and it is not the same as the number of people who bought cocaine from Colombian suppliers.

The consequences for the Andean and Colombian side were direct. A large and growing market increased the quantities that could be sold, supported the expansion of Colombian organizations, and raised the stakes of enforcement. The perception in Washington that cocaine was a national emergency produced policies, from interdiction to pressure on source countries, that Colombian officials found both useful and intrusive.

Section 97.6

The Transnational Cocaine Economy: Production, Commerce, and Distribution

It helps to picture the cocaine economy as a chain of stages in which value increased with distance from the field. At one end were coca-growing households in Peru, Bolivia, and, increasingly, Colombia. They sold leaf or paste to buyers who came to them. In the next stage, base was transported to processing sites, mostly in Colombia, where chemists and laborers converted it into cocaine hydrochloride using solvents and other chemicals, many of them imported legally and diverted. Then came export, usually arranged by organizations that purchased in bulk and shipped northward. In the destination country, importers sold to wholesalers, who sold to mid-level distributors, and these in turn sold to retail dealers and consumers.

The relationships among these actors were unequal. A farmer who sold leaf had little bargaining power and faced the risks of crop destruction and arrest, but the farmer's replacement cost was low, since another farmer could be found. The Colombian organizations that processed and exported held greater power because they controlled finance, chemicals, transport, and contacts abroad. In the destination country, wholesale and retail sellers handled the highest legal risks of street arrest and violence, yet most captured relatively small shares of the overall profit. The pattern of concentrated profit, distributed risk, and unequal power is characteristic of an illicit commodity chain.

The difference between agricultural value and final value was very large. The value of coca leaf at the farm gate was small compared with the value of cocaine sold at retail in the United States, after multiplication through processing, transport, risk premiums, and repeated markups. But this difference does not measure profit. A large share of the retail price paid for the costs of distribution, security, bribery, and losses to seizure, and much of the retail markup stayed in the consumer country with dealers. For this reason, aggregate estimates of "the drug trade's value" are poor guides to what Colombian organizations earned.

The role of intermediaries was crucial. Pilots, boat captains, chemists, accountants, lawyers, couriers, and corrupt officials were paid, and their services created local economies of employment. Women appeared in these chains as couriers, financial managers, and household heads who managed assets when men were killed, jailed, or in hiding, but they are underrepresented in the available sources, which were produced largely by male police and journalists. The Colombian diaspora in the United States provided some personnel and connections, although most Colombian migrants had nothing to do with the trade and often suffered from the stigma it created.

Finally, the transnational character of the system meant that no single state could regulate it. Chemical supplies might come from North America and Europe. Money passed through banks in several countries. Consumers were in wealthy societies whose policies shaped demand. This is why enforcement in one place could redirect the trade rather than end it, a theme developed in §97.19.

Section 97.7

Trafficking Corridors and the Geography of International Markets

Broad patterns of geography, without operational details, show how the market changed. In the late 1970s and early 1980s the main entry for Colombian cocaine into the United States was South Florida. Its proximity to the Caribbean, its large airspace and coastline, the presence of Colombian and Cuban-exile communities, and its role as a banking center made Miami the first great market and financial hub. The Bahamas and other Caribbean islands served as transit points. The case of Norman's Cay in the Bahamas, associated with Carlos Lehder, became notorious, and the Bahamian government held an inquiry in 1983 and 1984 into corruption linked to the trade.

U.S. enforcement responded. In 1982 the Reagan administration created a South Florida Task Force under Vice President George H. W. Bush, and enforcement pressure on the Florida route increased. The DEA's 1985 assessment, quoted in §97.4, describes how pressure in one place coincided with diversification elsewhere. Over the mid-1980s, as pressure on the Florida approach increased, a larger share of cocaine reached U.S. markets through Central America and Mexico, and distribution extended to other cities, including Los Angeles, New York, Houston, and Chicago. The Mexican connection grew in importance during the later 1980s, and Chapter 101 returns to it as the foundation of Mexico's own trafficking organizations. For this chapter, it is enough to note that Mexican traffickers at first served as transporters and subcontractors for Colombian suppliers before building independent power.

European demand was small relative to the U.S. market in the 1980s but began to grow, and the DEA in 1985 already noted expansion into Western Europe, Canada, and Asia. Spain and Portugal, with links to Latin America through language and migration, became important European entry points in later years. Cali's organizations are often credited with developing European routes as the U.S. market became more saturated and enforcement intensified, although the evidence is stronger for the early 1990s than for the 1980s.

Within South America, the Caribbean coast, the Pacific coast, and the Amazon and Orinoco basins each served particular functions. Panama, with its banking system and shipping, played a prominent role, discussed further in Chapter 92. Brazil's rivers and airspace, the Southern Cone, and West Africa would later become routes as well. This chapter avoids describing methods of concealment or transport. What matters historically is the pattern: enforcement shifted routes, trade adapted, and organizations that could adjust, by using new corridors and new partners, survived.

Section 97.8

Prices, Profits, Competition, and the Economics of Illicit Trade

Cocaine prices in the United States did not rise with demand in the 1980s. Official sources recorded the opposite. Retail and wholesale prices declined while purity rose, and the 1988 GAO review of antidrug efforts in Colombia and Bolivia stated that availability and purity had steadily increased since 1981 while prices had fallen. The pattern is a sign of expanding supply and competition, and it means that enforcement did not succeed in restricting the market over the period.

Why did high revenues create incentives for violence and corruption? A legal business with high profits attracts competitors who may undercut it. An illegal business cannot ask courts to protect contracts or property, so sellers use private enforcement, which may mean threats, killings, and bribery, and so the willingness to use violence becomes a competitive asset. High margins also finance corruption: a bribe that would be prohibitive in an ordinary business can be small relative to the value of a shipment. This logic helps explain the repeated cases of attacks on judges and journalists and of payments to police, though it does not explain which individuals chose violence or why violence varied between organizations.

Quantity, not only price, mattered. Cocaine is compact and valuable, and large shipments could produce large revenue. Trafficking organizations that could move volume enjoyed economies of scale, but they also faced the risk that a single seizure would cause large losses. This encouraged diversification of shipments and routes, cooperation among traffickers to share transport and insurance against seizure, and the development of stable relationships with corrupt officials.

Estimates of trafficker revenue must be treated with great caution. Journalists and officials sometimes claimed that particular organizations earned billions of dollars annually, but these claims depend on assumptions about volume, price, and costs that were rarely documented. The estimated retail value of the U.S. market is not the revenue received in Colombia, and the amount that was repatriated or invested in Colombia was only part of what was earned. Likewise, one organization's income cannot be inferred from a total market value. Students should regard stated fortunes, such as magazine lists of billionaires, as journalistic estimates and not as audited facts.

Competition was also a matter of relationships. The leading Colombian organizations did not simply fight for market share; they cooperated on shipments, shared information, and sometimes arbitrated disputes, while also competing for suppliers, routes, and territory. The later history of conflict between Medellín and Cali networks, discussed in Division IV, shows how cooperation could collapse when rivals disagreed or when enforcement created suspicion of betrayal.

At each stage of the trade, a different price was being measured. A farm-gate quotation for a kilogram of leaf was not a price for coca paste, refined cocaine in a producing country, a wholesale transaction in Miami, or a small retail sale in a U.S. neighborhood. One cannot multiply the final street price by an estimated crop and call the result Colombian cartel income: processing losses, transport, payments to intermediaries, confiscations, retail margins, and changing purity all intervene. The DEA's May 1985 assessment itself recorded substantial fluctuations during 1984, with enforcement pressure increasing prices in certain South American markets even while U.S. wholesale prices remained well below the levels recorded earlier in the decade. The coexistence of those trends warns against treating the entire international chain as a market with one price. The information came from intelligence reporting and observed transactions, not a census of every sale.

Illicit markets also created unusually severe problems of measurement. A dramatic seizure might reflect an expansion in trafficking, a more capable enforcement unit, or an unusually successful investigation. A laboratory's estimated maximum output was not proof that it had operated at maximum capacity for an entire year. Prices reported in different currencies, at different purity levels, and for different quantities could not be compared without adjustment. These limitations do not render economic analysis impossible. They require historians to identify what is observed, what is modeled, and what is speculative before claiming that an enforcement policy raised costs, reduced consumption, or transferred revenue between regions.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Prohibition and International Enforcement Increase the Value and Organizational Power of Cocaine Trafficking?

The question: Did the legal prohibition of cocaine, and the enforcement that accompanied it, unintentionally raise the profits and strengthen the organizations that supplied the market?

Interpretation A — Prohibition created the conditions for organized trafficking: Analysts in this tradition argue that making a drug illegal creates a risk premium and a market in which only those able to use violence, bribery, and secrecy can operate. Enforcement that fails to eliminate demand raises costs and selects for more ruthless and better-organized suppliers. On this view, the scale of Colombian organizations and their power over institutions were partly products of the legal regime.
Interpretation B — Demand and local conditions were decisive: Other scholars emphasize demand in rich countries, the commercial skills and networks of Colombian entrepreneurs, weak state capacity, and political decisions inside Colombia. They note that prohibition existed for decades before the boom, and that countries with comparable laws did not produce comparable organizations. On this view, prohibition was a necessary condition, not a sufficient explanation, and its effects depended on circumstances.

Evidence: Both views draw on economic analyses of illicit markets, such as the work of Peter Reuter and Mark Kleiman on risks and prices, on Francisco Thoumi's studies of Colombia and the Andes, on Paul Gootenberg's history of the transition from legal to illegal cocaine, and on official assessments such as the 1988 GAO report. A true experiment is impossible. Historians cannot rerun the 1980s under a different legal regime, and comparisons with other times and places are imperfect.

Why it matters: The question shapes how responsibility is assigned and what policies appear sensible. If prohibition generated the organizations, then reforming policy might change the market. If demand and local conditions were primary, enforcement and institution-building might matter more. Historians are generally cautious about sweeping claims in either direction.

Section 97.9

The Formation of the Medellín Trafficking Networks

Medellín in the 1970s was Colombia's second city and the capital of Antioquia, a department with a distinct regional identity built on coffee, mining, textiles, and commerce. Its entrepreneurs, called paisas, had a reputation for commercial energy, and the region had a long history of contraband, which moved untaxed goods from Panama and the Caribbean coast into the interior. The city's growth had been rapid, driven by migration from the countryside, including families displaced by violence in the 1950s. Slum neighborhoods spread up the valley's hillsides, and the industrial economy that had provided jobs began to struggle in the following decades. These conditions did not cause trafficking, but they shaped the pool of labor and the social setting in which it grew.

The networks that took shape in the late 1970s were composed of men from varied backgrounds. Some came from families with commercial or ranching wealth, others from modest origins in contraband or petty crime. They bought base from Peru and Bolivia, operated or financed laboratories, arranged shipments to the United States, and recruited pilots, couriers, and security personnel. Cooperation among them was practical. Sharing the costs of a shipment, pooling transport, or providing an alternative route spread risk and allowed larger volumes. Such arrangements did not require a single boss, and several of the most important figures worked with considerable independence while collaborating at key moments.

Where did the expression "Medellín Cartel" come from? It was popularized in the 1980s by U.S. officials and journalists, and it was applied by many Colombians as well. The word suggested a formal organization, a single corporation with a command structure and a coordinated market strategy. Contemporary and retrospective law-enforcement documents describe something less tidy: multiple trafficking organizations cooperating, sharing infrastructure and security, and sometimes joining together for political purposes, such as opposing extradition. Their alliance was real, but so were their independent interests and rivalries.

An episode from late 1981 illustrates both cooperation and private armed organization. After guerrillas of the M-19 kidnapped Martha Nieves Ochoa, a sister of the Ochoa brothers, in November 1981, traffickers and associates announced a group called Muerte a Secuestradores (MAS, "Death to Kidnappers") and the woman was freed in early 1982. Reports linked the group to a collective response by trafficking families and to armed actors, military personnel, and landowners who had their own reasons for opposing guerrillas. The case shows early convergence between criminal wealth, private violence, and the wider conflict, subjects Chapter 99 treats in detail.

The Medellín networks were never limited to Antioquia. They employed people in other regions, Florida, Panama, and the Caribbean. They invested in land, including large rural estates in the Magdalena valley and elsewhere, which also became a basis for later paramilitary politics. In studying the emergence of the networks, historians face a thin evidentiary base, composed of police files, court testimony from later trials, journalism, and memoirs. Each has limits, and statements by former associates, often produced in plea negotiations or for profit, require corroboration.

Historical Vocabulary
Cartel versus Criminal Network

In economics, a cartel is an agreement among producers to restrict output or fix prices so as to raise profits. A criminal network is a set of individuals and groups linked by cooperation, trust, and exchange, without necessarily having a unified command. The term "cartel" was applied to Colombian trafficking groups by officials and the press from the early 1980s.

The word helps readers see that cooperation existed, but it can mislead. It implies a hierarchy and price control that the sources do not clearly show, and it encourages the idea that removing a leader would end an organization. This chapter uses "Medellín Cartel" and "Cali Cartel" because they are the standard historical labels, and it treats them as shorthand for networks of cooperating organizations with varying degrees of coordination.

Section 97.10

Pablo Escobar: From Criminal Entrepreneur to Nationally Prominent Trafficker

Pablo Emilio Escobar Gaviria was born on 1 December 1949 in Rionegro, Antioquia, and grew up in the nearby municipality of Envigado, in a family of modest means. Accounts of his youth, including reports of petty theft and involvement in contraband, vary in reliability, because many were produced decades later by relatives, associates, and journalists. What is well documented is that by the mid-1970s he was active in illegal commerce, that he moved into cocaine in the second half of the decade, and that by the early 1980s he was among the most visible and wealthiest figures in the trade. Gustavo Gaviria, a cousin, was a close partner.

Escobar's growth was not simply the result of ruthlessness, although his willingness to use violence became notorious. He and his partners organized large-volume shipments, secured access to base supplies, and developed relationships with suppliers and buyers. His wealth supported investments in real estate, a country estate, the Hacienda Nápoles in Puerto Triunfo, with a private zoo that later became a symbol of excess, aircraft, and other assets. Magazine lists of the world's wealthiest people in the late 1980s included him, though, as noted in §97.8, such estimates were speculative.

He cultivated public visibility in a way that many traffickers did not. He sponsored football fields and lighting in poor neighborhoods, supported housing construction for families living near Medellín's garbage dump in the Moravia area under a project later associated with the slogan "Medellín sin Tugurios" (Medellín without slums), and built a civic movement that spoke to disadvantaged residents. In 1982 he was elected as an alternate representative (suplente) in the Chamber of Representatives on a dissident Liberal list, a position that gave him parliamentary immunity when he substituted for the principal member. Justice Minister Rodrigo Lara Bonilla and the newspaper El Espectador publicly exposed his background in 1983, and he was pushed out of Congress. Lara Bonilla was assassinated on 30 April 1984. The consequences are the subject of Chapter 98.

The source problem is acute. Escobar's biography is among the most heavily mythologized of any twentieth-century Latin American figure. Colombian researchers such as Alonso Salazar have produced careful, interview-based accounts of Medellín's society, notably La parábola de Pablo (2001) and No nacimos pa' semilla (1990) on young hired killers. Journalist Mark Bowden's Killing Pablo (2001) offers a narrative of the hunt for Escobar from a largely law-enforcement perspective. Memoirs by relatives and associates are self-interested and inconsistent. Films and television series dramatize events, invent scenes and dialogue, and often present a charismatic antihero. The more famous the figure, the greater the risk that fiction replaces documentation.

Popular culture also distorts relative importance. A focus on Escobar obscures other Medellín figures, the Cali organizations, and the farmers, officials, and consumers who made up the system. It can also overshadow the victims: judges, journalists, police officers, candidates, civilians killed in bombings, and families of those who disappeared, whose experiences appear in the Colombian Truth Commission's final report and in survivors' testimony. A responsible history takes Escobar seriously as one important actor, and it declines to adopt either his self-presentation as a benefactor or the entertainment industry's version of him.

Section 97.11

The Ochoa Family, Carlos Lehder, Rodríguez Gacha, and the Wider Medellín Network

The Ochoa brothers, Jorge Luis, Juan David, and Fabio Ochoa Vásquez, came from a prominent Antioquian family. Their father, Fabio Ochoa Restrepo, was a known breeder of Paso Fino horses, and the family's wealth and social standing differed from Escobar's more modest origins. The Ochoas participated in the early cocaine business, built connections in Miami, and sometimes appear in the sources as brokers and logistics managers. Their involvement shows that the trade drew from respectable commercial families as well as from marginal groups.

Carlos Lehder Rivas, born in Armenia, Quindío, had lived in the United States, where he was involved in car smuggling and drug dealing before returning to Colombia. He became associated with large-scale shipments using aircraft and with the Bahamian island of Norman's Cay. He was also known for ideological and political posturing, and he founded a movement with nationalist and anti-extradition rhetoric. Lehder was arrested in Colombia in early 1987 and extradited to the United States, where he was convicted in 1988 and sentenced to life imprisonment plus 135 years. His case shows the logistics of the Caribbean route and, because he was extradited, the importance of extradition in the late 1980s.

José Gonzalo Rodríguez Gacha, known as "El Mexicano," came from Pacho in Cundinamarca and had earlier ties to the emerald trade, which had its own history of private violence. He was known for investing in rural land and for connections with armed groups in the Magdalena Medio, and he died in 1989. Gustavo Gaviria, Escobar's cousin and partner, was killed in 1990. Others in the wider network, including pilots, chemists, accountants, lawyers, regional bosses, and security chiefs, are less well known but were indispensable.

The reasons that cooperation did not eliminate rivalry are visible in these biographies. The individuals had different origins, assets, and ambitions. Lehder's logistics served others, but he also pursued independent political plans. Rodríguez Gacha's rural investments aligned him with landowners and paramilitary actors in ways that differed from Escobar's urban base. The Ochoas, whose family ties and social status gave them different options, sometimes pursued negotiations with the state that others resisted. All shared a common interest in resisting extradition and in protecting shipments, and that common interest produced collective action in the later 1980s. They also competed for access to suppliers, personnel, and political favor. When police pressure rose and violence increased, suspicion of betrayal grew.

The categories used by enforcement agencies tended to blur these differences. Indictments and law-enforcement summaries sometimes presented leaders as top bosses of a hierarchy. Court findings, where they exist, are narrower and concern specific charges against specific defendants. Students must separate an allegation in an indictment from a conviction and from an assessment published by an agency. A declassified 1994 DEA memorandum offers a useful retrospective view of the structure.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Centralized Cartels, Loose Alliances, or Changing Networks?

The question: How should historians characterize the organization of the Medellín and Cali groups?

Interpretation A — Cartels with coordination and shared strategy: Law-enforcement and journalistic accounts of the 1980s often described a cartel with common leadership, shared decisions about shipments and political strategy, and mechanisms to settle disputes. Collective action against extradition and shared security arrangements supported this picture.
Interpretation B — Loose alliances and fluid networks: Later analysts, including DEA retrospectives and academic studies of illicit markets, emphasize that the organizations operated independently, formed temporary partnerships, and competed. Structures could change with arrests, killings, and shifts in markets, and the term "cartel" overstates their cohesion.

Evidence: The debate depends on police files, indictments, trial testimony, intelligence assessments, journalism, and memoirs, each with its own bias. Researchers on illicit markets also stress that economic cartels are hard to sustain without legal enforcement of agreements.

Why it matters: If cartels were centralized, targeting leaders might dismantle them. If networks were fluid, removing leaders might produce fragmentation, new competitors, or more violence. Chapter 98 examines how the later history bore on that question. These interpretations are not entirely opposed, since cooperation could be strong on some matters and weak on others.

Section 97.12

Medellín: Criminal Wealth, Urban Society, and the Construction of Power

Illicit wealth entered Medellín visibly. Land and housing speculation, luxury consumption, new construction, and the opening of businesses changed parts of the city, while the money also supported informal employment. Pilots, mechanics, accountants, lawyers, bodyguards, drivers, and domestic workers found employment in households and enterprises linked to the trade. For many young men in poor comunas, particularly in the north-east of the city, criminal employment offered income and status that formal jobs rarely matched. The sociologist and journalist Alonso Salazar documented how some of these young men, hired as sicarios (paid killers), described their ambitions in terms of money, motorcycles, and protection for their mothers, and how many died young.

The relationship between traffickers and local communities involved patronage. Escobar's housing initiatives and sports facilities created goodwill in neighborhoods where the state's presence was thin. Support for families, funerals, and local celebrations built loyalty and a network of informants. Such patronage has parallels with earlier forms of political clientelism in Colombia and elsewhere in Latin America, but it was backed by the capacity for coercion.

Intimidation and corruption were integral to the system and not incidental. Traffickers threatened or bribed local officials, police officers, and judges, and they killed those who resisted. Violence also reached ordinary residents, whose neighborhoods became arenas of conflict. Charitable spending did not legitimize this violence. A housing project does not cancel a killing, and benefits distributed by an organization that coerces its beneficiaries are not freely given. Moreover, many residents of poor neighborhoods rejected the traffickers and organized peace initiatives and community groups. To describe Medellín only in terms of the criminal elite erases these people.

The effects on urban space and markets were contradictory. Illicit money inflated property values in some areas and financed construction, which gave a short-term boost to certain sectors, and then left behind debts, damaged institutions, and distrust. Violence in the late 1980s and early 1990s would make Medellín one of the most dangerous cities in the world, an outcome examined in Chapter 98 together with the city's later urban transformation. In this chapter the key point is that cocaine wealth gave a small group of criminal entrepreneurs a form of private power, financial, social, and coercive, that competed with the authority of public institutions.

The politics of patronage need to be reconstructed from particular projects and witnesses rather than inferred from the mere existence of new housing. For a family in an underserved neighborhood, access to a roof or a small local job could be a concrete improvement; for the trafficker financing that benefit, the same expenditure could cultivate loyalty, public visibility, and a reputation as a benefactor. Local residents did not all respond alike. Some accepted assistance while rejecting violence, some admired an apparent challenge to entrenched elites, and others feared the armed authority that accompanied criminal wealth. Such ambivalence helps explain why criminal entrepreneurs could become prominent in places neglected by municipal government without securing unanimous or enduring community support.

There was also a distinction between construction financed by illicit proceeds and an economy made broadly prosperous by those proceeds. Visible spending could coexist with insecure employment, rising costs for housing, and the loss of public trust. The benefits of illicit income concentrated among a limited network of owners and employees, whereas threats, intimidation, and the diversion of police and judicial resources affected far wider populations. A responsible local history should therefore compare residents' testimony, urban records, social policy, and documented prosecutions rather than equating extravagant consumption or charitable publicity with economic development.

Section 97.13

The Rise of the Cali Trafficking Organizations

Cali, the capital of the Valle del Cauca, was a city of sugar wealth, commerce, and professional classes, with a Pacific port at Buenaventura within reach. Its social structure differed from Medellín's. Elite families, clubs, banks, and agricultural firms were prominent, and the region had Afro-Colombian communities along the Pacific coast and in the Cauca valley, as well as a large population of migrants. These features influenced the way local trafficking organizations built relationships with business and politics.

The best-known Cali leaders were the brothers Gilberto and Miguel Rodríguez Orejuela, José Santacruz Londoño, and Hélmer "Pacho" Herrera. Reports on their backgrounds are varied and often come from law-enforcement sources and journalists. The Rodríguez Orejuela brothers are generally described as having begun in modest commercial roles and, according to some accounts, in other criminal activities before turning to cocaine in the 1970s. Santacruz was linked with early work in New York and with the organization of the trade's U.S. distribution. Herrera was associated with the Cali and northern Valle networks. Such details deserve caution until corroborated by court records.

Why did the Cali organizations expand alongside the Medellín networks rather than displace them? The cocaine market in the early 1980s was large enough to support several large suppliers, and entry barriers were not prohibitive for experienced smugglers. The Cali groups developed their own relationships with suppliers of base, with transporters, and with distributors in New York and other cities. They also cultivated a reputation for discretion and business-like conduct, in contrast with the confrontation that characterized Escobar. Early in the 1980s, the groups seem to have cooperated at points and to have kept their distance from one another at others.

The Cali organizations invested in legitimate-seeming enterprises: construction, retail, pharmacies, agriculture, real estate, radio stations, and sports. U.S. authorities later alleged that drugstore chains, financial institutions, and a football club were connected with the leaders, and these allegations led to financial sanctions and prosecutions. Allegations concerning such assets must be distinguished from court findings, and different assets have different evidentiary records. Gilberto and Miguel Rodríguez Orejuela were later convicted in the United States after pleading guilty to trafficking conspiracy, a conviction that postdates this chapter's period and that does not resolve every earlier claim.

As the 1980s progressed, the Cali organizations became increasingly important. As enforcement intensified against Medellín groups, especially after 1989, Cali's relative prominence grew. Their role in the early 1990s is outside this chapter's scope, but the roots lie in the 1980s expansion described here.

The institutional identity of Cali's networks changed across time and investigators' purposes. Calling the Rodríguez Orejuela brothers, Santacruz, and Herrera one organization can clarify cooperation and broad commercial influence, but the label can hide separate businesses, unequal authority, and changes in partnerships. As in Medellín, relationships were not reducible to a formal company chart. Law-enforcement reports often grouped people together because they shared contacts or appeared in an investigation; a historical account must ask whether those links prove common ownership, temporary cooperation, or simply repeated commercial association. This is especially important when later prosecutions and testimonies are used to reconstruct the less well-documented 1980s.

Section 97.14

Medellín and Cali: Cooperation, Rivalry, and Different Organizational Strategies

Both Medellín and Cali were networks of cocaine entrepreneurs who bought base, processed it, and exported it to the United States and, later, elsewhere. They sometimes shared routes, intelligence, and partners, and they sometimes competed over markets, particularly in New York. Reported conflicts between Medellín and Cali figures in the later 1980s, including disputes over control of markets and personnel and episodes of retaliation, show that cooperation was conditional.

Differences in strategy and style are well attested, though sometimes exaggerated. Escobar and some Medellín figures adopted a confrontational posture toward the state and the political class, including public political activity, violence against officials, and campaigns against extradition. Cali's leaders preferred, according to most accounts, discretion, negotiation, bribery, and the use of lawyers and front businesses, with violence used selectively. Cali figures also sought to influence politicians and the press through contributions and social contacts. The contrast was real, but its meaning is easily overstated: discretion is not the absence of violence, threats, and corruption.

Public visibility differed too. Escobar became an international celebrity and a symbol. Cali's leaders attracted less media attention until the early 1990s, partly because of their strategy and partly because Escobar's conflict with the state dominated the news. This disparity in visibility has led some observers to treat Medellín as violent and Cali as business-like, a contrast that can mislead students about the actual distribution of harm.

Leadership relationships also differed. The Medellín networks had a more personalized, charismatic center of gravity in the person of Escobar, although others kept independent roles. The Cali leaders operated as a small group of partners, with several leaders who shared decisions, which gave the organization some resilience against the loss of a single leader. How well these characterizations hold is a matter of evidence, and the comparison below summarizes the main points.

The familiar contrast between an ostentatious Medellín organization and a discreet Cali organization has some explanatory value but also serious limits. Both relied on international commercial networks, intermediaries, and relationships with legitimate businesses; both sought insulation from enforcement and opportunities to influence public institutions. Their leaders differed in public presentation and in the timing and prominence of violent confrontations with the Colombian state, yet neither network operated without coercion. The DEA's retrospective 1994 memorandum expressly described multiple Medellín groups cooperating rather than a single command, and it noted that some associates had relationships with Cali figures during earlier years. The sources therefore support comparison of shifting patterns of association, not an absolute division between a violent organization and a peaceful one.

Organizational comparison must also control for chronology. An assessment made after Escobar's death describes a world already transformed by assassinations, defections, arrests, and the weakening of some Medellín networks. It cannot be read backward as an exact organizational diagram for 1978 or 1982. The two cities also housed many legal firms and communities entirely unconnected to cocaine. Regional histories should not turn Medellín and Cali themselves into interchangeable synonyms for their most notorious traffickers. The analytical question is how commercial relationships, political influence, and criminal coercion evolved within and across those cities, and why their trajectories diverged.

Section 97.15

Money Laundering, Illicit Capital, and Colombia's Legal Economy

A trade that produces large amounts of cash faces a problem: the money must be hidden, stored, and put to use without attracting attention. Money laundering is the process of disguising the criminal origin of funds. Historically, the concept became important in the 1970s and 1980s. The United States enacted the Bank Secrecy Act in 1970 and the Money Laundering Control Act in 1986, which criminalized laundering as a separate offense, and in 1979 federal agents launched Operation Greenback in South Florida to trace cash movements. The 1988 UN Convention (§97.19) extended the principle internationally. This chapter does not describe laundering techniques, because the history that matters is the effect of illicit capital on economies and institutions.

For Colombia, the movement of illicit proceeds into property, commerce, and enterprise had several channels. Cash earned abroad had to be returned, converted, or invested. Contemporary Colombian and foreign observers described unusual features of the country's exchange-control regime, which made some inflows of foreign currency less carefully examined, as well as the existence of large informal and contraband economies in which cash circulated without scrutiny. Real estate, cattle ranches, construction, commerce, and financial firms absorbed capital, and so did sports, media, and entertainment. Many such enterprises were legitimate in operation and were funded in part by illicit money, which makes them difficult to disentangle.

Measuring illicit capital is very hard. Estimates by journalists, think tanks, and some economists varied widely, depending on assumptions about drug revenues, repatriation rates, and the share used for legal purposes. Such figures are not equivalent to national-accounting data. Historians should distinguish three things: the amount of money earned abroad, the amount returned to Colombia, and the amount that remained in the country. Only the last bears on domestic markets, and its size is deeply uncertain.

Effects on markets can be described with caution. Property values in certain areas rose, and construction and commerce in some cities benefited from the inflow, but inflated prices also distorted land markets and could drive out firms that lacked access to cheap capital. Illicit actors' acquisition of rural land, especially in the Magdalena valley, the Caribbean coast, and the eastern plains, intensified concentration of landholding and altered local power, with consequences for the armed conflict (Chapter 99). Firms with hidden sources of finance could compete unfairly, and the presence of such money complicated regulation and public trust.

Investigators faced regulatory and evidentiary obstacles. Banking secrecy, weak records, limited cooperation among countries, and the difficulty of proving the origin of funds in court impeded cases. Colombian and U.S. authorities had different laws and priorities, and until the 1988 convention and subsequent national laws, many jurisdictions did not criminalize laundering. Tracking a payment across borders required resources and cooperation that were scarce.

Claims about the scale of money laundering require particular restraint. An investigator might establish that a specific property was acquired with criminal proceeds through bank records, testimony, and a judicial finding. That does not establish what proportion of an entire city's property market—or Colombia's national output—was financed in the same way. Transfers through several jurisdictions may obscure the location of the initial earnings, and the same sum may be counted repeatedly if investigators add together successive transactions. Even where substantial illicit investment was documented, a rise in local real-estate prices could also reflect ordinary urban demand, inflation, credit conditions, or public infrastructure. This difference between a proven transaction and an inferred national total is central to the study of illicit capital.

The regulatory response involved more than criminal punishment. Banks, company registries, tax authorities, and courts faced difficult questions about proof of ownership, reporting obligations, and the ability to trace assets across borders. International cooperation expanded, but differing national laws and standards complicated investigations. The chapter's purpose is to explain why hidden capital weakened accountability and affected apparently legitimate markets, not to assume that every rapidly growing company was a front or that all imported wealth had the same provenance.

Historical Vocabulary
Money Laundering and Illicit Capital

Money laundering is the concealment of the criminal origin of money so that it can be used in the legal economy. Illicit capital refers to wealth derived from illegal activity, whether it is held in cash, property, businesses, or financial accounts. The two terms describe a process and a stock of assets.

Both ideas are important because trafficking profits would be of limited use if they could not be spent or invested safely. At the same time, the presence of illicit capital in an economy is hard to measure, and sensational claims about "narco-money" built into national economies often rest on assumption. Historians treat such estimates as contested evidence.

Section 97.16

Corruption, Political Influence, and Institutional Vulnerability

Criminal wealth could reach institutions in several ways: financing campaigns, bribing police and prosecutors, intimidating judges, paying local officials, and cultivating business and media allies. Colombia's institutions were not uniformly weak. The Supreme Court, the Council of State, the Attorney General's office, parts of the National Police, the press, and many politicians resisted, and many paid with their lives. But institutional resources were limited, and the combination of money and violence exploited that limitation.

Elections provided an entry point. Escobar's candidacy in 1982 and the later exposure by Lara Bonilla showed how a trafficker could gain a platform in formal politics. Local governments were especially vulnerable: municipal officials controlled land use, contracts, and policing at the local level, and their salaries and security were modest. National institutions were harder to reach but not immune. The justice system, whose judges were poorly paid and exposed to threats, faced a choice between bribes and violence that came to be summarized in popular language as plata o plomo (silver or lead), a phrase that captured the stakes even when it simplified them.

Historians must distinguish proven corruption from allegation. Public accusations, newspaper investigations, congressional hearings, intelligence reports, indictments, and convictions have different evidentiary weight. In later years, for example, investigations into allegations that Cali-linked money reached a 1994 presidential campaign produced a major political crisis in which the president denied knowledge and was cleared by Congress in 1996. That episode lies outside this chapter, but it illustrates the problem of establishing responsibility in cases where money, influence, and denial intersected.

Differences between national and municipal institutions are important. The judiciary and national police bore the brunt of the confrontation described in Chapter 98, while many municipalities depended on local power brokers. Corruption also varied by region and institution, and it did not imply that all officials were corrupt. Colombia's courts, journalists, and security forces included individuals who investigated and prosecuted traffickers at high personal cost. Public confidence in institutions suffered nonetheless, since citizens could not always tell which officials were incorruptible, and since impunity for killings of judges, journalists, and officers weakened the rule of law.

A careful chronology is necessary when claims about political financing appear in later sources. The controversy surrounding President Ernesto Samper concerns the 1994 election and the congressional proceedings of 1996, after the core period examined here. In June 1996 Colombia's Chamber of Representatives voted not to pursue proceedings against Samper. That decision must be described as a political and legal disposition of a specific case, not as independent proof that all accusations about trafficker financing were untrue. The later controversy is relevant as evidence of the enduring institutional challenge, but its detailed narrative belongs in a subsequent chapter. Claims about payments in the 1980s require their own dated documentary support.

Criminal intimidation and corruption also placed different pressures on different branches of government. An honest local official might be threatened without accepting a bribe; a court might lack the evidence or security to proceed even when suspicions were widespread; and national anticorruption policy might be frustrated by weak local administration. Conversely, Colombian investigators, journalists, judges, and ministers repeatedly challenged trafficking interests at considerable personal risk. Their work is evidence against any suggestion that the Colombian state was uniformly captured. Distinguishing complicity, institutional incapacity, and active resistance makes the history more accurate than treating every failure to prosecute as evidence of corruption.

Section 97.17

The Colombian State and the Early Response to the Cocaine Economy

Colombia's institutions for confronting the drug trade were limited and divided. The National Police, with a specialized antinarcotics unit, carried out most operations. The Administrative Department of Security (DAS) handled intelligence and some investigations. The Ministry of Justice supervised prosecutors and prisons, and a National Narcotics Council coordinated policy under statutory authority that dated from the 1970s. Judges handled cases under severe risk. The armed forces had been oriented toward counterinsurgency and, in the early 1980s, were not primarily assigned to drug enforcement. Funding, equipment, secure communications, and protection for officials were limited, and the country's territory included large areas with minimal state presence.

Early enforcement included operations against marijuana, the aerial eradication of which was reported by officials as substantial in 1986, and against coca-processing laboratories in the lowlands. The most important operation of the early period was Tranquilandia. In March 1984 Colombian police, aided by information and support from the DEA, raided a complex of cocaine laboratories and airstrips in the Yarí region of Caquetá, in the eastern lowlands. Reported seizures were large, measured in multiple tons of cocaine and other materials, though accounts differ on the totals. The operation demonstrated the scale of lowland processing and the possibility of cooperation, and it was a blow to major traffickers. It was also, as Colombia's Truth Commission and contemporary reporting show, followed within weeks by the assassination of Justice Minister Rodrigo Lara Bonilla on 30 April 1984. This chapter treats that event as a turning point and leaves its political consequences, including extradition and the war against the state, to Chapter 98.

On 31 January 1986 Colombia adopted Law 30, the National Narcotics Statute. It consolidated legislation on narcotics, set penalties for cultivation, processing, and trafficking, regulated the use of precursor and controlled substances, defined the role of the National Narcotics Council, addressed treatment of seized property, and defined a "personal dose" for consumption. It shows the Colombian state attempting to build a comprehensive legal framework. Its enforcement depended on institutions that remained under pressure.

Tensions between sovereignty and international assistance shaped policy. Washington provided funding, equipment, training, and intelligence, and it expected action, including extradition of traffickers under the 1979 bilateral treaty. Colombian officials weighed domestic concerns: the risk of retaliation, nationalist objections to extradition, and doubts about U.S. priorities. Public opinion and political leaders disagreed, and the legal basis for extradition itself came before the courts. Some officials favored a strong response, others sought negotiation, and some were corrupted. The 1988 GAO review concluded that U.S.-supported efforts had not produced major reductions in coca and marijuana production and trafficking and recommended that Colombia use its resources, including military ones, more effectively.

The General Accounting Office's November 1988 review allows a more precise assessment of early enforcement than isolated publicity claims. Its table 2.2, compiled from Department of State statistics, reports Colombian cocaine-base and cocaine-hydrochloride seizures of 9,461 kilograms in 1985, 4,317 in 1986, and 5,496 in 1987. Recorded cocaine laboratory destruction rose from 653 to 1,355 over those years, while reported arrests rose from 2,840 to 4,578. These are administrative enforcement outputs, not a count of the total cocaine market. They do not even move in the same direction from year to year, which makes a simple success-or-failure score inappropriate. Table 2.3 separately estimated coca cultivated in Colombia at 15,500 hectares in 1985 and 25,000 in 1987, a rise of approximately 61 percent; it did not show a doubling of cultivated area.

GAO also examined the quality of the evidence underlying those statistics. Some eradication results provided by Colombian police could not be independently verified by U.S. officials, while program evaluations were irregular and logistical shortages complicated operations. Its report recorded $33.8 million actually expended in fiscal 1987 on U.S.-supported programs across Colombia and Bolivia and $41.6 million planned for fiscal 1988—different accounting categories that must not be summed and called actual expenditure. The report was written for U.S. policymakers and judged programs against supply-reduction goals; it was not a neutral account of rural livelihoods or an exhaustive inventory of Colombian institutions. Its central value is showing the distance between reported enforcement activity and the continuing growth of a difficult-to-measure trade.

Section 97.18

Cocaine Wealth, Inequality, Violence, and Everyday Life

The cocaine economy affected households very unevenly. Coca farmers in the frontier colonization zones of Caquetá, Guaviare, and Putumayo, where coca cultivation expanded in the 1980s, gained cash income but lived with insecurity: they depended on buyers, risked arrest and herbicide spraying, and often lacked schools, health posts, and roads. Many were migrants from other regions who had come in search of land. In towns near coca zones, a boom economy of merchants, bars, transport, and services appeared, along with violence and dependence on a volatile market.

In cities, the effects were also unequal. Some professionals, such as lawyers, accountants, and bankers, and some small businesses benefited from the circulation of money, while public employees such as judges, police officers, and prosecutors faced threats and low salaries. Journalists who reported on trafficking risked their lives. The killing of Guillermo Cano, editor of El Espectador, on 17 December 1986 became a symbol of the danger. Businesses had to deal with extortion, kidnapping, and the distortion of competition by firms with hidden capital. Residents of neighborhoods where criminal groups recruited and fought endured insecurity.

Young people in poor urban neighborhoods faced a particular set of choices. Criminal employment as lookouts, couriers, or hired killers offered earnings and status that formal work seldom provided, and organizations recruited in neighborhoods where unemployment and school dropout were high. This helps explain why Medellín's homicide rate rose so sharply in the late 1980s and early 1990s, but it does not mean that most young people joined. The majority did not, and many families and community groups sought to keep their children out and to build alternatives.

Women's experiences varied: as farmers and harvesters in coca zones, as workers in laboratories or households, as relatives of men who were killed or jailed, as victims of violence, and as leaders in community and human-rights organizations who demanded justice. Such roles are less visible in official sources but appear in oral histories and later truth-commission testimony.

Public-health consequences were felt in Colombia as in the United States. Domestic consumption of coca-derived products, including a cheap smokable paste known as bazuco, appeared in Colombian cities in the 1980s, and treatment resources were scarce. The social costs of violence and corruption, which included loss of life, displacement, fear, and distrust of institutions, fell on society as a whole. The next feature examines how different groups experienced the same developments.

Local vulnerability was not limited to people directly employed in illicit commerce. Shopkeepers and transport workers could see demand rise when criminal money circulated, only to face instability when an investigation, arrest, or dispute interrupted local spending. Teachers and health workers operated in communities where scarce public resources competed with the more visible rewards offered by criminal patrons. Families of victims, witnesses, and threatened journalists confronted a different economy of risk: a decision to cooperate with authorities could affect employment, mobility, and personal safety. Those experiences rarely appear in national production statistics, yet they were central to how ordinary Colombians understood the cocaine era.

Public discussion also framed people who lived in drug-producing regions as if they were collectively responsible for trafficking. That stigma obscured differences among subsistence farmers, traders, coerced participants, organized criminals, and residents who opposed the trade. Both in Colombia and the United States, the impact of drugs was shaped by neighborhood resources, public-health provision, law enforcement, and existing inequalities. Treating those populations as historical actors with unequal choices produces a more defensible social history than treating every resident merely as a beneficiary or victim of cartel power.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Neighborhoods, and the Costs of an Illicit Boom

For a coca-growing household in the Peruvian or Bolivian lowlands, daily life revolved around planting, harvesting, drying, and selling leaf, with children and women contributing labor. Income was higher than from many alternatives and far lower than the value of the final product. Households faced the threat of eradication, armed groups, and unreliable markets, and they often lived far from clinics and schools.

In Medellín's hillside neighborhoods, families lived with the presence of armed young men, with funeral processions and neighborhood patrons, and with the fear that a son might be recruited or killed. Mothers appear prominently in accounts such as Salazar's, as figures who tried to protect children and who often benefited from, and were compromised by, the money they received. In Cali, residents of middle-class neighborhoods encountered illicit wealth in the form of new businesses, nightlife, and sports sponsorship, while poorer migrants from the Pacific coast and the countryside faced the same insecurity as other urban poor.

Public employees and journalists lived with threats. Judges in provincial towns, who often had no security, were reportedly warned and pressured, and some resigned. Public-health workers in Colombian and U.S. cities saw growing numbers of patients, including infants born to mothers who used cocaine. The silences in the sources, especially for farmers, women, and the urban poor, are themselves a reminder that the history of cocaine has mostly been written from the viewpoint of enforcement and of the famous.

Section 97.19

The Globalization of Cocaine Markets and International Drug Control

By the late 1980s cocaine was a global commodity. The U.S. market remained the largest, but supply was expanding to Canada, Western Europe, and other regions, as the DEA had already noted in 1985. Cooperation among police forces, customs services, and financial regulators increased, through bilateral agreements and multilateral bodies. In 1986 the Organization of American States established the Inter-American Drug Abuse Control Commission (CICAD) to coordinate regional policy. European states, many of which had long colonial and migration ties to Latin America, began to see the problem as a domestic concern.

The trade's geography changed over time because enforcement in one location tended to redirect activity. Pressure on Florida and the Caribbean coincided with more traffic through Central America and Mexico. Pressure on laboratories in one country encouraged relocation to others, and crop eradication in one area encouraged cultivation elsewhere, a pattern sometimes called the "balloon effect," in which squeezing one place makes the trade bulge in another. The pattern should not be overstated, since enforcement also imposed real costs and sometimes disrupted particular organizations, but the evidence of the 1980s shows adaptation more than elimination.

The principal legal instrument of the period was the United Nations Convention Against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances, adopted in Vienna on 20 December 1988 and in force from 11 November 1990. It required states to criminalize trafficking offenses and the laundering of proceeds, to permit confiscation of assets, to cooperate on extradition and mutual legal assistance, to control chemicals used in manufacture, and to eradicate illicit cultivation. It also contained a provision recognizing historical evidence of traditional licit use of coca, a concession to Andean states and Indigenous communities. The convention reflects a consensus on the criminal-justice approach and shows how many governments, including producing and consuming states, accepted a shared framework.

International cooperation operated through institutions with competing mandates. Diplomats negotiated treaty duties, police sought evidence and arrests, customs agencies measured seizures, financial officials addressed suspicious transfers, and public-health institutions monitored patterns of use and harm. The 1988 Vienna Convention, adopted in December but not in force until November 1990, reflected an expanding belief that national laws alone could not address an international illicit commodity. Its provisions concerning offenses, confiscation, extradition, mutual legal assistance, and precursor controls became a common legal framework; they did not guarantee the resources or political will needed for implementation. States differed in their constitutional rules, legal capacities, and willingness to cooperate.

The international debate also contained an unresolved distributional question. Consumer-country governments emphasized lowering availability, while Andean governments confronted the social and political costs of enforcement in rural districts. Bolivia and Peru distinguished some lawful coca cultivation from illicit production; Colombian officials resisted descriptions of their country that ignored the size of foreign demand. These disagreements did not mean international action was impossible. They meant that a durable policy required attention both to criminal distribution and to legitimate livelihoods, health systems, judicial capacity, and the interests of sovereign states.

Section 97.20

The Cocaine Economy at the End of the 1980s: A Crisis of Wealth, Violence, and State Authority

By the end of the 1980s the elements of the chapter's argument had come together. Andean farmers supplied leaf and base for an expanding illicit market. Colombian entrepreneurs, building on the networks and skills of the marijuana boom and on the country's geography and commerce, organized processing, export, and finance. U.S. demand, from powder users in the late 1970s and early 1980s and then crack users, provided a large and profitable market. Prohibition shaped risks and prices without restricting supply. The Medellín and Cali organizations, which cooperated and competed, accumulated wealth that they used to buy property, businesses, protection, and influence.

The consequences for Colombia were severe. Public institutions, from courts to municipal governments, were under pressure from bribery and violence. Journalists, judges, police officers, and politicians were killed. Urban neighborhoods suffered from recruited young killers and rising homicide, and rural frontier regions saw new forms of conflict. The legal economy absorbed illicit capital in ways that are hard to measure. The state's early responses, including Tranquilandia, Law 30, and cooperation with U.S. agencies, had real effects but did not reduce supply, as the GAO concluded in 1988.

The year 1989 would sharpen the crisis. The assassination of presidential candidate Luis Carlos Galán in August, further attacks on officials and civilians, and the government's decision to resume an all-out campaign against traffickers opened a period of confrontation over extradition, terrorism, judicial independence, and sovereignty. Colombia's 1991 Constitution would soon prohibit the extradition of Colombian nationals, a measure that was later reversed. These events, and the death of Escobar in December 1993, belong to Chapter 98. What matters here is that the confrontation grew from the economic and institutional history described in this chapter: a private, concentrated power built on an international market faced a state with limited capacity and divided priorities.

A transitional point deserves emphasis. The cocaine economy would not end with Escobar or with the weakening of the Medellín networks, because it rested on demand, supply, and a legal regime that persisted. The Cali organizations and, later, smaller successors, as well as Mexican organizations whose power began to grow in the late 1980s, would continue the trade. In this sense Chapter 97 explains why the system survived the destruction of its most famous participants.

The comparison between Medellín and Cali further complicates the idea that the history can be told as the rise and fall of individual leaders. Commercial knowledge, financial contacts, and distribution relationships survived arrests and changes of leadership because they were spread across many participants. At the same time, the international commodity chain depended on communities that received only a modest share of its final income and that experienced enforcement and violence very differently from senior traffickers. The unequal distribution of rewards and risks helps explain why attempts to eliminate a prominent organization did not necessarily eliminate the underlying market.

By the turn of the 1990s, Colombia faced a struggle over the authority to make and enforce law. The preceding history makes that conflict intelligible: prolonged consumer demand, large illicit profits, the accumulation of local influence, and disagreements over extradition and justice had produced a challenge that exceeded routine criminal prosecution. This chapter has established the system and its principal organizations; the next chapter examines the escalated campaign of terrorism and state confrontation. The boundary matters because economic explanation is a precondition for understanding the violence, not a substitute for documenting the individual acts and responsibilities involved.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Legal Cocaine and Regional Smuggling to the Transnational Cocaine Economy

What Changed

  • Cocaine moved from a legal pharmaceutical and consumer commodity to an illicit one
  • The center of commercial organization moved from Peruvian and European firms to Colombian networks
  • Regional smuggling of marijuana became a global trade in a far more valuable product
  • Enforcement became international, with the DEA, treaties, and certification

What Stayed the Same

  • Andean farmers remained the source of the raw material, with low returns
  • Contraband traditions and commercial know-how shaped who entered the trade
  • Weak state presence in frontier regions continued to shape opportunities
  • Traditional coca use persisted despite legal restriction

Who experienced change most directly: Colombian officials, urban neighborhoods in Medellín and Cali, and U.S. communities affected by crack. Greater continuity: Andean highland communities that kept traditional coca practices. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the boom was a transformation of an existing commodity and network, not an ancient inheritance or a sudden invention.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How Coca, Demand, Prohibition, and Incentives Produced an Expanding Illicit Trade

Long-term causes

Andean coca cultivation; nineteenth-century commercialization of cocaine; the shift to prohibition; Colombian contraband traditions and regional commercial networks.

Short-term causes

Rising U.S. demand in the 1970s and 1980s; the marijuana networks and their disruption; economic crises and migration in Peru and Bolivia; limited state capacity in Colombia.

Event or transformation

Colombian entrepreneurs organize the purchase, processing, export, and financing of cocaine, creating the Medellín and Cali networks.

Immediate effects

Large illicit revenues; investment in property and business; corruption and violence; falling prices and rising purity in the United States.

Long-term consequences

A confrontation between private criminal power and the state; an international legal regime centered on criminal justice; durable adaptation of the trade after the fall of its best-known organizers.

Historical complexity

No single factor suffices. Poverty, prohibition, demand, entrepreneurship, and weak institutions interacted, and most poor communities and most Colombians were not part of the trade.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the rise of the Medellín and Cali organizations was the product of interacting historical forces: Andean coca cultivation, expanding international demand, prohibition, unequal economic opportunities, preexisting commercial and smuggling networks, and the ability of criminal entrepreneurs to accumulate and protect extraordinary profits. No single factor suffices, and the Colombian organizations did not create the global cocaine market alone.

The first foundation is the distinction between coca and cocaine. Coca leaf has a long Andean history as a mild stimulant, a medicine, and a ritual object. Cocaine, isolated in 1859 and 1860, was a nineteenth-century scientific and commercial product that was legal until restrictions accumulated in the early twentieth century. The illicit industry grew from a legal commodity whose status changed, and from Andean knowledge of cultivation and trade.

International drug control, culminating in the 1961 Single Convention and in U.S. institutions such as the DEA, created the legal setting for illicit trade. The convention placed coca leaf under strict control and called for ending coca chewing, treating an Indigenous practice as an anomaly. Prohibition raised risks and prices and made violence, bribery, and secrecy commercially valuable, although whether it created organized trafficking, or only shaped it, remains debated.

In the 1980s Peru and Bolivia were the principal coca-growing countries, and Colombia was the center of processing, commercial organization, and export. Growers, many of them migrants facing economic crisis and weak state services, earned modest incomes and were not criminal organizations. Colombian cultivation expanded later, and the UN dates the point at which it surpassed the traditional growers to the late 1990s.

Colombia's role grew from its marijuana boom of the 1960s and 1970s, which built routes, contacts, and expertise, and from its geography between supply and markets, its ports, its contraband traditions, and its limited state presence in frontier regions. Cocaine offered much higher value per unit of weight than marijuana, and entrepreneurs shifted to it as U.S. demand rose in the late 1970s.

U.S. demand expanded from the recreational cocaine market of the 1970s to the crack epidemic of the mid-1980s. Survey data showed declining casual use among young adults after 1985 while emergency-room episodes, seizures, and purity rose, a contrast that illustrates why different measures must not be conflated. Demand, risk, and competition shaped prices, which fell as purity rose.

The Medellín networks were composed of cooperating organizations, among them those of Escobar, the Ochoa family, Lehder, Rodríguez Gacha, and Gaviria. The label "Medellín Cartel" is useful but overstates cohesion, as a retrospective DEA assessment itself suggests. Escobar's fame, amplified by popular culture, can obscure other actors and the victims. Cocaine wealth reshaped Medellín through investment, patronage, and employment, but charity did not legitimize coercion.

The Cali organizations, led by the Rodríguez Orejuela brothers, Santacruz, and Herrera, expanded alongside Medellín, relying more on discretion, negotiation, and business holdings, although their discretion did not mean an absence of violence or corruption. Comparison shows differences in style and visibility, as well as cooperation and rivalry, and it warns against a simple contrast between violent and peaceful organizations.

Illicit capital entered Colombia's legal economy through property, commerce, and finance, with effects that are difficult to measure and often exaggerated. Criminal wealth tested elections, local governments, police, and courts, but many officials, judges, and journalists resisted at great cost. Historians must separate proven corruption from allegation and avoid suggesting that all officials were compromised.

The Colombian state responded with Tranquilandia in 1984, Law 30 in 1986, and cooperation with U.S. agencies, while the international community adopted the 1988 UN Convention. A 1988 U.S. audit concluded that efforts had not produced major reductions in production or trafficking. Enforcement often redirected rather than eliminated activity, and sovereignty and extradition became fraught issues.

By 1990 Colombia faced a crisis of wealth, violence, and state authority that Chapter 98 narrates. The central lesson of this chapter is that the cocaine economy transformed Colombia because an international illicit market created concentrated private power that challenged institutions, distorted legal activity, and generated profound social costs, and that the system outlasted its best-known organizers.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Colombia's cocaine crisis was built from a long chain of historical connections. An Andean plant, a European chemical discovery, a legal regime that turned a commodity illicit, North American demand, and Colombian commercial networks were joined in the 1970s and 1980s. The result was a concentration of private power that no single figure can explain. Escobar and the Cali leaders matter, but the system of growers, intermediaries, bankers, officials, and consumers is what made them possible.

The chapter also shows why careful reading of evidence matters. Seizures, hectares, retail values, survey results, and indictments each measure something different, and the agencies that produced them had purposes of their own. Students who learn to ask what a number counts, and who produced it, will read the later history of drug policy more critically, from Colombia to Mexico.

Chapter 98 takes up the confrontation that followed: the assassinations, bombings, and extradition battles that tested Colombian institutions, the hunt for Escobar, and the fragmentation of the cartels. Chapter 99 turns to guerrillas, paramilitaries, and Plan Colombia, where the drug economy joined the armed conflict. Together with Part XXIII on Mexico, they trace how a trade first organized in Colombia became a hemispheric system that survived the fall of its most famous organizers.
