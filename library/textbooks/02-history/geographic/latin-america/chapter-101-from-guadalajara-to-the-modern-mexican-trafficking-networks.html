From Guadalajara to the Modern Mexican Trafficking Networks

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: An Old Illicit Economy Reorganizes

The modern Mexican trafficking organizations were not born in a single city, in a single decade, or at a single meeting. They grew out of an illicit economy that was already decades old when Guadalajara became its most visible urban center, and they took their later forms when that center was broken by an enforcement crisis.

On the afternoon of 7 February 1985, Enrique “Kiki” Camarena, a special agent of the United States Drug Enforcement Administration, left the American consulate in Guadalajara and was abducted. A month later, on 5 March, his body and that of Alfredo Zavala Avelar, a Mexican pilot who had worked with him, were found in the state of Michoacán. The case became one of the largest murder investigations in the history of American drug enforcement, and it forced both governments to confront what they knew, and what they had tolerated, about drug trafficking in western Mexico. It is the best-known episode in this chapter, but it is not the beginning of the story and it is not the end.

This chapter asks how a set of local and regional practices (cultivating opium poppy and marijuana in the mountains of the northwest, smuggling goods and people across a long border, selling protection through police and political office) became a set of interconnected trafficking networks able to move drugs from Colombia through Mexico into the United States. Its answer has several parts. Prohibition, adopted unevenly in Mexico and the United States between 1910 and 1930, turned existing commerce into illegal commerce. Rural poverty and weak infrastructure made illicit crops attractive. American demand, especially from the 1960s onward, raised the profits of supplying it. A one-party political system, in which local officials and police enjoyed wide discretion, made protection a commodity. Enforcement campaigns, from Operation Intercept in 1969 to the military deployment known as Operation Condor in 1977 and the investigations that followed the Camarena murder, changed the geography and organization of the trade without ending it.

The chapter's central caution concerns labels. The phrase “Guadalajara Cartel” appears in countless books, documentaries, and television dramas as the name of a single, centrally administered criminal corporation that controlled Mexican trafficking until its leader divided the country among his lieutenants. Recent archival research challenges that picture. Carlos Pérez Ricart and Jack Pannell have argued that the historical organization was a shifting network of relationships and that the cartel label became common retrospectively, while other institutional histories describe a more integrated organization under Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo. Students will meet both interpretations here. The chapter does not settle the argument by decree. It asks what each kind of evidence can and cannot show.

The chapter also resists a second simplification: that the story is only about famous traffickers. Rural producers who grew poppy because maize did not pay, border women who carried small quantities across a river, soldiers who burned fields, villagers displaced by eradication campaigns, journalists and judicial police who investigated and sometimes died, and officials who protected or prosecuted are all part of it. Large claims about “the Mexican state” controlling or fighting the drug trade must be tested against what particular governors, police commanders, intelligence officers, and presidents are documented to have done.

Evidence deserves attention from the outset. Mexican and American government records tell different stories because they were written for different purposes: eradication statistics reward the agency that reports them; diplomatic cables record what officials believed on a given day; DEA histories commemorate an agent and an institution. Memoirs and journalistic investigations supply detail that courts have not always tested. Scholars such as Isaac Campos, Luis Astorga, Benjamin T. Smith, and Wil G. Pansters have reconstructed the earlier history from archives that were long closed, including, in recent years, the records of the Dirección Federal de Seguridad held by Mexico’s Archivo General de la Nación. Throughout, this chapter distinguishes among contemporaneous documents, judicial findings, formal accusations, participant testimony, and unresolved interpretation.

The chapter’s boundaries matter. It runs from the 1910s to about 1993, with its emphasis on 1969–1993. Its Colombian material concerns Colombian organizations only through their relationships with Mexican networks; the rise of the Medellín and Cali organizations and the Colombian state’s confrontation with them belong to Chapters 97 and 98. It introduces the Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and Gulf organizations at their origins; their leaders, structures, alliances, and wars belong to Chapter 102. The post-2006 security offensive and its human costs belong to Chapter 103, and the synthetic-drug economy and CJNG to Chapter 104. Anything that looks like those later systems should not be read back into the 1980s.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a particular place in Volume V. After tracing the Colombian cocaine economy and the long armed conflict in Part XXII, the textbook turns to Mexico. The historical argument that links the two is that the cocaine boom of the 1980s did not create Mexican organized crime; it connected an older Mexican illicit economy to the most profitable new commodity in the hemisphere. What changed when the Guadalajara-centered networks were broken was the organization and distribution of criminal power, not its existence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how changing Mexican and international law between 1910 and 1930 transformed existing commerce in opium, marijuana, and other substances into prohibited markets.
  2. Describe the rural narcotics economy of Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua without treating every producer as a member of an organized criminal enterprise.
  3. Trace the development of border smuggling and regional entrepreneurs, including the roles of women, from the 1920s through the 1960s.
  4. Analyze how police, local officials, and the PRI’s political system produced protection arrangements, distinguishing documented local relations from claims of a single national policy.
  5. Assess the effects of Operation Intercept (1969) and Operation Condor (1977) on Mexican sovereignty, rural society, and the geography of trafficking.
  6. Compare the interpretation of the Guadalajara organization as a unified cartel with the interpretation of it as a flexible network, and evaluate the evidence for each.
  7. Distinguish the distinct backgrounds and roles of Félix Gallardo, Caro Quintero, and Fonseca Carrillo, and the limits of labels such as “Jefe de Jefes.”
  8. Explain the DFS’s role in Mexican intelligence and its documented and alleged relationships with trafficking, without equating the institution with the crimes of individuals.
  9. Reconstruct the Camarena case from February 1985 to 1989, classifying claims as documented, judicially established, alleged, or unresolved.
  10. Evaluate why the supposed single “division of territories” is a weak basis for understanding the origins of the Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and Gulf organizations.
  11. Interpret quantitative evidence on eradication, demand, and enforcement, and distinguish seizures, arrests, and hectares destroyed from the size of the illicit trade.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XXIII’s argument that Mexican organized crime must be understood through political economy rather than biography.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did changes in law, rather than changes in human behavior alone, create the Mexican drug trade as a criminal economy?
  2. What was the relationship between rural poverty, geography, and the expansion of illicit crops, and how can historians avoid describing whole regions as criminal?
  3. How did the political system of the PRI era shape the form that protection took, and how should historians weigh claims of “state control” against evidence of local autonomy?
  4. Did aggressive enforcement campaigns suppress trafficking, or did they also change its geography and organization?
  5. Why did certain traffickers concentrate in Guadalajara, and what can and cannot be shown about whether they formed a single organization?
  6. How should historians treat a case such as the Camarena murder, where memoir, journalism, indictment, and judicial finding all exist but do not agree?
  7. Why did Mexico’s role in the cocaine economy grow in the 1980s, and what role did Colombian organizations play in that change?
  8. What does the history of the Gulf region suggest about the limits of a single genealogy for Mexican trafficking organizations?
  9. How do official statistics (hectares destroyed, arrests, seizures) shape and distort our picture of enforcement and of the trade itself?

Historical Context

Earlier chapters supply the setting. Chapter 71 described the Porfiriato, and Chapters 72 through 74 traced the Mexican Revolution, the Constitution of 1917, and the consolidation of the postrevolutionary state under the party that became the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI). Those chapters showed a state that built its authority by bargaining with regional strongmen, labor and peasant organizations, and the military, and that tolerated considerable local autonomy in return for loyalty. The same bargaining logic, historians argue, shaped how the state dealt with illicit commerce. Chapters 95 and 97 through 100 described the neoliberal restructuring of the 1980s and 1990s and the Colombian cocaine economy; this chapter is the point at which those two stories meet in Mexico.

The geography of the story is specific. The mountains of the Sierra Madre Occidental, where the states of Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua meet, offered steep, remote, and well-watered terrain suited to opium poppy and marijuana and far from roads and police posts. The coastal plains of Sinaloa, by contrast, became a center of irrigated commercial agriculture and of the cities, such as Culiacán, in which rural migrants and merchants mixed. To the north lay a border of roughly three thousand kilometers, anchored by paired cities such as Tijuana and San Diego, Ciudad Juárez and El Paso, and Matamoros and Brownsville, in which commerce, labor, and families crossed daily. To the west of the central plateau, Guadalajara, the capital of Jalisco, was Mexico’s second city, with airports, banks, a university, and a prosperous commercial elite.

Two legal histories frame the period. Mexico’s drug laws developed in dialogue with international agreements and, increasingly, with pressure from the United States, where the Harrison Act of 1914 and later statutes restricted narcotics and where alcohol Prohibition from 1920 to 1933 created a lucrative border smuggling trade. Mexican officials and doctors did not always accept American models; the interwar debate over whether addiction was a medical problem or a crime, examined in Section 101.4, shows that other paths were considered and abandoned. By the 1960s, however, a prohibitionist consensus had hardened on both sides of the border, and the United States had made drug control a major element of its foreign policy.

Modern scholarship has also revised older narratives. Isaac Campos has shown that Mexican anxieties about marijuana arose from Mexican, not American, sources and long preceded the 1920 prohibition. Luis Astorga argued that the relationship between politicians and traffickers was historically one of political subordination of the latter, a view that later scholars have refined. Benjamin T. Smith has documented a more fragmented system in which army officers, police, local politicians, and agents of the federal government all extracted payments, and in which the “protection” of traffickers was often unstable and contested. Wil Pansters and Smith have traced how changing forms of protection shaped violence in Sinaloa between 1940 and 1980. The chapter draws on all of these without presuming that they agree.

A final piece of context concerns vocabulary. The words “narco,” “cartel,” and “mafia” entered public usage at different times and carried different meanings in Mexico and the United States. “Cartel” in particular, borrowed from the economics of price-fixing, was applied by American and Mexican officials and journalists to criminal organizations in ways that implied centralized control. Whether that implication was accurate for the 1980s is one of the chapter’s two historical debates.

Section 101.1

Narcotics, Prohibition, and the Mexican Revolution, 1910–1930

In 1910, none of the substances now at the center of the drug trade was illegal throughout Mexico in the sense later understood. Opium, in the form of laudanum and other preparations, was sold in pharmacies, as it was across the Atlantic world. Cocaine and morphine appeared in patent medicines. Marijuana, known in Mexico as mariguana or by nicknames such as la yerba, was grown and sold in small quantities and smoked chiefly by soldiers, prisoners, and the urban poor. Chinese immigrants, who had settled in Sinaloa, Sonora, and Baja California in considerable numbers since the late nineteenth century, smoked opium and in some places grew poppy. None of this was a “drug trade” in the modern sense. It was a set of commodities whose legal and moral status was changing.

Isaac Campos’s study of the origins of Mexico’s drug laws has shown that the status of marijuana changed first through Mexican beliefs rather than American pressure. From the nineteenth century, Mexican doctors, journalists, and officials associated the plant with madness and violence, partly on the basis of popular stories about its effects on soldiers and prisoners. These beliefs were not unanimous, and they were not simply imported. They were part of the wider anxiety of the Porfirian and revolutionary periods about degeneration, alcoholism, and the “race,” a vocabulary shared by Mexican reformers, physicians, and, later, the framers of the Constitution. Evidence for this comes from newspapers, medical theses, and criminal records, which show the plant moving from a folk intoxicant to a social menace in the public imagination well before 1920.

The Revolution accelerated the reclassification. Reformers in the 1910s argued that a modern, healthy citizenry required control of alcohol and drugs, and the Constitution of 1917 gave the federal government unusual power in this field. Article 73 placed responsibility for campaigns against alcoholism and against the sale of substances that harm individuals and the “race” in the hands of a federal health authority, the Consejo de Salubridad General, whose regulations were to be enforced by administrative authorities. This was a public-health framework: the Constitution did not create a penal code against drugs. But it provided a legal route by which the federal government could regulate what had been a local matter, and it embedded in Mexican law a vocabulary of social hygiene that would later be applied to the policing of drugs.

International agreements pushed in the same direction. The international conferences at Shanghai in 1909 and The Hague in 1912, followed by the American Harrison Act of 1914, established the model of national control of opium and cocaine that Mexican reformers could cite. Around 1920, the Mexican federal government issued sweeping restrictions on the cultivation, commerce, and distribution of opiates, cocaine, and marijuana, justified in the language of public health and protection of the “race.” Historians differ on how closely the measures were coordinated with American demands, and on how effectively they were enforced at first; what the sources show is that, by the early 1920s, activities that had been permitted or tolerated were prohibited in law.

Prohibition did not suddenly create a criminal underworld, but it changed incentives. Once opium and marijuana were illegal, prices rose, producers had to hide their crops, and sellers needed protection from the very officials who enforced the law. The same period saw armed revolutionary factions and regional strongmen seeking revenue wherever they could find it. In Baja California, the governor and military commander Esteban Cantú, who ruled the northern territory in practice from 1915 to 1920, drew revenue from taxing gambling, alcohol, and opium, an arrangement that historians cite as an early example of how local authorities profited from vice they might have suppressed. In the United States, meanwhile, national alcohol Prohibition from 1920 gave border towns a lucrative smuggling economy that would train a generation of smugglers and corrupt a generation of customs and police officials on both sides.

For these reasons, it is more accurate to say that Mexican prohibition reorganized an existing set of commercial activities into illegal markets than to say that it launched a trafficking industry. The distinction matters. If the origin of Mexican drug trafficking is placed in the 1980s, it appears as the work of a few ambitious criminals. If it is placed in the 1910s and 1920s, it appears as the product of legal choices, political bargaining, and shifting international norms, in which criminals, officials, and reformers all participated.

Historical Vocabulary
Prohibition, Smuggling, Narcotics Markets, and Trafficking Organizations

Prohibition is the legal ban of the production, sale, or possession of a commodity. In this chapter it refers both to the American alcohol ban of 1920–1933 and to the broader legal restriction of narcotics. Smuggling is the unlawful movement of goods across a border; it can involve legal goods that evade taxes and tariffs as well as banned goods. Narcotics market is a loose term for the buying and selling of controlled substances; historians use it to emphasize that production, transport, sale, and consumption are linked but separate activities with different participants. A trafficking organization coordinates several of these activities, and may range from a family firm to a loose alliance.

The terms matter because early Mexican history contains smuggling and cultivation without necessarily containing organizations. A farmer who grew poppy, a woman who carried a package across the river, and a regional intermediary who bought opium gum were all participants in an illicit market, but they were not necessarily members of a single enterprise. Modern language, which tends to collapse these roles into “cartel,” can erase these distinctions.

Primary Source Window

Public Health and the Origins of Mexican Prohibition, 1917–1920

Creators: The Constituent Congress of 1916–17 (Constitution, Article 73) and the federal executive (1920 regulations on cultivation and commerce of substances deemed harmful) · Dates: 1917; 1920 · Type: Constitutional text and executive regulation

Context: The Constitution of 1917 was the product of revolutionary compromise and gave the federal government expansive powers over social questions. In 1920, in the period after the revolutionary civil wars and during the transition to the presidency of Álvaro Obregón, the executive issued regulations restricting a list of substances.

Paraphrase, not quotation: the constitutional text assigns a federal health authority power to act against alcoholism and against the sale of substances that poison the individual and degrade the human “race,” and gives its measures force of law until Congress acts. The 1920 regulations apply a similar rationale to the cultivation and trade of opiates, cocaine, and marijuana, treating them as threats to public health and the national stock. (Students should consult the original Spanish texts in the Diario Oficial and in legal compilations for exact wording.)

Analysis: Both texts frame drugs as a problem of public health and national vitality, not primarily of crime. That framing shows the Mexican origins of prohibition and complicates the claim that it was simply an American import. The texts do not show how they were enforced, who was punished, or how ordinary growers and consumers experienced them. They are prescriptive, not descriptive, and they are products of reformist elites. The perspectives of farmers, Chinese opium smokers, and soldiers who used marijuana are missing. Retrospective official histories, including those published decades later, tend to compress this slow legal process into a single founding date; the original texts show a series of overlapping measures.

Section 101.2

Sinaloa, the Golden Triangle, and the Rural Narcotics Economy

The mountains where Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua meet became the center of Mexican poppy and marijuana cultivation because of a combination of geography, climate, and poverty. The western slopes of the Sierra Madre Occidental receive enough rain and have enough elevation to support poppy and cannabis; the terrain is rugged, roads were few until the second half of the twentieth century, and police and army posts were distant. The region is often called the Golden Triangle (Triángulo Dorado), a name borrowed, historians note, from the later and larger opium region of Southeast Asia, and the label should be used with care. It describes a zone of overlapping municipalities, not a single criminal territory, and it describes communities with very different relationships to the trade.

Opium poppy cultivation in northwestern Mexico is usually traced to Chinese immigrants in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, though Mexican farmers soon took up the crop. It grew significantly from the 1930s and especially in the 1940s, when the demand for morphine during the Second World War and the closure of other supply routes raised the value of opium, a connection that historians such as Benjamin T. Smith have emphasized. After the war, the crop continued to expand to supply a growing American heroin market. Cultivation was seasonal. In the highlands, families planted poppy alongside maize and beans; the gomeros, who scored the poppy pods to collect the latex or “gum” (goma), worked in the winter and spring; and intermediaries purchased the gum and transported it down the mountain to Culiacán and other towns. Marijuana, planted in the same terrain, became more prominent in the 1960s as American demand grew.

Why did rural families grow these crops? The principal reason was economic. Highland farmers in Sinaloa and Durango had small plots, little access to credit, poor transportation, and low returns from maize, beans, and coffee. Poppy gum was compact, valuable, and easy to carry; a small harvest could earn more than a large harvest of staples. Local merchants and intermediaries offered cash, supplies, and sometimes advances. In many communities, the trade was woven into local life, supporting stores, schools, and religious festivals. Studies by Mexican scholars and historians describe a rural society in which the line between licit and illicit income was blurred, and in which villagers who grew poppy did not think of themselves as members of a criminal organization.

That last point requires emphasis. The history of the northwest has often been told as if every producer were part of an organized criminal enterprise. The evidence does not support this. Most growers were peasants who sold to intermediaries and had little power over prices; they were at the bottom of the commodity chain and at the greatest risk from eradication and army raids. Above them stood buyers, transporters, and urban financiers, some of them local strongmen who had political connections. The organization of this middle layer, who bought, stored, and moved the product, varied by place and time. Explaining the later concentration of trafficking power requires attention to how that middle layer was able to accumulate capital and political protection.

The state’s response was campaigns of eradication and policing. From the late 1940s, the Mexican government conducted antidrug campaigns that involved the army and the federal Attorney General’s office, with the declared aim of destroying poppy and marijuana fields. These campaigns were often episodic and unevenly funded, and they created opportunities for soldiers and police to demand payments from growers and intermediaries. Historians who have worked in Sinaloan archives find in these records a recurring pattern of raids, arrests of small cultivators, and negotiated tolerance for better-connected figures. The campaigns also contributed to an early pattern of displacement, in which farmers moved cultivation deeper into the mountains or into neighboring states.

Evidence for the rural narcotics economy is uneven. Government eradication reports describe what the state targeted, not what existed. Oral histories and local studies, for example from the Sinaloan highlands, record the experience of growers, but they are scattered and sometimes shaped by later reputations. Journalistic accounts from the 1970s and after, and the work of historians drawing on state and municipal archives, are the main sources for the period before 1970. Students should treat any claim that gives precise figures for early production with caution, since contemporaries did not measure the clandestine economy reliably.

Section 101.3

Border Smuggling, Regional Entrepreneurs, and Early Criminal Networks

The U.S.–Mexican border, formalized after the war of 1846–1848 and the Gadsden Purchase, was from the beginning a line across which goods, workers, and families moved, and where differences in law and price created opportunities for profit. Nineteenth-century smuggling involved livestock, cloth, and tariff-evading consumer goods. In the twentieth century, American Prohibition made alcohol the most lucrative contraband, and border cities, among them Tijuana, Mexicali, Ciudad Juárez, and Matamoros, developed tourism and nightlife economies catering to Americans and to those who supplied them. These economies produced entrepreneurs, from bar owners to transporters, who understood how to cross the border, bribe inspectors, and manage risk, and some of them later turned to narcotics.

Women were prominent in these early networks, a fact often obscured by later narratives. In Mexico City in the 1930s and 1940s, María Dolores Estévez Zuleta, known as “Lola la Chata,” became famous as a seller of marijuana, opium, and other drugs; her case, studied by historians of Mexican crime, shows a woman running a substantial operation in the capital while navigating police and press. Along the border, women crossed with small quantities in clothing and household goods, and women ran households and businesses that supported smuggling. In Ciudad Juárez, Ignacia “La Nacha” Jasso became known for selling heroin from the 1930s, operating through family and neighborhood networks until her prosecution in later decades; she died in 1999. Her career demonstrates both the scale a family-based enterprise could reach and its dependence on tolerance by local police.

In Tamaulipas, Matamoros became the base of Juan Nepomuceno Guerra (1915–2001), a businessman and political figure whose career as a smuggler, from contraband alcohol and goods to, according to American and Mexican investigators, narcotics and other illicit trade, spanned much of the twentieth century. Guerra’s reputation owed something to myth. He cultivated relationships with local politicians and became a figure of local standing, and the extent of his involvement in narcotics, as distinct from broader smuggling, has been disputed. What the historical record does show is that northeastern Mexico had its own tradition of cross-border contraband, rooted in the geography of the Rio Grande and the commerce of Matamoros and Reynosa, which developed largely independently of the Sinaloan poppy economy. This distinction returns in Section 101.19.

Baja California, Sonora, and Chihuahua added further regional traditions. In Baja California, as noted earlier, authority over vice had been a source of revenue for officials since the revolutionary period, and Tijuana’s economy of entertainment and cross-border commerce made it a node in the heroin and marijuana trades. Chihuahua, with the mountain routes of the Sierra Tarahumara and the border city of Ciudad Juárez, connected highland producers to American markets. Each region developed its own pattern of intermediaries, police arrangements, and family networks. The scholarship collected in Histories of Drug Trafficking in Twentieth-Century Mexico is valuable precisely because it shows these differences rather than a single national model.

The organizations that emerged from these regions were typically small, family-based, and tied to local politics. They depended on trusted relatives and friends, on connections to local police and officials, and on ability to cross the border, which meant relationships with American buyers or intermediaries on the other side. They rarely had the scale or the bureaucratic structure suggested by the word “cartel.” Their wealth and ambition were limited by American wholesale networks, by the cost of bribing officials, and by the relatively low volume of the trade. What changed in the 1970s and 1980s was not only the scale of demand but the access of some of these regional entrepreneurs to capital, aircraft, and political relationships beyond their home regions.

Comparing the early border and rural networks with what came later is one of the chapter’s central analytical tasks. The earlier networks suggest that illicit commerce could be extensive without being centralized, and that the participation of women, border residents, and ordinary farmers was substantial. They also suggest that the Mexican drug trade was never entirely “imported” from Colombia or the United States. It grew in a Mexican context and was shaped by Mexican institutions.

Section 101.4

The PRI State, Police Protection, and the Political Economy of Illicit Markets

To understand why illicit commerce persisted despite prohibition, historians examine how the postrevolutionary state worked. The party that governed Mexico from the 1920s to 2000, successively the PNR, PRM, and PRI, did not control every locality through central command. It built a system of bargains in which governors, municipal presidents, labor leaders, army officers, and police commanders enjoyed considerable discretion in return for political loyalty, and in which presidents rotated every six years. Within this system, a local official might tolerate illegal activity, extract payments, and suppress rivals, so long as order was maintained and the higher authorities were satisfied.

Protection took several forms, and these should be distinguished. At the lowest level, police and soldiers extracted bribes from growers, transporters, and sellers in exchange for not arresting them or destroying their crops. Above this, municipal and state officials, including at times governors, could allow trade in return for payments or personal investment, or could target rivals of favored traffickers. At the federal level, agencies such as the Attorney General’s office, the army, and, from 1947, the Dirección Federal de Seguridad (DFS) had the authority to investigate, arrest, and eradicate, and individuals within them could exercise that authority selectively. Scholars have used terms such as “protection racket” and “selective enforcement” to describe these arrangements. The point of the vocabulary is that protection was not a single policy but a repertoire of practices.

The historiography divides on how to characterize the relationship between traffickers and politicians. Luis Astorga’s influential work argued that, through much of the twentieth century, political power dominated and regulated the drug trade, with traffickers subordinate to officials rather than the reverse. Later historians, notably Benjamin T. Smith, have emphasized a more fragmented and unstable picture, in which many actors, including army officers, local police, state governors, and federal agents, competed to extract revenue, and in which no single authority could guarantee protection. Wil Pansters and Smith, examining Sinaloa from 1940 to 1980, showed how changing forms of political protection and shifts in the relationship between state and local authority shaped patterns of violence. These studies are taken up in Historical Debate I, below.

The interwar and wartime period also produced a road not taken. In the late 1930s, Leopoldo Salazar Viniegra, a Mexican physician who directed federal narcotics policy, argued that the existing repressive approach created the black market and that addicts should be treated as patients. The Federal Regulation on Toxicomanías, issued in 1940 under President Lázaro Cárdenas, allowed doctors to dispense drugs to registered addicts under controlled conditions. The United States responded with pressure, including restrictions on exports of medicinal narcotics to Mexico, and the regulation was suspended within months. The episode shows that Mexican officials had options, that American pressure helped close them, and that the criminal framework that prevailed was a policy choice, not an inevitability.

In this setting, claims that “the Mexican state” protected all trafficking require careful treatment. There is substantial evidence for local and regional arrangements, for the involvement of particular officials, and for patterns of selective prosecution. The evidence for a single, nationwide policy deliberately managed from the presidency to protect all trafficking is much weaker and has been disputed. A historian’s task is to name the actor, the place, the period, and the type of evidence, and to resist generalizing from a documented governor or police commander to the whole government. The same caution applies in the opposite direction. The existence of honest investigators, soldiers who burned fields, and prosecutors who pursued traffickers shows that the state was not merely complicit.

Section 101.5

U.S. Drug Demand and the Expansion of Mexican Trafficking, 1960–1975

The growth of Mexican trafficking in the 1960s and early 1970s was driven by a combination of American demand and Mexican supply, and neither alone explains it. In the United States, the 1960s brought a rapid rise in marijuana use among young people, associated with the counterculture, college campuses, and opposition to the Vietnam War, and a continuing, geographically concentrated heroin market in cities such as New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles. Surveys from the early 1970s found that a substantial minority of young Americans had tried marijuana, and the number of regular users grew through the decade. Marijuana consumption spread beyond marginal groups into the middle class, creating a mass market that older, smaller smuggling networks could not have imagined.

Mexico was well positioned to supply it. Marijuana could be grown in many parts of the country, it was bulky but inexpensive to produce, and the border was long and permeable. Estimates from the period, which should be treated with caution, held that the large majority of marijuana consumed in the United States in the early 1970s came from Mexico. Price differentials, the central economic incentive, were large: a kilogram bought from a Mexican grower could sell for many times as much on the American side, with the markup reflecting the risks of crossing and distribution. Growers in Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua, and increasingly in Michoacán, Guerrero, and Oaxaca, responded by planting more.

Heroin followed a different path. For decades, the principal source of heroin in the United States had been the so-called French Connection, in which Turkish opium was refined in Marseille. In the early 1970s, a Turkish ban on poppy cultivation and French and American enforcement disrupted that route, and Mexican “brown” heroin, a less refined product, took a growing share of the American market. By the mid-1970s, American officials estimated that Mexico supplied a large share, in some estimates a majority, of the heroin sold in the United States, though such estimates were based on laboratory sampling of seized drugs and are imprecise. The shift was important for Mexico because heroin was far more profitable per kilogram than marijuana, and because it increased the importance of poppy-growing regions in the northwest.

American politics gave the issue unprecedented prominence. President Richard Nixon declared drug abuse “public enemy number one” in 1971, and his administration created new agencies to coordinate enforcement; in 1973 it consolidated several of them into the Drug Enforcement Administration. The new agency expanded the American presence in Mexico, posting agents in consulates and working, with varying degrees of cooperation, with Mexican counterparts. This created a new institutional relationship, in which American agents reported on Mexican corruption and Mexican officials resented American intrusion.

It is important to distinguish Mexico’s role as a producer in this period from its later role as a transit country for Colombian cocaine. In the 1960s and early 1970s, the principal Mexican contributions to the American drug market were marijuana and heroin made from Mexican-grown crops, and the principal organizations were small and regional. Cocaine, at that time, moved mainly through the Caribbean and Florida. The reorientation of routes toward Mexico, examined in Section 101.12, was a later development, and students should not read the cocaine economy of the 1980s back into the 1960s.

Finally, American demand shaped Mexican politics as well as Mexican markets. The American market made illicit crops profitable, but American policy shaped the form that Mexican enforcement took, by demanding eradication, certifying cooperation, and conditioning aid. Mexican officials were aware that visible action could ease American pressure, a calculation that influenced the timing and scale of campaigns, including those examined in the next two sections.

Section 101.6

Operation Intercept, Border Enforcement, and Mexican Sovereignty

On 21 September 1969, the administration of President Richard Nixon launched Operation Intercept, an effort to inspect vehicles and pedestrians crossing the border from Mexico on a scale never before attempted. Customs and Border Patrol officers, supported by additional personnel, searched cars, trucks, and individuals at crossing points from California to Texas, producing long delays. The operation was announced as a campaign against smuggling of marijuana, heroin, and dangerous drugs. Its architects included officials in the Treasury and Justice Departments and, by his own later account, the future Watergate figure G. Gordon Liddy, who had helped plan it.

The effects on the border were immediate and large. Lines of cars stretched for hours at major crossings; Mexican merchants, hotel owners, and workers who depended on cross-border traffic lost income; and American businesses, from border retailers to agricultural importers, complained. The inspections seized relatively modest quantities of drugs. A National Security Council memorandum of 30 September 1969, published in the Foreign Relations of the United States series, records the administration’s consideration of the diplomatic consequences, and the strains Intercept placed on relations with the government of President Gustavo Díaz Ordaz. Mexican officials protested that they were being treated as suspects, that the measures violated the spirit of friendly relations, and that Mexican sovereignty and dignity were at issue.

Historians have asked what Intercept was for. Interpreted narrowly, it was a law-enforcement operation to reduce smuggling. Many scholars argue, however, that its main purpose was diplomatic leverage: to demonstrate American seriousness and to compel Mexico to cooperate more actively against drug production. The way the operation ended supports this interpretation. After about three weeks, in the second week of October, the United States relaxed the inspections after the two governments reached an understanding on greater cooperation, which was presented as a joint effort sometimes called Operation Cooperation. Mexican officials, for their part, agreed to intensify action against cultivation and trafficking. In this sense Intercept worked as bargaining, even though it did little to reduce the flow of drugs.

Mexican reactions were not uniform. The Díaz Ordaz government, already shaken by the repression of the student movement in Tlatelolco in October 1968, was sensitive to charges of subordination to the United States, and public opinion in Mexico was largely critical of the American operation. At the same time, Mexican officials recognized that cooperation could secure goodwill and avoid further disruption, and they began to emphasize their own eradication efforts. The episode taught Mexican policymakers that visible antidrug action could reduce American pressure, and it taught American officials that economic disruption could produce quick concessions. Both lessons shaped later campaigns.

Intercept also revealed the limits of border enforcement. Drugs could be moved through remote crossings, hidden in legitimate commerce, or smuggled by air, and the demand that drew them remained. Inspection at the border did not address production in the Mexican highlands, the supply networks in American cities, or the corruption on both sides that made smuggling possible. The operation’s lasting legacy was therefore political and institutional: it linked drug policy to sovereignty in Mexican political debate, and it prefigured the pattern of intermittent American pressure, followed by Mexican cooperation, that would recur in 1985 and later decades.

Section 101.7

Operation Condor: Military Eradication, Repression, and Rural Displacement

A note on names is necessary. The Mexican antidrug campaign of the late 1970s, known as Operation Condor, is unrelated to the transnational network of political repression among South American dictatorships that Chapter 87 describes under the same name. The coincidence of names is just that; students should not conflate the two, though, as discussed below, the Mexican campaign did overlap in time with Mexico’s own counterinsurgency against leftist guerrillas.

Operation Intercept led to Mexican promises, and the 1970s saw a steady intensification of Mexican antidrug policy. The government of Luis Echeverría (1970–1976) expanded eradication, and the army, which had taken part in poppy destruction since the late 1940s, became more heavily involved. Mexico also acquired aircraft and herbicides, some with American support, and began aerial spraying. By the mid-1970s, the pattern of earlier campaigns, in which small deployments burned fields and made arrests, was giving way to larger operations. Some retrospective accounts date the beginning of Condor to 1975–1976, when these earlier efforts were being intensified under the Attorney General’s office. A U.S. military historical study, however, identifies 16 January 1977 as the arrival of the first task force of the formal operation in the northwest. Students should distinguish preparatory policies from the formal deployment.

The formal operation, launched under President José López Portillo, concentrated thousands of army troops and federal agents in the mountains of Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua. Commonly cited figures speak of roughly ten thousand soldiers, but such numbers come from retrospective accounts and should be treated as approximate. The troops destroyed fields, burned crops, sprayed herbicides from aircraft, and set up checkpoints. The campaign also used herbicides, including paraquat, whose use on marijuana provoked controversy in the United States in 1978 after reports that contaminated marijuana might reach American consumers; Congress subsequently restricted American funding for paraquat spraying. The operation was presented by both governments as a success, and early reports claimed large reductions in cultivation. The U.S. General Accounting Office later found that eradication initially reduced the opium poppy and marijuana crops significantly, but that farmers developed new techniques that made eradication difficult.

For rural communities the campaign was a disruption and, in many places, a catastrophe. Mexican historians who have worked with local archives and testimonies, including studies of the Sinaloan highlands (the “Altos de Sinaloa”), document detentions without warrant, destruction of food crops along with illicit ones, beatings and torture, killings, and the burning of homes. Communities fled, and many of the displaced went to Culiacán, Tijuana, and other cities. Some peasants had grown poppy for years and had no other cash crop; others were swept up by suspicion. The language of the sources often blurs the line between growers and traffickers, and the state’s own records have little to say about the experiences of those who suffered.

The campaign overlapped with Mexico’s counterinsurgency against guerrilla groups and with what is often called the “dirty war” of the 1960s and 1970s, in which security forces, including the army and the DFS, detained and disappeared leftist militants and suspected sympathizers, especially in Guerrero. Scholars have noted that some of the same units, methods, and legal practices appeared in both contexts, and that antidrug operations could be used to pursue political aims. The evidence for operational overlap varies by case, and historians caution against assuming that every drug campaign was a counterinsurgency operation. What can be said is that both involved the military in internal policing, with limited judicial oversight, a precedent relevant to the later militarization examined in Chapter 103.

The campaign’s long-term results were mixed. Eradication reduced the visible cultivation in some areas, but growers moved, shrank their plots, hid crops among legal plants, or planted in more remote areas. Trafficking organizations, meanwhile, adapted, shifting operations to places less exposed to military presence, and, as the next section argues, urban hubs such as Guadalajara became more attractive. It would be wrong to say that Condor alone caused this relocation. But the military campaign was one of the pressures that changed the geography of the trade, and it left a legacy of distrust between rural communities and the army.

Everyday Life and Social History

Eradication, Illicit Income, and Life in the Sierra Communities

For a highland family, the drug economy was a seasonal rhythm. Parents and children cleared plots, planted poppy or marijuana beside maize, weeded, and, for poppy, scored the pods at the right time, a delicate and exhausting task. Sales brought cash for medicine, school expenses, tools, and festivals. In communities with few roads, merchants who bought gum also supplied goods and credit, and the cash economy that illicit crops sustained supported shops, transport, and small construction.

The costs were equally real. Army and police raids brought fear of arrest, extortion, and violence; eradication destroyed fields and sometimes the maize and beans planted among them; and displacement scattered families to cities where they entered the poor neighborhoods of Culiacán, Tijuana, and Guadalajara. Women often bore the burden of keeping households together when men were arrested or fled, and some became intermediaries themselves. Children grew up with the knowledge that income and danger were linked. Local officials and police acquired power through their ability to protect or to arrest, a source of everyday intimidation that later shaped how communities viewed both the state and traffickers.

Sources for these experiences are limited: oral histories, local studies, press reports, and later memoirs. They are strongest for moments of dramatic abuse and weakest for the routine texture of daily life. The silences, once again, are part of the evidence.

Section 101.8

From Sinaloa to Guadalajara: Migration and the Concentration of Trafficking Networks

By the late 1970s, a number of traffickers originally from Sinaloa had established themselves in Guadalajara. The movement was not a single migration and not an organized “relocation of headquarters.” It was a gradual shift, over several years, of individuals, families, and businesses toward a city that offered advantages that the mountains and the coastal towns did not. Explaining it requires attention to several reasons, none of which alone is sufficient.

The first was the pressure of enforcement. Operation Condor and earlier campaigns made life harder in the Sinaloan highlands and in the cities, such as Culiacán, whose police and army were closely watched. The killing in 1978 of Pedro Avilés Pérez, an early Sinaloan trafficker who had pioneered the use of aircraft, removed an established figure and left associates to reorganize. The second was commerce. Guadalajara, Mexico’s second city, had international and domestic airports, banks, real-estate markets, and a large commercial class in which new wealth could be absorbed and laundered. A trafficker could buy ranches, businesses, and houses there without standing out as he would in a mountain town. The third was connection. Guadalajara lay between the Sinaloan producing regions and the central and southern parts of the country, and it was close to the Pacific coast and to Michoacán and Jalisco, where marijuana and later cocaine could be stored and moved. The fourth was social. Family and friendship ties linked Sinaloan migrants, and once a few had settled, others followed.

Overlapping criminal enterprises developed in this urban setting. Traffickers who had grown up in the highlands, like Rafael Caro Quintero and Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo, and a former state police officer from Culiacán, Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo, formed relationships of business and friendship, sharing information, pilots, and routes. They also cultivated relationships with local police, state officials, and members of the federal security services. Their wealth made them visible in Guadalajara society, and their money flowed into hotels, restaurants, and real estate. Whether this concentration constituted a single organization, a federation, or simply a cluster of overlapping partnerships is exactly what scholars debate.

The debate is central to the chapter’s method. The conventional view, found in many journalistic and some institutional accounts, describes a “Guadalajara Cartel” under Félix Gallardo’s leadership that integrated production, transport, protection, and sales across much of Mexico. Carlos Pérez Ricart and Jack Pannell, drawing on archival research, argue instead that the historical organization was a changing network of relationships, that the cartel label became widespread only retrospectively, and that Mexican and American agencies used it for their own purposes. Institutional histories, such as the characterization of the Félix Gallardo organization as a precursor of later Mexican groups, describe a more integrated enterprise. The disagreement turns on evidence, on definitions of “organization,” and on whether similar behavior by different traffickers implies a single command structure. Historical Debate II, below, develops it.

The chapter’s position is to use the term “Guadalajara-centered networks,” which is descriptive, and to reserve “cartel” for discussion of the label itself. The sources show a concentration of traffickers, shared business arrangements, and access to protection. They do not, in the view of many historians, show a corporate hierarchy that controlled all Mexican trafficking. What they do show is enough to explain why enforcement efforts after 1984, which focused on this cluster, had such large effects on the organization of the trade.

Section 101.9

Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo and the Emergence of the Guadalajara Organization

Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo was born in 1946 in Culiacán, Sinaloa. By most accounts he served as a state judicial police officer in Sinaloa in his youth, and he is reported to have had connections to the state’s political class, including the governor Leopoldo Sánchez Celis (1963–1968), though the nature of those connections rests largely on later journalistic and judicial accounts rather than on contemporary documents. These details, repeated in countless biographies, illustrate a recurring problem: much of what is “known” about the early lives of traffickers comes from confessions, police profiles, and newspaper reporting composed after their fame.

What is better documented is his role in the late 1970s and 1980s. Through earlier Sinaloan figures such as Pedro Avilés Pérez, he learned the business of moving marijuana and heroin north. By the early 1980s, he was living in Guadalajara and had become, according to Mexican and American investigators, a coordinator of arrangements among traffickers: negotiating shipments, mediating disputes, arranging protection from police and officials, and connecting Mexican producers to foreign suppliers, including, as the next sections show, Colombian and Honduran contacts. He was known by nicknames, among them “El Padrino” (the Godfather) and, in later retellings, “Jefe de Jefes” (Boss of Bosses).

The labels need unpacking. “El Padrino” suggests personal authority and patronage, a recognition that younger traffickers sought his guidance. “Jefe de Jefes” suggests a formal hierarchy, which is the point disputed by scholars: if he was a boss of bosses, there was a command structure, but if he was a respected intermediary and broker, then the relationships were looser. Popular culture, including television dramas, adopted the stronger version, and the use of the word “cartel,” borrowed from economics, implied price-fixing and centralized control. Whether either implication fits the evidence is the subject of the chapter’s second debate.

The business Félix Gallardo is credited with organizing was not only drugs. He invested in legitimate enterprises and in real estate, maintained relationships with officials in several states, and used his own network of associates, family, and friends. Honduran trafficker Juan Matta-Ballesteros, who ran an airline and had connections to Colombian suppliers, is reported by investigators and in court proceedings to have been a key partner in moving cocaine. This introduces what became the decisive development of the 1980s, discussed in Section 101.12: the connection between Mexican organizations, with their routes and protection, and Colombian cocaine, with its profits.

Félix Gallardo’s later legal history belongs to Section 101.16. For now, two points should be stressed. First, he was one of several important figures, not the sole inventor of Mexican trafficking, and the evidence for his role as coordinator is stronger than the evidence for his command over every Mexican trafficker. Second, his story illustrates how a trafficker’s career could connect rural origins, police service, political relationships, and urban business. It is a story about a network, not a lone criminal genius.

Section 101.10

Rafael Caro Quintero, Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo, and the Trafficking Partnership

Rafael Caro Quintero, born in 1952 in the Badiraguato municipality of Sinaloa, belonged to a younger generation. He rose through marijuana, and he is associated with large-scale cultivation of seedless marijuana (sinsemilla), a higher-potency product that fetched higher prices in the American market. The name that attached to him, and the one that mattered most to enforcement agencies, was Rancho El Búfalo, the huge marijuana site in Chihuahua described in Section 101.13. His wealth and flamboyance made him a prominent figure in Guadalajara, and his reputation for violence was repeatedly emphasized by investigators, though individual allegations differ in their evidentiary basis.

Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo, known as “Don Neto,” was older, born in the early 1940s in the same mountain region, and had begun in the trade earlier. He had connections to the older Sinaloan networks, including that of Pedro Avilés Pérez, and his family relationships extended into the later Juárez organization: his nephew was Amado Carrillo Fuentes, a name that appears in Section 101.18. Fonseca’s importance lay in his established networks, his seniority, and his connections to families and suppliers across the northwest. If Félix Gallardo is often portrayed as the broker and Caro Quintero as the producer, Fonseca can be portrayed as the elder statesman, but such role labels, drawn from popular accounts, simplify more than they clarify.

What kind of relationship linked the three? Contemporary investigators and later accounts describe cooperation: shared aircraft, pooled shipments, introductions to officials and suppliers, and mediation of disputes. They also describe divisions of responsibility, with different men concentrating on production, transport, or relations with officials, and competitive tensions, since each had his own men, money, and ambitions. Personal alliances, friendships, and family ties mattered, as they did in earlier regional networks. To describe the arrangement as a rigid corporate hierarchy would presume what the evidence has not established. To describe it as a mere coincidence of independent actors would ignore documented coordination.

The differences among the three are important to keep in view. Their backgrounds differed in age, geography within the sierra, and degree of political connection. Their legal histories also differ, and later proceedings, which continued for decades, should not be confused with the contemporaneous record. In outline, both Caro Quintero and Fonseca Carrillo were arrested in April 1985 and convicted in Mexico in connection with the Camarena case. Caro Quintero was released in 2013 when a Mexican appeals court overturned his sentence on procedural grounds, was re-arrested in July 2022, and was transferred to the United States in 2025; those later events lie beyond this chapter and are mentioned only to warn students that legal outcomes changed over decades.

Another point deserves emphasis. The interpretation of the relationships among these men depends heavily on what kind of evidence is used. Trial testimony, prosecution narratives, DEA reports, memoirs by informants, and journalistic reconstructions each offer different pictures. A prosecutor needs to show conspiracy; a journalist may favor the clearest narrative; an informant may exaggerate his own importance. None offers an unmediated view.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate II: Was the Guadalajara Cartel a Single Organization or a Retrospective Construction?

The question: Did the traffickers concentrated in Guadalajara in the early 1980s form a centralized organization under Félix Gallardo’s leadership, or is the “Guadalajara Cartel” a label later applied to a looser network of overlapping relationships?

Interpretation A — A relatively integrated organization: Institutional and journalistic histories describe an organization in which Félix Gallardo coordinated production, transport, protection, and sales across much of Mexico, with Caro Quintero and Fonseca Carrillo as principal partners. The Wilson Center’s characterization of the Félix Gallardo organization as an important precursor of later Mexican groups reflects this view. Proponents point to documented coordination, shared resources, common protection arrangements, and the concentrated enforcement response after 1985.
Interpretation B — A flexible network and a retrospective label: Carlos Pérez Ricart and Jack Pannell, using archival research, argue that the historical organization was a changing network of relationships, that traffickers retained substantial autonomy, and that the “cartel” label became widespread retrospectively, used by police and journalists and then adopted in popular culture. On this view, the label creates a false impression of corporate hierarchy and projects later organizational forms backward.

Evidence: Both sides draw on DEA and Mexican police files, court records, and testimony. The difficulty is that these sources were produced by agencies with an interest in presenting targets as organized and powerful. The history of the word “cartel,” borrowed from economic language to describe price-fixing producers, is itself evidence: investigators used it before it fit, and the label shaped how later sources described the past. Evidence of coordination (shared flights, joint meetings) does not by itself establish command.

Why it matters: If a single organization existed, the 1989 arrest of its leader looks like a decapitation that produced successor groups. If instead there was a network, the arrest looks like the removal of an important broker that rearranged relationships. The interpretation also affects how historians read popular narratives about the “division” of territories (Section 101.17) and how policymakers draw lessons about attacking leaders.

Student task: State which interpretation you find more persuasive for the early 1980s and why. Then identify one kind of document, not described in this chapter, that could help decide the question.

Section 101.11

Political Protection, the DFS, and the Boundaries of State Complicity

The Dirección Federal de Seguridad was established in 1947 during the presidency of Miguel Alemán as an intelligence and political-security agency attached to the Interior Ministry (Secretaría de Gobernación). Its stated mission was the protection of national security and political order. In practice, through much of its history, the DFS conducted surveillance of political opponents, labor leaders, students, and guerrillas, and its agents were implicated in detentions, torture, and disappearances during the dirty war of the 1960s and 1970s. It was not created as a counternarcotics agency, nor as a criminal organization; its history is first of all a history of political policing.

Its relationship with drug trafficking is a matter of documented allegation and, in some cases, judicial finding. By the late 1970s and early 1980s, DFS agents had federal credentials, weapons, and broad discretion, and there is substantial evidence, from American investigators, Mexican journalists, and later judicial and archival sources, that some agents and some commanders accepted payment from traffickers, shared information, and provided identification that shielded traffickers from police. Reports that traffickers carried DFS credentials appear frequently in accounts of the period. The agency’s directors in this era, including Miguel Nazar Haro (1978–1982) and José Antonio Zorrilla Pérez (1982–1985), were controversial, and Zorrilla was later convicted in connection with the 1984 murder of the journalist Manuel Buendía, a case that opened questions about the DFS’s links to criminal and political networks. The precise relationship between the Buendía case and drug trafficking is disputed, and students should not treat it as established.

These facts must be placed in a larger institutional picture. The DFS was one among several security bodies, which included the federal judicial police, the army, and military intelligence, with overlapping and sometimes competing mandates. Corruption was not confined to one agency. At the same time, honest officials and investigators existed, and some pursued traffickers at personal risk. To say that “the DFS protected the traffickers” collapses these distinctions. A more careful statement would identify which agents, in which period, are documented as receiving payments or providing protection, and which claims rest on allegation or testimony.

The Camarena case brought the question into the open. American officials, and many Mexican journalists, argued that senior Mexican security officials had been involved in or had facilitated the traffickers’ activities, and the case contributed to the pressure that led to the agency’s dissolution. Mexico’s Archivo General de la Nación records 29 November 1985 as the date the DFS was formally ended in an institutional reorganization, with its functions passing to a new intelligence directorate (the Dirección de Investigación y Seguridad Nacional, later reorganized into CISEN in 1989). It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that the agency’s dissolution ended corruption or illegal security relationships. Personnel moved to other agencies, practices persisted, and the political incentives that made protection valuable remained.

The record of the DFS has become much more accessible in recent years. The agency’s archives are held by the Archivo General de la Nación, and their consultation was opened to the public in February 2020, allowing historians to compare earlier journalistic claims with documents. Students should expect that the picture will keep changing as researchers use these records.

Historical Vocabulary
Corruption, Political Protection, Impunity, and State Authority

Corruption is the use of public office for private gain, ranging from small bribes to systematic extortion. Political protection refers to arrangements by which officials, in exchange for payment, loyalty, or other benefits, shield a person or business from enforcement, or direct enforcement at rivals. Impunity is the condition in which wrongdoing goes unpunished, whether because of protection, weak institutions, or the intimidation of witnesses. State authority is the legitimate power of government institutions to make and enforce rules.

These terms are related but distinct. Corruption in a police force does not mean that the government as a whole directs crime; protection can be purchased by traffickers or imposed by officials; impunity can exist without any particular protector. Historians use the vocabulary to name specific relationships and avoid sweeping claims such as “the state is the cartel.”

Historical Debate

Historical Debate I: Did the Mexican State Control the Drug Trade?

The question: Was the drug trade in the PRI era managed by the Mexican state through selective protection and enforcement, or was it the product of corruption, local autonomy, and unstable, contested relationships?

Interpretation A — State-managed protection and selective enforcement: Drawing on Luis Astorga’s work and on testimony and journalism, some interpretations emphasize that political authorities regulated the trade, that traffickers were subordinate to officials, and that enforcement was used to discipline or favor particular actors. Evidence includes documented relationships between traffickers and governors, police commanders, and security agents, and the apparent protection of some figures for years.
Interpretation B — Corruption, fragmentation, and limits of coordination: Scholarship by Benjamin T. Smith, Wil Pansters, and others stresses that many actors (soldiers, police, local politicians, federal agents) extracted payments independently, that protection was unstable and could be withdrawn, and that no single authority controlled the trade. Federal agencies sometimes pursued traffickers, and American pressure could override local arrangements.

Evidence: Both views draw on archival records, trial files, press accounts, and oral testimony. Documents establish specific relationships; they rarely demonstrate a nationwide policy. Absence of enforcement can reflect protection, incapacity, or deliberate decision, and the sources seldom distinguish among them.

Why it matters: If the state controlled the trade, the later breakdown of one-party rule and the rise of violence might be read as the collapse of a regulating system. If instead the trade was fragmented, violence might reflect competition among many actors as protection arrangements changed. Neither reading is equivalent to claims about a particular official, and students should specify which actor and period each claim concerns.

Section 101.12

Colombian Cocaine and the Transformation of Mexican Trafficking

Chapters 97 and 98 described the rise of Colombian cocaine organizations; this section concerns what that rise meant for Mexico. Until the early 1980s, most cocaine reaching the United States traveled by air and sea through the Caribbean, especially the Bahamas, and entered through southern Florida. The enforcement response, culminating in the South Florida Task Force established in 1982 under Vice President George H. W. Bush, increased the risks of these routes. At the same time, Colombian organizations were seeking alternative paths and partners, and Mexico, with its long border, established smuggling expertise, and networks of protection, offered them.

Mexican traffickers did not at first dominate the trade. Early arrangements, as described by investigators, treated them as service providers who received fees for transporting cocaine across Mexico and into the United States, while Colombian organizations owned the product and the greatest share of the profit. Figures such as Juan Matta-Ballesteros served as connectors between Colombian suppliers and Mexican networks, and Félix Gallardo is described as arranging access to routes, airstrips, and protection. This relationship gave Mexican organizations access to far larger sums of money than marijuana and heroin had produced, and it exposed them to new partners, new technologies such as aircraft and communications equipment, and new risks.

The cocaine connection transformed Mexican trafficking in several ways. It increased the capital available for bribes, weapons, and aircraft, which deepened the corrupting power of the trade. It extended the reach of Mexican groups across international borders and made their business more dependent on relations with foreign partners. It shifted attention from rural crop regions to logistics: warehouses, airstrips, safe houses, and the management of crossings. And it created competition among Mexican groups for the role of intermediary, with different organizations forging relationships with different Colombian suppliers, including those associated with the Medellín and Cali organizations. Competition among Colombian organizations spilled into Mexico as well, since different Colombian groups worked with different Mexican partners.

The relationship evolved over time. In the later 1980s and the 1990s, according to investigators and scholars, Mexican organizations increasingly demanded payment in cocaine rather than cash, which gave them a share of the product and greater independence and profit; the developments of that later stage belong to Chapter 102. By the mid-1990s, a U.S. General Accounting Office report noted that maritime vessels were used to move an estimated two-thirds of the cocaine entering Mexico, with shipments then moving overland to the United States, a reminder that the transit trade involved sea and air routes as well as land corridors. Estimates of the share of American-bound cocaine that crossed Mexico rose through the late 1980s and early 1990s, but the figures come from enforcement estimates rather than measurement, and students should treat any precise percentage with caution.

Why did Mexico’s role change? Part of the answer lies in the economics of enforcement and the geography of routes, as described. Part lies in Mexico’s institutional environment, where traffickers could buy protection, and part in the existing expertise of Mexican smugglers. Part, finally, lies in relations among organizations that cannot be reduced to economics: personal introductions, trust, and the willingness to use violence. The result was that Mexican networks, which had been producers and smugglers of marijuana and heroin, became partners in the cocaine business, tying the Mexican illicit economy to the largest profits in the hemisphere.

Section 101.13

The El Búfalo Operation and the Escalation of Enforcement, 1984

In November 1984, Mexican federal forces raided a huge marijuana cultivation and processing site known as Rancho El Búfalo, in the state of Chihuahua. The operation, supported by information from American sources, including DEA agents working out of Guadalajara, destroyed what authorities described as one of the largest marijuana plantations ever found. It involved Mexican federal police and army units, who arrived at a site where, according to later accounts, thousands of workers had been employed in planting, tending, and processing the crop. The raid is remembered because it reached a site that was, by most accounts, associated with Rafael Caro Quintero, and because it preceded by only a few weeks the events of February 1985.

The scale of El Búfalo is disputed. Contemporary and later accounts speak of an area of hundreds or thousands of hectares, of thousands of workers, and of a quantity of marijuana measured in thousands of tons, with valuations running into the billions of dollars. These numbers circulated widely, in part because they served the interests of those who reported them: officials could emphasize the seriousness of the enemy and the success of the raid, and traffickers’ enemies could emphasize their power. The estimates of market value in particular depend on assumptions about price, quality, and quantity that cannot be verified. Students should distinguish the verified, such as the fact of the raid, the participation of identified agencies, and seizures actually recorded, from the estimated, such as retail value or the number of workers.

The operation shows how intelligence cooperation worked in practice. American officials supplied information, and Mexican agencies carried out the raid, with the cooperation of Mexican officials who, at that stage, were willing to act. Accounts of the operation differ on who initiated it, how much Mexican authorities knew in advance, and whether the raid was delayed by corruption. What is clear is that the raid was a large operation by the standards of the time and that it damaged the networks associated with Caro Quintero and Fonseca Carrillo. Their losses, in crops, money, and standing, placed pressure on the relationships that had held the Guadalajara-centered networks together.

The consequences were also political. For the United States, the raid demonstrated that Mexican cooperation was possible; for Mexican officials, it was a demonstration of action in response to American pressure. For traffickers, it raised the question of who had provided information, and in the intensely suspicious atmosphere of the following months, American agents in Guadalajara became targets of anger. Historians caution against treating the raid as the single cause of what followed. The motives of those who abducted Camarena are known only through testimony and allegations, and those motives may have been overdetermined.

Section 101.14

February 1985: The Abduction and Murder of Enrique Camarena and Alfredo Zavala

Enrique “Kiki” Camarena was born in 1947 in Mexicali and raised in Calexico, California. He served in the U.S. Marine Corps, worked as a police officer and in Customs, and joined the DEA in 1974; the agency’s official record states that he was posted to Guadalajara in 1981. By 1985 he had been working there for several years, cultivating informants and gathering information on marijuana and cocaine trafficking in western Mexico. He is remembered at the DEA as a symbol of agents killed in the line of duty, and his death has been memorialized in the agency’s Wall of Honor.

On 7 February 1985, according to DEA records, Camarena was abducted in Guadalajara as he left the American consulate to meet his wife for lunch. The Mexican pilot Alfredo Zavala Avelar, who had flown surveillance missions and worked with American agents in locating plantations, was abducted at about the same time. A search by American and Mexican investigators followed, accompanied by strong American diplomatic pressure. On 5 March 1985 the bodies of the two men were found in the state of Michoacán. The FBI conducted extensive forensic work in the investigation.

The families of the victims bore the immediate cost. Camarena’s wife and children, and Zavala’s family, endured the weeks of uncertainty and then the discovery of the bodies. In the United States, Camarena’s death produced public attention and later campaigns by neighbors and colleagues; in Mexico, the death of Zavala, a Mexican citizen who worked with American agents, received far less attention at the time, a difference that historians of memory have remarked on. The disparity illustrates how institutions and publics create different memories of the same event.

What is established about the murders? Physical evidence, the recovery of the bodies, and the identification of remains are documented in official records. Mexican courts convicted Rafael Caro Quintero and others in connection with the killings, and American courts later convicted several defendants in proceedings related to the case. What remains disputed includes the precise sequence of the abduction, the roles of individual participants, the extent of involvement of Mexican officials, and the motives, including whether the killing was ordered by senior figures in the Guadalajara-associated network or by particular individuals acting on their own account. Allegations by informants, memoirs of former agents, and investigative journalism have offered numerous accounts, some of which involve claims about officials and intelligence agencies that courts have not established.

Each major claim should be classified. A claim supported by contemporaneous documentation and physical evidence (the date of abduction, the date and place of the discovery of the bodies) belongs in one category. A claim established by a final judicial finding (the convictions of particular defendants) belongs in another. A claim made in an indictment or by a witness, but not tested or accepted by a court, belongs in a third, and speculation or interpretation belongs in a fourth. This classification is the working method of the historian; the temptation to treat a vivid and widely repeated allegation as established is strong, and it should be resisted.

The chapter does not treat Camarena only as an individual victim, though his death was a human tragedy. His murder matters historically because it exposed weaknesses in Mexican and American institutions, produced the most intensive investigation of the period, and set in motion changes in enforcement, diplomacy, and the organization of trafficking.

Section 101.15

Operation Leyenda, Mexican Investigations, and the International Crisis

The abduction of Camarena prompted an extraordinary American response. The DEA formed a special investigative effort, Operation Leyenda (Operation Legend), formally established in May 1985, whose purpose was to find those responsible for the murder and to pursue the case through the courts. Leyenda drew on agents from many offices, and for years it was among the largest murder investigations in the agency’s history. Its work involved interviews, informants, surveillance, forensic analysis, and, increasingly, efforts to persuade Mexican authorities to arrest and prosecute suspects.

The investigation was not solely American. Mexican federal judicial police and prosecutors carried out searches, arrests, and interrogations, and Mexican courts tried and convicted defendants. These efforts were uneven. Some Mexican investigators pursued the case with energy and at risk, and Mexican forensic and judicial work produced convictions; others obstructed, leaked information, or protected suspects. To narrate the case entirely through American institutions, as most popular accounts do, distorts it. The American perspective is amply documented in agency files and memoirs, but the Mexican side of the investigation, preserved in court records and the accounts of Mexican officers and journalists, is essential to understanding what happened and why it took the form it did.

The diplomatic crisis was serious. American officials believed that Mexican authorities were slow or complicit, and they used pressure at several levels. In February 1985, American customs authorities intensified inspections at the border, which Mexican officials and many commentators compared with Operation Intercept and criticized as coercive. American legislators and officials publicly accused Mexico of corruption, and Mexican officials resented what they regarded as insults to sovereignty. Tensions persisted for years. In 1990, American-hired agents abducted a Mexican physician, Humberto Álvarez Machaín, accused of helping to prolong Camarena’s life during torture, and brought him to the United States for trial. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in 1992 that the abduction did not bar prosecution, and a federal judge later acquitted Álvarez Machaín for lack of evidence. Mexico protested the abduction as a violation of its sovereignty, and the case became one of the most contested episodes of bilateral legal relations.

Evidence and testimony posed major problems. Many of the case’s most dramatic claims rested on testimony from informants and defendants with reasons to bargain, and on statements made in custody. Courts evaluated them with different standards in Mexico and the United States. Witnesses recanted, memoirs emerged decades later, and journalists reported claims about higher officials that were never tested in court. Historians must treat these materials as sources that require corroboration, not as proof.

The case’s effects on bilateral relations were lasting. It strengthened American demands for extradition and for access to Mexican investigations. It fed a perception in the United States that Mexican institutions were corrupt, and it fed in Mexico a resentment of American unilateralism. It also contributed to institutional change on the Mexican side, including the reorganization of security agencies. The DEA and Mexican authorities nevertheless developed new modes of cooperation, though marked by recurring crises of trust.

Section 101.16

The Arrest of Félix Gallardo in 1989 and the Decline of the Guadalajara Network

The arrests that followed the Camarena murder began in the spring of 1985. Rafael Caro Quintero was arrested in Costa Rica in early April 1985 and returned to Mexico; Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo was arrested in Puerto Vallarta a few days later. Both were prosecuted in Mexico and convicted in connection with the Camarena case. The arrests removed two of the three best-known figures of the Guadalajara-associated group, and dealt a blow to the relationships among them. The seizure of their assets and the investigation of their associates disrupted business arrangements and exposed officials who had protected them.

Félix Gallardo remained free for four more years. During that period, according to investigators, he continued to coordinate trafficking and to maintain relationships, including with Colombian suppliers, while adapting to the new risks by moving among cities and relying on a smaller circle. He was arrested on 8 April 1989 in Guadalajara by Mexican federal police, a date confirmed by an American embassy cable of 10 April 1989. The arrest occurred a few months after Carlos Salinas de Gortari took office as president, at a time when the new government was seeking to demonstrate its commitment to law enforcement and to improve relations with the United States; historians note that the timing served political purposes, though the arrest was also the product of years of investigation.

The arrest had several consequences. It removed a key coordinator, and, according to investigators, destabilized the relationships he had managed. It led to prosecution for the Camarena murder and for trafficking, and he was eventually convicted and imprisoned. It also raised questions about the arrangements by which he had operated for years. It would be a mistake to claim that his arrest ended the networks he had helped link, because many of his associates and rivals remained active, but it removed a central point of contact.

Why did Guadalajara-centered cooperation weaken? Several factors worked together. Enforcement pressure after 1985, including the arrests, asset seizures, and heightened surveillance, made cooperation riskier. The loss of the DFS and the reorganization of the security services disrupted established protection arrangements, which had to be renegotiated with different officials. Rivalries within and among networks, always present, intensified as traffickers competed for the roles and routes that had been controlled by those arrested. And the wealth generated by cocaine increased the stakes of every dispute. Historians who emphasize a unified cartel read the arrest as a decapitation. Those who see a network read it as the removal of a pivotal node that rearranged relationships.

Dates must be handled carefully. The arrests of Caro Quintero and Fonseca Carrillo (April 1985), the arrest of Félix Gallardo (8 April 1989), the extraditions, trials, and sentences that followed, and the later developments in each man’s legal history occurred in different years and should not be conflated, a common error in popular retellings that compress four years into a single scene.

Section 101.17

The Fragmentation of the Guadalajara Networks and the Myth of a Single Division

Popular accounts of Mexican drug history contain a dramatic scene. After his arrest was imminent, or shortly before it, Félix Gallardo is said to have summoned the leading traffickers to a meeting, often placed in a resort such as Acapulco in 1989, and divided Mexico’s trafficking routes (plazas) among them: Tijuana to one group, Juárez to another, the Gulf coast to a third, and the Pacific coast and the northwest to the Sinaloan traffickers who would form the core of what became the Sinaloa organization. In this story, one meeting created every modern cartel. It has been repeated in books, journalism, and television, and it has the appeal of a founding myth.

Historians approach the story with caution. The accounts of the meeting vary in date, location, and participants, and no contemporaneous document that would corroborate a single division has been presented. The story appears largely in later retellings, based on informants, memoirs, and journalistic reconstructions, which often copy one another. It presumes a degree of centralized authority that Félix Gallardo may not have had, particularly if the interpretation of Guadalajara as a flexible network is correct. It also ignores the independent development of groups, such as those in the Gulf region, whose origins lay elsewhere. A modest version of the story, in which traffickers met, discussed arrangements, and agreed on some accommodations, is plausible, since such meetings were common. The grand version, in which a single boss allocated the whole country, is a claim that requires documentary corroboration that has not been shown.

What is documented is more prosaic. After 1985, the arrests of key figures and the dissolution of the DFS disrupted relationships. After 1989, the removal of Félix Gallardo took away a broker who had managed disputes. Traffickers who had worked together in Guadalajara reorganized, some returning to home regions, some forming new partnerships, others competing for the same routes and contacts. Rivalries that had been contained turned violent in places. Mexican and American investigators in the early 1990s observed these developments and described them with the language of cartels and territories, a language that shaped both their policies and the historical record.

Why does the distinction between a documented fragmentation and a mythic division matter? It matters because the myth implies a plan: a single act that produced the later map of Mexican organized crime. A more accurate account stresses contingency. Arrests happened when they happened, protection arrangements changed with changing officials, and individuals made choices that were not dictated by any master plan. The later cartel system emerged from these contingent changes, built on older regional foundations. The myth also flatters the idea that Mexican crime was made by great men, which the rest of this chapter has argued against.

Historical Vocabulary
Cartel, Criminal Network, Regional Organization, and Fragmentation

In economics, a cartel is a group of producers who agree to fix prices or limit output. Applied to criminal organizations, the word suggests centralized coordination and market control. A criminal network is a set of relationships among individuals and groups engaged in illicit activity, with varying degrees of hierarchy. A regional organization is a trafficking group tied to a particular area, often a border crossing or plaza, whose leaders negotiate with officials, rivals, and suppliers there. Fragmentation is the process by which a larger structure or set of relationships breaks into smaller units, often after arrests, deaths, or conflicts among leaders.

These distinctions matter because the word “cartel” entered Mexican and American usage as a label before historians had examined whether it fit. Its use can imply a hierarchy that sources do not show. This chapter uses “network” and “organization” for the 1980s and “cartel” where the term is part of the historical record or the debate about it.

Section 101.18

The Emergence of Sinaloa, Tijuana, and Juárez Organizations

Three organizations stand out in the early 1990s as heirs, in different ways, to the Sinaloan trafficking tradition. They are introduced here at their origins; their organization, alliances, and wars are the subject of Chapter 102, and students should read this section as a preview of lines of development, not a full history.

The Sinaloa-based networks drew on traffickers who had worked within the Félix Gallardo orbit or alongside it. Joaquín Guzmán Loera, born in 1957 in the mountain community of La Tuna in Badiraguato, rose in the 1980s and early 1990s, working with Héctor Luis Palma Salazar, and with Ismael Zambada García, who had been in the trade for decades. Guzmán was arrested in Guatemala in June 1993 and handed over to Mexico; Palma was arrested in 1995. The important point for this chapter is not their later careers but their positions: they were Sinaloan operators in a network of relationships that had been reorganized, not outsiders, and their prominence followed the removal of older figures.

The Tijuana organization was associated with the Arellano Félix family, brothers who came from a Sinaloan family that had moved to Baja California and who built an organization around the Tijuana border crossing, with links to the Sinaloan networks and, in some accounts, to Félix Gallardo through family connections. Tijuana’s historical significance as a border city with a long tradition of vice economies and its proximity to California made it a valuable plaza. The Arellano Félix organization acquired a reputation for violence and for corrupting officials, and its relations with the Sinaloan groups deteriorated into open conflict.

The Juárez organization was associated with the Carrillo Fuentes family, notably Amado Carrillo Fuentes, a nephew of Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo, and with figures such as Rafael Aguilar Guajardo, a former security official reported to have run the Juárez plaza until his killing in 1993, after which Carrillo Fuentes consolidated control. Ciudad Juárez had its own long history of border smuggling, including the career of La Nacha Jasso, and the Juárez organization built on that tradition as well as on connections to the Sinaloan network. Carrillo Fuentes became known for using aircraft to move cocaine, a development that belongs to Chapter 102.

The year 1993 provides a useful marker of rivalry. In May 1993, Cardinal Juan Jesús Posadas Ocampo, the Archbishop of Guadalajara, was shot dead at the Guadalajara airport during a gunfight that officials attributed to a clash between the Arellano Félix organization and Guzmán’s group, with the cardinal caught in the crossfire. The official explanation has been disputed by some Church figures and others, who suspected a targeted killing, and the case remains contested. The incident shows both how dangerous the rivalries had become and how weak the state’s ability to explain violence was. Students should treat it as an example of contested evidence as well as of escalating violence.

These organizations should not be described as simple creations of one meeting or as copies of a single template. Each had its own regional base, family ties, and relationships with officials, and each emerged through a combination of continuity with older networks and the opportunities created by the weakening of the Guadalajara-centered structure.

Section 101.19

The Gulf Organization and Mexico’s Northeastern Smuggling Traditions

The history of the Gulf organization differs from that of the Sinaloan lineages. Its roots lie in the northeastern border region of Tamaulipas, whose commercial and smuggling traditions, as Section 101.3 noted, long predated the 1980s. The Rio Grande valley, the ports and highways around Matamoros, Reynosa, and Nuevo Laredo, and the commerce of the Gulf coast supported a regional economy of contraband that involved alcohol, consumer goods, and, over time, narcotics. Within this world, Juan Nepomuceno Guerra of Matamoros was a central figure for decades, combining legitimate business, political influence, and, according to investigators, smuggling activity.

The organization associated with Juan García Ábrego, born in 1944 and based in Matamoros, developed from these northeastern traditions and from family ties to Guerra, to whom García Ábrego was related. In the 1980s, according to investigators, he expanded into the cocaine trade, working with Colombian suppliers, including organizations linked to Cali, and building an organization with a base in Tamaulipas and routes into Texas. García Ábrego was arrested in Monterrey in January 1996 and expelled to the United States, where he was convicted of drug trafficking charges. That later history is mentioned here only to place the organization chronologically; Chapter 102 treats its development and its eventual transformation, including the emergence of the armed group Los Zetas.

Why does the distinction matter? A genealogy that derives all modern organizations from Guadalajara cannot explain the Gulf group, whose origins were in a separate regional tradition, whose leaders had different backgrounds, and whose connections to Colombian suppliers developed on their own terms. The northeastern case shows that different Mexican regions produced distinct criminal organizations, shaped by different geographies, economies, and political arrangements. It also shows why historians distrust any single national story.

The Gulf case also illustrates the importance of comparing evidence. For northeastern Mexico, historians rely on regional studies, American court records, and Mexican press, while the evidence for the Guadalajara network is dominated by the Camarena case documents. Each evidentiary landscape shapes the picture that emerges: the Camarena case produced extensive records and made Guadalajara visible; the northeast, with less comparable investigation in the 1980s, remained less visible to historians until later.

Section 101.20

From Regional Criminal Networks to the Modern Cartel Era

By the early 1990s, several developments had converged. The long-standing illicit economy of the northwest and the border had been joined to the Colombian cocaine trade, vastly increasing the money available. Protection arrangements, once managed by a mix of local officials and the DFS, had been disrupted and had to be renegotiated. Enforcement pressure after Camarena had broken the Guadalajara-centered cooperation, and the arrests of its best-known figures created vacancies. Regional organizations in Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and Tamaulipas were consolidating, with their own leaders, routes, and relationships.

Political change formed the background. The government of Carlos Salinas de Gortari (1988–1994) pursued market reforms and a trade agreement with the United States and Canada (examined in Chapter 95), and sought to demonstrate its cooperation in drug enforcement. The PRI’s monopoly on power, which had underwritten the older systems of protection, was weakening, though it would not end until 2000. In this setting, protection arrangements that had been coordinated, however loosely, within a centralized party system became more fragmented and competitive. Scholars debate how much this political change affected organized crime, but most agree that changing relationships between traffickers and officials were a necessary part of the story.

American policy continued to shape the field. In January 1990, the first National Drug Control Strategy under President George H. W. Bush assessed Mexico as a source of marijuana and heroin and as a transit country for cocaine. The United States increased aid and pressure, and its annual certification process, which judged countries’ cooperation, became a recurring point of friction. Mexican officials, for their part, sought to show results and to protect sovereignty. The pattern of American pressure and Mexican response, begun with Intercept, continued.

The chapter’s central conclusions can now be stated. First, modern Mexican trafficking did not begin with the Guadalajara-centered networks of the 1980s. It grew from older systems of commercial smuggling, rural cultivation, local political protection, and relationships with police and officials. Second, American demand and changing prohibition policies transformed the scale and profitability of these older arrangements, and enforcement campaigns such as Intercept and Condor changed their geography without ending them. Third, the connection with Colombian cocaine in the 1980s gave Mexican organizations unprecedented capital and reach. Fourth, the Camarena crisis and the arrests of 1985 and 1989 broke the Guadalajara-centered cooperation, but fragmentation did not eliminate the trade: it contributed to the rise of regional organizations. Fifth, there is no documentary basis for a single genealogy that traces all modern groups to one organization or one meeting.

The decline of the Guadalajara-centered networks did not create Mexican organized crime; it changed the organization and distribution of criminal power within a much older illicit economy. Whether the regional organizations of the 1990s would compete violently, cooperate, or be dismantled was not determined by the events of the 1980s. That outcome depended on later choices by traffickers and officials, on changes in Mexican politics, and on international markets. Chapter 102 takes up these questions: How did the Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and Gulf organizations structure themselves and relate to one another? How did their rivalries become wars? What was the origin of Los Zetas? And how did the end of one-party rule alter the relationships among traffickers, officials, and the military?

Border residents: Communities that depended on cross-border commerce experienced Intercept and later inspections as economic disruption and as an affront to their ordinary lives.
Police investigators and agents: Some Mexican and American investigators pursued traffickers at personal risk; others accepted payments or obstructed. Within one agency, perspectives and conduct differed.
Journalists: Reporters who investigated traffickers and officials, sometimes at risk of death, supplied much of the early documentation, but their sources were often officials or informants with their own interests.
Victims’ families: The relatives of Camarena and Zavala endured weeks of uncertainty and loss; the families of rural people killed or displaced by campaigns seldom received comparable public attention.
Regional politicians and government officials: Some profited from protection, some opposed traffickers, and some used antidrug campaigns to meet American demands; their calculations varied by place and period.
Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Modern Mexican Trafficking Organizations Emerged

Long-term causes

Prohibition laws of 1917–1920; rural poverty and isolated terrain; border smuggling traditions; the PRI system’s local discretion; growing American demand from the 1960s.

Short-term causes

Condor’s disruption of the sierra; the concentration of traffickers in Guadalajara; Colombian cocaine seeking new routes; enforcement escalation in 1984–1985.

Event or transformation

The Camarena crisis and the arrests of 1985 and 1989 broke the Guadalajara-centered cooperation and rearranged relationships among traffickers.

Immediate effects

Loss of key coordinators; disruption of protection arrangements; renegotiated alliances; growing prominence of regional organizations.

Long-term consequences

Competing regional organizations in Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and the Gulf region, with rivalries examined in Chapter 102.

Historical complexity

No single cause or actor produced the outcome. Enforcement changed the trade’s organization but did not end it, and fragmentation was contingent, not planned.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Mexican Criminal Networks and State Institutions, 1930s–1993

What Changed

  • Scale: from small family networks to organizations handling large volumes and sums of money
  • Commodities: from marijuana and heroin to transit of Colombian cocaine
  • Geography: from rural production and border towns to an urban hub, then to regional organizations
  • Enforcement: from episodic local campaigns to military deployment and bilateral investigation
  • Institutions: from the DFS-era security system to a reorganized security apparatus

What Stayed the Same

  • American demand and the profit created by prohibition
  • Reliance on political and police protection
  • The importance of family and regional ties
  • The vulnerability of rural growers to eradication and abuse
  • Recurring cycles of American pressure and Mexican response

Who experienced change most directly: Traffickers and officials near the Camarena case, and rural families displaced by Condor. Greater continuity: Peasant growers, whose economic position changed little, and border residents. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the 1980s crisis rearranged an existing illicit economy rather than creating one, which is the foundation for understanding Chapter 102.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that modern Mexican drug trafficking did not begin with the Guadalajara-centered networks of the 1980s. It grew from an older illicit economy built on opium and marijuana cultivation in the northwest, border smuggling, local political protection, and relationships between traffickers, police, and officials. American demand and changing prohibition policies transformed the scale and profitability of that economy, and enforcement campaigns altered its geography without ending it.

Prohibition was the starting point. Between 1910 and 1930 Mexican public-health reformers, the Constitution of 1917, international agreements, and the 1920 national restrictions turned opium, cocaine, and marijuana from tolerated or unregulated commodities into prohibited ones. The Mexican origins of this process, documented by Isaac Campos for marijuana, show that prohibition was not simply imported. The 1940 regulation under Salazar Viniegra shows that alternatives existed and that American pressure helped close them.

The rural economy of Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua rested on poor farmers who grew poppy and marijuana because they paid better than staples. They were at the bottom of a commodity chain, vulnerable to eradication and extortion, and should not be equated with organized criminals. Above them, intermediaries and regional strongmen accumulated capital and protection. On the border, smuggling traditions in Tijuana, Ciudad Juárez, and Matamoros produced entrepreneurs, including women such as La Nacha Jasso, and in Tamaulipas a tradition that developed largely independently of Sinaloa.

The PRI-era state shaped the form of protection. Local officials, police, army officers, and agents of federal agencies including the DFS enjoyed wide discretion, and many extracted payments or selectively enforced the law. Historians disagree about whether this amounted to state management of the trade, as Astorga’s work suggested, or to a fragmented competition among many extractors, as Smith and Pansters emphasize. The chapter urged attention to specific actors and periods rather than sweeping claims about “the state.”

American demand rose from the 1960s as marijuana use spread and Mexican heroin filled the gap left by disruptions to the French Connection. Operation Intercept in September 1969 turned drug policy into a question of sovereignty and worked mainly as diplomatic leverage. The Mexican military campaign known as Operation Condor, formally beginning with a task force on 16 January 1977, destroyed crops, displaced communities, and produced abuses documented by Mexican historians, while the GAO later concluded that eradication’s early gains were eroded as farmers adapted.

Eradication, economic opportunity, family ties, and political relationships contributed to the concentration of several Sinaloan traffickers in Guadalajara in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Whether these men formed a single “Guadalajara Cartel” under Félix Gallardo, as conventional and some institutional accounts hold, or a flexible network that later observers labeled a cartel, as Pérez Ricart and Pannell argue, remains debated. The chapter used “Guadalajara-centered networks” as a descriptive term and stressed that Félix Gallardo, Caro Quintero, and Fonseca Carrillo had different backgrounds, roles, and legal histories.

The cocaine connection transformed the stakes. As enforcement in the Caribbean made older routes riskier, Colombian organizations turned to Mexican partners, who initially served as paid transporters. The relationship gave Mexican organizations vast new capital and tied the Mexican illicit economy to the hemisphere’s most profitable commodity. The DFS, a political-security agency with a record of repression, became entangled in allegations of protection, and its dissolution on 29 November 1985 did not end corruption.

The raid on El Búfalo in November 1984, the abduction of Camarena and Zavala on 7 February 1985, and the discovery of their bodies on 5 March created the era’s great enforcement crisis. Operation Leyenda, Mexican investigations, and diplomatic confrontation followed, including the later controversy over the abduction of Humberto Álvarez Machaín. The chapter insisted on classifying claims as documented, judicially established, alleged, or unresolved, and on remembering both victims and the institutional lessons of the case.

The arrests of Caro Quintero and Fonseca Carrillo in April 1985 and of Félix Gallardo on 8 April 1989 weakened Guadalajara-centered cooperation, as did the disruption of protection arrangements and rising rivalries. The popular story that Félix Gallardo divided Mexico among successors in a single meeting is poorly documented and should be treated as a retrospective claim, not an established fact.

The Sinaloa, Tijuana, and Juárez organizations emerged through different combinations of continuity with older networks and opportunities created by fragmentation. The Gulf organization, rooted in northeastern smuggling traditions and the Guerra and García Ábrego lineage, had a distinct history. The chapter therefore rejected the genealogy that runs from a Guadalajara cartel to one national division to every modern group, in favor of several independent or overlapping lines of development.

Quantitative evidence, including Mexican eradication and arrest figures, U.S. survey data, and State Department and GAO reporting, supports cautious conclusions. Hectares destroyed, arrests, and seizures measure enforcement activity, not the size of the trade or the success of policy. Survey data show cocaine use peaking around 1985 and falling by 1990, a pattern that cannot by itself determine Mexico’s share of trafficking.

The central conclusion is that the decline of the Guadalajara-centered networks did not create Mexican organized crime. It changed the organization and distribution of criminal power within a much older illicit economy. Whether the regional organizations of the early 1990s would fight, cooperate, or fragment was not determined by 1993, and that question opens Chapter 102.

Key Terms

Prohibition
Legal ban on the production, sale, or possession of a commodity; in this chapter, both U.S. alcohol Prohibition (1920–1933) and the legal restriction of narcotics.
Smuggling
Unlawful movement of goods across a border, whether to evade tariffs or because the goods are banned.
Trafficking organization
A group coordinating several stages of an illicit trade, ranging from family firms to loose alliances.
Consejo de Salubridad General
Federal health authority empowered by the 1917 Constitution to act against alcoholism and harmful substances.
Golden Triangle (Triángulo Dorado)
Mountain zone where Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua meet, associated with poppy and marijuana cultivation; a geographic label, not a single criminal territory.
Gomero
Worker who scores opium poppy pods to collect latex (goma).
Plaza
A trafficking route or locality, typically a border crossing or city, whose use is negotiated with officials and rivals.
Sinsemilla
Seedless, higher-potency marijuana, associated with the more commercial production of the 1970s and 1980s.
PRI system
The political order in which one party governed Mexico from the 1920s to 2000, with wide local discretion in exchange for loyalty.
Political protection
Arrangements by which officials shield actors from enforcement, or direct enforcement at rivals, in exchange for payment or loyalty.
Corruption
Use of public office for private gain, from petty bribes to systematic extortion.
Impunity
Condition in which wrongdoing goes unpunished, through protection, weak institutions, or intimidation.
Selective enforcement
Uneven application of the law that targets some actors while tolerating others.
Federal Regulation on Toxicomanías (1940)
Mexican regulation treating addiction medically; suspended within months under U.S. pressure.
Operation Intercept
U.S. border inspection campaign beginning 21 September 1969; functioned largely as diplomatic leverage.
Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA)
U.S. agency created in 1973 to consolidate federal drug enforcement.
Operation Condor (Mexico)
Mexican military eradication campaign in the northwest, formally deployed from 16 January 1977; unrelated to the South American repression network of the same name.
Eradication
Destruction of illicit crops by burning, manual removal, or herbicides.
Interdiction
Interception of drugs in transit, as through seizures at borders, ports, or in the air.
Paraquat
Herbicide used on Mexican marijuana whose use provoked a 1978 U.S. controversy.
Sovereignty
A state’s supreme authority within its territory; central to Mexican objections to U.S. enforcement measures.
Transit country
A country through which drugs pass between producers and consumers.
Guadalajara-centered networks
This chapter’s descriptive term for traffickers concentrated in Guadalajara in the late 1970s and 1980s, avoiding the assumption of a single cartel.
Cartel
Economic term for producers fixing prices; applied to criminal groups to imply centralized control, an implication disputed for the 1980s.
Criminal network
A set of relationships among actors in illicit activity with varying degrees of hierarchy.
Retrospective label
A name applied to past events by later observers, which may impose features the original actors did not have.
Fragmentation
Breakup of a larger structure of relationships into smaller units after arrests, deaths, or disputes.
Dirección Federal de Seguridad (DFS)
Mexican political-security agency, 1947 to 29 November 1985, implicated in repression and in allegations of protecting traffickers.
Dirty war
Mexican state counterinsurgency of the 1960s and 1970s, involving detention, torture, and disappearance of suspected leftists.
El Búfalo
Large marijuana site in Chihuahua raided in November 1984.
Operation Leyenda
DEA investigation, formally established in May 1985, into the Camarena murder.
Indictment
Formal accusation of a crime; it is an allegation, not proof of guilt.
Judicial finding
A fact established by a court’s final ruling, distinct from allegation or testimony.
Álvarez Machaín case
1990–1992 dispute over the abduction of a Mexican physician to face trial in the Camarena case; the U.S. Supreme Court upheld jurisdiction, and he was later acquitted.
Money laundering
Concealing the illicit origin of money by investing it in legitimate assets and businesses.
Certification
U.S. annual determination of foreign countries’ cooperation in drug control, a source of bilateral friction.
Gulf organization
Trafficking group rooted in Tamaulipas smuggling traditions, associated with the Guerra and García Ábrego lineage.
Archivo General de la Nación (AGN)
Mexico’s national archive, custodian of DFS records and antidrug campaign documents.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that organized crime is a historical product, shaped by laws, markets, institutions, and choices, not a force that appears fully formed. Prohibition created illegal markets; poverty and geography made illicit crops attractive; American demand made them profitable; and a political system built on local discretion made protection a commodity. Seen this way, the story is not primarily about notorious individuals but about the conditions that made their careers possible.

It also shows the costs of imprecise history. Labels such as “cartel,” stories of a single meeting that divided a nation, and sweeping claims about “the state” make a complicated record easier to tell but harder to understand. Learning to ask who produced a source, for what purpose, and what it cannot show is a method that students will need again in Chapter 102 and across Part XXIII.

The chapter also leaves lasting legacies. The pattern of American pressure followed by Mexican response, the militarization of drug control and its costs to rural communities, the disputes over sovereignty, and the entanglement of security institutions with illicit markets all recur in later decades. They should not be read as proof that later events were inevitable, but they explain why certain choices seemed available and others did not.

Chapter 102 takes up the organizations that emerged from this history. It asks how the Sinaloa, Tijuana, Juárez, and Gulf organizations structured themselves, how their rivalries became wars, and how a new armed group, Los Zetas, changed the character of Mexican organized crime. Students should carry forward the view that these organizations inherited an older illicit economy, and that the events of 1985 to 1993 rearranged criminal power rather than inventing it.
