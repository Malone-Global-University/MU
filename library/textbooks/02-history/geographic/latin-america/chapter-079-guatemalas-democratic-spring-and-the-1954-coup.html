Guatemala's Democratic Spring and the 1954 Coup

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Government That Surrendered Power

On 27 June 1954 the elected president of Guatemala went on the radio and announced that he was giving up office. Nine days earlier a small force had crossed the border from Honduras. It never came close to taking the capital. The government fell because its own army would not fight for it, because aircraft and clandestine broadcasts had spread fear and confusion, and because the most powerful government in the hemisphere had decided that it should go.

Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán had been inaugurated in March 1951 under a constitution written after the revolution of 1944. He had won the presidency in an election against a field of opponents, and his government had enacted a land reform that reached hundreds of thousands of rural Guatemalans. Yet by the evening of 27 June he was transferring authority to the head of the armed forces, Colonel Carlos Enrique Díaz, while aircraft attacks, rumors of advancing columns, and the unwillingness of senior officers to resist had hollowed out his position. Within days the colonel who received the presidency from him had himself been removed, and within weeks a Guatemalan officer trained and financed by the Central Intelligence Agency was heading the government. This chapter asks how a constitutional government reached that point.

The answer has two halves, and a student who learns only one will misunderstand the event. The first half is the history of Guatemala itself. For a decade after the fall of the dictator Jorge Ubico in 1944, Guatemalans built a new political order: a democratic constitution, a labor code, a social-security institute, expanded education, and, after 1952, a land reform. Teachers, students, urban workers, rural laborers, Maya communities, army officers, employers, landowners, and party organizers all shaped it, and they quarreled with one another. The second half is the history of the Cold War. The government of the United States, convinced that Guatemala was becoming a Soviet outpost, organized a covert operation, code-named PBSUCCESS, to remove Árbenz. The declassified record leaves no doubt that this happened. What requires careful analysis is how the intervention worked on divisions that already existed inside Guatemalan society and its army.

This chapter therefore refuses two simplifications. One treats the Guatemalan Revolution as a puppet show in which the CIA and the United Fruit Company pulled every string, and in which Guatemalans appear only as victims. The other treats the events of 1954 as an internal Guatemalan affair in which the United States was a minor bystander. Both are wrong. The intervention was real, organized, and decisive in the sense that no comparable overthrow occurred without it. But it succeeded in part because many Guatemalan landowners, merchants, Church leaders, and officers had their own reasons to want the reform process ended, and because the reformers had not secured the loyalty of the institution that held the guns.

The chapter is organized around four connected developments: democratization, labor reform, agrarian transformation, and counterrevolution. Division I (sections 79.1 to 79.4) traces the Ubico dictatorship, the protest movement of June 1944, the military uprising of October 1944, and the Constitution of 1945. Division II (79.5 to 79.8) follows the presidency of Juan José Arévalo. Division III (79.9 to 79.12) covers Árbenz, Decree 900, and the confrontation over land and foreign-owned property. Division IV (79.13 to 79.16) examines communism, regional diplomacy, and the preparation of the coup. Division V (79.17 to 79.20) narrates June 1954 and its aftermath and asks how historians have interpreted it.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually contested. Guatemalan sources include the constitution, the Labor Code, Decree 900, congressional and agrarian-agency records, newspapers, union publications, and the testimony of participants. The United States has declassified a large body of diplomatic and intelligence material, including the Department of State's retrospective Foreign Relations volume on Guatemala and CIA records released through the National Archives and the National Security Archive. Each kind of source has limits. Diplomatic cables record what U.S. officials believed and wanted to be believed. CIA files record what the agency planned and chose to preserve. Árbenz's own speeches were political acts. Maya and campesino voices appear mostly in petitions, in the records of agrarian committees, and in later oral testimony, which means that the people most affected by the reform are often seen through documents written for or about them.

The chapter has firm boundaries. It does not narrate the Guatemalan civil war of the 1960s to 1996, which Chapter 91 treats, nor the Cuban Revolution, which follows in Chapter 80. It carries the story only as far as the consolidation of the Castillo Armas government in 1954 to 1957, enough to show what was dismantled. Its comparison with earlier chapters is selective: Chapter 75 explained the regional crisis of export economies, Chapter 77 the Peronist construction of a labor-based politics, and Chapter 78 the Bolivian revolution of 1952 with its own agrarian reform and Indigenous campesino mobilization. Guatemala belongs in that company, and its ending is what sets it apart.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the structure of landholding, labor coercion, and foreign capital under the Ubico dictatorship, and distinguish how these affected Maya communities, Ladino workers, landowners, and commercial elites.
  2. Distinguish the removal of Ubico in July 1944 from the revolutionary uprising of 20 October 1944, and explain why the first did not create democracy.
  3. Analyze the Constitution of 1945 as an expansion of political participation that nevertheless retained unequal voting conditions.
  4. Explain Arévalo's doctrine of spiritual socialism and the aims, enforcement, and limits of the Labor Code of 1947.
  5. Evaluate the reach of social security, education, and public health policy, especially for women and for Indigenous and rural communities.
  6. Assess the death of Francisco Javier Arana in 1949, distinguishing established events from competing accusations.
  7. Compare the political approaches and economic priorities of Arévalo and Árbenz.
  8. Explain the legal provisions of Decree 900 and analyze how campesino and Maya organizing shaped its implementation.
  9. Distinguish corporate interest, property-law dispute, economic nationalism, and Cold War security policy in the confrontation with United Fruit.
  10. Assess the real but limited influence of Guatemalan communists and the divergent ways Guatemalans interpreted the reforms.
  11. Trace the evolution from PBFORTUNE to PBSUCCESS and the combined military, psychological, and diplomatic operation against Árbenz.
  12. Interpret competing explanations of the 1954 overthrow and the sources through which historians reconstruct it.

Historical Context

Guatemala in the 1930s was a small, overwhelmingly rural country with a majority population of Maya ancestry, speaking more than twenty distinct languages, and a minority classed as Ladino, a category examined in the vocabulary box in section 79.1. Its export economy rested on coffee, introduced as the main crop by liberal governments after 1871, and on bananas. The liberal reforms of the late nineteenth century, described in Chapter 68 and Chapter 69, had transferred land from Indigenous communities and from Church holdings to coffee planters and had created legal systems that compelled Maya men to labor on plantations. Foreign investors, among them German coffee planters in Alta Verapaz and United States firms in transport, electricity, and bananas, were woven into the structure of the state.

The depression of the 1930s, the subject of Chapter 75, struck a country whose revenue depended on coffee. Many Latin American states responded with industrialization, labor legislation, and populist politics. Guatemala responded differently. General Jorge Ubico, who took office in 1931, answered the crisis with austerity, tight fiscal control, public works built on coerced labor, and a policing system that reached into the countryside. He abolished the older system of debt-bondage and replaced it with laws requiring landless and land-poor men to work on plantations or on roads. Where Vargas in Brazil built corporatist institutions and Perón later built a mass movement, Ubico built a surveillance state.

The political context changed in 1941 to 1945, when the Second World War made democracy a stated purpose of the Allied cause. Guatemala joined the war against the Axis, and Ubico, who admired European authoritarians, was nevertheless the ally of the United States. Educated Guatemalans, including students at the national university and teachers in the capital, read Allied statements about freedom and noted the contradiction between those statements and the regime that was Washington's partner. Revolutionary and democratic currents elsewhere in Latin America, including the fall of dictators in Central America in 1944, influenced them. The student movement and the movement of teachers described in section 79.2 drew on this atmosphere while acting out of Guatemalan grievances.

After 1944, Guatemala became part of a global rivalry that was just taking shape. The Cold War, whose Latin American course Chapter 80 and later chapters trace, had barely begun when the revolution started. By 1950 it had hardened. Anti-communism hardened in Washington, and the Korean War raised the stakes. Guatemalan reformers who had begun by looking to Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal found themselves classified as suspect. Meanwhile the United States was a large buyer of Guatemalan coffee and bananas, and the United Fruit Company, headquartered in Boston, controlled the banana trade, the principal railway, and the main Caribbean port. Students should keep two timelines in view throughout the chapter: Guatemala's internal sequence of reforms and conflicts, and the evolving international sequence of alarm, planning, and intervention. They intersect in 1952 to 1954, but they do not begin together.

The Ubico Dictatorship, Landholding Inequality, and the Structure of Guatemalan Society

Jorge Ubico, a general who had served as a provincial governor, won an uncontested election in 1930 and took office in February 1931. He governed for thirteen years. His regime is often described through his personality: a self-styled modernizer who admired Napoleon, toured the country on a motorcycle, dismissed ministers by telephone, and demanded personal loyalty. But the regime is better understood through its institutions. Ubico concentrated authority in the presidency, replaced elected local officials with appointed intendants, built a national police and informant network, and kept the army under his personal control. Congress, the courts, and the press existed, but only within limits the president set. He balanced the budget during the depression by cutting spending and public salaries, and Guatemala's creditors regarded him as a model of fiscal discipline.

The economic order that Ubico protected rested on land and labor. Land ownership in Guatemala was among the most concentrated in Latin America. The agricultural census of 1950, the first modern national count, showed that a small fraction of owners, on the order of two percent, held roughly seventy percent of farmland, while the great majority of holdings were smaller than a few hectares and could not support a household. These figures come from a census taken near the end of the period, and scholars treat them as approximations, but the pattern is not in doubt. Coffee estates (fincas) dominated the Pacific piedmont and the northern highlands of Alta Verapaz, and the banana lands along both coasts belonged largely to a single foreign company. Much plantation land lay idle, held for prestige, speculation, or future expansion, a fact that later gave the agrarian reform its legal foothold.

Labor was the other half of the system. Coffee estates needed large numbers of workers during the harvest, and the highland Maya communities, whose own plots were small and often insufficient, supplied them. Liberal governments since the 1870s had used debt-bondage and forced-labor drafts to secure that supply. In the 1930s Ubico formally abolished the older debt system but replaced it with regulations that achieved similar ends. A vagrancy law required men who held little or no land to work a stipulated number of days on plantations each year, with figures commonly cited in the range of one hundred to one hundred fifty days depending on their holdings, and to carry a booklet recording their labor. A road law required men to give unpaid labor on public works each year or pay a fee, a burden that fell hardest on the poor. Local officials, police, and landowners enforced these rules, and scholars have noted a decree that gave landowners wide immunity for violence committed in defense of property. Those who fell behind could be punished, jailed, or beaten.

Guatemalan society was divided by class, region, and ethnicity, and these divisions intersected without matching exactly. Maya communities, who made up a majority of the population, lived mainly in the western and central highlands and in Alta Verapaz and spoke languages including K'iche', Mam, Kaqchikel, and Q'eqchi'. Their experience of the regime depended on whether their municipality retained communal land, how close it was to plantations, and what labor demands were placed on it. Ladinos, a category defined by language, dress, and social position rather than strict ancestry, included small farmers, artisans, shopkeepers, clerks, schoolteachers, soldiers, and the owners of large estates. Urban workers in the capital, in railway shops, printing, and light manufacturing, were few and closely watched. Landowners were not unified either: the coffee planters of German origin in Alta Verapaz, who lost property when Guatemala entered the Second World War and seized Axis holdings, had interests different from those of Guatemalan-born planters or of capital-city merchants.

Foreign capital completed the picture. The United Fruit Company, whose operations the chapter takes up fully in section 79.12, had arrived at the turn of the century. By the Ubico years it owned or controlled the main railway linking the capital to the Caribbean coast, the port of Puerto Barrios, and large banana plantations in Izabal, and in the 1930s it obtained favorable contracts to develop land on the Pacific side near Tiquisate. Historians such as Paul Dosal have shown how the company's growth depended on arrangements with Ubico's government. Electricity, the telephone system, and other services were also under U.S. ownership. The state's revenue, its exports, and its transport therefore depended on a small number of foreign firms and planters, a structure the revolutionaries of 1944 would call dependent and feudal and their opponents would call orderly.

Historical Vocabulary
Ladino

Ladino is the term used in Guatemala for the non-Indigenous or culturally non-Maya part of the population, including people of mixed descent as well as Indigenous people who adopted Spanish language and dress. Its meaning has shifted over time, and it overlaps with, but is not identical to, the colonial category mestizo.

The boundary between Maya and Ladino was social as much as biological, and individuals and families could cross it. The category matters in this chapter because the revolution's rhetoric of national unity often assumed that Maya peoples would become Ladino, and because Ladinos filled most positions in local government, schools, and the agrarian bureaucracy. Students should not read it as a fixed racial type.

Section 79.2

Students, Teachers, Urban Opposition, and the Overthrow of Ubico in 1944

Ubico's regime appeared stable until 1944. Opposition existed, but it was scattered, and the dictator's police had crushed earlier conspiracies. Several developments combined in the early 1940s to change the conditions. The war brought an economic squeeze: imports became scarce, prices rose, and wages stagnated. Allied propaganda spoke of freedom and democracy, and Guatemalans who read it drew the obvious conclusion about their own government. An educated urban middle class, including lawyers, teachers, university students, journalists, and small businessmen, had grown beyond what a dictatorship that fired and jailed dissent could comfortably contain. Their grievances were political more than economic: the absence of free expression, the control of the university by presidential appointees, and the arbitrary power of the police.

The spark came from outside. In May 1944 a popular movement, including a general strike, forced the dictator of neighboring El Salvador, Maximiliano Hernández Martínez, from power. The news electrified Guatemalan activists, who saw that a regime resting on force could fall to civilian pressure. In June, students at the national university, especially in the law faculty, began to organize demonstrations and strikes. They were joined by teachers, whose profession in Guatemala was poorly paid and heavily policed, and by professional associations. Ubico responded by suspending constitutional guarantees and declaring martial law, but the movement spread. A petition signed by hundreds of respected citizens, often called the Memorial of the 311 and reproduced in the Primary Source Window below, demanded that the government end the state of siege and restore basic rights.

The decisive confrontation took place on 25 June 1944. A large, orderly march in the capital was met by cavalry and gunfire. Among those killed was María Chinchilla Recinos, a schoolteacher, whose death became the movement's emblem and gave the fall of the regime a woman martyr whom later governments honored. The killing did not terrify the capital as the regime hoped. Businesses closed, professionals stayed home, and the country's elites, including many who had tolerated the dictatorship for years, withdrew their support. Ubico found that the army, though loyal, could not run the country against a general stoppage. On 1 July 1944 he resigned and handed authority to a triumvirate of generals.

The July resignation was not the end of the old order. Within days the National Assembly, which Ubico's supporters controlled, named General Federico Ponce Vaides provisional president. Ponce had been one of the triumvirate and was a loyal Ubico associate. He promised elections but sought to manage them so that he or an ally would win, kept the police and army in the hands of Ubico's officers, and allowed harassment of students, journalists, and opposition organizers. Political parties began to form, notably among teachers, university graduates, and exiles returning from abroad, but they operated under threat. Students and workers also began to organize trade unions in defiance of the law. The political opening of July, in other words, was real but fragile, and it might have been closed if Ponce had retained the army's loyalty. That is why historians distinguish the protest movement of June to July from the uprising of October.

Understanding the origins of the movement matters for the larger argument of the chapter. The democratic opening began as a domestic Guatemalan mobilization, led by an urban middle class and joined by workers, and it did not originate from foreign intervention. It also had definite social limits. The leaders of June 1944 were largely Ladino professionals from the capital, and rural Maya communities, though they had the most to gain from the end of forced labor, were not the movement's organizers. Their entry into politics would come later, through unions and the agrarian reform.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window 1: The June 1944 Petition and the Protest Movement

Creators: Guatemalan lawyers, professionals, teachers, students, and other citizens · Date: June 1944 · Document type: Collective petition and contemporary protest statements; later reproduced in press and in historical collections · Place: Guatemala City

Context: The petition was presented after Ubico had declared martial law and suspended constitutional guarantees. Its signatories were drawn from the educated urban public rather than from the plantations or Maya communities. Because the exact wording circulates in several versions, this window gives a paraphrase.

Editorial paraphrase of the petition's central demands: the signatories asked the president to lift the state of siege, to restore the constitutional guarantees on speech, assembly, and personal security, and to allow citizens to express their views without fear of imprisonment. The tone is respectful, even deferential, and it frames the demand as loyal advice to a head of state rather than as a call for his removal.

Analysis: The petition shows how modest the movement's initial demands were. Its authors asked for legal protections, not revolution, and the regime's violent reaction is what turned a petition into a political crisis. The limits are also clear. The signatories were an educated minority, the petition says nothing about land or forced labor, and the surviving texts were selected and republished by later participants who gave the movement a coherence it may not have had at the time. Missing are the voices of rural workers, Maya communities, and the police and soldiers who enforced martial law.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the petition ask for, and what does it leave out?
  2. Who created it, and why might they have chosen a respectful tone?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the social base of the June movement?
  4. What can historians not establish from the petition alone about how Ubico's resignation came about?
Section 79.3

The Revolution of October 20, 1944, and the Revolutionary Government Junta

By the autumn of 1944 the Ponce government faced a growing coalition. Opposition parties, student groups, and trade-union organizers were pressing for free elections, and Ponce's attempts to rig them were visible. Inside the army a conspiracy took shape among younger officers, many of them graduates of the Escuela Politécnica, the military academy, who resented the promotion of Ubico's old guard and were exposed to the democratic language of the war. They sought out civilian allies. The principal military figures were Major Francisco Javier Arana, a commander in the capital, and Captain Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán, a young instructor at the academy. The civilian link was Jorge Toriello Garrido, a businessman who had financed and organized the opposition. Without such a link between officers and civilians, the revolt might have remained an ordinary barracks coup.

The uprising began on 19 October and became decisive on 20 October 1944. Troops loyal to the conspirators seized key positions, civilians, including students and workers, took up arms distributed from captured stocks, and fighting centered on the fortress of San José and on barracks in the capital. The fighting lasted about a day, with significant loss of life, and ended when Ponce's forces surrendered or collapsed. Ponce himself sought refuge abroad. Casualty figures vary in the sources and are not firmly established. The military uprising and popular participation were intertwined, and later political memory emphasized one or the other depending on who was telling the story.

The revolution created a three-man Revolutionary Government Junta consisting of Arana, Árbenz, and Toriello. Official presidential chronologies, including that of the Historical Clarification Commission, place the junta in office from 20 October 1944 to 15 March 1945. Its composition was meaningful: two army officers who would later be rivals, and one civilian whose presence signaled that the revolution was not intended as a barracks regime. The junta moved quickly. It restored constitutional guarantees, suspended the Ubico-era assembly and constitution, ended press censorship, and promised free elections for a president and a constituent assembly. It began to dismantle some of the coercive labor system, including measures against the vagrancy regulations, and it granted autonomy to the national university, one of the students' principal demands. It also organized the elections that would turn a revolutionary junta into a constitutional government.

The elections of December 1944 were the first open contests in the country's modern history. Women, workers, and many rural people were involved for the first time in campaign activity, though the franchise remained limited, as the next section explains. Juan José Arévalo, an educator who had spent years in exile in Argentina and returned in the autumn of 1944, won an overwhelming majority, in the neighborhood of eighty-five percent of the vote according to the common accounts. His victory showed the breadth of support for the revolution, and it also reflected the weakness of any organized opposition: the old regime's supporters were discredited, and the parties that backed him included teachers' organizations and a range of reformist groups rather than a single disciplined party.

Students should keep the sequence clear. The June protests removed Ubico but left the old military establishment under Ponce. The October uprising overthrew that establishment and began the revolutionary government. These were distinct events with different participants. The first was predominantly civilian, urban, and middle-class; the second was a joint operation of younger officers and civilians that depended on weapons and fighting. Treating them as one "1944 revolution" erases the fact that democracy in Guatemala was not won by a single stroke and that the army's role in October, which brought Arana and Árbenz to prominence, would shape the political history of the next decade.

Section 79.4

The Constitution of 1945, Electoral Reform, and the Foundations of Democratic Government

The Constituent Assembly elected in December 1944 approved a new constitution on 11 March 1945, four days before Arévalo's inauguration. Its drafters were lawyers, teachers, and professionals, many of them veterans of the 1944 movement, and they drew on constitutional models from across the Americas, including the Mexican Constitution of 1917, described in Chapter 73, with its social rights, as well as liberal traditions of separation of powers. The result was a document that restored civil liberties, limited presidential power, and committed the state to social responsibility.

Its provisions on rights were broad. It guaranteed freedom of the press, assembly, and association, restored the writ of protection, and recognized the right of workers to organize. It established the principle that private property has a social function and may be limited or expropriated for social interest with compensation, a clause that would later provide a constitutional foundation for the agrarian reform. It confirmed the autonomy of the national university and of municipalities. It created a political system with a president elected for a fixed single term and a unicameral congress, weakening the executive that Ubico had embodied. It also created the post of chief of the armed forces, giving the army a distinct institutional voice, a provision whose consequences appear in section 79.8.

The constitution's treatment of voting shows both its ambition and its limits. The franchise was broader than ever before, but it was not universal or equal. Literate men were required to vote, and their votes were secret. Illiterate men could vote too, but through a public procedure that placed them under the eyes of local authorities and employers. Women were granted political citizenship, but only literate women could vote, and for them voting was optional rather than required. Illiterate women, who in Guatemala meant a very large proportion of Maya women and of rural women generally, were excluded. Active-duty soldiers were barred from voting. Because literacy rates were low, especially in rural and Indigenous areas, these rules meant that a large share of the adult population remained outside or on the margins of electoral politics. The constitution also contained a provision barring political organizations of an international or foreign character, which was widely understood as directed against communist parties, and which shows that anti-communism was written into the democratic framework from the beginning.

How did these arrangements compare with Ubico's? The contrast is real. Under Ubico, elections had been plebiscites with a single candidate, constitutional guarantees were suspended at will, unions were illegal, the press was censored, and local officials were appointed from the capital. Under the 1945 constitution there were contested elections, parties and unions competed, newspapers criticized the government, and municipalities could elect their officials. Historians who study this period, including Piero Gleijeses and Jim Handy, emphasize that these changes transformed political life even if they did not create universal suffrage. A rural Maya community that had been ruled by an appointed intendant could now elect a mayor, even if many of its members could not read the ballot.

The constitution therefore established what later reformers sought to use and defend. It gave Arévalo and Árbenz a legal framework within which to pass labor and agrarian laws, and it supplied the language of rights and social responsibility that Guatemalans invoked in petitions and speeches. It did not by itself resolve the power of landowners or of the army, and its limited franchise meant that the people most affected by the later land reform had little electoral weight. That tension, between a democratic form and a society where political participation was unequal, runs through the next decade.

Division II — Juan José Arévalo and the Democratic Reform Government · Section 79.5

Juan José Arévalo, Spiritual Socialism, and the First Democratic Presidency

Juan José Arévalo, born in 1904, was a teacher by training who had studied in Guatemala and then earned a doctorate in philosophy and education in Argentina, where he lived for roughly fifteen years and worked in universities and ministries. He was thus an intellectual whose formation owed more to Latin American educational thought and to Argentine and European philosophy than to party politics. When Ubico fell, he returned and was drawn into a campaign by teachers and reformers who needed a candidate without ties to the old order and without a military record. He won the election of December 1944 and was inaugurated on 15 March 1945, the first president in Guatemala's history to take office after a free and contested election.

His governing philosophy was socialismo espiritual, spiritual socialism, described in the vocabulary box below. It was less an economic doctrine than an ethical one. Arévalo argued that the central problem of Guatemala was the humiliation of the individual under dictatorship, and that political freedom, education, and social welfare were required to restore human dignity. He insisted that his program was distinct from Marxism, which he criticized as materialist, and from the capitalism of the plantation owners. The state's role, in his view, was to protect individuals from exploitation and to expand opportunity, not to abolish property. This put him closer to the reformist traditions of Mexico, which Chapter 73 and Chapter 74 described, than to Soviet communism, and it distinguished him from the nationalism of Vargas or Perón, who relied on organized labor as a mobilized base.

Arévalo governed in a difficult political environment. His support came from a shifting coalition of teachers, students, professionals, and union leaders, organized in parties that were personal and fluid. Congress was often fractious, and the army, represented by Arana as chief of the armed forces, held independent power. Landowners and business associations were suspicious of a government that encouraged unions, and the Catholic hierarchy, led by Archbishop Mariano Rossell Arellano, grew increasingly critical as the government's rhetoric of social reform appeared to it as secularizing and radical. The United States, for its part, had initially welcomed the end of Ubico but became uneasy about the labor movement and about the presence of communists among union organizers.

Arévalo's critics at the time, and many later, charged that his language of "spiritual socialism" was vague and that his government tolerated or encouraged communist influence. His defenders replied that he expelled or restricted some communist organizers, that he never nationalized industry or abolished property, and that he governed within the constitution. The truth is that the label hid a pragmatic reformism. He pursued concrete reforms in labor law, social security, and education, but he avoided structural changes to landholding. That restraint is what distinguishes his presidency from his successor's, and it explains why historians treat the two as connected but different.

Historical Vocabulary
Socialismo espiritual (Spiritual socialism)

Spiritual socialism was Arévalo's term for a humanist philosophy of social reform that stressed human dignity, education, political freedom, and the state's duty to reduce inequality, while rejecting class struggle and materialist Marxism.

The phrase matters because opponents seized on the word "socialism" while supporters stressed "spiritual." It is a poor guide to actual policy, which was more pragmatic than the term implies. It should not be confused with Soviet-style communism or with the socialist parties of Europe, and students should treat it as a statement of values rather than a program.

Section 79.6

The Labor Code of 1947, Trade Unions, and the Expansion of Workers' Rights

The central legislative achievement of Arévalo's presidency was the Labor Code. Drafted by a commission of lawyers and labor advisers, it was issued as Legislative Decree 330 on 8 February 1947, published on 20 February, and took effect on 1 May 1947, a date chosen to coincide with International Workers' Day. The code replaced a patchwork of regulations with a comprehensive framework. It recognized the right of workers to form unions, set rules for collective bargaining, limited working hours, regulated overtime and rest, established severance protections, and created labor courts to hear disputes. It recognized the right to strike under defined procedures and required conciliation efforts before strikes could be declared legal.

The reform had precedents. Chapters 76 and 77 described the corporatist labor institutions of Vargas's Brazil and the Peronist labor movement of Argentina. Guatemala's code differed in important ways. It did not create a state-run union system or tie labor to a single party or leader. Instead, it set legal conditions in which unions could form independently, and many did, though the state and political parties sought influence over them. Within a few years unions existed in railway shops, printing, textile mills, the port, and public employment, and by the early 1950s federations such as the Confederación General de Trabajadores de Guatemala claimed memberships in the scores of thousands. Those figures, supplied by the federations themselves, should be treated as claims.

Rural labor was the hardest case. Guatemala was overwhelmingly agrarian, and the code's protections mattered most where they were hardest to enforce. The code and its regulations included provisions that limited where rural unions could form, using thresholds such as the size of the workforce on an estate, and plantation owners resisted organizing on their property through dismissals, blacklists, and the use of local authorities. Banana workers at the United Fruit Company's installations, who were concentrated in large numbers on the Caribbean and Pacific coasts, did organize effective unions and win contracts, and the labor courts sometimes ruled in their favor. On coffee estates and in the highlands the picture was slower and more uneven, and Handy and Cindy Forster have shown how rural workers used the new laws selectively, sometimes aggressively, and often at risk.

The gap between law and practice is a central theme of the period. A right recognized on paper might be unenforceable on a distant estate where the landowner, the local mayor, and the police were allied. Employers' associations, including the agricultural and commercial chambers, denounced the code as an invitation to class conflict and lobbied for amendments. Some reformers shared the frustration from the other side: they thought the code did too little for rural workers, retained burdensome restrictions on strikes, and left the agrarian problem untouched. In 1948 amendments tempered parts of the original, reflecting the balance of political forces. The code also became a lightning rod because communist organizers were active in the labor movement and because U.S. firms, including the United Fruit Company, were among the employers it affected.

The meaning of the code is therefore double. It transformed urban and plantation labor relations in a country where unions had been illegal and workers had no legal recourse. It also taught rural Guatemalans that the state could be used to contest the power of landowners, a lesson that fed directly into the demand for land. Many workers who first used labor courts in the late 1940s became petitioners for land in the early 1950s.

Section 79.7

Social Security, Education, Public Health, Women, and Indigenous Citizenship

The reform governments expanded the state's social role. In October 1946 Congress created the Instituto Guatemalteco de Seguridad Social (IGSS) through Decree 295, dated 30 October 1946, according to the institute's own official history. The IGSS began with a limited program, initially concentrated on workplace accidents and on workers in the capital and a few departments, and expanded over later years to maternity and illness coverage. This gradual scope is typical. The institute was an important institutional innovation and a model for later social-insurance expansion, but in the late 1940s and early 1950s it reached only a small portion of the working population, mostly urban and formal-sector employees. Rural workers, domestic servants, and most Maya households were outside it.

Education was Arévalo's passion. The government expanded school construction, trained more teachers, launched literacy campaigns, and increased the education budget. It made schooling a visible symbol of the revolution. Teachers, who had been prominent in the 1944 movement, became a reliable political constituency and often served as local organizers. But the reach of the schools was uneven. Rural schools were few, teachers sometimes spoke no Mayan language, and instruction was in Spanish. Many Maya children did not attend, either because there was no school nearby or because families needed their labor. Literacy rates rose but remained low in rural and Indigenous communities, and the literacy requirement for voting meant that educational inequality had direct political consequences.

Public-health programs followed a similar pattern. The government built clinics, ran vaccination and sanitation campaigns, and sought to extend basic services, but malaria, intestinal disease, and malnutrition remained widespread, and health facilities were concentrated in towns. Indigenous policy was shaped by a mid-century Latin American approach called indigenismo, which treated Indigenous peoples as a national problem to be solved through education, health, and integration. A national Indigenist Institute was established in the mid-1940s. This approach was often paternalistic. It assumed that Maya identity would give way to a Ladino national culture and did not recognize Maya communities as political actors with their own institutions, though communities used the new rights to elect their own officials and pursue local claims.

Women entered politics in new ways, though within limits. The 1945 constitution granted literate women the vote on an optional basis and excluded illiterate women, a division that reflected class and ethnic inequality as much as gender. Women who had been teachers, students, and professionals joined parties and unions, and by the later 1940s women's organizations, among them the Alianza Femenina Guatemalteca, were campaigning for broader rights and for social policies affecting mothers and children. The labor code included protections for women workers and maternity provisions. Maya women, who worked in households, markets, and plantation harvests, were largely absent from formal politics, though some became active in unions and agrarian committees, especially in the early 1950s.

Students should avoid two errors. One is to treat education and social security as having reached every Guatemalan; they did not. The other is to dismiss them as symbolic. For urban teachers, railway workers, and parents who saw a school built in their village, the changes were concrete. The official promise of national inclusion ran ahead of its practice, and the gap was widest where language, race, geography, and poverty converged. This gap helps explain why Árbenz's later turn to land, rather than to more schools or clinics, answered the demands of rural Guatemalans.

Section 79.8

Political Opposition, Military Conspiracies, and the Death of Francisco Javier Arana

Arévalo's presidency was not calm. Conservative opponents, including landowners, merchants, some Church leaders, and officers loyal to the old order, plotted against him, and the government repeatedly announced the discovery of conspiracies. Arévalo and later writers counted some two dozen or more attempts; such counts are partly rhetorical, since governments define conspiracies broadly, but numerous real plots did occur. The government met them with arrests, exile, and occasionally force, and critics charged that it used the threat of conspiracy to restrict opponents, while supporters replied that a fragile democracy had a right to defend itself.

The reformers were divided as well. The parties that had backed Arévalo fragmented into personalist factions. Unions and political organizations quarreled over leadership, and the labor movement divided between moderates and radicals, with communists increasingly influential among organizers. Landowners and employers formed associations to resist labor demands. And the army, which had played a decisive role in October 1944, was divided by ambition and ideology. Younger officers who admired the reforms, led by Árbenz, who served as minister of defense, clashed with more conservative officers around Arana, who held the powerful post of chief of the armed forces.

Arana was the most formidable rival. A career officer who had been a junta member and who commanded the loyalty of much of the army, he was widely regarded as a likely presidential candidate in 1950. Several accounts describe a political understanding, sometimes called the Pact of the Barranco, reached in 1945, under which Arana would support Arévalo's term in return for the army's backing for Arana's candidacy; its exact terms are disputed. By 1949 Arana was pressing the president to remove Árbenz's allies from the government and from key military posts, and the tension was public.

On 18 July 1949 Arana was killed in a gunfight at a bridge near Amatitlán, southeast of the capital, after he and Arévalo's government had apparently reached the point of confrontation. His death touched off fighting in the capital and elsewhere between his supporters and loyal troops, with significant casualties, and the revolt was put down within a few days. The facts of the shooting are contested. Official accounts said Arana was killed resisting arrest. Some accounts hold that he was lured to the site; others blame various actors. Piero Gleijeses published a detailed examination of the evidence in 1990, concluding that it pointed toward a plot involving Árbenz and his associates, while other scholars and participants have argued for different reconstructions, and the question of who ordered the killing and with what authority remains unresolved by the surviving evidence. Students should note what is established: that Arana was killed on 18 July 1949, that the killing occurred in the context of an open confrontation with the government, and that a short but bloody uprising followed. What remains disputed is individual responsibility and the sequence of decisions.

The consequences were large. Arana's removal eliminated the most powerful conservative figure in the army and cleared the path for Árbenz, who was elected president the next year. It also left a legacy of suspicion. Opponents of the revolution cited the killing as evidence that the reform movement would use violence against rivals, and many officers who had admired Arana grew more hostile. Arévalo nevertheless completed his term and transferred power to his elected successor in March 1951, a feat that few Latin American governments of the era could claim after so many plots. Yet the episode shows why the government remained vulnerable even before the agrarian reform: it governed with an army that was divided, partly sympathetic, and partly waiting.

Division III — Jacobo Árbenz, Agrarian Reform, and the Struggle over Land · Section 79.9

The Election of Jacobo Árbenz and the Program of Economic Modernization

Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán was born in 1913 in Quetzaltenango, the son of a Swiss immigrant pharmacist and a Guatemalan mother, attended the Escuela Politécnica, and rose as an army officer. He was one of the young officers who joined the October 1944 conspiracy and served on the junta, and under Arévalo he was minister of defense. His marriage to María Cristina Vilanova, a Salvadoran from a landowning family who took an active interest in social questions, is often cited as an influence on his thinking, though the extent of that influence is difficult to measure. After Arana's death he was the leading figure in the revolutionary camp.

The presidential election of November 1950 pitted Árbenz against a group of opponents, the most prominent being General Miguel Ydígoras Fuentes, an Ubico-era officer. Árbenz won roughly two-thirds of the votes cast, according to the usual accounts, in a campaign in which he promised to continue the revolution and to transform the economy. He was inaugurated on 15 March 1951, in the first peaceful transfer of power from one elected president to another in modern Guatemalan history. In his inaugural address he stated a goal that became the touchstone of his government: to convert Guatemala from a dependent country with a semi-colonial economy into a modern capitalist one, and to raise the standard of living of the majority.

That goal makes sense of the program. Árbenz's economic plan focused on three infrastructure projects that would reduce Guatemala's reliance on foreign-owned facilities. The first was an Atlantic highway linking the capital to the Caribbean coast, which would compete with the railway controlled by the United Fruit Company. The second was a new Caribbean port, Santo Tomás de Castilla, an alternative to the company-controlled Puerto Barrios. The third was a publicly owned hydroelectric plant at Jurún Marinalá, intended to challenge the near-monopoly of a U.S.-owned electric company. These projects were financed largely from domestic revenue and reflected a development strategy of economic nationalism, in line with the import-substitution thinking described in Chapter 75: build a domestic market, use the state to invest, and reduce dependence on foreign suppliers.

Árbenz's approach differed from Arévalo's in character and emphasis. Arévalo had stressed political freedom, education, and a moral reorientation, and had pursued social legislation without altering the structure of landholding. Árbenz, a soldier and an administrator more than a philosopher, put economic transformation first, and he was ready to use the state's power to confront entrenched property interests. He also relied more openly on advisers from the organized left, including communists, whom Arévalo had kept at greater distance. Yet there were substantial continuities. He operated within the constitution, preserved the Labor Code and social-security institute, and continued to expand schooling. Historians such as Gleijeses emphasize his commitment to capitalist development; his critics in the United States and Guatemala emphasized the company he kept.

The point to hold onto is that agrarian reform was part of a package. Árbenz saw land redistribution as a way of expanding the domestic market by turning landless laborers into smallholders with purchasing power, of increasing agricultural production, and of ending the semi-feudal labor arrangements that kept the countryside poor. The roads, port, and power plant were the infrastructure of the same strategy. To treat Decree 900 as the whole of his policy, or as a purely ideological gesture, misses the economic logic that linked it to the rest.

Section 79.10

Decree 900: The Agrarian Reform Law of June 17, 1952

The need for land reform was not controversial in itself. Almost every observer of Guatemala, including U.S. officials and representatives of international agencies, acknowledged that land was extremely unequally distributed, that large estates left much land uncultivated, and that hundreds of thousands of rural families had too little land to live on. Rural workers might be landless or might hold plots too small to feed a household, and they depended on seasonal plantation labor that paid little. The argument was over how much to change, how fast, and at whose cost. Árbenz's answer was Decree 900, approved by Congress on 17 June 1952 and signed into law the same day.

The law did not authorize seizure of every large farm: eligibility depended on cultivation, land use, and statutory exceptions. Article 10 protected holdings up to two caballerías (about 90 hectares) regardless of cultivation and protected estates between two and six caballerías (roughly 90–270 hectares) when at least two-thirds of the land was cultivated. Article 32 addressed eligible large, idle or improperly exploited estates above six caballerías. The printed law gives an inconsistent hectare conversion, so the original caballería unit is preferable. Productive commercial land was protected under specified conditions, not by an unconditional blanket immunity. Certain national estates and legally eligible municipal lands could be redistributed, but Article 10(c) expressly exempted existing Indigenous and campesino community lands from expropriation. Article 33 also provided a procedure favoring communities in some conflicts with municipalities over land rights.

Compensation was a central feature, and it generated the sharpest controversy. Owners were to be paid in agrarian-reform bonds redeemable over twenty-five years at three percent interest, with the value of the land determined by the amount the owner had declared for property-tax purposes as of 9 May 1952. The Department of State's published diplomatic record confirms the date of the law and this declared-value basis for compensation. The system followed from a simple logic: owners had long underdeclared their land to reduce taxes, and the government proposed to take them at their word. Owners objected that the declared values bore little relation to market value.

The law established local agrarian committees, departmental commissions, a National Agrarian Council, and a National Agrarian Department to investigate petitions, review disputes, and authorize awards. Its property arrangements were not uniform. Article 4 allowed nationalized land to be allotted through lifetime usufruct or rental and permitted certain directly awarded private-title parcels up to eighteen hectares; Article 32 also contemplated private ownership when eligible beneficiaries so chose. Titles carried conditions and restrictions on sale. Under Article 39, a lifetime usufruct ended at the holder's death, but dependent survivors enjoyed priority to apply for a new grant; this was not automatic inheritance of the same right. The statute also prohibited unpaid or servile labor arrangements, including working for the landlord in lieu of rent. Thus redistribution combined land access, varying forms of legal tenure, and reform of labor relations.

What was the ambition? In the language of its framers, the law aimed to end the feudal-like relations in the countryside, to create a class of independent commercial farmers, and to build a domestic market. It was a capitalist land reform in design: it distinguished idle from productive land, paid compensation, and left commercial agriculture intact. Its critics saw something else. To landowners, any law that allowed committees including peasants and unionists to decide which land was idle, and that used the owners' own tax declarations against them, appeared as an assault on property. To U.S. officials, the law, and the political forces running it, appeared as evidence of communist influence. These different readings are examined in the divisions that follow.

Historical Vocabulary
Usufruct (usufructo)

Usufruct is the legal right to use a piece of land and enjoy its produce without owning it outright. Under Decree 900, many recipients received land in lifetime usufruct, in return for a modest payment, with provisions allowing heirs to inherit the right.

The term matters because the statute did not grant every recipient the same legal estate: the state retained ownership of many redistributed parcels, while some eligible beneficiaries could receive restricted private title. Article 39 ended lifetime usufruct at the holder’s death but gave the widow, children, or economically dependent survivors priority to apply for the same land. Students should distinguish secure access, private title, conditional use, and automatic inheritance, which the law did not provide for usufruct.

Analysis: The text shows the law's careful architecture of thresholds and exemptions, which complicates the claim that it permitted blanket confiscation. It also shows why compensation based on declared values would provoke a dispute with owners who had understated their holdings. What a statute cannot show is how committees interpreted "idle" land, how disputes were resolved, or how families experienced redistribution. The text was also read by foreign audiences through their own assumptions: U.S. officials noticed its ideological resonance as much as its exemptions.

Section 79.11

Campesino Mobilization, Indigenous Communities, and the Implementation of Agrarian Reform

A land reform does not implement itself. Decree 900 set up procedures, but the reform proceeded only because rural people used them. The central mechanism was the petition. Groups of campesinos, workers on a plantation, residents of a village, or members of a union, applied to local agrarian committees for the expropriation of idle land on a nearby estate, and the application then moved up through the departmental and national bodies. This structure gave rural communities an unprecedented legal channel and created thousands of local committees, whose members were often teachers, union organizers, small farmers, and community leaders.

Rural organization was growing even before 1952. The Confederación Nacional Campesina de Guatemala was founded in 1950 and built on local leagues and unions, and the labor federations also reached into the countryside. By 1953 and 1954 these organizations claimed memberships in the hundreds of thousands, though such figures are the organizations' own and are probably inflated. Political parties and the communist-led labor leadership also sent organizers. Handy and Forster have emphasized that the process was not simply top-down: local people often took the initiative, sometimes ahead of the state, and sometimes in ways the national leadership did not expect or approve, including land occupations.

Maya communities took part, but unevenly, and their participation depended on local conditions. In some highland municipalities, communities petitioned for the return of communal or municipal lands that had been taken by estates in the era of liberal reform, using the new law to pursue old grievances. In others, caution prevailed: where there was little estate land nearby, or where community leaders feared reprisals from landowners and local officials, or where a history of conflict with Ladino neighbors made new alliances difficult, participation was limited. Language mattered, since petitions had to be written in Spanish, usually by intermediaries, and the bureaucratic procedures favored communities with literate allies. Political divisions inside Maya communities between generations, between those with land and those without, and between Catholic and other religious groups also shaped responses.

Regional comparison shows the variety. On the Pacific coast, where large estates and the banana and coffee lands were concentrated and where workers were already organized, the reform moved fastest and affected the most land. In the central highlands, communities such as Kaqchikel-speaking municipalities in Chimaltenango pursued smaller claims on adjoining estates. In Alta Verapaz, a region of coffee estates, many of them formerly German, and of Q'eqchi' communities that had been dispossessed over decades, the reform redistributed national farms and some private land but faced strong local landowner resistance. In Izabal and the Caribbean lowlands, United Fruit workers' unions made their own demands. In the western highlands, with fewer large estates, the reform's direct effect was more limited.

The record shows gains and conflict together. Tens of thousands of rural households participated in petitions and awards, and conditions on some estates improved as workers gained alternatives. Yet committees could err or become vehicles for local rivalry; boundary and eligibility disputes multiplied; owners resorted to litigation, dismissals, and violence; and some activists were killed. A traceable case is the Unión Campesina of San Vicente Pacaya's land petition concerning El Barretal, preserved through the CEH's citation to an INTA agrarian file (see Source Window 6). Expropriation, award, occupation, and retention were not identical outcomes: the CEH records reversals and removals after 1954. Published estimates describe hundreds of thousands of hectares involved and roughly 100,000 households affected, with definitions varying. These estimates mix petitions, expropriations, awards, use rights, and durable possession: different measures with incomplete records.

Section 79.12

United Fruit, Foreign-Owned Infrastructure, and the Politics of Compensation

The United Fruit Company, founded in 1899 and headquartered in Boston, was the dominant foreign enterprise in Guatemala. Its operations in the country centered on banana plantations in Izabal on the Caribbean side and, from the 1930s, on the Pacific side near Tiquisate. It controlled the International Railways of Central America, the main railway to the Caribbean, and operated the port facilities at Puerto Barrios, so that it could carry its own fruit and charge others for shipping. Its landholdings were enormous; it owned or controlled well over half a million acres, according to commonly cited figures, of which it cultivated only a minor fraction. It was also the country's largest private employer. Its labor policies, tax exemptions, and contracts had been negotiated with Ubico and, after 1944, were exposed to a government with different priorities.

The company's workers were among the first to take advantage of the Labor Code. Banana workers formed unions in the late 1940s, struck, and won contracts. Under Árbenz, the labor conflict was joined by a conflict over land. When Decree 900 took effect, workers at the company's Tiquisate and Bananera operations filed petitions for expropriation of idle company land. On 26 February 1953 the National Agrarian Council issued an order, signed by Árbenz, calling for the expropriation of approximately 234,000 acres of United Fruit land near Tiquisate, as the Department of State's published record documents. In February 1954 the government announced the final expropriation of an additional 172,532 acres near Bananera on the Caribbean slope. Together these totals amounted to nearly 400,000 acres.

Compensation became the heart of the diplomatic dispute. The Guatemalan government proposed twenty-five-year agrarian bonds based on the company's own declared taxable land values; United Fruit and the U.S. government insisted that those figures fell far below market value. For the Tiquisate expropriation alone, contemporary reporting gives a Guatemalan offer of approximately $594,572 for about 234,000 acres and a U.S.-backed demand of approximately $15.85 million for that same tract. The separate Bananera taking involved a different property and compensation offer, which must not be conflated with Tiquisate. State Department diplomatic notes asserted a requirement of prompt, adequate, and effective compensation and proposed negotiations or arbitration. Guatemala maintained that the taking followed national law and that the dispute was subject to its own courts and sovereignty. These rival positions should be read in the specific context of each property and date rather than as one unqualified aggregate dispute.

Students should distinguish four overlapping elements, which later interpretations of the coup often blur. The first is corporate interest: the company lost land and faced a government hostile to its way of doing business, and it lobbied Washington through public relations campaigns and personal connections. Several U.S. officials had past ties to the company or its law firms, and after the coup at least one, Walter Bedell Smith, joined its board. The second is a property-law dispute about the standard of compensation. The third is Guatemalan economic nationalism: Árbenz and his supporters regarded the company's control of railroads, ports, and land as an obstacle to national development, and the highway and port projects aimed to break that control. The fourth is U.S. Cold War policy, which defined the Guatemalan government as a security threat on grounds that went beyond the company's complaints.

These strands reinforced one another but were not identical. Officials in Washington said repeatedly that they were not acting merely for the company, and the declassified record shows that security concerns about communist influence drove the covert operation. Yet the expropriation of a major U.S. investor's property made Guatemala a visible case, and the company's lobbying helped to keep it on the agenda. A historian who explains the coup only by bananas, or only by ideology, leaves out the way these interests fed each other.

Division IV — Cold War Politics, Domestic Opposition, and the Preparation of the Coup · Section 79.13

Communists, Reformist Nationalists, Landowners, and the Struggle over Guatemala's Political Direction

Guatemala had a small Marxist tradition before 1944, which Ubico's police largely suppressed. After the revolution, communists appeared among the organizers of unions, among teachers and students, and in study circles that spread Marxist ideas. Arévalo's government, wary of them, sometimes restricted their activity and expelled some activists. The communists regrouped in a clandestine party founded around 1949, and in December 1952, under Árbenz, the party emerged in the open under the name Partido Guatemalteco del Trabajo, the PGT. In the congressional elections of January 1953 it won a small minority of seats, often given as four. Its leaders, including José Manuel Fortuny, worked closely with labor leaders such as Víctor Manuel Gutiérrez.

The scale of communist influence requires proportion. The PGT's membership was small, estimated by observers in the low thousands, and its representation in Congress and the cabinet was limited. It never governed. But its influence exceeded its numbers because its members were disciplined, educated, and committed, and held leadership positions in labor federations, among agrarian organizers, and in the government's agrarian and propaganda offices. Árbenz, who did not join the party, consulted communist advisers and valued their organizing capacity and their dedication to the land reform. The historian's task is to avoid both dismissing this influence and inflating it into proof that the Soviet Union controlled Guatemala. No Soviet embassy existed in Guatemala City, and the connections between the PGT and Moscow, which the CIA searched for after the coup, proved far thinner than U.S. officials had claimed.

Guatemalans interpreted the reforms through very different lenses. For reformers and for many teachers, urban workers, and professionals, the revolution was democratic modernization: the end of dictatorship, the extension of rights, the construction of a modern state. For many campesinos and Maya villagers, it was a social revolution that offered land, a legal voice, and relief from coerced labor. For landowners and business associations, it was a threat to property and to the labor discipline on which their enterprises depended; they saw committees of peasants judging their land and unions confronting their managers as symptoms of social disorder. For leaders of the Catholic Church, which had lost land and influence under the nineteenth-century liberals and now saw rising secularism and organized Marxism, it was a danger to faith; Archbishop Mariano Rossell Arellano issued a pastoral letter in April 1954 denouncing communism. For Washington and for militant anti-communists, it was a Soviet beachhead.

Opposition organized itself in response. Newspapers, student groups, business chambers, and the Church campaigned against the government and against communism. Exiled officers and politicians, notably Carlos Castillo Armas, plotted from neighboring countries. Within the army, some officers who had accepted reform in 1944 grew uneasy as the labor and agrarian movements grew and as communist influence became visible. Árbenz, for his part, increasingly relied on a small circle of advisers and on loyal officers, while the political parties of the governing coalition grew more dependent on the president.

The constraint on historical judgment is that both sides' claims are partly true. Communists did hold real influence, and the agrarian reform did empower rural organizers who were Marxists. But supporters of land reform were not all communists, and many who favored redistribution were Catholics, nationalists, or moderate liberals. The anti-communist label simplified a complex political field in ways that served those who wanted the reform stopped. Equally, the defenders of the revolution sometimes minimized communist influence in order to rebut accusations. A careful account must hold both facts together.

Section 79.14

Guatemala, the United States, and Central American Diplomacy

The Guatemalan reform governments operated in an international setting that grew more hostile each year. The United States had welcomed the end of Ubico and recognized the Arévalo government, but its policy shifted as the Cold War intensified. Under Harry Truman, U.S. officials grew concerned by the labor movement, by the presence of communists in it, by Guatemala's refusal to cooperate on regional anti-communist measures, and by the treatment of U.S. firms. Washington refused to sell arms to the Guatemalan military, a decision that later pushed Guatemala toward European suppliers. After the Korean War began in 1950, perceived communist threats in the hemisphere gained urgency.

The policy hardened under Dwight Eisenhower, who took office in January 1953, with John Foster Dulles as secretary of state and his brother Allen Dulles as director of central intelligence. They shared a view that neutral or reformist governments could become fronts for Soviet influence. Intelligence assessments emphasized communist infiltration of the Guatemalan government and labor movement and the risk that Guatemala would become a base for spreading revolution. John Peurifoy, an assertive diplomat, became ambassador in October 1953 and reported pessimistically that the government was moving toward communism. The land reform and the conflict with United Fruit sharpened these concerns, and the declassified record shows that officials intermixed corporate grievances with security fears, though the latter drove the covert program.

The Guatemalan government's actual foreign relationships were more limited than the U.S. narrative suggested. It had no diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union. Its relations with Czechoslovakia and other bloc states consisted largely of the arms purchase of 1954, which came after other suppliers refused to sell. Its closest sympathizers in the region were not Moscow but reform-minded governments and movements, including Mexico, whose political tradition resembled its own, and, to a degree, Argentina under Perón. Arévalo had also supported anti-dictator exiles, including the Caribbean Legion, which made dictators in neighboring countries into enemies.

The neighbors mattered. Honduras, under governments friendly to Washington, offered territory, airfields, and a border with Guatemala that was easily crossed. Nicaragua's Anastasio Somoza, a long-time U.S. ally who despised Arévalo and Árbenz, offered training sites and support. The Dominican Republic's Trujillo, Venezuela's Pérez Jiménez, and the Salvadoran government also cooperated at various points. Geography was destiny in the practical sense that opponents could organize in exile in nearby countries, recruit, train, and cross the border, while Guatemala had few diplomatic options and a limited military capacity to deter them. Castillo Armas, who took part in failed uprisings in 1949 and 1950 and escaped imprisonment in 1951, became the focus of exile planning.

The result was an asymmetry. Guatemala was small, poor, and diplomatically isolated; its adversary was the hemisphere's dominant power, with allies on its borders. The Guatemalan government tried to counter with appeals to international law and sympathetic Latin American governments, but these were weak instruments against a state determined to act. This helps explain why the later campaign could combine covert force, diplomatic pressure, and regional bases with confidence that the Guatemalan government would find few effective responses.

Section 79.15

From PBFORTUNE to PBSUCCESS: CIA Planning and the Organization of Intervention

The covert effort against Árbenz came in stages. The first was PBFORTUNE, authorized under the Truman administration in 1952. It aimed to supply arms and support to Guatemalan exiles, including Castillo Armas, through the cooperation of Somoza. It collapsed in the autumn of 1952 when its existence became known to U.S. State Department officials who objected, and when the secrecy of the operation was compromised, in part through indiscretion in Central America. The Truman administration abandoned it. That abandonment is the reason historians distinguish it from the later operation: PBFORTUNE was a proposed and aborted plan, not the overthrow itself.

PBSUCCESS, the second and decisive operation, was organized under Eisenhower during 1953 and 1954. President Eisenhower approved it in August 1953, at a time when the CIA had also helped to overthrow Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh in Iran, an operation that encouraged confidence in the agency's methods. Planning proceeded through a headquarters in the United States, with a major base in Florida and training and logistical facilities in Nicaragua and Honduras. The plan combined three components: a political-action effort to build an exile movement and to attract defectors; a propaganda and psychological effort to isolate and discredit the Guatemalan government; and a paramilitary effort to create a credible armed force, supported by aircraft flown by contract pilots. The budget ran to a few million dollars, modest for the scale of the result.

The operation sought to influence Guatemala's regular army as much as to defeat it. Planners understood that a small force could not defeat the army in a straight fight, so they aimed to create doubt among officers about the government's survival and about U.S. intentions, and to persuade them to remove the president or refuse to fight. They cultivated contacts among officers, used propaganda to portray Árbenz as a Soviet agent, and held out the prospect that the United States would not tolerate his government. Declassified records also show that planners prepared lists of individuals to be neutralized and studied assassination as a tool; scholars who have examined the record find that such planning is documented, while evidence that the agency carried out assassinations during PBSUCCESS is much weaker. Students should keep the confirmed (planning and operation of the overthrow) separate from the unresolved (individual decisions and unrealized proposals).

Carlos Castillo Armas, a lieutenant colonel born in 1914 and trained in part in the United States, was chosen as the figurehead. He depended on external support for money, weapons, aircraft, and logistics, but he was not a puppet in the sense of lacking aims. He wanted to overthrow the revolution, restore property to its former owners, and rule himself, and he bargained hard with U.S. officials and with the Guatemalan officers who would eventually take part in the negotiations of July. The U.S. planners had considered other candidates, including Ydígoras Fuentes, and settled on Castillo Armas partly because he was more pliable and less ambitious than they feared others would be.

A third project, PBHISTORY, followed the coup. In the weeks after Árbenz fell, a CIA team collected a large volume of records from Guatemalan government offices and from the PGT and labor organizations, reportedly on the order of a hundred thousand or more documents, to find evidence of Soviet direction and to use in anti-communist propaganda and in identifying suspects. The effort turned up less proof of Soviet control than its sponsors expected, though the files helped the new government compile lists of suspected communists. PBFORTUNE, PBSUCCESS, and PBHISTORY were separate projects with different purposes, and records on them survive in the National Archives and in the National Security Archive's collections.

Section 79.16

Propaganda, Anticommunism, Hemispheric Diplomacy, and Psychological Warfare

The campaign against Árbenz was fought on three fronts at once. The first was diplomacy. At the Tenth Inter-American Conference in Caracas in March 1954, Secretary Dulles pressed the Organization of American States to adopt a declaration condemning intervention of international communism in the hemisphere and authorizing consultation if any state came under its control. The declaration, approved on 28 March 1954 by a wide majority, was understood by all delegates to refer to Guatemala. Guatemala's foreign minister, Guillermo Toriello, argued that the resolution would legitimize intervention in the internal affairs of member states; Guatemala voted against, while Mexico and Argentina abstained. Many Latin American governments voted yes under U.S. pressure, some because they had their own anti-communist concerns, and some because they depended on U.S. goodwill. The conference thus showed both the reach of U.S. influence and the limits of regional enthusiasm.

The second front was information. In the months before the invasion, the CIA ran a clandestine radio station that presented itself as the voice of an internal opposition movement, the Voice of Liberation, broadcasting from outside the country while claiming to operate inside it. It broadcast rumors, alarming reports, and warnings of coming attack, exaggerating rebel strength and creating the impression that the government was crumbling. Leaflets were dropped, forged messages and threatening letters were circulated to officers and officials, and planes overflew the capital to demonstrate vulnerability. The objective was to induce panic and distrust and to make resistance seem pointless.

The third front was the army. Planners concentrated on convincing officers that the United States would not accept Árbenz, that the government's days were numbered, and that the army's institutional interests lay in removing him. The Guatemalan government made the task easier by its own measures. In early June 1954, alarmed by plots and by news of an impending attack, it suspended constitutional guarantees and arrested suspected opponents, steps that alienated some officers and civilians and fed the narrative of an authoritarian regime. The government also had real fears about the army's loyalty, as the next section shows.

Students must distinguish authentic reports from deliberately deceptive ones. Some contemporary accounts of rebel victories, of troop movements, and of a vast liberation army were fabricated or exaggerated by CIA propaganda; others, such as the arrival of a Czechoslovak arms shipment, were real. The Guatemalan government's own communications, including its public statements and censorship, also shaped information. A historian reading a newspaper from June 1954 must therefore ask whether a report came from a verified source, from a broadcast aimed at causing panic, or from a government concerned to maintain morale.

The central lesson of this campaign is that the military balance on the battlefield alone cannot explain the outcome of June 1954. The invading force was small and, as the next section shows, accomplished little in combat. What the operation achieved was to change what Guatemalans, especially army officers, believed about their government's prospects and about the intentions of the United States. Diplomatic pressure, propaganda, and force worked together, and each depended on the others. To weigh them separately is useful for analysis but misleading as a description of how events unfolded.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How Agrarian Reform, Domestic Opposition, and International Intervention Combined

Long-term causes

Extreme concentration of land and labor coercion; dependence on foreign-owned infrastructure; an army with an independent political voice; the emergence of anti-communism in U.S. policy.

Short-term causes

Decree 900 and the United Fruit expropriations; growing communist influence in labor and agrarian bodies; Árbenz's reliance on a narrow circle; the Eisenhower administration's decision for covert action.

Event or transformation

The 1954 overthrow: a small invasion, air attacks, propaganda, and diplomatic pressure, followed by the army's refusal to defend the government.

Immediate effects

Árbenz's resignation, short-lived juntas, the rise of Castillo Armas, arrests and exile, and the dismantling of reform institutions.

Long-term consequences

Reversal of land reform; weakened unions and parties; a precedent of military politics and anti-communist repression that shaped later decades; lasting distrust of U.S. intervention in Latin America.

Historical complexity

No single factor suffices. Reform alienated powerful groups, the army was divided, and the United States organized the removal, but domestic opponents and officers made it possible.
Division V — The June 1954 Overthrow and the Destruction of the Democratic Reform Process · Section 79.17

The Invasion of June 18, 1954: Armed Conflict, Air Attacks, and the Crisis of the Army

By the spring of 1954 the Guatemalan government knew that an attack was coming. It had reasons to rearm. The United States had refused to sell it weapons, and its army's equipment was old. In May 1954 a Swedish-flagged freighter, the Alfhem, delivered a shipment of arms from Czechoslovakia at Puerto Barrios. The Guatemalan government treated the purchase as a response to a shortage and a defensive necessity. Washington treated it as proof that Moscow was arming a client in the hemisphere and used it to justify accelerating the operation and to strengthen the arms and aircraft supplied to Guatemala's neighbors. The sequence is an example of how an action that Guatemalans saw as self-defense could be read in Washington as confirmation of a threat.

The invasion began on 18 June 1954. Castillo Armas led a column across the border from Honduras toward the town of Esquipulas, with other small groups crossing at several points, in all a force usually estimated at around five hundred men, though counts differ. The force was poorly equipped, minimally trained, and lacked heavy weapons. Against it stood a Guatemalan army of several thousand regular soldiers with artillery and a few aircraft. On paper the regular army could have crushed the invaders, and in several clashes it did defeat or contain rebel groups. The invaders captured border towns and made limited advances toward the interior but failed to seize any major objective.

Air power and propaganda made the difference. Aircraft flown by contract pilots and operated from Nicaraguan and Honduran bases bombed and strafed targets, including military installations, fuel storage near Puerto San José, and sites in and around the capital, and the British freighter Springfjord was bombed and set ablaze off Puerto San José on 27 June 1954; contemporary accounts differed over the precise extent of its loss. These attacks did limited material damage but great psychological damage. Because the Voice of Liberation described invented victories and warned of larger attacks, many Guatemalans, including officers, could not tell what was real. Rumors multiplied that the rebels had thousands of men and that the United States would intervene directly. Many officers who might have fought a ragged column of exiles hesitated in the face of what they thought was the whole power of the hemisphere's greatest state.

The government's greatest worry was the army itself. Árbenz knew that many officers disliked the reforms, resented communist influence, and might refuse to fight, and he feared that a victorious army might turn on him. In the crisis he considered arming workers and campesinos, who would have defended the government with greater loyalty. He attempted to distribute arms to organized civilians in a limited way, but the army's leadership resisted, regarding armed civilians as a threat to the army's own position. The result was a stalemate of fears: officers who would not fight for a government they distrusted, a president who could not trust his army, and an armed people who had not been armed. The declassified U.S. record, including the Department of State's published diplomatic history, confirms that the invasion began on 18 June and that pressure from within the military was crucial in leading to Árbenz's resignation.

Students should therefore distinguish three things. The invading force's battlefield achievements were small. The wider psychological pressure of bombing, broadcasting, and the shadow of U.S. intervention was large. And the decisive event was the loss of the army's will to defend the government. Historians generally agree that the combination, not any one element, destabilized the regime. The episode shows how a state can fall without being defeated in battle.

Section 79.18

The Resignation of Árbenz, the Military Juntas, and the Installation of Castillo Armas

The final week was decided in the capital. By about 25 June, the officers around Colonel Carlos Enrique Díaz, the head of the armed forces, had made clear that they would not carry on the fight if Árbenz remained, and U.S. Ambassador Peurifoy was in contact with them. On the evening of 27 June 1954 Árbenz announced his resignation in a radio address, handing authority to Díaz. He said that he was stepping down to preserve the achievements of the revolution and to deprive the invaders of a pretext, and he denounced the aggression, naming the United Fruit Company and the United States as its authors and rejecting the claim that his government was communist. He then sought asylum in the Mexican embassy.

Castillo Armas did not become president at that moment. Díaz, who had been installed as head of a junta, was not acceptable to Washington, in part because he had allowed Árbenz's speech to criticize the United States. The Americans pressed for his removal, and on 29 June Díaz was forced out by a new three-man junta led by Colonel Elfego Monzón, a more reliably anti-communist officer. This junta, however, did not satisfy Castillo Armas, who insisted on a place in the government and on the integration of his forces, and the situation was a standoff between two groups of officers, both dependent on the support of U.S. officials.

The resolution came through negotiation in San Salvador, where the U.S. ambassador to Guatemala and the ambassador to El Salvador, working with Salvadoran President Óscar Osorio, brought Monzón and Castillo Armas together on 30 June and 1 July. A truce agreement was concluded in the early hours of 2 July. Under it, Castillo Armas and an associate joined the junta, with Monzón remaining its head. On 3 July Castillo Armas flew to the capital on a U.S. aircraft. Within days, two of the junta's members, who had reportedly made their own arrangement with Castillo Armas, resigned, and Castillo Armas took over the leadership of the junta on 7 or 8 July, depending on the source. He was formally installed as president on 1 September, and a plebiscite held in October, with open voting, returned an overwhelming result in his favor.

Students should keep this sequence in view, because it corrects the impression of an immediate transfer to Castillo Armas. Three governments, those of Árbenz, Díaz, and Monzón, rose and fell in less than a week, and the outcome was shaped by Guatemalan officers' rivalries, by the leverage of U.S. diplomats, and by Castillo Armas's insistence on the prize he had been promised. U.S. government records document both the shifting junta negotiations and the decisive influence of external diplomatic and intelligence pressure. The new leaders were not simply appointed by Washington. They were Guatemalan officers who bargained, but they bargained under conditions that U.S. power had created.

The resignation address and the diplomatic cables should be read together, as the Primary Source Window shows. Árbenz's speech was a political act addressed to the Guatemalan public and to history. The cables were reports from officials to superiors, written to describe their own influence and to guide policy. Neither is a neutral narration. A reader who uses only the speech sees a president defending his legacy; a reader who uses only the cables sees the crisis as a problem of U.S. management. Together they show a government undone from outside and from within.

Multiple Perspectives

Campesinos and rural workers: Many saw the reform as a chance for land, an end to forced labor, and a legal route to contest estate owners. After 1954 many lost land and faced reprisal.

Reformers and government supporters: Teachers, professionals, and political leaders saw a democratic modernization, though they differed about how far and fast to go and about the role of communists.
Military officers: Some backed the reforms, some resented them, and many feared a loss of the army's autonomy. Their decision not to fight in June 1954 was decisive.
U.S. policymakers: They saw a communist threat, a threat to American investors, and a test of the Cold War in the hemisphere, and they believed covert action could remove it cheaply.
Section 79.19

Counterrevolution: Reversing Agrarian Reform, Repressing Labor, and Reconstructing Political Authority

The new government moved quickly to dismantle the reform order. In July 1954 it created a national committee for defense against communism with powers to investigate and blacklist, and in the months that followed it issued a preventive law against communism that criminalized communist activity and broadly defined suspicion. Political parties that had supported Árbenz were banned or dissolved, and the PGT was outlawed. Arrests followed in large numbers, and estimates of the people detained, exiled, or placed on lists run from many thousands to figures near seventy thousand for names compiled by the committee over the next years. Those counts are approximate, and the records of killings are incomplete. Many leaders sought asylum in embassies and fled the country, among them Árbenz and Fortuny; others were imprisoned or killed.

Labor organizations suffered the first and heaviest blows. The government canceled the registration of the great majority of unions, removed leaders, and investigated the labor movement, as the U.S. diplomatic record acknowledges. Federations that had claimed scores or hundreds of thousands of members were broken up, and rural organizations lost their legal standing. Employers regained the ability to dismiss organizers, and workers who had used the labor courts found themselves vulnerable. The Labor Code remained on the books in modified form, but the institutions that had enforced it were weakened.

Land reform was reversed. Decrees in the months after the coup repealed Decree 900, abolished the agrarian bodies, and restored land to former owners, with the return of United Fruit's expropriated property among the first measures. Some recipients were evicted, others were left with uncertain status, and many families that had received land returned to estate labor. Over time, the government adopted an Agrarian Statute in 1956 that shifted the emphasis away from redistribution of existing estates toward colonization of frontier land and sales of land, and the state's capacity to carry out meaningful reform was gone. Estimates of how much land was returned vary, and some families kept land through informal arrangements, but the overall direction was clear.

Indigenous communities and rural households experienced the counterrevolution in particular ways. Local officials who had worked with agrarian committees were replaced; campesino leaders were arrested or killed; and communities that had petitioned for land were exposed to retaliation by landowners and local authorities. In many places the old labor controls returned in practice if not always in law. Teachers and government employees were dismissed from public posts on suspicion of leftist sympathies, and university students and professors were investigated. The new regime also restricted the franchise: its political statutes limited the vote of illiterate citizens, reversing a feature of the 1945 system, and its plebiscite was conducted in a manner that offered little real choice.

The Castillo Armas government was authoritarian, anti-communist, and heavily dependent on U.S. aid, which flowed in at high levels. It was not a simple restoration of the Ubico order, since it faced a changed society and its own factional conflicts. Castillo Armas himself was assassinated on 26 July 1957 by a member of his palace guard, in a killing whose motives are still unclear, and the political instability of the following years showed how weak the new order's foundations were. This chapter does not trace those years. It is enough to note that the counterrevolution destroyed the institutions and legal protections of the reform decade and that the people who lost most were the rural and working-class Guatemalans who had briefly gained a political voice.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was the 1954 Overthrow Principally Driven by Cold War Ideology, Economic Interests, or Domestic Political Conflict?

The question: Historians agree that the CIA organized the overthrow. They disagree about which causes weighed most heavily in the decision and in its success. Scholarship does not divide into two camps but into overlapping emphases, so three interpretations are presented here.

Interpretation A — Cold War ideology and security: Richard Immerman and others argue that the central motive was a genuine fear, widespread in the Eisenhower administration, that communists were gaining control of Guatemala and would turn it into a Soviet base. On this view, officials misjudged the threat, but they acted from strategic conviction, and the United Fruit dispute was a secondary irritant.
Interpretation B — Economic interests: Stephen Schlesinger and Stephen Kinzer, in their well-known account, emphasize the role of the United Fruit Company, its lobbying, and its connections to U.S. officials, suggesting that the corporation's interests were a driving force behind the intervention. Later scholars have often accepted the company's influence but questioned whether it was the decisive cause.
Interpretation C — Domestic political conflict: Jim Handy and others stress that Guatemalan landowners, Church leaders, officers, and opposition politicians were actively working against the reform before and during the U.S. operation, and that the army's divisions were decisive. Piero Gleijeses, while emphasizing U.S. responsibility, argues that Washington feared the reform as a social and political example and not simply the PGT.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same sources read differently: declassified policy papers that stress communism, records of company lobbying and personal ties, and Guatemalan evidence of domestic opposition and army behavior. Ideology, interest, and domestic politics are hard to separate in the documents because officials used all of these arguments at once.

Why it matters: If ideology was the main cause, the coup appears as a Cold War miscalculation. If corporate interest dominated, it looks like economic imperialism. If domestic conflict was central, the Guatemalan opponents appear as agents rather than instruments. Most contemporary historians combine these emphases, but the weights they assign affect how responsibility, inevitability, and alternatives are judged.

Student task: Write a short paragraph defending the weight you would give to each cause, and identify one piece of evidence that would change your assessment.

Section 79.20

Guatemala's Democratic Spring: Achievements, Destruction, and Historical Memory

The Guatemalan Revolution of 1944 to 1954 deserves to be assessed as a historical process of its own, not only as the prelude to a coup. In ten years Guatemalans replaced a dictatorship with contested elections and a constitution that limited executive power, legalized unions and gave them courts and procedures, created a social-security institute, expanded schools and clinics, ended the most coercive labor laws, and redistributed land on a scale unprecedented in the country. These changes altered what many Guatemalans thought the state owed them and what they could demand of it.

The limits were equally real. Voting remained unequal, especially for illiterate women and for rural Maya people. Social programs reached cities and a few regions more than the countryside. Indigenous policy remained paternalistic and assimilationist. The reform coalition was fractious, depended heavily on two presidents, and never secured the loyalty of the army. The land reform, whatever its gains, generated conflicts, errors, and a bureaucratic apparatus that sometimes overreached. Compared with Bolivia's revolution of 1952, described in Chapter 78, Guatemala's reform was less able to remake the army and the state, and, unlike Mexico's revolution in Chapter 74, it never built a party and military order capable of defending itself.

Historians have offered several explanations for why it ended as it did, and they are compatible more often than they conflict. The overthrow was a domestic counterrevolution by landowners, conservative officers, and Church and business leaders who feared the loss of property and status. It was a Cold War intervention by a superpower that saw communism where there was reform and judged covert action cheap. It was a corporate conflict in which a powerful firm lost land and helped keep Washington's attention. It was a military betrayal in which officers abandoned a constitutional president. And it was the result of the vulnerabilities of a reform government, which held electoral legitimacy but little coercive power, confronting enemies who held both money and guns.

The relation of 1954 to Guatemala's later history requires restraint. The destruction of the reform process closed legal avenues of change and strengthened an army that saw itself as the guardian against subversion, and these conditions contributed to the decades of repression and armed conflict that followed, the subject of Chapter 91. But the civil war, which began in the early 1960s, was not an automatic consequence of the coup. Choices made by Guatemalan officers, politicians, guerrilla organizers, and foreign governments produced it. The Historical Clarification Commission, whose report appeared in 1999, concluded that more than two hundred thousand people were killed or disappeared in the conflict, that the great majority of victims were Maya, and that state forces were responsible for the large majority of the violations it documented. The same year, President Bill Clinton, visiting Guatemala, said that U.S. support for military forces engaged in repression had been wrong. The relation of 1954 to the war is thus one of conditions, not of fate. The coup also reverberated outside Guatemala: observers in Latin America, including the Argentine physician Ernesto "Che" Guevara, who was living in Guatemala in 1954, drew lessons that fed into the revolutionary politics of Chapter 80.

Reconstructing this history depends on sources of different kinds, and each fills gaps in the others. Declassified diplomatic and intelligence documents show what Washington planned and thought. Guatemalan legal records, agrarian files, newspapers, and union papers show how reforms worked. Survivor testimony and the Historical Clarification Commission's report preserve voices that official papers silence. Historians such as Kirsten Weld have also shown how the discovery of a long-forgotten police archive in 2005 opened new evidence on state repression. Memory remains politically alive: in 2023 Guatemalans elected as president Bernardo Arévalo, a diplomat and the son of Juan José Arévalo, who campaigned against corruption and invoked the heritage of 1944, and who took office in January 2024 after contested efforts to block the transfer. Those recent events lie beyond this chapter's period, and students should consult up-to-date sources about them.

The scene in the countryside after July 1954 is where the chapter ends. Rural families who had held land for a year or two faced eviction orders and landowners returning with the backing of the new authorities. Union halls were closed and their leaders dispersed. Teachers waited to learn whether they would be dismissed. Political opponents of the new government confronted arrest, exile, or silence, while officials rebuilt a state organized around anti-communism. The constitution of 1945 and the promises of 1944 did not vanish from memory, but their institutions were gone. One question remains, and it is the one the chapter has tried to answer: why did an elected reform government with significant popular support prove unable to preserve constitutional rule?

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Guatemala before 1944, during the Democratic Spring, and after 1954

What Changed

  • From dictatorship under Ubico, to contested elections and a constitution in 1944 to 1954, to an authoritarian anti-communist regime after 1954
  • From illegal unions, to legal unions with courts, to dissolved unions
  • From coerced labor laws, to their abolition, to the return of labor controls in practice
  • From concentrated landholding, to partial redistribution, to the restoration of most estates

What Stayed the Same

  • Highly unequal land distribution, with a small group of owners holding most farmland
  • Dependence on coffee and bananas and on foreign markets and firms
  • A powerful army with a political role
  • The marginal position of rural Maya populations in national politics

Who experienced change most directly: Union members, campesino organizers, and recipients of land gained most during the reform decade and lost most after 1954. Who experienced greater continuity: Large landowners and the army, whose basic position survived all three periods, though both were pressured between 1944 and 1954. Why the pattern matters: The decade was a real rupture, but its reversal shows that reform without a secure military and institutional base could be undone quickly, and it shows how much of the old order persisted beneath the new institutions.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Guatemalan Revolution of 1944 to 1954 and its overthrow in June 1954, and argued that the event must be understood as the interaction of a Guatemalan reform process, strong Guatemalan opposition, and a covert U.S. operation. Reducing it to any one of these misreads the history.

Under Jorge Ubico, who governed from 1931 to 1944, Guatemala was a country of concentrated landholding, coerced rural labor, foreign-controlled infrastructure, and police surveillance. Maya communities, Ladino workers and farmers, urban professionals, landowners, and foreign firms experienced this order very differently, and its inequalities defined the agenda of the revolution that followed.

The revolution began as a domestic mobilization. In June 1944 students, teachers, professionals, and businesses demanded rights and then stopped working after the killing of the teacher María Chinchilla; Ubico resigned on 1 July. That outcome was not democracy, because General Ponce Vaides tried to preserve the old order. The uprising of 20 October 1944, led by younger officers and civilians, overthrew him and installed a junta of Arana, Árbenz, and Toriello. These two events are distinct and should not be merged.

The Constitution of 1945 restored liberties, limited the executive, protected university and municipal autonomy, and defined property as having a social function. It expanded political participation but kept the vote unequal: literate women could vote optionally, illiterate women could not, and illiterate men voted under an open procedure. The Democratic Spring was real, but it was not universal democracy.

Juan José Arévalo, president from 1945 to 1951, pursued a humanist "spiritual socialism" that emphasized dignity, education, and social welfare. His government issued the Labor Code of 1947, created the IGSS in 1946, and expanded schooling and health services. These reforms transformed labor relations and politics, but enforcement was uneven, rural and Indigenous Guatemalans were reached least, and the army remained a divided and independent force. The killing of Arana in July 1949 removed a conservative rival but left questions of responsibility unresolved.

Jacobo Árbenz, president from 1951, pursued economic modernization through highways, a port, and hydroelectric power designed to reduce dependence on foreign-owned facilities. Decree 900 of 17 June 1952 expropriated idle portions of large estates, paid compensation in bonds based on declared tax values, and exempted cultivated and small properties. Campesinos and Maya communities used local committees to petition for land, and the reform reached many families, though results varied by region, were marred by disputes and some abuses, and are reported in statistics that must be read with care about what is being counted.

The reform brought Guatemala into conflict with the United Fruit Company, whose land was expropriated in 1953 and 1954, and with the Eisenhower administration. Corporate interest, property-law disputes, Guatemalan economic nationalism, and Cold War security policy overlapped without being identical. Communists, organized in the PGT, held real influence in labor and agrarian bodies beyond their small numbers, but there is no evidence that the Soviet Union controlled the government. Landowners, Church leaders, business groups, and officers interpreted the reforms as threats to property, faith, and order.

The CIA's PBSUCCESS, approved in 1953 after the abandonment of PBFORTUNE, combined political action, propaganda, and a small paramilitary force led by Castillo Armas, supported by Honduras and Nicaragua and by diplomatic pressure at Caracas. The invasion of 18 June 1954 achieved little on the ground, but aircraft, radio broadcasts, and the shadow of U.S. power destroyed the army's will to fight. Árbenz resigned on 27 June, and the transfer to Castillo Armas ran through the short-lived juntas of Díaz and Monzón and the negotiations in San Salvador.

The counterrevolution banned parties, suppressed unions, repealed Decree 900, restored land to owners, and compiled lists of suspected communists, with the heaviest costs falling on rural and working-class Guatemalans. Its relationship to the later civil war is one of conditions, not inevitability. The chapter's final question stands: why did an elected reform government with significant popular support prove unable to preserve constitutional rule? The answer lies in the combination of a divided army, powerful domestic opponents, and an external power committed to its removal.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows both the possibilities and the fragility of reform in twentieth-century Latin America. In ten years Guatemalans built institutions of rights, labor law, social welfare, and land redistribution in a country where such things had been unthinkable under Ubico. They did so in a region where Chapters 76, 77, and 78 describe other experiments with mass politics and state-led development, and in a hemisphere where the Cold War was changing the terms on which such experiments could survive.

It also shows that outcomes depend on who holds the instruments of force. The reform government held an electoral mandate and the support of many workers and campesinos, but it did not hold the loyalty of the army, and it faced domestic opponents who preferred a different order and a foreign power prepared to act. Indigenous and rural Guatemalans, who gained most from the reform, had the least electoral weight and the least protection, and they paid heavily when it was reversed.

The legacies spread beyond Guatemala. The overthrow strengthened an army that saw itself as the guardian against subversion and closed legal routes of change, conditions that, together with later choices, fed the conflict examined in Chapter 91. It taught many Latin Americans that reform within constitutional limits could be destroyed from outside, a lesson that helps explain the approach of the revolutionaries who triumphed in Cuba in 1959. And it left a historical memory, preserved in documents, testimony, and politics, that still shapes how Guatemalans and others judge the promises of 1944.

Chapter 80 turns to Cuba, where a revolution that began as an armed movement against the dictator Batista became the central event of the hemispheric Cold War. Students should carry forward the Guatemalan lesson, not as a determinism but as a question: how did reformers, revolutionaries, military officers, and outside powers each learn from 1954, and what did they do with what they learned?
