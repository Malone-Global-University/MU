Bourbon and Pombaline Reform in the Eighteenth Century

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Empires That Tried to Remake Themselves

In 1762 a British fleet took Havana, the best-defended port in Spanish America. Within two years Spain was rebuilding the city's fortresses, reorganizing its militias, and rethinking how its American possessions were governed, taxed, and defended. That chain of consequences, running from a military humiliation to new tax offices in the Andes and new regiments in the Caribbean, is the subject of this chapter.

Volume III begins in the eighteenth century, when the Spanish and Portuguese monarchies, each in its own way, tried to make their American empires more profitable, more secure, and more obedient. Spain's Bourbon kings and ministers redrew viceroyalties, installed salaried intendants, raised and collected taxes more systematically, created royal monopolies, rewrote the rules of Atlantic trade, built fortresses, and recruited American soldiers. In Portugal, the secretary of state Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, later the Marquis of Pombal, chartered monopoly companies, dissolved the Society of Jesus in Portuguese territories, declared Indigenous people free in law while placing their communities under lay supervision, and moved the capital of Brazil south to Rio de Janeiro. Historians call these programs the Bourbon reforms and the Pombaline reforms. The shared label hides large differences, and one of this chapter's tasks is to keep them distinct.

The central argument is that these reforms increased royal power in important ways, and that they also redistributed privilege, intensified demands on colonial populations, provoked resistance, and exposed tensions within imperial government. Revenue rose, fortresses and regiments multiplied, and some ports and mining districts prospered. At the same time, tobacco growers, small traders, tribute-paying villagers, American-born officeholders, and Indigenous communities in Amazonia encountered new obligations or lost old protections. Reform was not a single plan carried out without interruption. It was a sequence of measures, driven by war, fiscal need, and the shifting priorities of ministers, and it landed unevenly on different regions and social groups.

The chapter also resists a common shortcut, which treats these reforms as the first step on a straight road to independence. Reform did not make independence inevitable. Many colonial elites benefited from commerce, mining, militia commissions, and royal patronage, and loyalty to the crown persisted alongside grievances. What reform did was alter institutions, expectations, and the balance of obligations between crowns and colonial societies, and those alterations help explain the crises examined in Chapters 49 through 51: the great rebellions of the late eighteenth century, the Haitian Revolution, and the collapse of imperial legitimacy after 1808.

The evidence is largely institutional and fiscal. Historians work with royal decrees (reales cédulas), instructions to officials, the accounts of colonial treasuries (cajas reales), reports of royal inspectors, customs and shipping registers, and the petitions, lawsuits, and riot investigations through which colonial subjects answered the crown. Contemporary reform writings, such as the memoranda circulating among Spanish and Portuguese ministers, show what reformers hoped to achieve, while trial records and local correspondence show what happened when policy reached villages and port districts. Each type of source reflects a particular vantage point, and most were produced by officials. Indigenous, enslaved, and female voices usually appear indirectly, through petitions written by notaries or through the testimony recorded after disturbances.

The chapter follows its five divisions. The first examines dynastic change, reformist ideas, and the reorganization of Spanish American government. The second turns to treasuries, monopolies, mining, and commerce. The third addresses war, fortification, the church, and colonial society. The fourth takes up Portuguese Brazil and the Pombaline program on its own terms, giving particular attention to Amazonia. The fifth considers resistance, compares the two reform programs, and assesses the position of the Iberian empires around 1800. The chapter connects to Part XI's larger argument that independence emerged from an Atlantic crisis of legitimacy whose roots lay in imperial reform, colonial rebellion, and revolutionary events beyond Iberia.

Boundaries matter. Chapters 40 and 41 described the original structures of Spanish and Portuguese colonial government, Chapter 42 the history of silver mining, Chapter 46 the religious history of the colonial church, and Chapter 47 the general history of resistance. This chapter treats those subjects only insofar as eighteenth-century reform changed them. The great rebellions of Túpac Amaru II, Túpac Katari, and the comuneros belong to Chapter 49, the Haitian Revolution to Chapter 50, and the Napoleonic crisis to Chapter 51.

Historical Context

By 1700 the Spanish and Portuguese empires in the Americas were more than two centuries old and had developed distinctive systems of government, described in Chapters 40 and 41. Spanish America was governed through viceroys, high courts (audiencias), town councils (cabildos), and a large corps of local officials, many of whom had bought their positions or acquired them through patronage. Royal authority operated through negotiation with local elites, a pattern often summarized in the formula that royal orders were to be respected but did not always have to be carried out. Portuguese Brazil had grown from coastal captaincies into a colony whose sugar, tobacco, and, from the 1690s, gold from Minas Gerais made it the center of the Portuguese empire. Both empires depended on enslaved African labor, on Indigenous tribute and labor, and on silver or gold that moved through Atlantic ports.

The seventeenth century had weakened Spain, whose Habsburg monarchy lost European wars and territories and, historians have long argued, allowed much colonial revenue and commerce to be diverted into the hands of local officials, foreign smugglers, and merchants of other nations. Portugal, restored to independence under the House of Braganza in 1640, relied on alliance with England and on Brazilian gold. In both cases, the metropolis was in some respects economically dependent on the wealth of its colonies and, through trade, on the manufactures of rival European states.

Eighteenth-century warfare raised the stakes. The War of Spanish Succession, the wars of the 1740s, and above all the Seven Years' War (1756–1763) showed that control of ports and sea lanes in the Atlantic could be decided by fleets and expeditionary armies. The empires of Britain and France, whose commercial and fiscal systems seemed more efficient, offered models and rivals. Ministers in Madrid and Lisbon read Britain's success as a lesson in the relationship between commerce, revenue, and naval power, though they drew different conclusions about what to copy.

Historians have long debated how to describe what followed. Older accounts, shaped by the memory of independence, often treated the reforms as a "second conquest" in which a revived Spanish state reasserted control over autonomous colonial societies. More recent scholarship has stressed that the reforms were uneven, that they were negotiated with local elites, that they began well before Charles III, and that many colonial groups gained from them. This chapter draws on both traditions and avoids a single label. It also treats Portugal separately, because the Pombaline program, though often grouped with the Bourbon reforms, had its own institutions, chronology, and regional priorities.

Division I · Section 48.1

The Eighteenth-Century Imperial Crisis and the Rise of Bourbon Reform

The reform era began with a dynastic crisis. When Charles II of Spain died childless in 1700, he left his realms, including the American empire, to Philip of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV of France. Austria, England, and the Dutch Republic, fearing a union of French and Spanish power, went to war. The War of the Spanish Succession lasted until 1713–1714. At the Treaties of Utrecht and Rastatt, Philip V was recognized as king of Spain and its American possessions, but Spain lost its Italian and Flemish territories. Britain gained Gibraltar and Minorca, along with the asiento, a contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America, and the right to send a limited cargo ship each year to the fairs at Portobelo. For Spain's ministers, the settlement fixed a lesson: the empire's strength depended on the Americas, and the Americas were exposed to rivals.

Early Bourbon reform was cautious and piecemeal. Ministers created specialized secretariats of state for the navy and the Indies, expanded the navy, and in 1717 moved the Casa de Contratación, the agency that regulated transatlantic trade, from Seville to Cádiz. The viceroyalty of New Granada was first created in 1717, abolished in 1723 as too costly, and restored definitively in 1739. Students should note this sequence carefully; New Granada did not exist continuously from 1717. The restoration came in a decade of renewed war with Britain, when the security of the Caribbean coast and the Isthmus of Panama was urgent. Many measures of these years were experiments that were reversed or never fully implemented.

Intensity grew after the middle of the century and reached its height under Charles III (1759–1788), especially after the shock of 1762. Historians therefore distinguish an earlier phase, in which ministers experimented with commercial and administrative adjustments, from a later phase, in which royal officials pursued fiscal extraction, military expansion, and territorial reorganization on a larger scale. Reform was not a single plan implemented without interruption. It reflected changing priorities, the turnover of ministers, and the pressure of war.

Portugal's circumstances were different, and the Pombaline program should not be treated as a variant of the Spanish one. The Portuguese monarchy of João V (1706–1750) had been enriched by Brazilian gold and diamonds and ruled without summoning the traditional assembly of the realm, but its commerce and manufactures depended heavily on Britain through the Methuen treaty of 1703 and related arrangements. Gold revenue declined in mid-century, while the Portuguese state faced competition from Spanish expansion on the southern frontier and anxiety about its British dependence. These pressures, together with the personal ascendancy of a particular minister after 1750, shaped a program with different institutions and different targets, as Division IV shows.

Division I · Section 48.2

Reformist Monarchy, Enlightenment, Regalism, and State Power

Ideas shaped the reforms, though less as abstract philosophy than as practical political economy. Spanish writers and officials of the mid-century, among them José del Campillo, whose 1743 treatise on the economic government of the Indies circulated among ministers, argued that the American territories should be treated as sources of revenue and markets for Spanish goods, and that Spain's weakness came from the leakage of silver and trade to foreigners and from inefficient local government. Later ministers such as Pedro Rodríguez de Campomanes and José de Gálvez were administrators trained in law and finance who believed that better information, professional officials, and rational taxation would enlarge both royal power and public welfare. In Portugal, so-called estrangeirados, men educated or posted abroad, urged similar changes.

These currents are often grouped as the Enlightenment, and their influence is real. But the reformers were generally not liberals. They wished to strengthen the sovereignty of the monarch, reduce the autonomy of corporate bodies such as the church, guilds, and merchant tribunals, and make government more efficient. They did not generally intend to dissolve monarchy, end colonial subordination, or abolish slavery. Enlightened science in the Americas also had a practical side: botanical expeditions in New Granada and Peru, mining academies in New Spain, and learned societies and periodicals in Lima and elsewhere served imperial knowledge and local pride at once.

Historical Vocabulary
Regalismo

Regalismo (regalism) refers to the doctrine that the monarch possessed inherent rights (regalías) over ecclesiastical matters within the kingdom, including appointments, property, and the circulation of papal documents. Spanish and Portuguese rulers had long exercised royal patronage over the church in America; eighteenth-century regalists claimed more, and linked their claims to sovereignty.

The concept matters because it explains why the Jesuit expulsions and the secularization of parishes were not merely religious disputes but assertions of state authority. It should not be confused with hostility to Catholicism. Regalist ministers were generally devout Catholics who sought to subordinate church institutions to royal control, and the Spanish crown's 1753 concordat with the papacy, which expanded royal patronage, illustrates the point.

Reformers also worked within constraints. The empires lacked reliable statistics, the distances were enormous, and royal officials often had to rely on the cooperation of local elites. Reform therefore advanced by experiment, by special inspections, and by recruiting new kinds of officials, and it repeatedly collided with established interests. The next sections show how those collisions played out.

Division I · Section 48.3

Reorganizing Spanish America: Viceroyalties and Territorial Government

The crown redrew the administrative map of Spanish South America in the course of the century. The creation of the viceroyalty of New Granada, with its capital at Santafé de Bogotá, responded to the difficulty of governing the northern Andes and Caribbean coast from Lima, and its restoration in 1739 coincided with the British threat to Cartagena. In 1776, after the Spanish crown's contest with Portugal over the River Plate, Madrid created the viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata with its capital at Buenos Aires. The new jurisdiction included the territory of present-day Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay and, importantly, Upper Peru, the highland region, including the silver center of Potosí, that had belonged to the viceroyalty of Peru. Lima therefore lost both territory and a valuable revenue source, while Buenos Aires acquired an Atlantic outlet for highland silver.

Other jurisdictions were upgraded or created. The captaincy general of Venezuela was constituted in 1777, joining earlier fiscal and military arrangements, and the captaincies general of Chile and Guatemala continued as semi-autonomous commands. On New Spain's northern frontier the crown set up the Commandancy General of the Internal Provinces in 1776 to coordinate defense against Indigenous groups and foreign rivals. These changes recognized that regional distinctions, distance, and military threat required more flexible command structures than the old two-viceroyalty system.

The new boundaries had consequences beyond bureaucracy. Merchants in Lima resented the loss of control over the Potosí trade, while merchants in Buenos Aires gained a legal path for silver exports and imports. Regional elites in places such as Caracas gained a resident captain general and treasury and thus more proximate royal government. Territorial reorganization also expanded the number of salaried officials, with attendant costs, and it is easier to document in legislation than in daily practice, where older jurisdictional habits persisted.

Division I · Section 48.4

Intendants, Royal Visitors, and the Expansion of Administrative Oversight

The most characteristic Bourbon institution was the intendant. The model came from France, and Spain tried it first at home, experimenting with intendants in the 1710s and restoring them in 1749. In America the earliest intendancies were established in Cuba in the 1760s after the loss of Havana, and in Venezuela in the 1770s. General intendancy ordinances followed for the Río de la Plata in 1782, Peru in 1784, and New Spain in 1786, and Chile and other territories were organized on similar lines. Intendants were salaried royal appointees with authority over finance, justice, public order, and, in some cases, military supply. They replaced the older corregidores and alcaldes mayores, local officials who had often lived on illicit profits from the Indigenous communities they supervised, and were assisted by subordinate subdelegados in districts.

Historical Vocabulary
Intendencia, visitador, visita general

An intendencia was an administrative province governed by an intendant, a salaried official responsible for the four "causes" of finance, justice, war, and public administration. A visitador was a royal inspector sent to investigate government and make recommendations, and a visita general was an inspection covering an entire viceroyalty or major region.

The visita was an old institution, but Bourbon ministers used it as an instrument of policy. The best-known example, José de Gálvez's inspection of New Spain from 1765, produced reforms in taxation, tobacco, and frontier government. Students should not confuse the intendant, a permanent governor, with the visitador, whose commission was temporary though its effects could be lasting.

The reform's logic was fiscal and political. Visitors such as Gálvez, who toured New Spain between 1765 and 1771, and José Antonio de Areche, who conducted a visita in Peru from the late 1770s, sought to reform tax collection, curb abuses by local officials, and recommend new institutions. Gálvez became minister of the Indies in 1776 and drove the intendancy program from Madrid. His time in New Spain also included harsh repression of the 1767 disturbances that followed the Jesuit expulsion, and the later visita in New Granada, led by the regent-visitor Juan Francisco Gutiérrez de Piñeres, preceded the comunero rebellion of 1781, a subject for Chapter 49.

Implementation varied. Intendants often clashed with viceroys, audiencias, and cabildos, and the allocation of fiscal authority between viceroys and superintendents of the treasury was adjusted several times. In some regions, such as the Río de la Plata and New Spain, the system was extensively applied; in others, such as parts of New Granada, it was incomplete. Scholars debate whether intendancies improved collection, as the crown intended, or whether they chiefly added costs. The evidence suggests improved revenue in many districts alongside conflict with local elites, and it suggests that the intendants' success depended heavily on how they negotiated with existing local power.

Division II · Section 48.5

The Royal Treasury and the Drive for Greater Revenue

The Real Hacienda, the system of royal treasuries (cajas reales) scattered across the Indies, was the crown's principal means of drawing wealth from the Americas. Its income came from many sources: the quinto or royal fifth on mined silver, taxes on trade, such as customs duties and the alcabala, a sales tax; Indigenous tribute; the crown's share of the church tithe; and revenue from monopolies and the sale of offices and papers. Much of the money never left America. A large portion was spent locally on salaries, fortifications, and administration, and part was sent as situados, subsidies from richer treasuries such as those of New Spain and Peru to poorer or more exposed ones such as Havana, Cartagena, and Florida.

Bourbon reform in this field was mainly about collection rather than invention. Several taxes had been auctioned to private tax farmers, who collected them in exchange for a fixed payment. The crown increasingly replaced such farming with direct administration. In New Spain, for example, the alcabala of Mexico City was taken from the merchant guild's farm and placed under royal officials in the 1750s. Rates were also raised, often in stages during wartime, and new customs houses were placed in interior towns. Direct collection shifted profit from tax farmers and merchant guilds to the crown, but it required salaried collectors and inspectors, whose cost partly offset the gain and whose presence made the tax visible to the people who paid it. Distinguishing improved collection from increased rates is important: much of the rise in revenue came from stricter enforcement and wider application, though rate increases also occurred, and both were felt locally.

Estimates of revenue growth vary with method, but scholars generally agree that royal income from the Americas rose substantially in the second half of the century, with the largest gains in New Spain. Indigenous tribute remained a significant source, and censuses of tribute payers were used to improve its collection. Free people of African descent were also subject to tribute in some regions. Tribute, a legacy of earlier conquest, thus shows continuity amid reform. Higher revenue paid for armies and fortifications, as the following sections show, and so tied taxation to security in ways that colonial subjects understood.

Division II · Section 48.6

Royal Monopolies, Consumption Taxes, and Everyday Economic Life

The crown also turned to monopolies, called estancos, which reserved the manufacture or sale of a commodity to royal agents. Tobacco was the leading example. Cuban cultivation was brought under a royal purchasing monopoly early in the century, provoking armed protests by tobacco growers around 1717 and in the following years. In New Spain, the estanco was established in the mid-1760s, and a large royal cigar and cigarette factory was later opened in Mexico City. Peru introduced a tobacco monopoly earlier, in the 1750s. Playing cards, gunpowder, salt, and other items were also monopolized, and the crown regulated or taxed the production and sale of alcoholic beverages, including the cane spirit aguardiente and, in some regions, the maize and agave drinks consumed by poorer people.

These measures reached people who rarely met a viceroy. Small farmers who grew tobacco were required to sell to the state at set prices, and unlicensed cultivation was punished. Retailers had to buy from official sources. Guards searched houses and roads for contraband, and smuggling became both a livelihood and a form of resistance. For poorer consumers, monopoly prices and taxes meant higher costs for habitual goods. For royal officials, estancos were attractive because they generated revenue without raising the tribute and customs burdens that had previously supplied income, and some were quite profitable. They also, historians have noted, turned many ordinary people into targets of enforcement.

Everyday Life and Social History

Tobacco Growers, Cigar Makers, Market Traders, and Tax Enforcement

For the families who grew tobacco in the valleys of Cuba or the hills of New Spain, the estanco changed the terms of their livelihood. The crown set prices and designated buyers, so a bad price or a late payment fell directly on household income. Growers who sold outside the system risked fines, seizure, and imprisonment, but unofficial markets persisted because they paid better or were closer to hand.

In Mexico City, the royal tobacco factory gathered thousands of workers into a single enterprise, and women made up a substantial share of the labor force, rolling cigarettes and cigars for modest wages. Their work illustrates how reform created new forms of urban employment and new forms of supervision. Market traders, taverns, and small shopkeepers experienced the alcabala, aguardiente controls, and inspection as recurring intrusions, and neighborhood networks often protected evasion. Such everyday experiences are documented unevenly, in enforcement records and riot investigations rather than in the words of the taxed.

Resentment was widespread but not uniform. Where the estanco brought secure purchase of the crop, some growers benefited, and larger merchants and officials benefited from contracts and appointments. The resentment that did arise was directed less at royal authority in the abstract than at specific demands and at the outsiders, often peninsular officials, who enforced them. The disturbances examined in Section 48.20, in Quito, in Cuba, and elsewhere, grew largely from this grievance.

Division II · Section 48.7

Mining Reform, Mercury, Credit, and the Production of Imperial Wealth

Silver remained the most valuable American export, and Bourbon ministers sought to increase its output, while Chapter 42 has already explained the long history of colonial mining. Reformers saw several obstacles: the high price and uncertain supply of mercury, essential to the amalgamation process; the shortage of credit; technical backwardness; and the fiscal burden on producers. The crown lowered the price at which royal mercury was sold to miners, particularly in New Spain, and sought supplies from Almadén in Spain and, increasingly, from European sources beyond Spain. It also relaxed or reduced some taxes and gave nobility titles and privileges to successful miners.

The institutional centerpiece was the Royal Mining Tribunal of New Spain, established in the late 1770s with its own judges and funds, followed by the mining ordinances of 1783 that codified rights to mines, mining litigation, and technical education. A mining college opened in Mexico City in the early 1790s, and technical experts were recruited from Europe. In Peru and Upper Peru, the crown sought to rescue the declining mines of Huancavelica, the mercury source, and extended its supervision over mining credit. Output in New Spain rose strongly, with the great mines of Guanajuato prominent, while Upper Peruvian production recovered more modestly, and the mita, the system of rotational labor drafts for Potosí, continued under the supervision of colonial officials.

The institutional history of credit at Potosí shows how reform worked in practice. In 1746–1747 the azogueros, the owners of the silver-refining mills, organized a credit and silver-purchasing institution of their own to relieve their chronic indebtedness. The crown took partial direction of it in 1752 and made it fully royal in 1779, when it became the Banco de San Carlos de Potosí, a different institution from the Banco de San Carlos founded in Madrid in 1782. In broad terms, such a bank bought silver from producers, advanced credit, and handled supplies, including mercury, so that the crown gained closer control over the flow of silver and its taxation. The mill owners who obtained credit on favorable terms were the principal beneficiaries, while labor continued to depend on the mita and wage arrangements described above. Mining reform thus linked silver purchasing, mercury supply, credit, and official supervision in a system that favored an established mining elite and the treasury.

Mining policy shows how reform linked producers, merchants, laborers, and the treasury. Successful mine owners, many of them Basque or Spanish-born merchants who married into local families, acquired wealth and titles, and merchant financiers provided credit. Miners themselves worked under a mixture of wage labor, partnership shares, and, in the Andes, coerced rotation, and conditions remained dangerous. In effect, reform rewarded entrepreneurs and the treasury, while the burdens of heavy labor remained concentrated among Indigenous and poor workers, a pattern that recurs throughout the chapter.

Division II · Section 48.8

Commercial Reform and the Reglamento de Comercio Libre of 1778

For two centuries Spanish trade with America had been organized through a monopoly. Sailings were concentrated at Seville and then Cádiz, convoys (flotas and galeones) sailed on a fixed schedule to Veracruz and to Portobelo, and merchants of a few consulados, notably at Seville or Cádiz, Mexico City, and Lima, dominated exchange. In practice, much of the merchandise was manufactured abroad and passed through Spanish merchants as intermediaries, while contraband trade with British, Dutch, and French merchants flourished in the Caribbean and on the River Plate. Reformers argued that the system restricted supply, raised prices, encouraged smuggling, and favored a small group of privileged merchants.

Change came in stages. From the 1740s, individual register ships replaced the periodic fleets, and during the war years the Pacific coast was supplied by way of Cape Horn. In 1765 the crown opened trade between a number of Spanish ports and the Caribbean islands, and during the following decade it extended similar permission to other regions. The comprehensive measure was the Reglamento y Aranceles Reales para el Comercio Libre de España a Indias, issued on 12 October 1778. It named thirteen Spanish ports, among them Seville, Cádiz, Málaga, Barcelona, and Santander, along with Palma de Mallorca and Santa Cruz de Tenerife, and some two dozen American ports, including Havana, Cartagena, Buenos Aires, Montevideo, Valparaíso, Callao, and Guayaquil. It abolished a series of older shipping charges, reduced and standardized duties, and favored Spanish and American products over foreign ones. The Cádiz monopoly was thereby formally ended. New Spain, reserved for a separate regulation, and Venezuela, whose ports were excepted because of the Caracas company, were brought into the system in 1789.

Paraphrase of the document's provisions (the single phrase quoted is from the preamble): The king describes the policy as a comercio libre y protegido, meaning a trade both free and protected, among Spaniards in Europe and in America. Article 4 lists the Spanish ports allowed to trade, and Article 5 lists American ports, noting that Venezuelan ports were temporarily excepted and that trade with New Spain would be regulated separately. Article 6 abolishes several shipping charges. Later articles set lower duties for the smaller American ports and higher ones for the larger, charge foreign goods more than Spanish ones, forbid the shipment of foreign wines and spirits, and exempt many Spanish manufactures from duties for ten years. They also preserve the sales tax (alcabala) in America. Analysis: The decree's language of freedom has to be read against its provisions. The freedom was freedom within the Spanish empire: ships, owners, and most crews had to be Spanish, foreign goods were taxed more heavily or banned, and a detailed system of registration, inspection, and penalties kept revenue and control in royal hands. What changed was access. Many ports could now trade directly, old charges disappeared, and the monopoly of Cádiz ended. The differences in duty by port show that the crown used the tariff to encourage trade where it wished to stimulate it, and the preference for Spanish goods shows that the measure served metropolitan production as well as colonial consumption. The source records the monarchy's intentions and rules, not their results; merchants' accounts, customs registers, and shipping records are needed to see how the decree worked in practice. It also says nothing directly about the producers, consumers, and enslaved people whose labor and purchasing sustained the trade.

  1. What does the regulation say it will do, and for whom?
  2. Who created it, and what purposes shaped its wording?
  3. What does the pattern of duties and prohibitions reveal about the monarchy's idea of commercial freedom?
  4. What can historians not establish from the regulation alone?

It is essential to see what comercio libre was not. It was not free trade in the later sense of open international commerce, since foreigners were excluded and mercantilist assumptions endured. Spain remained largely a re-exporter of foreign manufactures and a recipient of American raw materials and silver, and Spanish industry, apart from Catalan textiles, grew only modestly. The measure was an attempt to improve revenue and imperial commerce by widening access within a closed system, and its effects varied by place and war conditions, since wars with Britain in 1779–1783 and again in the 1790s interrupted shipping.

Division II · Section 48.9

Regional Winners and Losers: Ports, Merchants, and Colonial Markets

The commercial reforms redistributed opportunity among ports. Havana prospered as a naval base, a tobacco and, increasingly, sugar exporter, and a recipient of situado revenues, and the crown's decree of February 1789 widening the slave trade accelerated plantation development, tying commercial expansion directly to slavery. Buenos Aires and Montevideo, previously marginal, became major commercial and naval centers, exporting silver from Upper Peru, hides, and salted meat, and importing manufactures and enslaved Africans; their merchants formed a consulado in the 1790s. Smaller ports such as those of Venezuela, Chile, and Guayaquil benefited from exports of cacao, wheat, and other goods, and in Venezuela the privileged Guipuzcoana Company, founded in 1728, had earlier provoked local revolt before its dissolution in the 1780s.

Losers existed too. The ending of the fleets undermined Veracruz as a fair town and the merchants of Mexico City and Lima who had dominated distribution and credit, although many of them adapted by investing in mining, estates, and new commercial networks. Lima's loss of Upper Peru, and its lack of an equivalent to Buenos Aires, reduced its commercial primacy. Local textile producers, such as obraje workshops in Quito and Puebla, faced competition from imported cloth. Because gains were regional, not universal, grievances mixed with new prosperity, and historians therefore avoid describing the reforms as having uniformly benefited or harmed America.

Slavery tied these changes together. As sugar, tobacco, cacao, and coffee production expanded, so did demand for enslaved labor, which the crown permitted more freely after 1789, in part to match Britain and France. Commercial expansion thus deepened the Atlantic slave system in Cuba, Venezuela, and elsewhere, a development that would shape the nineteenth century and that stood in tension with later revolutionary ideals discussed in Chapters 50 and 53.

Division III · Section 48.10

Imperial Competition and the Shock of the Seven Years' War

Spain entered the Seven Years' War late. Bound to France by the Family Compact of 1761, it declared war on Britain in January 1762, and the British responded with expeditions against its most exposed possessions. A large British force besieged Havana in the summer of 1762, and after weeks of fighting, during which disease devastated the besiegers and the defenders alike, the city surrendered in August. In October a separate British expedition captured Manila. Havana was occupied for about ten months and Manila for about two years. During the occupation, Havana was opened to British commerce, and thousands of enslaved Africans were brought to the island, a stimulus to sugar production that outlasted British rule.

The Treaty of Paris in 1763 restored both cities to Spain, in exchange, in Havana's case, for Spanish cession of Florida to Britain. France compensated its ally by ceding Louisiana west of the Mississippi, which Spain took as a buffer. The settlement left Spain's empire territorially intact but strategically changed. Havana was the hub of the treasure fleets and the key to the Gulf of Mexico, and its fall demonstrated that Spain's defenses, centered on fortresses garrisoned by small regular forces, could not withstand a determined naval expedition. Contemporary and later writers, including historians such as Allan Kuethe and Kenneth Andrien, have treated the loss as a turning point in the priorities of Charles III's ministers.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Capture of Havana and the Expansion of Military-Fiscal Reform

Long-term causes

Eighteenth-century Anglo-Spanish rivalry in the Caribbean, dependence of Spanish commerce on a few fortified ports, and the weakness of colonial revenue and defense inherited from the preceding century.

Short-term causes

Spain's entry into the Seven Years' War in 1762, the dispatch of a large British expedition, and the limited size and training of the Havana garrison.

Event or transformation

British forces took Havana in August 1762 and held it until the Peace of Paris returned it in 1763.

Immediate effects

New fortifications and a reorganized militia in Cuba; an intendancy and tighter fiscal administration; Spanish control of Louisiana as a buffer; heightened concern for other ports.

Long-term consequences

Expansion of colonial armies and militias, higher taxation to pay for defense, the intensification of reform under Gálvez, and growth of Cuban sugar and slavery.

Historical complexity

Spanish reform was already under way before 1762 and was not caused only by Havana. The defeat accelerated and focused existing tendencies, and its effects differed in Cuba, New Spain, and the Andes.

The connection between security and revenue is the key to the chapter. Defense required soldiers, ships, and stone, and these required money, which the crown sought to raise through the fiscal measures of Division II. A military shock in the Caribbean therefore altered the lives of tobacco growers in New Spain and tribute payers in the Andes. Historians accordingly speak of a military-fiscal logic running through late Bourbon policy.

Division III · Section 48.11

Fortifications, Standing Forces, Militias, and Colonial Defense

After 1763 the crown poured resources into defense. Havana received the massive fortress of San Carlos de la Cabaña and strengthened walls and batteries, and comparable works were built or rebuilt at San Juan in Puerto Rico, Cartagena, Montevideo, Callao, and other ports, along with naval facilities at Havana and elsewhere. Many such projects were financed through situados, the intercolonial subsidies sent from the treasuries of New Spain and Peru. The result was a considerable transfer of American revenue to the defense of the American empire itself, rather than to Spain, a point that complicates the older image of simple extraction.

The army also changed. In Cuba, Alejandro O'Reilly reorganized the garrison and militias in 1763 and 1764, and a similar structure was applied in Puerto Rico and then in New Spain, Peru, and New Granada. Regular army units, some sent from Spain, were supplemented by large militia forces recruited locally and trained periodically. Militias had existed before, but the new system systematized recruitment, uniforms, drill, and inspection, and expanded the numbers on paper and, to a lesser extent, in effective strength. Cost, local reluctance, and uneven training limited the system's effectiveness.

Participation was socially differentiated, and the evidence permits some detail. American-born Spaniards, or Creoles, were commissioned as militia officers, and their service brought the fuero militar, a legal privilege exempting officers and often their families from ordinary civil jurisdiction, as well as social prestige. Free pardo (mixed-ancestry) and moreno (Black) men served in separate battalions in Havana, Mexico, Lima, Cartagena, and other cities. For them, military service offered status, exemptions, and in some cases tribute relief, and it gave free people of African descent a corporate identity and a claim on the crown. Indigenous men served as auxiliaries in frontier regions. Enslaved men were employed as laborers and, in emergencies, as armed defenders, and some earned freedom through service, though their treatment was far less secure. Women appear in the record chiefly as dependents seeking military pensions and as householders supporting soldiers.

Even so, militia service had a dual character. It conferred privileges on those who served, and it demanded time and money from households, and communities sometimes resisted conscription. The militia also placed arms in the hands of American-born and free-colored men, a fact whose significance would become clear in the era of independence, though it is a mistake to read that later outcome back into the intentions of the reformers.

Division III · Section 48.12

Royal Authority over the Church and the Expulsion of the Jesuits

Regalist policy brought the crown into conflict with church institutions. The concordat of 1753 enlarged royal patronage, and ministers pressed for the transfer of Indian parishes from the regular orders, such as Franciscans and Dominicans, to the diocesan clergy under bishops and royal oversight, a process called secularization. The goal was to bring the clergy under closer royal supervision and to reduce the independence of the orders. Provincial church councils, as in Mexico in 1771, issued reforms in the same spirit, and bishops with regalist sympathies were appointed. The Inquisition continued, but with reduced prominence in the work of the crown.

The most dramatic measure was the expulsion of the Society of Jesus. The Jesuits had been influential teachers and missionaries, and owned productive estates, colleges, and missions throughout the Americas. In 1766, riots against the minister Esquilache in Madrid were blamed by the crown on Jesuit influence, and in early 1767 Charles III ordered the order expelled from all Spanish dominions. The order was executed in America in mid-1767 by secret orders opened on or about 25 June, and the Jesuits, some 2,200 to 2,400 in Spanish America by recent estimates, were arrested and shipped to Europe. In New Spain, the expulsion provoked riots in several towns, which Gálvez suppressed harshly. The Jesuit expulsion was thus a Spanish measure, distinct from and later than Portugal's, treated in Section 48.18.

The seized property, termed temporalidades, was administered by royal officials and in many cases auctioned. Jesuit haciendas, plantations, and obrajes passed to private buyers or the crown, producing revenue and a shift of property to new owners. Colleges closed or were reassigned, interrupting education in several regions and affecting access for Creole families. Missions suffered most: the Guaraní missions of Paraguay, which had been a flourishing network of villages, passed to civil administrators or other orders, and declined; the missions of Baja California, Sonora, and elsewhere passed to Franciscans and Dominicans. Indigenous communities, whose agriculture, craft, and governance had developed within the missions, endured new exploitation or dispersal. The expulsion thus shows reform's capacity to reorder institutions rapidly, and its costs, borne unevenly by those who had depended on them.

Division III · Section 48.13

Royal Officials, Creole Elites, and the Redistribution of Political Privilege

One of the most debated aspects of reform concerns its effect on American-born elites. In the seventeenth century, the crown's financial needs had led it to sell many offices, including seats on the high courts, and Creole families had gained a share in colonial government. From the mid-eighteenth century, the crown curtailed such sales and filled audiencia and senior administrative posts disproportionately with peninsular Spaniards, many of them trained jurists and officers. Studies of audiencia appointments have documented this shift. The new intendants, visitors, and officers were often outsiders, and some Creoles saw in them a threat to their influence and a disregard for local custom.

But the picture is not one of uniform exclusion. Many Creoles gained from reform. They obtained militia commissions, which carried the fuero and prestige. They invested in mining, commerce, and estates that expanded under new policies. They sat on cabildos and merchant consulados, and the crown continued to rely on local elites for tax collection, defense, and credit. New peninsular immigrants, particularly Basques and Spaniards from the northern coast, married into Creole families, blurring the line between categories. The terms Creole and peninsular described birthplace, but interests depended on wealth, region, and networks. Merchants in rising ports often supported commercial reform, while those in older monopolistic centers opposed it.

Reform therefore redistributed privilege more than it removed it. Some groups, such as certain Creole officeholders and monopolist merchants, lost relative position, while others, including mining entrepreneurs, militia officers, and port merchants, gained. Resentment of peninsular preference was real, and would later feed political rhetoric, but historians caution against treating Creole opposition as a coherent early nationalism. In the eighteenth century, most Creole grievances were framed as loyal complaints that royal ministers had violated customary good government.

Division III · Section 48.14

Reform and Colonial Society: Labor, Status, Burdens, and Opportunity

Reform reached ordinary people through taxes, labor demands, and legal categories. Indigenous communities continued to pay tribute, and in the Andes to provide mita labor, while the corregidores' compulsory sales of goods to Indigenous villagers, the reparto mercantil, which had been legalized in Peru in 1751, were abolished in 1780 after the outbreak of the Túpac Amaru II rebellion (see Chapter 49). The intendancy system, which formally replaced the corregidores, was not implemented in Peru until 1784. The two measures were therefore distinct: the abolition was a concession made in the midst of crisis, and the intendants a later administrative reorganization. Neither removed the underlying burdens of tribute and labor, and both coincided with higher taxes. A royal decree of 1770 ordered that Spanish be taught to replace Indigenous languages, though its enforcement was uneven. Artisans in guild-regulated trades faced new regulation and taxes. Miners and agricultural producers encountered both opportunities, such as lower mercury prices and expanding markets, and burdens. Urban authorities, in Mexico City and elsewhere, pursued campaigns against vagrancy, drunkenness, and disorder that targeted the poor.

Gender shaped experience. Women worked in the tobacco factory, in markets, as domestic servants, and as household heads, and widows often managed businesses and property. Indigenous women bore the household burdens of tribute and labor drafts, and enslaved women, in cities and on estates, labored under the double vulnerability of slavery and gender. Free people of African descent were subject to tribute in some places and gained opportunities in militias in others. For enslaved people, commercial reform meant expanded plantation agriculture, particularly in Cuba and Venezuela, and two royal measures of 1789 pulled in different directions. A decree of 28 February 1789 liberalized the slave trade to specified Caribbean destinations and to Caracas, temporarily admitting foreign traders so that more enslaved Africans could be brought to expanding plantations. An instruction of 31 May 1789 laid down standards for the religious instruction, food, clothing, work, and punishment of enslaved people. Slaveholders protested that it threatened their authority and their labor supply, and its application was soon suspended, so its standards were never continuously or effectively enforced. The commercial measure thus advanced while the protective one stalled. Reform therefore increased the number of enslaved people in some regions and did little to protect them.

Division IV · Section 48.15

Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo and the Pombaline Reform Program

The Portuguese program was the work of a minister more than a dynasty. Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, a diplomat who had served in London and Vienna, became secretary of state in 1750, at the start of the reign of José I. He was later ennobled as Count of Oeiras and, in 1769, Marquis of Pombal, the title by which the program is known. The great Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755, followed by fire and a tsunami, killed tens of thousands and destroyed much of the capital. Pombal's direction of the emergency response and the rebuilding greatly enhanced his authority, which he used to sideline rivals, including members of the high nobility, in a conflict that culminated in the harsh prosecution of the Távora family after an attempt on the king's life in 1758.

The program combined state-building, economic policy, and anticlericalism. Pombal sought to reduce Portugal's commercial dependence on Britain, strengthen royal finance through a new treasury, the Real Erário, established in 1761, and promote Portuguese trade through privileged companies. He reformed education, including the University of Coimbra, and curbed the power of the Inquisition and the Jesuits. His government pursued these aims in Brazil, which supplied the gold and commodity revenues on which the Portuguese state depended, and which needed defense against Spanish expansion in the south.

The Pombaline program differed from the Spanish in structure. Portugal did not create a new set of viceroyalties, intendancies, or militia systems on the Spanish model. Its characteristic instruments were monopoly companies, secular administration of Indigenous villages in Amazonia, the expulsion of the Jesuits, and the redirection of colonial policy toward defense of the south. It was also led by one dominant minister, and it ended with his fall in 1777 after the death of José I, when Queen Maria I dismissed him and reversed several of his policies. Brazil must be understood in its own right, not as a Portuguese version of the Spanish reforms.

Division IV · Section 48.16

Chartered Trading Companies, Mining Revenues, and Commercial Reorganization

Pombal used chartered companies as instruments of state-directed commerce. The Companhia Geral do Grão-Pará e Maranhão, established in 1755, received a monopoly over trade and the supply of enslaved Africans to the northern Amazonian state, as well as exports of products such as cacao, rice, and cotton. A second company, for Pernambuco and Paraíba, followed in 1759. The companies were intended to concentrate capital, stimulate agriculture, supply enslaved labor to regions that had long relied on Indigenous workers, and channel commerce through Lisbon under Portuguese control. They brought thousands of enslaved Africans to the north and shifted the region's labor system toward African slavery.

They also created conflict. Planters complained of high prices for slaves and low prices for produce, local merchants resented the monopoly, and officials were accused of favoring company interests. Both companies were wound up under Maria I, in 1778 and 1780. Their record is generally judged mixed: they increased exports and slave imports in some areas while generating debt and resentment. The Pombaline government also ended the convoy system between Portugal and Brazil in the mid-1760s and created a board of trade to regulate commerce, and it tightened control of the Brazilian gold and diamond revenues. The diamond district of Minas Gerais was placed under a royal monopoly, the Real Extração, in 1771. Taxes on gold, the royal fifth (quinto), remained central to Portuguese finance, and its collection through the periodic levies called derramas would later provoke resentment in Minas, discussed in Chapter 49.

Gold production in Minas Gerais had peaked around the middle of the century and was declining, which sharpened the crown's concern for revenue and contributed to efforts to diversify agriculture and exports. Commerce with Britain remained essential to the Portuguese economy despite Pombal's efforts, and Brazil's own commercial and slaveholding elites, planters in Bahia and Pernambuco, merchants in Rio, and mine owners in Minas, remained the principal beneficiaries of the colonial economy. Reform thus enlarged state supervision of commerce without displacing these groups.

Division IV · Section 48.17

Indigenous Freedom, the Diretório dos Índios, and the Reorganization of Amazonia

Pombaline policy in Amazonia is among the clearest examples of reform's contradictions. The northern territory, the State of Grão-Pará and Maranhão, was governed from Belém and São Luís, and from 1751 by Pombal's brother, Francisco Xavier de Mendonça Furtado, who also headed the Portuguese boundary commission under the Treaty of Madrid. Its economy had long relied on Indigenous labor, organized through mission villages (aldeias) administered by religious orders, especially the Jesuits, and through the distribution of Indigenous workers to settlers and the crown. Indigenous peoples had resisted, fled, and bargained, and epidemics had reduced their numbers.

In 1755, a series of laws changed this framework. A decree of April encouraged marriage between Portuguese settlers and Indigenous women, and removed legal stigma from such unions and their children. Laws of June declared Indigenous people free, abolished the temporal power of missionaries over the villages, and placed their secular administration in lay hands. These laws applied in the northern state and were not initially extended to all Brazil. In 1757 the governor issued the Diretório dos Índios, a detailed code of administration, and in 1758 it was extended to the whole of Brazil. The Diretório was abolished in 1798.

Historical Vocabulary
Diretório dos Índios, aldeia, língua geral

The Diretório dos Índios (Directory of the Indians) was the administrative code of 1757, extended in 1758, that governed former mission villages under lay directors appointed by the crown. An aldeia was an Indigenous village, often a mission settlement; under the Diretório many were converted into Portuguese-style towns (vilas) with Portuguese names. The língua geral was a lingua franca based on Tupi languages that had been widely used in Amazonia by missionaries, settlers, and Indigenous peoples; the Diretório banned its use in favor of Portuguese.

Students should distinguish the 1755 laws, which declared freedom and ended missionary temporal rule, from the 1757 Diretório, which defined how freed villagers would be governed. The Diretório is a case in which the language of freedom coexisted with supervision, mandatory labor service, and assimilation. It should not be read as either simple emancipation or simple enslavement; its effects must be judged by practice.

The Diretório's provisions combined emancipation and control. Directors, usually laymen, supervised each village, organized labor, and supervised trade, and received a share of production. Indigenous leaders (principais) kept some local offices. Villagers were required to work in agriculture and gathering forest products for the settlement, for the crown, and for settlers who received laborers by distribution, with wages paid in principle but often irregularly. Schools were to teach Portuguese, and Indigenous names and customs were discouraged. Settlements were reorganized as towns with Portuguese names, and settlers were encouraged to marry Indigenous women. The measures aimed to integrate Indigenous people into Portuguese society as subjects and workers, while securing a labor force for the colonial economy and a population to hold the frontier against Spain.

The distance between law and practice was large. Many directors exploited villagers, extracted labor beyond what the code prescribed, and enriched themselves. Indigenous people responded by petitioning, by flight into the forest, by working within the system, and in some cases by armed resistance, as with the Mura people along the Madeira River, who attacked colonial settlements. Disease continued to reduce the population. Historians debate whether the Diretório represented a progressive attempt at integration, a modernizing assertion of state power, or a more efficient system of coercion. Most agree that it ended mission control, extended lay supervision, and left Indigenous communities with freedom in law and substantial constraint in fact. It also shows that Indigenous people remained historical actors in the reform era, bargaining, resisting, and adapting within a system built to control them.

Division IV · Section 48.18

The Portuguese Expulsion of the Jesuits and the Reorganization of Religious Authority

The Portuguese crown's conflict with the Jesuits predated Spain's by nearly a decade. Pombal and his brother saw the Jesuits as obstacles to royal authority in Amazonia and as opponents of the Treaty of Madrid, particularly in the Guaraní missions on the southern frontier, and they accused the order of commercial abuses and of political resistance. After the attempt on the king in 1758, the government implicated several Jesuits, and by a decree of September 1759 it banished the order from Portugal and its dominions and confiscated its property. In Brazil, Jesuits were arrested and shipped to Europe, and their colleges, estates, and mission villages were seized. Relations between Portugal and Rome were broken for most of the following decade, and the papacy dissolved the order in 1773.

The consequences in Brazil were widespread. Jesuit properties, including productive estates, were sold or transferred to the crown. Missions in the Amazon came under the secular administration just described. Jesuit colleges, an important source of education in the colony, closed, and the crown created a system of royal classes (aulas régias) to replace them, with uneven results. Brazilian society lost teachers, missionaries, and landholders, and the crown gained property and authority over religious institutions.

Comparison with Spain's expulsion of 1767 is instructive. In both empires, regalist ministers moved against the order as a corporate rival to the crown, confiscated property, and reorganized missions and schools. The Portuguese expulsion occurred earlier, was driven by an immediate political conflict and by Amazonian and boundary issues, and was carried out by a dominant minister, while the Spanish decision followed the 1766 riots and was executed by simultaneous secret orders. The Portuguese measures were integrated into Pombal's larger plan for Amazonia, whereas the Spanish expulsion was part of a broader assertion of royal control over church institutions. Chapter 46 treats the earlier history of the missions and the religious orders, and this chapter concentrates on the royal intervention and its institutional results.

Division IV · Section 48.19

Territory, Defense, and the Transfer of Brazil's Capital to Rio de Janeiro

Frontier defense occupied Portuguese and Spanish ministers alike. The Treaty of Madrid of 1750, negotiated by the Portuguese diplomat Alexandre de Gusmão and Spanish counterparts, applied the principle of uti possidetis, that each crown would keep the territory it actually occupied, and exchanged the Portuguese enclave of Colônia do Sacramento on the River Plate for the Jesuit mission territory of the Sete Povos east of the Uruguay River. The Guaraní residents of those missions, who would have had to move, resisted, and the Guaraní War of 1754–1756 saw a combined Spanish-Portuguese force defeat them, with heavy losses. The treaty proved unworkable and was annulled by the Treaty of El Pardo in 1761.

Competition on the southern frontier continued. The new Spanish viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata launched an expedition under Pedro de Cevallos that took the island of Santa Catarina and Colônia do Sacramento in 1777, and the First Treaty of San Ildefonso, signed in October 1777, confirmed Spanish control of Colônia and the eastern bank of the river while returning Santa Catarina and the Rio Grande region to Portugal. Southern Brazil, settled in part by migrants from the Azores, became a militarized frontier, and the cattle economy of the south grew.

In 1763 the Portuguese crown moved the seat of the viceroy of Brazil from Salvador in the northeast to Rio de Janeiro. The reasons were strategic and economic: Rio was closer to the southern frontier and to Minas Gerais, whose gold and diamonds were shipped through its port, and its role in the Atlantic slave trade was growing. The move reflected the southward shift of the colony's center of gravity from the sugar northeast to the gold, cattle, and slave-trading south, and it laid a foundation for Rio's later importance, discussed in Chapter 56.

Division V · Section 48.20

Opposition, Negotiation, and the Uneven Enforcement of Reform

Colonial subjects responded to reform in many ways, and it helps to distinguish them. Petitions and lawsuits were the most common, and used the legal channels that colonial government itself provided. Protests, such as gatherings outside government buildings, expressed collective grievance. Riots involved violence against property or officials, often directed at tax collectors and monopoly agents. Organized rebellion, with leaders, programs, and armed forces, was rarer and belongs mainly to Chapter 49. Most opposition combined elements and was framed as loyalty to the king and opposition to bad officials.

Examples appear across the period. Tobacco growers in Cuba rebelled against the monopoly in the 1710s and 1720s. In Venezuela, Juan Francisco de León led an uprising in 1749 against the Guipuzcoana Company. In 1765, the neighborhoods of Quito rose against the new aguardiente monopoly and the reorganization of tax collection and customs, driving out royal officials for a time, and the crown eventually negotiated an amnesty. The Jesuit expulsion of 1767 produced riots in several New Spanish towns, which were suppressed with executions and punishments. In Portugal, a tumult in Porto in 1757 against a wine company was crushed harshly. In Amazonia, flight and raids by Indigenous groups such as the Mura challenged the Diretório.

Enforcement was uneven, and authorities frequently bargained. When resistance threatened revenue or security, officials suspended or modified policy, offered pardons, or removed unpopular collectors, and then later tried to restore the measure in a different form. The old colonial practice of acknowledging a royal order while delaying its application continued. This pattern reflects the limits of royal power: the crown could legislate, but its officials depended on local cooperation. It also helps explain why reform's effects differed across regions. The great rebellions of the 1780s, in the Andes and New Granada, arose from the accumulation of such grievances, and are the subject of Chapter 49.

Both programs strengthened royal government in measurable ways. Revenues rose, defenses improved in key ports, commerce expanded, and the crown gained control over religious institutions. Both met entrenched local institutions, and both depended on cooperation from local elites. Both also affected social groups differently, benefiting some merchants, miners, and officers, and burdening taxpayers, villagers, and workers. The Spanish program was broader and more bureaucratic, the Portuguese more concentrated in particular policies and more dependent on one minister. Where the Spanish crown expanded colonial armies and militias, Portugal relied more on existing structures. Where Portugal confronted a single powerful Jesuit presence in Amazonia and the southern missions, Spain confronted a wider ecclesiastical landscape.

The fiscal and social mechanisms differed in revealing ways. Spain raised money mainly through the machinery of the treasuries, customs houses, and estancos, which reached households and small producers across many regions, and it spent much of that money on colonial defense. Portugal relied more on privileged companies and royal monopolies over gold, diamonds, and trade, which concentrated the burden on merchants, planters, and mining districts, while its most intrusive social policy fell on Amazonian Indigenous communities through the Diretório. In both empires reform deepened dependence on enslaved labor and left Indigenous communities carrying obligations, and in both the crown bargained with local elites to make its measures work.

Neither was a single coherent plan, and neither fully achieved its goals. Fiscal gains were offset by costs of defense and administration, the commercial reforms favored some regions more than others, and Indigenous and enslaved populations bore heavy burdens. The question of coherence, whether reform was a program of modernization or a series of improvisations, is addressed in the Historical Debate below.

Division V · Section 48.22

Colonial Societies and the Limits of Imperial Reform by 1800

By 1800 the Iberian empires were in some respects stronger than they had been a century earlier. Spain's American treasuries produced larger revenues, its ports were more active, its mines in New Spain were productive, and its armies and militias were larger. Portugal's Brazil was richer in commodities and more integrated in the Atlantic economy, and the Portuguese crown had taken control of religious and Indigenous administration. At the same time, the reforms left unresolved tensions over taxation, privilege, autonomy, and political authority. Colonial societies had been taxed and supervised more closely, had acquired arms and administrative experience, and had become accustomed to petitioning, bargaining, and sometimes rising against particular measures.

Three levels should be distinguished: the crown's intentions, the actual effects of reform, and later political interpretations. Reformers sought to strengthen empire, not to dissolve it. The effects were mixed and varied by region and group. Later interpretations, especially in nineteenth-century nationalist writing, often portrayed the reforms as tyrannical impositions that justified independence, and such accounts must be treated as part of the politics of memory. A careful reading holds that reform helped create political and institutional conditions important to the crises examined in Chapters 49 through 51, but did not make independence inevitable.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Reform Strengthen the Iberian Empires or Undermine Their Legitimacy?

The question: Did the Bourbon and Pombaline reforms make the Iberian empires stronger and more efficient, or did they erode the consensus and local autonomy on which imperial rule depended?

Interpretation A — Reform strengthened the empires: Historians emphasizing this view point to higher revenues, improved defenses, expanded commerce, and more effective administration, and note that many colonial groups gained from reform and remained loyal. On this account the reforms made the empires more productive and secure, and later collapse was caused by external shocks, especially the events of 1808, rather than by reform itself. Scholars such as Kuethe and Andrien have stressed the uneven and negotiated character of reform and its real achievements.
Interpretation B — Reform strained legitimacy: Other historians, and many earlier ones who spoke of a "second conquest," emphasized the political costs: heavier taxation, intrusive officials, preference for peninsulars, attacks on corporate privileges, and the erosion of negotiated government. On this account reform weakened the bonds of loyalty and created grievances that fed rebellion and, later, independence movements.

Evidence: Both views rely on fiscal accounts, official correspondence, riot and rebellion records, petitions, and political writings. Revenue series support the first, while the pattern of protest and elite complaint supports the second. Much depends on region, period, and the social group studied.

Why it matters: The question affects how independence is explained. If reform strengthened empire, the collapse after 1808 appears contingent on Napoleonic events. If reform undermined legitimacy, independence appears more strongly rooted in earlier tensions. Many historians now combine both processes, seeing strengthened institutions and increased grievances operating together and unevenly.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, and identify what additional evidence, such as regional comparisons of revenue and protest, would help decide between them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Bourbon and Pombaline reforms were two distinct programs through which the Spanish and Portuguese monarchies tried to make their American empires more profitable, secure, and obedient, and that both strengthened royal power while redistributing privilege, intensifying demands, and provoking resistance. Neither was a single coherent plan carried out without interruption, and neither made independence inevitable.

The reforms grew from the dynastic and military crises of the eighteenth century. The accession of the Bourbons in Spain and the settlement of Utrecht left Spain's ministers convinced that the American empire was both essential and exposed. Early measures, such as the move of the Casa de Contratación to Cádiz and the first, short-lived creation of the viceroyalty of New Granada, were tentative. The pace increased under Charles III, particularly after the loss of Havana in 1762. Portugal followed a separate path, shaped by dependence on Britain, declining gold revenues, southern frontier conflict, and the dominance of the minister later called Pombal.

Reformers were influenced by Enlightenment political economy and regalist theory, but they sought to strengthen monarchy, not to dissolve it. They redrew the map of Spanish America with new viceroyalties and captaincies, installed salaried intendants, and used royal inspectors such as José de Gálvez to investigate and reorder colonial government. Implementation varied widely and depended on negotiation with entrenched local institutions.

Fiscal reform was central. The Real Hacienda moved toward direct collection of taxes, raised rates in stages, established customs houses, and created monopolies on tobacco, spirits, and other goods. These measures increased revenue but also reached tobacco growers, market traders, and consumers, producing resentment and, at times, riot. Mining reform lowered mercury costs, created the Royal Mining Tribunal and the 1783 ordinances, and rewarded entrepreneurs, while labor burdens remained concentrated among Indigenous and poor workers.

Commercial reform culminated in the 1778 Reglamento de Comercio Libre, which opened more Spanish and American ports to regulated imperial trade. It did not create free trade in the later sense, since foreigners remained excluded. Its effects were regionally uneven, favoring Havana, Buenos Aires, Montevideo, and several smaller ports while undermining older monopolies in Mexico City, Veracruz, and Lima. Commercial expansion tied closely to slavery, especially in Cuba and Venezuela, and the crown widened the slave trade in 1789.

The Seven Years' War and the British capture of Havana demonstrated Spanish vulnerability and led to fortification, army reorganization, and the large-scale use of colonial militias. Military service brought differentiated privileges and obligations to Creole officers, free pardo and moreno soldiers, Indigenous auxiliaries, and enslaved workers. At the same time, regalist policy brought the crown into conflict with church institutions, culminating in the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 and the transfer of their property, colleges, and missions to royal administration.

Pombaline Brazil followed its own course. Chartered companies, the end of the convoy system, and control of gold and diamond revenues reorganized commerce, while the 1755 laws and the 1757 Diretório declared Indigenous freedom and ended missionary rule but placed villagers under lay directors who organized labor and promoted assimilation. The Jesuits were expelled in 1759, and the capital moved to Rio de Janeiro in 1763 as the colony's weight shifted south. Frontier conflict with Spain produced the treaties of Madrid, El Pardo, and San Ildefonso.

Colonial subjects responded through petitions, protests, riots, and occasionally rebellion, and authorities frequently negotiated, suspended, or modified measures in response. The Quito disturbances of 1765, the Cuban tobacco protests, the 1767 riots, and Amazonian resistance illustrate this pattern. The great rebellions that followed belong to Chapter 49.

A comparison shows that both reforms strengthened royal government and encountered entrenched interests, but they differed in institutions, chronology, and priorities. The Spanish program was broader and institutionalized, while the Pombaline was concentrated and partly reversed after 1777. Historians disagree about whether reform strengthened the empires or strained their legitimacy, and the best-supported view holds that both processes operated together and unevenly.

Students should take away both a narrative and a method. The narrative is that eighteenth-century reform changed the relationships between crowns and colonial societies without ending the structures of monarchy, slavery, tribute, and mercantilism. The method is to distinguish what reformers intended, what actually happened, and how later writers interpreted it, and to read official sources with attention to whose experience they omit.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the eighteenth-century reforms were attempts to strengthen empires that changed the relationships through which those empires were experienced. Fortresses, regiments, customs houses, monopolies, and salaried officials made royal power more visible in colonial life, while commercial and mining policy created new winners and losers among regions and social groups. The reforms demonstrate that empires do not simply impose their will; they bargain, improvise, and reverse themselves under pressure.

The chapter also shows the value of keeping the Spanish and Portuguese cases distinct. Bourbon Spain built institutions that outlived their architects, while Pombaline Portugal pursued concentrated policies tied to one minister. In Amazonia, the Pombaline laws reveal how the language of freedom could coexist with new forms of control, and how Indigenous people continued to act as historical agents within systems designed to constrain them. Throughout, enslaved Africans and their descendants appear as the group least protected by reform and most affected by the commercial expansion it encouraged.

Chapter 49 takes up the consequences in the form of the great rebellions of the late eighteenth century, in which taxation, local officials, and Indigenous grievances produced mass movements. Chapter 50 turns to Saint-Domingue, where slavery, imperial rivalry, and revolution combined on a different scale, and Chapter 51 to the collapse of imperial legitimacy after 1808. Students should carry forward the view that reform left both strengthened institutions and unresolved tensions, and that the independence era grew from the interaction of those long-term conditions with sudden events, not from reform alone.
