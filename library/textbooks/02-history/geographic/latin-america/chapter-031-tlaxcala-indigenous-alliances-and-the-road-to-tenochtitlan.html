Tlaxcala, Indigenous Alliances, and the Road to Tenochtitlan

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: An Army That Changed Its Makeup on the Road

The column that entered Tenochtitlan in November 1519 was not the expedition that left the Gulf coast in August. Somewhere between Cempoala and the causeways of Lake Texcoco, a Spanish raiding party became the core of a multinational Indigenous coalition, and that transformation could not be accomplished by Cortés alone: Tlaxcalan rulers made the indispensable decision to turn warfare into alliance.

In mid-August 1519, the expedition described in Chapter 30 left the Totonac coast and climbed toward the central highlands. It numbered somewhat fewer than the roughly five hundred Spaniards who had landed in the spring, since some had been left to garrison the new settlement of Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz. It was accompanied by Totonac warriors and by porters, and it was guided by people who knew the routes. It was still small, legally insecure, and dependent on food and information it did not control. Three months later it would be lodged in the center of one of the largest cities in the Americas, with the ruler of that city in its custody. This chapter explains how that happened, and why none of it was inevitable.

The pivotal episode is the encounter with Tlaxcala. Popular accounts often say that the Tlaxcalans were allies of Cortés, and that is true, but it omits what came first. Tlaxcalan forces fought the newcomers in a series of serious engagements in September 1519, and those battles brought the expedition close to disaster. The Tlaxcalan rulers then chose, after debate, to make an alliance. They did so because they judged that partnership with the strangers could serve their own long struggle against the Triple Alliance, not because they were conquered and not because they were simply eager to be liberated. The alliance was a political act by a sovereign polity, and it transformed the military and logistical position of the expedition.

The coalition that resulted was powerful and also coercive. At Cholula, a sacred and commercial city with its own relations to the Mexica and its own rivalry with Tlaxcala, Spaniards and their Indigenous allies killed very large numbers of people in an episode that Spanish writers justified as preemption of a conspiracy and that later critics, including some Spanish ones, doubted. This chapter does not decide that question for students. It asks what each source claims, who benefited from the claim, and what can be established with reasonable confidence: a massacre occurred, Tlaxcalans and other allies took part, and the news changed how the advancing force was perceived in the Basin of Mexico.

The chapter then turns to Moctezuma II, who is treated here as a ruler confronting an unprecedented diplomatic problem. His court sent repeated embassies, tried to influence the route and intentions of the strangers, and eventually received them in the capital under formal conditions. The meeting of 8 November 1519 on the causeway into Tenochtitlan was an act of diplomacy conducted through a chain of interpreters in which Malintzin was indispensable. Within about a week the visitors had seized their host. The chapter closes on that captivity and on the unstable equilibrium it created, which is the situation Chapter 32 inherits.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. Cortés's Second Letter to Charles V, written in 1520, is a legal and political argument as much as a narrative, composed to defend a commander who had no clear authorization. Bernal Díaz del Castillo wrote decades later, partly to correct Francisco López de Gómara. Nahua accounts, notably Book 12 of the Florentine Codex, were composed after the conquest under colonial conditions. The Tlaxcalan perspective survives chiefly in the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, a pictorial manuscript, and in the later history of Diego Muñoz Camargo, both produced by a community that had strong reasons to emphasize the value of its alliance with Spain. Each source has a purpose, and none gives transparent access to what was said on the road, in Tlaxcala, in Cholula, or on the causeway.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It begins where Chapter 30 ended, with the expedition departing the coast, and it stops at the captivity of Moctezuma. It does not narrate Narváez's expedition, the departure of Cortés from the capital, the Tóxcatl massacre, the uprising, the Noche Triste, the smallpox epidemic, or the siege of 1521. Those belong to Chapter 32. The task here is to show that the road to Tenochtitlan was built by Indigenous political decisions and that the army on it was becoming, in manpower, overwhelmingly Indigenous.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the expedition's movement from the Gulf coast into the central highlands and the Indigenous labor, guidance, and provisioning that made it possible.
  2. Explain Tlaxcala as a composite, multiethnic polity governed collectively, and distinguish it from simplified labels such as "republic."
  3. Analyze the September 1519 fighting between Tlaxcalans and the expedition without attributing the outcome to technological superiority alone.
  4. Evaluate the roles of Xicotencatl the Elder, Maxixcatzin, and Xicotencatl the Younger while avoiding a simple "war faction versus peace faction" story.
  5. Assess why Tlaxcalan rulers moved from resistance to alliance, and why neither "submission" nor "liberation" adequately describes that decision.
  6. Trace how the Tlaxcalan alliance changed the expedition's manpower, logistics, intelligence, and political position.
  7. Explain Cholula's religious, commercial, and political importance and its rivalry with Tlaxcala before the massacre.
  8. Interpret competing accounts of the Cholula massacre and distinguish what is established from what is disputed.
  9. Describe Moctezuma II's diplomacy before and during the approach to Tenochtitlan as political decision-making rather than prophecy-driven passivity.
  10. Analyze the meeting of 8 November 1519 as formal diplomacy and evaluate the reliability of speeches attributed to Moctezuma.
  11. Explain the seizure of Moctezuma, conventionally dated 14 November 1519, and its consequences for Mexica government and Spanish security.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part VII's argument that the conquest was a multinational conflict shaped by Indigenous diplomacy and rivalry.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Tlaxcala fight the expedition before allying with it, and what does the sequence reveal about Tlaxcalan decision-making?
  2. How should historians describe Tlaxcala's alliance if "conquest by Spain" and "liberation from the Aztecs" are both inadequate?
  3. In what ways did the alliance change what the expedition could do, and what did it cost Tlaxcala?
  4. What made Cholula politically sensitive, and how did its relations with Tlaxcala and the Mexica shape the events there?
  5. How can historians treat an episode like the Cholula massacre when the main justifying source was written by the person responsible and has been challenged?
  6. What choices did Moctezuma II and his advisers face, and why might receiving the strangers have seemed a defensible policy?
  7. What can and cannot be known about what was said on the causeway on 8 November 1519?
  8. Why did the expedition's entry into the capital produce, within days, a crisis that Cortés resolved by taking the ruler captive?
  9. How does the chapter's evidence support or complicate the claim that Indigenous opponents of Mexica power were decisive?

Historical Context

Chapters 13 through 15 described the Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan and its tribute empire, and Chapter 15 introduced the polities that remained outside its control, including Tlaxcala. Chapter 30 showed how the Spaniards first met that world from the coast: through the tribute grievances of the Totonacs, through envoys of Moctezuma II, and through the interpreting skills of Malintzin and Jerónimo de Aguilar. By late summer 1519 the expedition had a legal base in Villa Rica and a Totonac partnership, but it had not yet faced a major Indigenous state in battle.

The central highlands were a densely settled, politically fragmented landscape. The Basin of Mexico held a chain of lakeside altepetl, with Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco at the center, and its population is estimated in the high hundreds of thousands, with large uncertainty. East of the Basin, across mountain passes, lay the Puebla-Tlaxcala region, where Tlaxcala, Huexotzinco, and Cholula formed a zone of competing polities. Relations among them were shaped by warfare, ritual combat, trade, marriage, and shifting alliance. The Triple Alliance had conquered much of the surrounding territory but had never absorbed Tlaxcala, which reportedly had limited access to goods such as cotton and salt, a hardship that Spanish writers noted and that Tlaxcalan memory later emphasized.

Spanish assumptions complicated what the newcomers perceived. The expedition's leaders carried the habits of Caribbean conquest and the Iberian practice of exploiting local divisions, and they described Indigenous rulers with titles such as "king" and "lord" that approximated but did not match Nahua institutions. They also wrote under pressure to justify their conduct. Cortés in particular was acting without secure royal authorization, so his letters present each decision as lawful, necessary, and loyal. Historians therefore read the sources against one another and with attention to what each author needed the story to prove.

Modern scholarship has also revised older narratives. The idea that the Mexica took Cortés for a returning god, rejected in Chapter 30, is not relied on here. Nor does this chapter accept the older image of Tlaxcala as simply a loyal ally of Spain, an image promoted by Tlaxcalan petitioners themselves in the colonial period because it secured privileges. Tlaxcalan leaders were acting in a political situation they understood far better than the Spaniards did, and the chapter treats them accordingly.

Section 31.1

From the Gulf Coast into the Central Highlands

The expedition left the Totonac region in mid-August 1519. Before departing, Cortés had arranged the defense of Villa Rica, leaving a garrison under Juan de Escalante, and had taken steps, described in Spanish accounts, to disable the ships and so remove the possibility of mass desertion. Cempoala and neighboring communities supplied warriors and porters for the march. The Spanish accounts give figures in the low thousands for Totonac fighters and in the hundreds for carriers, but these numbers are estimates written by participants and should be treated cautiously.

The route climbed from the tropical coastal plain through successive ecological zones to the cold, high country of the central plateau. The change was physically harsh. Spanish sources describe cold nights, hail, and thin air as the column crossed the mountains, and they note that the road ran through communities that were sometimes hostile, sometimes tribute-paying subjects of the Triple Alliance, and sometimes ready to talk. Local guides were essential, and so was the labor of the tamemes, the porters who carried artillery, ammunition, and baggage. Without Indigenous provisioning, the horses and the cannon could not have crossed the sierra.

Information traveled along the road as well. Totonac leaders warned the Spaniards about Mexica garrisons and advised them about Tlaxcala, according to Spanish narratives, and some accounts claim they urged the expedition to avoid the Tlaxcalans. Whatever the exact advice, the Spaniards were moving into a landscape of rivalries they only partly grasped. The force that crossed into Tlaxcalan territory was already a coalition in embryo: Spanish soldiers with horses and firearms, Totonac warriors and porters, and interpreters who connected them to the languages of the highlands.

Section 31.2

Tlaxcala: An Independent and Composite Indigenous Polity

Tlaxcala is often called a "republic," a label that appears in Spanish sources and in later writing. It is a useful shorthand for collective rule but a misleading one if students imagine a modern state. Tlaxcala was a confederation-like polity of Nahua and Otomí communities, centered on several principal altepetl or ruling sectors. These are conventionally identified as Tepeticpac, Ocotelulco, Tizatlan, and Quiahuiztlan, although the exact nature and boundaries of the divisions, and how they related to one another, remain debated. Each had its own ruling house and subordinate settlements, and major decisions required negotiation among them.

The polity's population included Nahua speakers and Otomí communities, among others, and Otomí settlements held important frontier zones. Tlaxcala was not isolated from the broader world. It traded, intermarried, and fought with its neighbors, and it participated in the ritual warfare practiced among the polities of the Puebla region. What distinguished it from most of its neighbors was that it had resisted incorporation into the Triple Alliance's tribute empire. Spanish accounts, and Tlaxcalan memory preserved in sources such as Muñoz Camargo, describe a state surrounded by Triple Alliance territory and cut off from certain imports. The extent of that blockade and its effects are difficult to measure and may have been emphasized in later retellings.

For the Spaniards, Tlaxcala was a surprise. They had heard of it from Totonac informants and had a vague sense that it was powerful, but they did not understand its structure. For the Tlaxcalans, the arrival of a foreign force with Totonac allies, tribute-paying subjects of their old enemy, posed a problem to be assessed carefully. Did the newcomers represent a new tool of the Triple Alliance, a new threat, or a new opportunity? The answers were not obvious, and the polity's leaders did not agree at once, which is the context for the events of the following sections.

Historical Vocabulary
Cabecera

The cabecera (Spanish, "head town") was a term Spaniards used for the principal town of an Indigenous jurisdiction, as distinct from its dependent settlements. Colonial writers applied it to the four principal divisions of Tlaxcala, and the word became part of the administrative vocabulary of New Spain.

The term is a Spanish translation of a political reality, and, like "king" or "cacique," it is an approximation. Using it helps students see that Tlaxcala was a polity with several centers of authority, and that decisions required coordination among them. It should not be read as implying that the four divisions were equal or that their relations were fixed.

Section 31.3

War between Tlaxcalans and the Expedition

The first clashes occurred on the Tlaxcalan frontier in late August or early September 1519. Spanish accounts describe an initial skirmish with a small force and then a much larger battle on open ground near Tecoac, in which the expedition faced large numbers of warriors, many of them Otomí and others drawn from Tlaxcalan communities. The Spanish sources give enormous figures for the Tlaxcalan forces, in the tens of thousands, which are almost certainly exaggerated, though the armies were clearly large. Those same sources acknowledge that the Spaniards came close to being overwhelmed.

The fighting was serious. Tlaxcalan warriors fought in organized units under commanders, used slings, bows, clubs set with obsidian blades, and spears, and attacked in coordinated waves. They were particularly dangerous because many aimed to capture their enemies alive, a practice that Spanish writers sometimes noted and that complicates the image of undisciplined mass assault. Horses and firearms mattered, especially in open country, but Spanish narratives themselves record wounded men, exhausted soldiers, and nights of acute anxiety. The Totonac auxiliaries fought alongside the Spaniards and bore casualties that the Spanish sources record unevenly.

Technology did not decide the outcome. Cavalry could break formations on level ground but was less useful in broken terrain, and the expedition's guns and crossbows were few and slow to reload. The Tlaxcalans adapted tactically, attacking at different times, including a night assault that the Spanish sources present as a surprise. The Spaniards also conducted raids against settlements, burning houses and taking captives, actions that expanded the violence and that Spanish writers described in the language of necessity. According to Spanish accounts, Cortés also ordered the mutilation of captured men he suspected of spying, a story that, like much in these narratives, comes from the perpetrators and may be selectively told.

The fighting lasted roughly two weeks and was accompanied by diplomacy from the start. Messengers passed between the sides, and Tlaxcalan leaders probed the intentions of the strangers while testing their strength. The war was therefore not a simple prelude to alliance. It was an episode in which each side assessed the other, and in which the Tlaxcalan leadership concluded, after real losses, that a different policy was warranted.

Section 31.4

Xicotencatl, Maxixcatzin, and Diplomacy during War

Tlaxcalan decision-making was collective, and the sources name several leaders. Xicotencatl the Elder, associated with Tizatlan, is described as a senior and respected figure. Maxixcatzin, associated with Ocotelulco, appears in Spanish and Tlaxcalan accounts as a leading advocate of alliance. Xicotencatl the Younger, the elder man's son, is presented as a military commander who led forces in the field. Other rulers, including those of Tepeticpac and Quiahuiztlan, also took part in the discussions, though the sources say less about them.

A common narrative casts the Younger as a hot-headed warrior opposed to a peace party led by his father and Maxixcatzin. That framing is too neat. It derives partly from Spanish sources that wanted a story of reasonable allies and a defeated war faction, and partly from later Tlaxcalan accounts shaped by the experience of alliance. The evidence suggests a more complex situation. Tlaxcalan leaders weighed continued resistance, the cost of war, the possibility of using the Spaniards against the Triple Alliance, and the risk that the strangers were already tied to Mexica diplomacy. These calculations involved competing sectors with different interests, and a military commander could reasonably judge the options differently from rulers responsible for the whole polity.

The Mexica, for their part, were not absent. According to Spanish accounts, Mexica envoys encouraged the expedition to avoid Tlaxcala and to go to Cholula instead, and Tlaxcalans suspected that the Spaniards might be agents of Tenochtitlan. The Tlaxcalan leaders therefore had to decide not only what the Spaniards could do to them, but what the Spaniards could do for or against the Triple Alliance. Their willingness to negotiate while fighting, and to absorb what they learned about the strangers' relationship to Moctezuma, reflects the sophistication of a polity accustomed to managing threats from a much larger neighbor.

Section 31.5

From Enemies to Allies: Why Tlaxcala Changed Course

By mid-September 1519, the Tlaxcalan leadership ended the fighting. The decision followed costly engagements and reflected a strategic judgment: that the strangers, though dangerous, might be turned into partners against the Triple Alliance. Spanish accounts describe the arrival of Tlaxcalan envoys offering peace and describe Xicotencatl the Elder and Maxixcatzin as meeting Cortés and offering hospitality. Tlaxcalan sources present the choice as a considered act of statecraft.

Several motives can be inferred, though the sources do not record them in the Tlaxcalans' own voice. The first was security. The Tlaxcalans had resisted Triple Alliance expansion for decades, and a force that had withstood their armies, and that was in conflict with Mexica tribute, was a potentially valuable partner. The second was autonomy. A coalition including a powerful outside force might weaken Tenochtitlan and strengthen Tlaxcala's position. The third was opportunity: alliance offered a chance at revenge against regional enemies, access to trade routes, and a share in whatever the coalition won. The fourth was risk management. Having tested the expedition, Tlaxcalan leaders knew it could not easily be destroyed and might become hostile if rebuffed.

This was neither submission to Spain nor simple liberation from Aztec oppression. The first phrase assumes the Tlaxcalans were conquered, but they negotiated from a position of political strength and retained their own governance. The second imports a later colonial story. Tlaxcalans did have grievances against the Triple Alliance, but they were pursuing a calculated advantage and bore enormous risk. They did not foresee that the alliance would eventually bring Spanish rule over them as well, and the privileges Tlaxcala obtained in the colonial period, examined in later chapters, were won through negotiation after the fact, using the memory of this alliance as a bargaining tool.

The alliance was consolidated through hospitality, gifts, and political negotiation in Tlaxcala itself. The expedition was housed and fed for roughly three weeks while it recovered from its wounds. During this time, Spaniards and Tlaxcalans exchanged information, the Spaniards presented their claims about the Spanish king and the Christian God, and the two sides planned the next stage. Some Spanish reports describe Cortés destroying idols and Tlaxcalan leaders resisting religious demands, which suggests that the partnership was negotiated rather than dictated.

Section 31.6

The Indigenous Coalition Takes Shape

The practical effects of the alliance were large. The Totonac auxiliaries who had come from the coast were now joined by Tlaxcalan warriors, porters, guides, cooks, messengers, and political advisers. These people carried loads, prepared food, scouted routes, and conveyed information about Cholula, the Basin of Mexico, and the Mexica court. Their numbers are not known precisely. Spanish accounts speak of thousands of Tlaxcalan warriors accompanying the expedition at various stages, and though these figures should be treated as estimates, the general picture is clear: the army advancing toward Tenochtitlan was becoming predominantly Indigenous in manpower. Spanish leadership and weapons remained important, but they now rested on a much larger Indigenous base.

Elite relationships reinforced the political bond. Tlaxcalan leaders gave women to Spanish captains, including, according to Spanish and Tlaxcalan sources, daughters of Xicotencatl the Elder. Such transfers carried political meaning in Mesoamerican diplomacy, signaling alliance and creating kinship-like ties. They also took place under conditions of unequal power, among men who were armed strangers, and they were accompanied by baptism and by Spanish assumptions that made the women's position more precarious. Historians therefore treat these women as participants in diplomacy whose actual choices and experiences are largely invisible in the sources. One of them, associated in Spanish accounts with Pedro de Alvarado, appears again in later records, but the fuller details of her life lie outside this chapter.

The coalition also changed the expedition's intelligence. Through Tlaxcalan informants and through Malintzin, who by now spoke Spanish and translated more directly, Cortés learned about the Basin, its lakes, its causeways, and the rivalries within the Triple Alliance. He also learned about Cholula, which the Tlaxcalans regarded with suspicion. That information shaped his decisions in the following weeks, and it was supplied by Indigenous people with their own agendas. The Tlaxcalans were not neutral informants. They were shaping the Spaniards' picture of the region in ways that served Tlaxcalan interests.

Section 31.7

From Tlaxcala toward Cholula

Cholula was one of the great religious and commercial centers of central Mexico and, in the Postclassic period, one of Mesoamerica's most important centers of the cult of Quetzalcoatl. Its famous Great Pyramid, the Tlachihualtepetl, was a much older sacred monument that had largely fallen out of active monumental use centuries before 1519; Cholula's principal Postclassic ceremonial center, including the major Temple of Quetzalcoatl, lay elsewhere in the city. Pilgrims came from far and wide. The city was a center of craft production, particularly fine ceramics, and a marketplace linked to long-distance trade. Its political arrangements were not those of Tlaxcala: it was governed by a group of leaders in a more ritually focused system, and its relationship to the Triple Alliance was closer than Tlaxcala's, although the details are debated.

The Tlaxcalans and Cholulans were rivals. Tlaxcalan leaders, according to Spanish accounts, warned Cortés that the Cholulans were treacherous and that the Mexica had influence in the city, and they proposed that the expedition avoid it. Cortés, who wanted to continue toward Tenochtitlan along a route that included Cholula, insisted on going, and the Mexica embassies were reportedly encouraging the same plan. The Tlaxcalans did not oppose the march and agreed to accompany it, but they pitched their camp outside the city rather than entering it, a detail recorded in Spanish accounts.

Why was Cholula politically sensitive? Because every party had a stake in it. Tlaxcala saw a rival; the Mexica had interests and possibly forces in the vicinity; Cholula's leaders were trying to manage the arrival of a large armed column with Tlaxcalan allies at their gates; and the Spaniards were advancing toward the capital and feared a trap. Students should hold these overlapping stakes in mind before turning to what happened, because the sources describing the events are all shaped by one or another of these interests.

Section 31.8

The Cholula Massacre and the Problem of Evidence

The expedition and its allies reached Cholula in mid-October 1519 and were received with hospitality. Spanish accounts say that after several days, food supplies diminished, Cholulan leaders became less forthcoming, and signs of hostility appeared. They then describe the discovery of an alleged plot: that the Cholulans, at Mexica instigation, planned to ambush the Spaniards as they left the city. Cortés's Second Letter and Díaz attribute this discovery partly to information from a woman of Cholula who confided in Malintzin, and partly to interrogation of local leaders and priests.

On the basis of this information, according to the Spanish narratives, Cortés assembled the Cholulan nobility in a courtyard, accused them, and then ordered an attack. Spanish soldiers, Tlaxcalans, and Totonacs killed very large numbers of people, and the city was then sacked. Estimates of the dead vary widely, from a few thousand in Cortés's own account to far higher figures in later critics such as Bartolomé de las Casas. None is reliable. What is clear is that the death toll was large, that Indigenous allies participated, and that the violence extended beyond the nobles gathered in the courtyard.

Whether a conspiracy existed is disputed. Cortés had every incentive to justify the killing, since a massacre of hosts who had received him peacefully could have damaged his standing with the Crown. Other contemporary testimony, including reports gathered later, questioned whether the plot was real, and some modern historians suspect that it was either exaggerated or invented, or that Cortés acted on fears and rumors, perhaps encouraged by Tlaxcalan interests. Others accept that some form of Mexica-linked plotting may have occurred. The sources do not allow certainty, and this chapter does not offer it. Students should instead ask who benefited from each version and how each source's position shapes its claims.

The consequences are easier to state. The massacre terrorized communities along the route and demonstrated that the coalition was willing to use extreme violence. It also reinforced the bond between Spaniards and Tlaxcalans, who participated in the slaughter and took plunder, and it deepened the rivalry between Tlaxcala and Cholula. After the killing, Cortés arranged a settlement of sorts, installing new Cholulan leaders and reconciling Cholula and Tlaxcala, at least formally. The news reached Tenochtitlan, and Moctezuma's court had to weigh it in deciding how to respond to the strangers.

Section 31.9

Moctezuma's Diplomacy and the Approach to the Basin of Mexico

Moctezuma II had been in contact with the expedition since April, and his court had sent embassies, gifts, and requests throughout. The Spanish accounts describe messages asking the Spaniards not to advance, then messages offering tribute to the Spanish king in exchange for their withdrawal, and then, as the expedition continued, increasingly ambiguous communications. The reasons for these shifts are not recorded in his own words. Historians infer that the court was debating how to respond to an unprecedented situation: a foreign force that had defeated or allied with Tlaxcala, that had broken a tribute relationship on the coast, and that now stood at the edge of the Basin.

Several policies were available. Moctezuma could attack the expedition in the passes or at Cholula, which risked a major battle with uncertain results and with a coalition now including Tlaxcalans. He could try to buy off the Spaniards with gifts, which had backfired by revealing wealth. He could attempt to control them by receiving them in the capital under conditions he set, observing their strength, and using the Triple Alliance's resources to contain them. The sources suggest that the Mexica ruler gravitated toward the third option, though accounts differ and some Spanish narratives claim he sought to lure the expedition into a trap. Moctezuma should be understood as a ruler weighing risks within a political system that included powerful rival lords, not as a figure paralyzed by prophecy.

The coalition advanced in late October and early November through the passes between the volcanoes Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl and descended into the Basin. At various points, Spanish sources describe messengers urging them to turn back, local leaders offering lodging, and communities reluctantly acknowledging the newcomers. Some lords, notably Cacama of Texcoco, came to meet them and escort them. The coalition passed through lakeside towns, including Amecameca and Ayotzingo, before reaching Iztapalapa, whose ruler Cuitláhuac, Moctezuma's brother, hosted them. The Spaniards described the dense population and the sight of the lakes and cities with wonder, as Díaz recalled long afterward, though his rhetoric of astonishment is part of a literary account.

Section 31.10

8 November 1519: Moctezuma and the Entry into Tenochtitlan

On 8 November 1519, the expedition and its allies moved along the causeway from Iztapalapa toward Tenochtitlan. Spanish and Nahua sources agree that the meeting was formal. Moctezuma came out with an entourage of lords, including Cacama of Texcoco, Cuitláhuac, and rulers or representatives of other altepetl, and was carried in a litter with attendants. Cortés dismounted. According to Spanish sources, the two exchanged gestures of greeting and ceremonial gifts, including necklaces, and Moctezuma then arranged for the visitors to be lodged.

Interpreters made the conversation possible. Malintzin translated between Nahuatl and, increasingly, Spanish, with Aguilar's help when needed. This meant that the words exchanged passed through at least two minds, each of which could emphasize, soften, or omit. Spanish and Nahua accounts record speeches by Moctezuma, but their content differs, and none can be treated as transcript. Cortés's Second Letter presents Moctezuma as acknowledging the Spanish king as his lord and effectively transferring sovereignty. This account had an obvious legal purpose, since it supported the claim that the Mexica had submitted voluntarily, and scholars generally regard it as a rhetorical construction. Nahua sources present a different picture, of a ruler giving a speech of welcome and hospitality, though these too were composed after the conquest.

The Spaniards and their allies were housed in the palace associated with Axayácatl, a former ruler, near the sacred precinct. It was a large complex, and the lodging of the allied forces there and elsewhere in the city must have required considerable organization by Mexica officials. Hospitality in Mesoamerican diplomacy was both honor and surveillance. A host could feed and control visitors, and accommodation in a defined space gave the Mexica a way to observe the strangers while limiting their movement. The meeting, in other words, was a diplomatic act with several possible meanings, and the participants may have interpreted it differently.

Section 31.11

Guests, Hosts, and an Uneasy Occupation

In the first days inside the city, the Spaniards and their allies observed Tenochtitlan with intense interest. Spanish accounts describe visits to the market at Tlatelolco, the temple precinct, and the palaces, and they record astonishment at the scale and order of the city. Population estimates for Tenochtitlan in this period are uncertain, with figures ranging from the low hundreds of thousands downward, and Spanish authors tended to exaggerate for effect. Whatever the precise number, the imbalance was stark: a few hundred Spaniards and some thousands of allies were lodged among a population many times larger.

Both sides had reasons to be uneasy. The Spaniards were aware that the Mexica could cut the causeways, which were also the city's connections to the mainland, and that they were dependent on their host for food. They also tried to impress on Moctezuma their religious claims, in tension with Mexica ritual life, and Spanish accounts describe friction over the display of Christian symbols and over human sacrifice. The Mexica, for their part, were divided. Some lords, including Cacama, seem to have favored accommodation, while others, including, in several sources, Cuitláhuac, were more skeptical. The court had to feed the visitors, manage their demands, and gather intelligence while deciding how to act.

The allies occupied an unusual position. Tlaxcalans were in the capital of their long-time enemy, as guests of a ruler whose empire they had resisted, accompanying a force that had been involved in recent violence at Cholula. The Totonacs were similarly exposed. Their presence in the city, and the mutual suspicion they must have generated, added to the volatility. The Spaniards in turn depended on these allies as the larger part of their strength, a fact that shaped how Cortés weighed any decision that might lead to fighting in the city.

In this situation, the political equilibrium was fragile. The visitors had entered by permission and were being hosted, but neither side trusted the other, and each side could imagine the other preparing to strike. The Spanish captains had good reason to consider how they might secure themselves. The Mexica court had good reason to consider how to remove them. What happened next was Cortés's answer to the problem.

Section 31.12

The Captivity of Moctezuma and the Breakdown of Diplomatic Equilibrium

Within about a week of entering Tenochtitlan, Cortés seized Moctezuma and moved him under Spanish guard into the quarters of Axayácatl. The conventional date is around 14 November 1519, though the sources are not precise. Spanish narratives justify the act partly by reference to events on the coast: reports that Mexica-affiliated forces under a lord named Quauhpopoca had attacked Spaniards and Totonac allies near Villa Rica, killing Juan de Escalante and others. Cortés presented the seizure as a measure necessary to hold the ruler responsible and to secure the expedition. As with Cholula, the justification is self-interested evidence, and historians treat it with caution.

Accounts of how Cortés persuaded or compelled Moctezuma to go with him vary. Spanish narratives say the ruler agreed, after argument and under threat, to move to the Spanish quarters, claiming he went of his own will. Nahua sources and later critics doubt that the move was voluntary. Whatever outward forms of consent were maintained, Moctezuma's freedom of action was now sharply constrained and the Spaniards used his presence to influence Mexica government. Historians commonly describe the arrangement as captivity or hostage-taking, although the precise nature of his status is debated: the sources also describe him retaining attendants, receiving visitors, issuing instructions, and continuing to perform aspects of rulership. Cortés himself says he decided to place Moctezuma under his control, so coercion is well attested, but some scholars, going back at least to Francis Brooks and renewed in recent work, argue that the sources portray a more ambiguous, negotiated arrangement. This partial visibility of the captivity to outsiders also complicated the Mexica response. Quauhpopoca was brought to the city and punished, and Spanish accounts describe a harsh treatment of him and others that reinforced the ruler's humiliation.

The consequences were immediate and contradictory. For the Spaniards, holding the ruler gave them leverage and a degree of protection. For the Mexica court, it created a crisis of authority: a tlatoani who was a captive could not lead, and rivals such as Cuitláhuac and Cacama had reason to reassess their positions. For the Indigenous allies, it raised the stakes of the coalition, since they were now in the city of their enemy alongside a force that had humiliated its ruler. The expedition had reached the center of Mexica power, but it was trapped within a metropolis that retained the capacity to resist, while holding as its security the very person whose captivity would make resistance more likely.

This is the point at which the chapter stops. The arrival of Pánfilo de Narváez, Cortés's departure from the capital, the Tóxcatl festival and the violence under Alvarado, the uprising, the death of Moctezuma, the Noche Triste, the smallpox epidemic, and the siege all belong to Chapter 32. What this chapter has established is the foundation for that conflict: that the road to Tenochtitlan was made by Indigenous political decisions, and that the situation in November 1519 was not a conquest completed but a precarious occupation with an uncertain future.

Historical Vocabulary
Carta de relación

A carta de relación ("letter of account") was a formal report written by a conquest leader to the Crown narrating his actions. Cortés wrote several, and the Second Letter, composed in 1520, covers the events of this chapter.

These letters were legal and political documents. Cortés wrote to justify an expedition launched without secure authorization, and so each decision appears as lawful and necessary. Students should read the letters as valuable testimony and as advocacy, and compare them with Indigenous and later Spanish accounts.

Primary Source Window

Accounts of the Meeting of Moctezuma and Cortés in Spanish and Nahua Sources

Creators: Hernán Cortés (Second Letter); Nahua authors and painters of the Florentine Codex, Book 12 · Dates: Cortés wrote in 1520; the Florentine Codex was compiled in the 1550s–1570s · Type: A legal-political report to the Crown and an Indigenous-authored colonial-era history

Context: Cortés wrote to Charles V to justify his actions and to claim that Moctezuma had willingly submitted. The Florentine Codex was compiled by Indigenous scholars working with the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún, decades after the fall of Tenochtitlan, under Spanish supervision and with the memory of defeat.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: Cortés presents a speech in which Moctezuma recognizes the Spanish king as his rightful lord and the Spaniards as emissaries of a ruler long expected, thereby giving a legal basis for Spanish authority. The Nahua account describes a formal reception on the causeway in which Moctezuma greets the visitors with honor and hospitality, and presents the encounter as ceremonial diplomacy whose consequences later proved disastrous.

Analysis: Both accounts were written after the events and in service of later purposes. Cortés needed legitimacy and gave the speech a juridical shape. The Nahua account reflects retrospective understanding of catastrophe, and may import later meanings. Neither preserves verbatim speech, since the words were translated through a chain of interpreters and then reworked in writing. Historians cannot recover what Moctezuma actually said or meant. They can say that the meeting was formal, that it involved gift exchange and hospitality, and that Spanish and Indigenous authors each shaped it to explain what followed.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was There a Plot at Cholula?

The question: Did Cholulan leaders, perhaps at Mexica urging, plan to ambush the expedition, or was the claim of a conspiracy a justification for a massacre that Cortés needed to defend?

Interpretation A — A real or plausible plot: Some historians accept that Cholulan or Mexica-linked planning against the expedition was plausible. The Spaniards were an armed force allied with Cholula's rivals, the Mexica had interests in the region, and the reduction of food supplies and other signs fit a shift toward hostility. On this view, Cortés's information may have been partly accurate, even if his response was brutal and exaggerated.
Interpretation B — A pretext or a mistaken panic: Other historians emphasize that the only detailed justification comes from the person who ordered the massacre, who needed to defend it. Contemporary and later testimony questioned the plot, and Tlaxcalan interests in weakening a rival may have shaped the warnings. On this view, the story may have been invented or exaggerated, or may reflect a spiral of fear and rumor.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited corpus: Cortés's letter, Díaz, other Spanish accounts, critics such as Las Casas, and later Indigenous recollections. None gives a Cholulan voice contemporary with the events.

Why it matters: The question affects how the conquest is understood. If the plot was real, the massacre looks like preemption. If it was a pretext, it looks like terror used to consolidate control. Either way, the event shows how a legal narrative shaped what Spanish writers recorded. The debate also illustrates the limits of the evidence: historians can establish that a large massacre occurred and that Indigenous allies took part, but they cannot settle motive with confidence.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, and identify what kind of additional evidence, if any, could change the balance.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Alliance, Massacre, and Reception

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom.

Tlaxcalan rulers: After costly fighting, they judged that partnership could serve their autonomy and security against the Triple Alliance. They negotiated from a position of strength and accepted serious risks.
Cholulans: They faced the arrival of a large armed column accompanied by their rivals. Whatever their leaders intended, the people of the city bore the violence that followed, and their own account is missing from the sources.
Mexica emissaries and Moctezuma's court: They tried to learn about, influence, and contain an unprecedented force, and they debated whether to resist, bargain, or receive the strangers.
Spanish expedition members: They were few, legally insecure, and dependent on allies, and they read events through suspicion and the logic of Caribbean conquest.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Coastal Partnership to Highland Coalition

What Changed

  • The expedition went from a small coastal partnership with the Totonacs to a coalition including a major independent polity
  • Indigenous manpower came to outnumber Spanish manpower by a very large margin
  • Diplomacy moved from the periphery of the Triple Alliance to its center
  • Violence escalated, from battles on the frontier to a massacre and then the seizure of a ruler

What Stayed the Same

  • The expedition remained dependent on Indigenous food, labor, information, and interpretation
  • Spanish accounts remained self-justifying, with Cortés shaping events for the Crown
  • Indigenous allies pursued their own goals against the Triple Alliance

Who experienced change most directly: Tlaxcalans and Cholulans faced the sharpest consequences, one through alliance, the other through massacre. Greater continuity: Triple Alliance structures remained in place until the seizure of the ruler. Why the pattern matters: The scale and consequence of Tlaxcalan partnership far exceeded the coastal arrangements, and it shows how Indigenous choices, not just Spanish action, drove the conquest.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How the Tlaxcalan Alliance Transformed the Expedition

Long-term causes

Decades of Tlaxcalan resistance to the Triple Alliance, regional rivalries among Puebla-Tlaxcala polities, and tribute-related grievances across the periphery of the Mexica system.

Short-term causes

Costly fighting in September 1519, Tlaxcalan debate about how to use the newcomers, and the expedition's own need for allies after near disaster.

Event or transformation

Tlaxcalan rulers ended the war and entered a negotiated alliance with the expedition.

Immediate effects

Warriors, porters, guides, and political advisers joined the march; intelligence on Cholula and the Basin improved; elite ties were forged.

Long-term consequences

A coalition predominantly Indigenous in manpower reached Tenochtitlan; Tlaxcala later negotiated colonial privileges based on the alliance.

Historical complexity

The alliance was a calculated political choice with unforeseen consequences. It did not mean submission, and it did not by itself cause Mexica defeat.
Everyday Life and Social History

Hosts, Porters, and the Households That Fed the Coalition

Hosting the expedition for roughly three weeks required enormous labor from Tlaxcalan households. Women ground maize and prepared tortillas, farmers delivered food, and communities provided lodging and care for the wounded. The same was true in Cholula and, later, in the towns and in Tenochtitlan itself, where officials had to organize provisions for visitors. These burdens are recorded unevenly, because Spanish authors cared about strategy and Indigenous sources recorded events in retrospect.

Porters and warriors came from ordinary families, and their absence affected fields and households. Women given to Spanish captains entered a world of danger and forced change, including baptism and sexual exploitation. For the people of Cholula, the massacre destroyed families and neighborhoods, and the survivors had to rebuild under a new local leadership imposed by the invaders. The silences in the sources about these experiences are themselves evidence of whose lives the surviving texts recorded and whose they did not.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the expedition's movement from the Gulf coast to the captivity of Moctezuma II in November 1519 and argued that this movement was driven by Indigenous political decisions. The Spaniards did not march to Tenochtitlan as an independent force. They went as part of a coalition whose makeup changed on the road, and whose manpower, logistics, and intelligence were overwhelmingly Indigenous.

The expedition left the coast with Totonac warriors and porters and climbed into the central highlands, depending on guides and provisioning from the first days. By the time it reached Tlaxcalan territory, it was already a mixed force, though still small and vulnerable.

Tlaxcala was a composite, multiethnic polity governed by several principal divisions, including Nahua and Otomí communities, and it had resisted incorporation into the Triple Alliance. Its leaders fought the expedition in September 1519 in serious engagements that nearly overwhelmed the Spaniards. The fighting was accompanied by diplomacy, and it did not reflect a simple victory of technology over numbers.

Tlaxcalan leaders, including Xicotencatl the Elder, Maxixcatzin, and Xicotencatl the Younger, debated how to respond. Their politics were collective, and the popular image of a war faction opposed to a peace faction is too simple. After costly fighting, the rulers decided that alliance could serve Tlaxcalan interests in autonomy, security, and opportunity against the Triple Alliance. That decision was neither submission to Spain nor straightforward liberation from Aztec oppression.

The alliance changed the expedition's capacity. Tlaxcalan warriors, porters, guides, cooks, messengers, and advisers joined the march, and elite relationships, including transfers of women under unequal conditions, reinforced the political bond. The army advancing on Tenochtitlan was predominantly Indigenous in numbers, though Spanish leadership and weapons remained important.

At Cholula, a major religious and commercial center and a Tlaxcalan rival, the coalition committed a large-scale massacre. Spanish sources justify it as the preemption of a plot, while critics doubted that any plot existed. Historians cannot settle the question, but they can establish that many people died, that Tlaxcalans and others took part, and that the event altered perceptions of the coalition and deepened regional rivalries.

Moctezuma II confronted an unprecedented diplomatic problem. His court sent embassies and gifts, debated resistance and accommodation, and ultimately received the expedition in the capital on 8 November 1519. The meeting was formal diplomacy conducted through interpreters, and the speeches attributed to Moctezuma, especially Cortés's claim of a transfer of sovereignty, are best read as shaped by the legal and political purposes of the authors.

The visitors were lodged in the palace associated with Axayácatl in a city whose population far exceeded theirs. Hosts and guests distrusted one another, and the presence of Tlaxcalan and Totonac allies in the enemy capital heightened tension. Within about a week, Cortés seized Moctezuma, conventionally around 14 November, and held him under guard. The Spanish justification, rooted in events near Veracruz, is self-interested evidence.

The captivity produced a contradictory situation. The invaders held the ruler, but they were trapped in a metropolis with the capacity to resist, and the Mexica court faced a crisis of authority. This is the unstable situation Chapter 32 inherits. Students should also take away a method: sources written by participants served their authors' purposes, Indigenous accounts were composed under colonial conditions, and neither provides a transparent window on what was said and done in 1519.

  5. How do hospitality and surveillance overlap in the reception of the expedition?
  6. What does the participation of Indigenous allies in violence reveal about the nature of the coalition?

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Spanish and Tlaxcalan Accounts of the Alliance

This project asks you to compare how Spanish and Tlaxcalan sources describe the alliance and to evaluate what each can and cannot reveal.

  1. Select an episode. Choose one: the September fighting, the negotiation of alliance, or the Cholula massacre.
  2. Locate Spanish accounts. Using translated editions of Cortés's letters and Díaz's memoir, summarize how each describes the episode.
  3. Locate Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous accounts. Using selections from the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, Muñoz Camargo, or the Florentine Codex, summarize their treatment.
  4. Compare. Identify agreements, contradictions, and silences, considering each source's date, author, and purpose.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what can be reconstructed with confidence, what remains uncertain, and what evidence would help.

Library resources to use: Translated primary-source collections on the conquest of Mexico, scholarly studies of Tlaxcala and Nahua-language sources, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Mesoamerican history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the road to Tenochtitlan was built by Indigenous decisions. Tlaxcalan rulers, having fought the strangers and measured them, chose an alliance that served their own aims, and in doing so changed the scale of the enterprise. The force that reached the capital was a coalition whose manpower, logistics, and knowledge were largely Indigenous, and whose outcome depended on rivalries that long predated Spain.

The chapter also shows how violence and legitimacy were bound together. Cholula and the seizure of Moctezuma were justified in texts written to protect their author, and the surviving evidence reflects those purposes. Learning to read such sources critically, and to ask whose voices are missing, is a skill that recurs through Volume II, from the conquest of the Andes to the construction of colonial law.

Chapter 32 takes up the consequences: the arrival of a rival Spanish force, the breakdown of the occupation, the uprising, the epidemic of smallpox, and the siege that destroyed Tenochtitlan in 1521. Students should carry forward the view that the allies who entered the capital in 1519 had interests of their own, and that the story of the fall of the city is as much a story of Mesoamerican politics as of Spanish arms.
