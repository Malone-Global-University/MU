The Porfiriato and the Crisis of the Díaz Regime

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A President Who Spoke of Democracy

In March 1908 Mexican readers learned that the man who had dominated their government for most of three decades had told a foreign journalist that the country might be ready for political competition. The statement was not a program, but it opened a question the regime had long suppressed: what would happen when Porfirio Díaz was gone?

In March 1908 an interview with President Porfirio Díaz appeared in the American monthly Pearson's Magazine, and within weeks its contents were circulating in Mexican newspapers and political circles. The journalist, James Creelman, reported that Díaz regarded Mexico as having advanced far enough to tolerate opposition parties and that he would welcome them. At that moment Díaz was in his late seventies and presided over a country transformed by railways, mines, factories, banks, and foreign capital. For politicians, editors, and reformers who had been excluded from meaningful electoral contests, the interview was a signal. For the regime, it was an embarrassment that could not easily be withdrawn.

This chapter asks how a government that produced stability and economic growth also produced the grievances, opposition, and succession crisis that ended it. The question is harder than it appears. The older picture of the Porfiriato as a simple tyranny of landowners and foreigners over a uniformly impoverished people does not survive close study, because Mexico under Díaz experienced real modernization: a vast expansion of railways, fiscal recovery, new industries, and a stronger central administration. The opposite picture, in which prosperity vindicated the regime, fails as well, because growth was unevenly shared, political participation was narrowly managed, and the regime's methods of rural and industrial control generated resentments that growth could not dissolve.

The story begins earlier, in 1876, when Díaz himself rose to power through a rebellion under the banner of nonreelection. The irony of that origin runs through the chapter. A movement that condemned presidents who clung to office produced a president who, with constitutional amendments, local allies, and carefully managed elections, remained in office with one four-year interruption until 1911. Understanding how this was accomplished requires attention to institutions (governors, jefes políticos, the army, and the rural police), to ideas (the positivist language of order and progress), and to the bargains through which Díaz bound regional elites to the national government.

The chapter's middle sections turn to the economy and to the countryside. Railroads and telegraphs integrated markets; foreign and domestic capital financed mines and factories; the state under Finance Minister José Yves Limantour balanced its books. Yet land laws that promised private property and development enabled surveying companies and large estates to absorb enormous territories, often at the expense of villages and Indigenous peoples. Rural labor took many forms, from wage work to debt-bound servitude, and Indigenous groups such as the Yaqui of Sonora and the Maya of Yucatán confronted military campaigns and displacement. These pages avoid treating all of rural Mexico as one place, since conditions in Morelos differed sharply from those in Yucatán or Chihuahua.

The later sections show grievance becoming organization. The independent press, liberal clubs, and the circle around Ricardo Flores Magón criticized the regime, while the strikes at Cananea in 1906 and in the textile districts of Puebla, Tlaxcala, and Veracruz in 1906 and 1907 revealed that industrial workers could challenge employers and the state. After the economic disturbances of 1907 and the Creelman interview, the succession question became impossible to ignore. Francisco I. Madero, a wealthy landowner from Coahuila, built an antireelectionist movement, was jailed, and then proclaimed the Plan of San Luis Potosí in October 1910, calling for armed action on 20 November.

The evidence deserves attention from the start. Official statistics, government reports, and the regime's celebratory publications present a Mexico of progress; opposition newspapers, labor petitions, and the reporting of foreign journalists such as John Kenneth Turner present a Mexico of repression and exploitation. Each kind of source has a purpose. Land records, company archives, and the work of modern historians such as Friedrich Katz, Alan Knight, and Paul Garner allow comparisons across them. The chapter stops at the threshold of revolutionary war, with only a brief account of Díaz's resignation in May 1911. Chapter 72 takes up the revolutionary campaigns, Madero's government, and the civil wars that followed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Díaz's path from the Plan of Tuxtepec in 1876 to the consolidation of his authority, distinguishing his presidencies, the González interval of 1880–1884, and his continuous rule from 1884 to 1911.
  2. Distinguish constitutional forms from actual electoral competition, and explain the legal effects of the amendments of 1878, 1887, 1890, and 1904.
  3. Explain how governors, jefes políticos, the army, the rurales, and patronage networks combined bargaining with coercion.
  4. Analyze the ideas of positivism and the científicos without treating the regime's officials as a single faction.
  5. Assess the achievements of Porfirian modernization in railways, finance, mining, industry, and cities, and identify who benefited and who did not.
  6. Distinguish among direct investment, lending, concessions, and domestic participation in the Porfirian economy.
  7. Compare the land laws of 1856, 1883, and 1894, and explain how surveying and titling produced property concentration with regional variation.
  8. Compare rural labor systems in northern ranches, central haciendas, southern plantations, and Yucatán's henequen economy, and evaluate the evidence for the Yaqui deportations.
  9. Distinguish among liberal reformers, Magonistas, labor organizers, regional elites, and Maderistas as forms of opposition.
  10. Interpret the Cananea and Río Blanco conflicts as related but distinct episodes of industrial labor conflict.
  11. Evaluate the Díaz–Creelman interview as a statement for a foreign audience and as a trigger of domestic politics.
  12. Explain why constitutional opposition in 1908–1910 became a call to arms in the Plan of San Luis Potosí, and why Díaz's fall was contingent rather than inevitable.

Historical Context

Mexico in 1867 had just emerged from half a century of instability. Independence in 1821 (Chapters 52 and 59) had been followed by constitutional experiments, civil wars, the loss of half the national territory to the United States (Chapter 63), the Reform Wars, and the French Intervention that installed Maximilian of Habsburg as emperor. The liberal republic of Benito Juárez survived, and its victory over the Empire in 1867 gave liberals a claim to national legitimacy. Díaz, then a young general from Oaxaca who had fought at Puebla and retaken that city and the capital in 1867, emerged from the war with a considerable military reputation and an ambition for national office.

The liberal victory left unresolved problems. The treasury was weak, the army was large and partly disloyal, regional strongmen controlled local politics, and the Constitution of 1857 had been enforced inconsistently. The Reform laws had attacked the Church's corporate property and, in principle, the corporate landholding of villages, which liberals regarded as obstacles to progress. Chapter 63 examined those measures, and this chapter picks up their long consequences for rural Mexico. Foreign creditors remained wary after the Empire, and the country possessed few railways, which meant that much of its territory remained commercially isolated.

Chapters 68 and 69 described the Latin American export boom and the oligarchic states that governed it, and Chapter 70 closed Volume III with the new hemispheric order shaped by the United States after 1898. Chapter 71 is a national case study within that comparative frame. It does not repeat the regional survey, and it does not retell U.S. hemispheric policy. It explains how in Mexico the combination of export growth, foreign capital, concentrated landholding, and a managed political order produced a regime that looked successful from the outside and brittle from within.

Interpretation of the Porfiriato has changed. Nationalist and revolutionary historians, writing after 1920, tended to portray it as a dark age that the Revolution redeemed. Later scholars, including Daniel Cosío Villegas and his collaborators, documented the regime's political and economic workings in detail, while specialists such as John Coatsworth emphasized that growth coexisted with structural obstacles to broader development. Historians such as Alan Knight stressed that the Revolution grew from many regional causes and not from a single national grievance. This chapter draws on that scholarship and treats the Porfiriato as a period with real achievements, real costs, and an unfinished political settlement.

The Rise of Porfirio Díaz and the Construction of Authoritarian Rule

How did Díaz transform an antireelectionist rebellion into a durable regime while retaining the outward institutions of a constitutional republic?

Section 71.1

The Restored Republic, the Plan of Tuxtepec, and Díaz's Rise to Power, 1867–1877

The republic that Benito Juárez restored in 1867 was legitimate but fragile. Its leaders confronted a depleted treasury, regional strongmen, and a large army whose officers expected reward. Juárez was reelected in 1867 and again in 1871, each time over the opposition of liberals who thought a president of such long tenure endangered republican government. Díaz, who had served under Juárez and then retired to Oaxaca, ran against him in both contests. After the 1871 election was decided in Congress in Juárez's favor, Díaz proclaimed the Plan de la Noria in November, a rebellion framed as a defense of effective suffrage against reelection. It failed. Juárez died in July 1872, and his successor Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada offered an amnesty, allowing Díaz to return to private life.

Lerdo governed as a capable and reform-minded liberal. He elevated the Reform laws to constitutional status, supported railway concessions (the Mexico City–Veracruz line was completed in 1873), and sought to rebuild the state. He also made enemies. Regional chiefs resented his centralism, Catholics resented anticlerical measures, and military men saw him as indifferent to their interests. When Lerdo sought reelection in 1876, the stage was set for a second challenge. The movement that emerged was not a single conspiracy but a coalition of discontents that Díaz was well placed to lead.

The Plan of Tuxtepec was proclaimed in Oaxaca in January 1876, and Díaz joined its rebellion against Lerdo. It condemned the government, asserted constitutional principles and opposed presidential reelection. The March 21 revisions at Palo Blanco, signed by Díaz, sharpened the challenge to controlled elections and established a conditional route to provisional authority: the president of the Supreme Court, or the relevant magistrate, could assume the executive only by accepting the plan within a specified period; otherwise the military leader would exercise it. This provision mattered because Chief Justice José María Iglesias later challenged Lerdo's reelection on a different constitutional claim. The revolt gained strength through the year, while liberals divided between a legal succession claim and Díaz's military insurgency.

The decisive confrontation came at Tecoac, in Tlaxcala, on 16 November 1876, where Díaz's forces defeated those of Lerdo's government. Lerdo left the capital days later, and Díaz entered it in late November. Iglesias's movement collapsed and he went into exile. Díaz held a provisional presidency, and after elections he took office constitutionally in May 1877. The new president had won power by arms under a banner of constitutionalism, and he moved quickly to reduce the number of rival armed men and to build alliances with former opponents and with his own officers.

The irony is plain. A movement that denounced reelection placed in power a man who would remain in the presidency, apart from the interval of 1880–1884, until 1911. Historians have offered several explanations. Some stress Díaz's tactical skill and his willingness to bargain with former enemies. Others point to the weakness of rival national institutions and the widespread desire for order after decades of conflict. Still others emphasize that the principle of nonreelection was never abandoned as a slogan; it was subordinated to practical politics and then amended in law. The sections that follow examine each step.

Primary Source Window I

The Plan of Tuxtepec (1876), with the Palo Blanco Revisions

Identified source edition: University of St Andrews, Palo Blanco revisions of 21 March 1876.

Creators: Authors of the rebellion in Oaxaca, with Porfirio Díaz as its military leader; later revisions at Palo Blanco, March 1876 · Date: January 1876; revised March 1876 · Type: Political manifesto of rebellion

Context: The plan was issued by rebels who justified armed action against a sitting president by appealing to the Constitution. It circulated in printed editions and was reprinted in later documentary collections.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation The plan declared Lerdo's government illegitimate, upheld the Constitution and the Reform laws as the basis of national order, and called for the principle of effective suffrage and nonreelection. At Palo Blanco, Article VI offered interim executive authority to the Supreme Court's presiding officer only upon explicit acceptance of the plan; absent acceptance, military command assumed that authority.

Analysis: The plan was a document of mobilization rather than a constitutional treatise. Its antireelectionist language was sincere in the sense that many supporters believed in it, and strategic in the sense that it gave a legal color to a military revolt. Its limitations are those of all pronunciamientos: it records what the rebels wished to proclaim, not what they would do once in power. Students should compare it with the amendments discussed in the next section. The voices of rural people, who supported or opposed the movement for local reasons, do not appear in the text.

Questions for students:

  1. What principles does the plan invoke to justify rebellion?
  2. Who wrote it, and what audiences did its authors hope to persuade?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the politics of 1876?
  4. What can the plan not tell us about how Díaz governed after 1877?
Section 71.2

Constitutional Government, Presidential Reelection, and the Consolidation of Personal Power

Díaz's first presidency, from 1877 to 1880, was devoted to consolidating control. He rotated military commanders, rewarded loyal officers with posts and concessions, negotiated with regional leaders, and worked to restore Mexico's standing with foreign creditors. In 1878 a constitutional amendment prohibited immediate reelection of the president and governors, a measure that gave legal form to the Tuxtepec principle. When his term ended, Díaz observed it. In December 1880 he handed the presidency to his ally Manuel González, a general who had fought alongside him and who governed until 1884.

González's administration was not simply a placeholder. It saw railway concessions, an expansion of banking, a conflict over foreign debt, and the colonization law of 1883, discussed in Section 71.9. It also saw charges of corruption and a financial crisis that left the treasury strained. Díaz, meanwhile, served as governor of Oaxaca and as a minister, and he remained a central figure. When the 1884 election approached, he was widely seen as the stabilizing alternative, and he returned to the presidency in December 1884 after the constitutionally required interval. From that point until his resignation in May 1911 his rule was continuous.

The legal path to continuity was gradual. A reform of 1887 allowed one immediate reelection. In 1890 the restriction was removed for the presidency, permitting indefinite reelection. Díaz was reelected in 1888, 1892, 1896, and 1900. In 1904 an amendment extended the presidential term to six years and restored the office of vice president, which the 1857 framework had lacked, a change widely understood as a provision for succession given the president's age. Díaz was reelected in 1904 with Ramón Corral as vice president and again in 1910. Each amendment passed a compliant Congress with the formal procedures of the 1857 Constitution observed.

Historians distinguish the constitution as written from the constitution as practiced. Elections took place, ballots were cast, and congresses met. But the competition that would have given these forms meaning was largely absent. Electoral rolls, local officials, and party machinery were controlled by the government and its allies, and opposition candidacies for the presidency were rarely viable. Congress, the judiciary, and state legislatures rarely checked the executive. The regime's characteristic achievement was thus a form of constitutional authoritarianism, in which the legitimacy of the 1857 Constitution was preserved while its competitive promises were not honored.

The consequences of this arrangement became clear only gradually. For decades the regime benefited from the appearance of legality and from widespread fatigue with civil war. But because legitimacy had been tied to one man and to a system of personal arrangements, the question of succession became increasingly dangerous. As Díaz aged and as new generations of Mexicans came to political awareness, the gap between constitutional language and political practice became a source of criticism, which later sections trace.

Section 71.3

Governors, Jefes Políticos, Rurales, and the Machinery of Porfirian Control

The Porfirian state was neither as centralized nor as personalistic as popular images suggest. Mexico's territory was large, communications were slow before the railways, and local power rested on landowners, military officers, and political bosses. Díaz's achievement was to link these local powers to the national executive through a web of obligations. Governors were the key figures. Díaz generally confirmed or placed governors who commanded local loyalty and could keep order, such as Bernardo Reyes in Nuevo León or the Terrazas-Creel family in Chihuahua, and he balanced them against rivals, often by rotating military commanders and offering federal posts. A governor who delivered stability and votes was left considerable freedom.

Below the governors were the jefes políticos, district officials appointed by governors who supervised municipalities, collected taxes, managed local elections, recruited conscripts, and settled disputes. They were the face of the state for many rural people, and their conduct varied. Some were capable administrators; others used their offices to favor friends, to seize resources, or to punish enemies. Complaints against them appear in petitions and legal records throughout the period. Because jefes políticos were typically drawn from local elite networks, their loyalty ran both to the governor and to local interests.

Coercion was part of the system. The army, reduced in size and professionalized, remained the final guarantee of order. The rurales, a federal rural police established under Juárez in 1861, were expanded under Díaz and recruited in part from former bandits. They patrolled roads, railways, and haciendas, and they were used against local rebellions and strikes. Reports describe summary executions, including the so-called ley fuga, in which prisoners were said to be shot while trying to escape, although the extent of the practice is hard to measure. Observers often summarized the regime's approach as a mixture of favors and punishments, sometimes rendered as "bread or the stick."

Yet force alone does not explain stability. The regime's characteristic method was negotiation. Díaz co-opted former enemies, including liberals, conservatives, and military rivals, through jobs, concessions, and honors. He cultivated personal correspondences with governors and local notables. He encouraged competition among his allies so that none became too powerful. In communities, he and his officials often arbitrated among rivals rather than imposing a single solution. These practices produced loyalty, but they also made the system dependent on Díaz's personal judgment, and they limited the capacity of the state to reform itself.

The reach of the central government was uneven. In the central valleys and along the railways it was substantial. In the far south, the northern frontier, and mountain districts it was thinner, and local arrangements prevailed. Historians therefore speak of a state that was stronger than any that Mexico had known since independence but weaker than the regime's propaganda suggested. The dependence on local elites mattered later, when the regime faced simultaneous challenges that its network of arrangements could not absorb.

Section 71.4

The Científicos, Positivism, Political Elites, and the Ideology of Order and Progress

The ideology of the Porfiriato drew on positivism, a nineteenth-century philosophy associated with Auguste Comte that held that society could be studied and improved by scientific methods. In Mexico, positivism entered public life through the educator Gabino Barreda, who after 1867 reorganized higher education around the National Preparatory School. Its graduates came to occupy posts in administration, law, medicine, and journalism. For them, "order" was a precondition of "progress," and strong government was justified by the need to produce material improvement after decades of disorder. The regime's slogan, often summarized as order and progress, expressed this outlook.

Justo Sierra was among the most influential of these intellectuals. A historian, journalist, and later minister of public instruction, he argued that political liberty should follow social evolution and that education was essential to national development. He also sought to build national institutions, and in 1910 he oversaw the re-founding of the Universidad Nacional during the centennial celebrations. José Yves Limantour, finance minister from 1893, represented a different current: a technocratic administrator of French descent who valued fiscal discipline, sound money, and efficient management.

The term científicos was applied to a circle of politicians and intellectuals associated with Limantour and with a manifesto of 1892 that called for scientific government. The label was often used by critics and has been used loosely since. It should not be taken to mean that all Porfirian officials belonged to a unified faction. Members of the circle included lawyers, bankers, and writers such as Rosendo Pineda, Francisco Bulnes, and Joaquín Casasús, and they differed among themselves. Others in the regime, including military men and regional politicians, resented them. Historians now see the científicos as one network among several, influential in finance and administration but not controlling all of government.

Public culture reinforced the ideology. The regime staged ceremonies, inaugurations, and national holidays, celebrated Independence and the Reform, and erected monuments along the Paseo de la Reforma. It promoted a history in which Díaz appeared as the heir of liberal heroes who had brought peace. The relationship with the Catholic Church shifted from the hostility of the Reform years to a policy of conciliation. Díaz did not repeal the Reform laws, but he did not enforce anticlerical provisions strictly, and the Church recovered influence in education, charity, and social organization. His marriage in 1881 to Carmen Romero Rubio, whose family had Lerdista and Catholic connections, symbolized the reconciliation.

Positivist language cut both ways. It supported the regime's claim to rational government, but it also provided critics with a standard by which to judge it. If government was to serve progress, then rural poverty, illiteracy, and political exclusion were failures. Young intellectuals educated in the regime's own institutions later turned the language of science and reform against its protectors. The ideology that justified order thus became one source of the expectations that the regime could not meet.

A philosophy that stressed observation, scientific method, and social order. In Mexico it shaped education and the language of government.
Concession
A government grant of rights, such as to build a railway, exploit a resource, or operate a utility, usually with subsidies or privileges. Concessions were a main instrument of Porfirian development policy.
Foreign investment
Capital from abroad, whether direct ownership of enterprises or loans and bonds. Chapter 71 distinguishes these, since ownership and lending had different consequences.
Division II · Sections 71.5–71.8 · c. 1876–1910

Economic Modernization, Foreign Investment, and Uneven Prosperity

How did the Porfirian state promote economic growth, and why did improvements in infrastructure, finance, and industry fail to distribute opportunities equally?

Section 71.5

Railroads, Telegraphs, Markets, and the Transformation of Mexican Infrastructure

When Díaz took power in 1876, Mexico had only a few hundred kilometers of railway, most of it the line between Mexico City and Veracruz that had been completed in 1873 after decades of delay. Over the next three and a half decades the network grew to close to twenty thousand kilometers. This was the most visible achievement of the Porfiriato and arguably its most consequential. The mountains and deserts that had made transport slow and expensive were crossed by iron lines financed by concessions, subsidies, and investment, much of it from the United States and Europe.

The government granted concessions that offered subsidies per kilometer built, land along the right of way, and exemptions from duties on equipment. In the 1880s American-financed companies completed lines from Mexico City north to Ciudad Juárez and from the capital to Laredo, linking the interior to the U.S. border. Other lines connected the capital with the Pacific, the Gulf ports, and the southern states. The telegraph network expanded alongside, and by the end of the period most major towns were linked to the capital. Postal and banking services followed the rails.

The consequences for markets were large. Freight costs fell sharply, and products that had been too bulky to move profitably, such as minerals, hides, cattle, cotton, and grain, entered national and international markets. Regional commercial centers such as Monterrey, Torreón, and Guadalajara grew as rail hubs. Mining districts could ship ore to smelters, and haciendas could sell to distant buyers. A national market began to take shape, although it was uneven: prices converged in connected regions while isolated districts remained dependent on local trade.

The railways also served political and military purposes. Troops could be moved quickly to suppress rebellions, to relieve garrisons, and to reach distant frontiers. The regime's ability to respond to unrest improved, and Díaz and his officials understood the strategic value of rail. At the same time, the network connected places that were profitable to foreign and domestic investors and left others relatively isolated. Much of the line ran north–south toward the U.S. border and to ports rather than creating a dense web among Mexican regions, a pattern that scholars have linked to Mexico's export orientation.

Railway construction also reshaped labor and land. Workers recruited for construction, many of them from rural villages, moved to new regions and were exposed to wage labor and new ideas. Railroad shops and operations created a skilled workforce, which later organized unions. Along the lines, land values rose, and landowners with access to the rails benefited, a development that interacted with the land legislation discussed below. The rails thus transformed not only commerce and strategy but also the distribution of property and opportunity.

Railway development changed the geography of competition among Mexican producers. A district on a through route might obtain cheaper coal, machines, and imported textiles while simultaneously finding a new market for ore, cattle, or commercial crops; another district, separated by a mountain range from the main line, might receive few such benefits. These changes could hurt established muleteers, artisans, and market towns even when aggregate trade expanded. They also made access to railway timetables, freight charges, sidings, and warehouses a source of economic advantage: large estates and mining firms could negotiate regular shipments, while small producers often relied on intermediaries. The lines were therefore not neutral conduits of growth. Their ownership, concessions, and routing decisions helped determine which regional interests acquired financial and political leverage. This was a major reason that infrastructure could strengthen national administration while widening differences among local economies.

Section 71.6

Foreign Capital, Public Finance, Mining Investment, and the Modernization of the Mexican State

The scale of foreign investment under Díaz was unprecedented, and historians stress the need to distinguish its forms. Direct investment meant ownership of enterprises such as mines, railways, smelters, and plantations by foreign companies. Lending meant bonds and loans to the Mexican government or to Mexican firms, creating debt rather than ownership. Concessions were grants by the state that conferred rights, which might be held by foreign or Mexican parties. Domestic participation included Mexican investors, bankers, and entrepreneurs, who were often partners of foreigners. Treating all of these as "foreign domination" obscures the different bargains involved.

American capital was concentrated in railways, mining, and smelting. British capital was important in railways, public utilities, and later in petroleum through the enterprise of Weetman Pearson. French investors held stakes in textile mills, department stores, and mining, notably at Santa Rosalía in Baja California. German firms were active in banking and trade. Precise totals are difficult, and estimates of the cumulative amounts by 1910 vary across sources, so students should treat figures with caution. What is well documented is that foreigners controlled a large share of the leading mines, railways, and large industrial and utility enterprises, and that the United States was becoming the dominant source of capital and trading partner.

Public finance was transformed under José Yves Limantour, who became finance minister in 1893 and served until 1911. The government achieved a budget surplus in the mid-1890s, renegotiated debt on better terms, improved tax collection, and reduced internal customs barriers. Banking expanded through institutions such as the Banco Nacional de México, formed in the 1880s, and the Banco de Londres y México, under laws that limited competition and favored established banks. In 1905 a monetary reform put Mexico on a gold-exchange standard, tying the silver-based peso to gold to ease trade and credit in a period of falling silver prices. These changes gave Mexico credit standing in international markets.

In 1908 the government created Ferrocarriles Nacionales de México by acquiring a majority interest in a consolidated railway company. It held about 51 percent of the shares; this was not the nationalization of every railway, and many lines remained privately owned. The step was motivated by concern about foreign control, by a desire to influence rates, and by a wish to rationalize operations. It illustrates the regime's active, if selective, role in the economy. The state regulated, subsidized, and sometimes owned, and was not simply a passive instrument of foreign investors.

Mexicans also invested and managed. Industrialists in Monterrey established the Cervecería Cuauhtémoc and the iron and steel works of the Fundidora, and merchants and bankers in Mexico City and the provinces participated in banks, mills, and urban property. Many Mexican entrepreneurs were connected to politics, and access to concessions, credit, and official favor shaped who prospered. The results were mixed: the modern sector grew and the state's capacity increased, but the benefits flowed disproportionately to those with capital and connections, and the economy became exposed to external shocks, as 1907 would show.

Section 71.7

Mining, Agriculture, Industry, and the Regional Economies of the Porfiriato

The Porfirian economy was a collection of regional economies, each with its own products, labor, and markets. In the north, silver, lead, zinc, and copper mining expanded with railway access and new technology. The copper district of Cananea in Sonora grew rapidly under American ownership, and the Boleo mines at Santa Rosalía were French-run. Industrial metals, in demand abroad, became more important than silver by the early twentieth century. Smelters at Monterrey, Aguascalientes, and elsewhere processed ore, and northern towns grew with the mining boom.

Agriculture was equally varied. Henequen, the fiber of the agave used to make binder twine, transformed Yucatán, where planters on large estates supplied a growing international demand, particularly from the United States. Sugar estates expanded in Morelos and Veracruz, and cotton and wheat were important in the Laguna region of the north. Coffee grew in Chiapas and Veracruz, and rubber, chicle, and tropical hardwoods were extracted in the south. Cattle ranching spread across the north. Meanwhile much of the central plateau continued to produce maize, beans, and other staples for local consumption, and these crops did not benefit from the export boom in the same way.

Access to transport, credit, irrigation, and markets explains much of the variation. Estates near railways and with capital invested in irrigation and machinery raised output, while producers lacking these resources, including many villages and small farmers, stagnated. Food production for the domestic market grew slowly, and by the end of the period maize prices and imports became a source of concern. This pattern, in which export crops expanded faster than staples, contributed to rural hardship in poor harvest years.

Industry grew but remained limited. Textile mills in Puebla, Veracruz, Tlaxcala, and Mexico City, breweries, cement and paper works, cigarette factories, and iron and steel mills at Monterrey constituted a real industrial sector. Many were established by foreign merchants or by Mexicans in partnership with them, and the use of electric power, particularly after the development of hydroelectric stations, spread. But industry employed a small fraction of the population, depended on protected markets and imported machinery, and was concentrated in a few cities. Mexico was industrializing, not industrialized, and historians caution against describing it as a fully modern industrial economy.

The regional pattern had political meaning. Regions that gained from the export economy, such as the north and Yucatán, were closely tied to the regime through their elites, while regions that were left behind or disrupted, such as parts of the central plateau and the south, accumulated grievances. The north, with its comparatively mobile population, wage labor, and proximity to the border, would later provide many leaders and soldiers of the Revolution. This regional diversity is a reason why the Revolution is best understood as a set of related struggles.

The northeast illustrates how these developments overlapped. Monterrey grew as a center of metallurgy, brewing, finance, and regional commerce, drawing on rail links to northern mining areas, the Gulf, and the United States. Its industrial economy created skilled employment and fortunes for merchants and manufacturers, yet it depended on protective policy, energy, transport, and the availability of workers whose bargaining power varied. In Yucatán, by contrast, the profitability of henequen reinforced plantation fortunes and intensified pressure to secure labor; prosperity in export statistics said little about the independence of the people cutting the fiber. In the Bajío and central plateau, a household might combine a village plot, seasonal hacienda work, and sales to a local market rather than participate primarily in international trade. These contrasts should prevent students from imagining one national economic experience. The same macroeconomic expansion supplied opportunities for some small producers, entrepreneurs, and migrants while limiting choices for others.

Section 71.8

Urban Growth, Middle Classes, Public Works, and the Unequal Benefits of Progress

Mexico City was the showcase of Porfirian modernization. Its population approached half a million by 1910, and the city was reshaped by public works: the Gran Canal drainage project, completed in 1900, to reduce flooding and improve sanitation; electric streetcars; electric lighting; paved streets; new public buildings; and expanding suburbs for the wealthy, such as Colonia Juárez. The Paseo de la Reforma was lined with monuments. Theaters, department stores, cafés, and the construction of the Palacio de Bellas Artes, begun in 1904, expressed an aspiration to be a modern capital in European style.

Other cities changed as well. Monterrey, Guadalajara, Puebla, and the port of Veracruz acquired new industries, utilities, and commercial districts. A growing middle class of lawyers, doctors, teachers, engineers, clerks, shopkeepers, and journalists emerged. Many were educated in the regime's schools and shared its faith in progress, but their careers depended on patronage, and the best positions went to those with connections. Frustrated professionals, journalists, and students became an important source of criticism.

For the majority the benefits were limited. Working-class neighborhoods were crowded, housing was inadequate, drainage and water were unreliable, and public services were poorest where residents were poorest. Real wages, according to many studies, stagnated or fell for much of the period even as prices rose. Literacy remained low: perhaps three-quarters of the population could not read in 1910. Health conditions were poor, and urban mortality, especially among infants, remained high. The contrast between elite districts and the crowded vecindades and outlying settlements was visible to every resident.

Women's lives changed in complicated ways. Domestic service was the largest occupation for urban women, and many others worked as seamstresses, cigar makers, street vendors, market traders, and factory operatives, often for lower pay than men. A smaller number became teachers, clerks, and typists. Girls' schools and normal schools expanded, and women's periodicals appeared in the early 1900s, creating new expectations about education and public life. Women also ran small businesses and households as heads of families. These new expectations had political consequences, since women would later take part in clubs, strikes, and revolutionary movements.

Official imagery presented all of this as progress. Photographs, exhibitions, and the great centennial celebrations of September 1910 displayed a Mexico of modern buildings and orderly crowds. Students should compare this record with evidence from factories, rural estates, and workers' neighborhoods. The imagery was truthful about what was built and misleading about who could enjoy it, and the contrast between the two is part of the history of the regime's legitimacy.

Public services also reflected class divisions inside the capital. New boulevards, electric lighting, public monuments, and modern drainage displayed the capacity and ambitions of the state, but poorer districts could lack reliable water, sanitation, and affordable transport to work. The modern city depended on people who washed clothes, carried food to markets, repaired tracks, cleaned houses, and built the very improvements celebrated in official photographs. Women worked in domestic service, retail trades, mills, and home-based production, usually alongside substantial unpaid household labor. Their opportunities and vulnerabilities differed from those of male professionals and industrial entrepreneurs. The regime's language of hygiene and progress sometimes treated the poor as an object of supervision rather than as participants in decisions over housing and services. Studying both civic works and household budgets permits a more complete account of modernization.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Porfirian Modernization Strengthen Mexico or Undermine Its Own Achievements?

The question: Was the economic transformation of the Porfiriato a foundation for later development, or did the political and social arrangements that accompanied it limit and ultimately destroy its gains?

Interpretation A — Foundations of modern Mexico: Some scholars emphasize the real achievements: fiscal order, infrastructure, a banking system, an industrial base, and state capacity that later governments inherited. On this view, the revolution interrupted a period of growth, and many institutions it built survived.
Interpretation B — Growth without broad development: Other scholars, such as John Coatsworth in Growth against Development, argue that growth was real but was constrained by structures that concentrated benefits, kept wages low, left agriculture underdeveloped, and excluded most people from political voice. On this view, growth and discontent were linked, not opposed.

Evidence: Both sides use trade statistics, railway and production data, wage series, land records, and political evidence, and both face limits. Many statistics were compiled by the regime, wage and price series are patchy, and regional variation is large.

Why it matters: The question affects how the Revolution is understood: as a rupture with a relatively successful order, as a response to its failures, or as both. Many historians now take a middle path, stressing that the achievements were real and that the political order was too narrow to manage the conflicts they produced.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation and identify what additional evidence, such as regional wage records or village land petitions, would help judge between them.

Division III · Sections 71.9–71.12 · c. 1856–1910

Land Concentration, Rural Labor, and Indigenous Resistance

How did land policy, commercial agriculture, and territorial expansion create different patterns of rural inequality and Indigenous resistance?

Section 71.9

Liberal Land Reform, Surveying Companies, and the Concentration of Property

Porfirian land policy had roots in the liberal Reform. The Lerdo Law of June 1856 required civil and ecclesiastical corporations to sell their real property, and in practice it came to cover the communal holdings of many villages, which liberals regarded as relics that blocked the formation of a class of small proprietors. Article 27 of the Constitution of 1857 confirmed the principle. The expected result was a countryside of independent farmers. What often happened instead was that villagers who lacked cash, legal knowledge, or titles lost land to neighbors, speculators, and estate owners, while the Church's lands passed largely to wealthy buyers.

A second strand of policy concerned terrenos baldíos, nominally vacant public lands. Laws of 1863 and 1875 provided for the survey, sale, and colonization of such lands. The presumption that large areas were empty reflected a legal definition, not a social reality: many such lands were used by Indigenous communities, herders, and farmers whose claims lacked formal documents. Under the colonization law of 15 December 1883, enacted during Manuel González's presidency, private surveying companies, the compañías deslindadoras, could measure public lands and receive a share, commonly a third, of what they surveyed, in return for covering the costs. The law was meant to promote colonization and to settle boundaries, and it did both of these things in some places, but it also created strong incentives to define as many lands as possible as vacant.

The March 1894 law, enacted under Díaz, removed older requirements that newly acquired vacant lands be populated, enclosed, and cultivated, and it removed restrictions that had limited certain large transfers by surveying companies. It did not abolish surveying: the statute retained denunciation, notice to neighboring landholders, measurement, boundary demarcation, publication, and review as steps in establishing claims, including the survey procedure set out in Article 27. In practice, those formal requirements often favored investors able to hire surveyors and lawyers, while communities relying on older documents or customary boundaries could contest a claim only at substantial expense. Together with the 1883 law and administrative practice, the new rules facilitated the accumulation of very large properties by well-connected owners and companies. In Chihuahua, the Terrazas family built extensive holdings, and investors such as the American publisher William Randolph Hearst acquired large estates. Surveying companies operated across extensive regions; the resulting transfers cannot be equated automatically with land that had been socially unoccupied.

The effects varied by region and by the strength of local claims. In the north, where population was sparse and land was often used for ranching, large estates expanded readily. In the south and parts of the center, where villages held land under old titles or custom, conflict was frequent. Communities with formally recognized colonial titles could sometimes defend their claims, while those relying on custom were vulnerable. Litigation in courts was costly, and judges and officials often favored powerful neighbors. Many villages nevertheless fought for years through petitions, legal suits, and appeals to Díaz himself, and some won. Earlier generations of historians claimed that over 90 percent of rural communities lacked land by 1910, but later scholars have questioned such statistics and emphasized regional variation and the persistence of village landholding in many areas.

The legislation must be kept distinct in historical analysis. The Lerdo Law of 1856 was a Reform measure with ideological purposes. The 1883 law belonged to González's presidency, though Díaz's government later implemented and extended its logic. The 1894 law was a Porfirian measure that removed constraints. Treating them as a single "Díaz land law" obscures the different intentions and effects. What they shared was a view of land as a commodity to be made secure for investors and developers, and a legal framework that gave advantages to those who could produce titles and afford surveys.

Cause and Effect: Land Legislation, Surveyed Property, and Rural Grievance

Long-term causes

Liberal belief in individual property, the Reform attack on corporate holdings, state weakness and fiscal need, and demand for land created by railways and export markets.

Short-term causes

The laws of 1883 and 1894, the profit incentives of surveying companies, and the ability of well-connected buyers to obtain titles.

Event or transformation

Large-scale surveying, titling, and sale of land, with accumulation of vast estates in some regions.

Immediate effects

Land litigation, loss of village lands in some districts, growth of estates, and more laborers dependent on estate employment.

Long-term consequences

Rural grievances that later fed agrarian demands in the Revolution, especially in regions where villages had retained land and then lost it.

Dispossession varied by region, and many villages kept or defended land. Land conflict was one cause of revolution, not the only one.
Section 71.10

Haciendas, Debt Peonage, Rural Wages, and the Conditions of Agricultural Labor

Rural labor in Porfirian Mexico took many forms, and generalization is dangerous. On large central haciendas, a core of resident workers, the peones acasillados, lived on the estate, received a small wage, a ration of maize, and access to a plot, and were often indebted to the estate. Alongside them were tenants and sharecroppers who farmed estate land for a rent or a share of the crop, and seasonal workers who came from nearby villages for planting and harvest. Estate stores, the tiendas de raya, sold goods on credit and could entrap workers in debt, though not all estates operated them in the same way.

Debt peonage was real and significant but not universal. In central Mexico many workers were relatively free to leave, and landowners competed for labor in some seasons. The advances they offered were as much a means of attracting workers as of binding them. In the north, ranching and mining areas offered comparatively higher wages and more mobile labor, and many northerners worked for wages or as tenants. In the south, the situation was harsher. On henequen plantations in Yucatán and on tobacco, rubber, and coffee plantations in Oaxaca, Chiapas, and Tabasco, labor was recruited by enganchadores who used advances, deception, and sometimes force, and workers faced violence, long hours, and difficulty leaving.

Real wages were low and, in many regions, did not keep pace with the cost of maize and other staples. Work was seasonal and insecure, and families depended on multiple earners. Women worked in household production, in the fields at peak seasons, in domestic service on estates, and in marketing. Children began work early, as herders, field hands, and domestic servants. In some places children inherited their parents' debts in practice, though the legal status of such debts was disputed.

Workers were not passive. They bargained over wages and rations, fled to other estates or to cities, petitioned authorities, and sometimes joined bandits or rebels. Their capacity to leave depended on local conditions: the availability of other work, the distance to the next estate, the stance of the jefe político, and the presence of kin. Where land was available nearby or where northern mining and railway work offered alternatives, estate owners had to adjust. In the south, where options were fewer and the state enforced debts, workers had less room.

Historians still debate the extent of coercion. Early revolutionary writers portrayed rural Mexico as a vast system of slavery. Revisionist scholars have shown that wage labor, tenancy, and negotiated arrangements predominated in many areas and that debt could serve as a mutual obligation, not only a trap. Yet the evidence from the southern plantations, from contract recruitment, and from the use of state power to return fugitives shows that outright coercion existed on a large scale in particular regions. The accurate picture is of a spectrum from relatively free wage labor to near-servitude, with regional concentrations of the harshest forms.

Section 71.11

The Yaqui Wars, Deportations, and the Struggle for Indigenous Autonomy

The Yaqui (Yoeme) people of the Río Yaqui valley in Sonora had defended their territory and self-government since the colonial period. Organized in eight principal river pueblos with their own governance and religious institutions, they farmed the fertile valley, worked for wages on estates and in mines, and maintained a strong sense of collective rights. Nineteenth-century Sonoran elites, eager to develop irrigation and agriculture, wanted the valley's land and labor. The conflict was therefore about territory, water, autonomy, and labor, not simply about "Indian wars."

In the 1870s and 1880s a Yaqui leader, José María Leyva, known as Cajeme, built a regional movement that governed the valley and resisted state authority. Federal and state forces defeated him in the 1880s, and he was captured and executed in 1887. Resistance continued under other leaders, notably Juan Maldonado, called Tetabiate, and fighting persisted in the Bacatete mountains and river valleys through the 1890s and into the early twentieth century. A peace in 1897 proved temporary. The conflict drew on regional alliances, including some among other Sonoran Indigenous peoples such as the Mayo, and on the willingness of many Yaquis to work for wages while contesting control of their land.

Under Díaz the state combined military campaigns with colonization and land survey. Companies and settlers, including American-backed ventures, sought the valley for irrigation and cultivation. Sonoran officials, among them Governor Rafael Izábal, and military commanders carried out campaigns after 1900 that broke organized resistance and also targeted peaceful communities. From about 1900, and on a larger scale after 1904, authorities arrested Yaquis and deported them out of Sonora, many to henequen plantations in Yucatán and to other labor destinations, where they were sold or assigned to employers. Families were often separated, and mortality among deportees was reportedly high.

The evidence requires careful handling. Government documents, contracts, and military reports record campaigns and some deportations. Testimony by Yaqui survivors, by Sonoran witnesses, and by journalists describes conditions. John Kenneth Turner's 1909 articles for American Magazine, later published as Barbarous Mexico, offered dramatic accounts, including claims about the number of deportees and their deaths. Turner was a sharp critic of the regime, and his reporting is valuable evidence of both conditions and the case made against Díaz in the United States. But his larger numbers, and those of other journalists, cannot be treated as independently established demographic facts. Estimates of the numbers deported range widely, from several thousand to figures many times larger, and scholars such as Evelyn Hu-DeHart have worked to establish what the records can support.

Yaqui resistance continued in new forms. Deportees, dispersed across the country, formed communities, escaped, and returned to Sonora; others worked in mines and railroads and later took part in the Revolution on various sides. Yaqui leaders and communities negotiated, fought, petitioned, and adapted, and their survival as a people through this period is itself evidence of their agency. The Yaqui case shows how development policy and Indigenous dispossession were connected in Porfirian Mexico, and how coercive labor and territorial expansion could reinforce each other.

Section 71.12

Rural Grievances in Morelos, Yucatán, Oaxaca, and Southern Mexico

Morelos, a small state south of Mexico City, shows how commercial agriculture could transform a region. Sugar estates, long established, expanded in the late nineteenth century with railways, new machinery, and rising demand. Estates sought more land and, above all, more water, and they pressed on neighboring villages that had held their lands since the colonial period. Villages defended themselves with colonial titles and lawsuits, but expanding planters, aided by officials, often prevailed. Many villagers became dependent on estate work for part of the year while keeping small plots. The state's population was relatively dense, and the competition for land and water was acute.

In Anenecuilco, a village in Morelos, local leaders spent years pursuing claims against the neighboring hacienda over land and water. Emiliano Zapata, born around 1879, was a villager and small horse breeder who took part in these struggles. In 1909 he was chosen as head of the village council, and his involvement in the dispute is documented in local records. Zapata's later military and political role belongs to Chapter 72. What matters here is that his motives grew from a local conflict over documented village rights, one that unfolded in a region where an expanding commercial economy had intensified pressure on villages that remained relatively cohesive.

Yucatán presented a different pattern. Henequen planters, a small elite closely tied to the regime and to American buyers, controlled land and labor. The peninsula's Maya population, many of whom had once fought in the Caste War that began in 1847, worked on estates under heavy debt and constraint, supplemented by deportees from Sonora and contract laborers from elsewhere. In the east, the Maya of the independent Cruzob state around Chan Santa Cruz maintained autonomy for decades until Mexican forces took the town in 1901, and the territory of Quintana Roo was created in 1902. The closing phase of the Caste War thus coincided with the henequen boom, and the conquest of the east opened land and resources to development.

Oaxaca, Díaz's home state, was ethnically diverse and had many Indigenous communities with strong local institutions. Some villages retained their lands and negotiated with authorities, while in other areas, particularly the Valle Nacional tobacco district, harsh recruitment and labor practices were reported. In Chiapas, Tabasco, and other southern states, plantations of coffee, rubber, and mahogany extraction relied on contract and debt labor, and Indigenous communities sometimes lost land to coffee growers. Elsewhere, southern villagers retained substantial autonomy and local resources, and some benefited from regional trade.

Differences among these regions are essential. Village communities with landholding traditions, such as those in Morelos and parts of Oaxaca, reacted to loss of land and water. Estate laborers, such as those of Yucatán or Veracruz, were more concerned with wages, debt, and control. Indigenous groups confronted by conquest and removal, such as the Yaqui and eastern Maya, resisted territorial and political incorporation. Authorities ranged from relatively responsive to repressive. The Revolution would draw on all of these grievances, but it would not unify them, a fact that explains much of its later complexity.

Division IV · Sections 71.13–71.16 · c. 1890–1907

Political Opposition, Labor Unrest, and the Erosion of Porfirian Legitimacy

How did constitutional opposition, radical political journalism, and organized workers' movements expose the limitations of Porfirian political stability?

Section 71.13

The Independent Press, Liberal Clubs, Civic Opposition, and Political Repression

Opposition to Díaz existed from the beginning, but it changed character over time. In the 1880s and early 1890s critics were mostly journalists, students, and liberals who objected to repeated reelection and to the abandonment of Reform principles. In 1892 student demonstrations in Mexico City against another reelection were broken up by the authorities. Newspapers that criticized the regime were fined, closed, or had their editors imprisoned, while the government subsidized friendly papers and used legal prosecution for libel and sedition to discourage others. Fewer independent voices survived than in the Restored Republic.

Repression took several forms. Editors such as Filomeno Mata were repeatedly jailed, and presses were seized. Some critics were exiled, and others were pressured through police surveillance, loss of employment, or the withdrawal of official favors. At the same time the regime used patronage to absorb potential opponents: critics could be offered government posts, honors, or legal business. This combination of punishment and co-optation limited organized opposition without eliminating discontent, and it shaped the form that dissent took: clubs, newspapers, and conspiracies that operated at the margins of legality.

A new phase began around 1900. In that year Camilo Arriaga, a mining engineer and liberal from San Luis Potosí, helped found the Liberal Club Ponciano Arriaga, and in 1901 a congress of liberal clubs met in San Luis Potosí to denounce clerical influence and the political order. The Flores Magón brothers, then young journalists, were involved, and Regeneración began to publish in 1900. Though the movement began with a broadly liberal anticlerical program, the clubs soon turned to criticism of the regime. The authorities closed the congress and arrested participants.

Distinctions are important. Liberal reformers sought to restore the Constitution of 1857 and effective suffrage; they criticized the regime from within the liberal tradition, and many were middle-class professionals. Regional elites resented the regime's centralizing tendencies and its favoritism. Radical critics, including socialists and anarchists, wanted deeper change in labor and property relations. Labor activists focused on wages and working conditions. These strands overlapped but did not form one movement, and their later divisions explain much about the Revolution's complexity.

By 1905 repression and exile had pushed many opponents across the border, where they organized with greater freedom, though the regime pursued them with the cooperation of private agents and, at times, U.S. authorities. The experience taught critics that peaceful reform within the existing system seemed unlikely. This sense of closed channels is central to the radicalization of the opposition after 1900, and it prepared the ground for the program of 1906.

Section 71.14

Ricardo Flores Magón, Regeneración, and the Mexican Liberal Party

Ricardo Flores Magón, born in Oaxaca in 1873, and his brothers Jesús and Enrique became the most prominent radical opponents of Díaz. Regeneración, founded in 1900 as an opposition newspaper of legal criticism, was repeatedly suppressed. Ricardo was imprisoned in 1903, and after his release the brothers went into exile in the United States in 1904. They moved among Texas, Missouri, and later California, publishing, organizing, and enduring surveillance and arrest by U.S. authorities acting partly at Mexican request. Their circle included Juan Sarabia, Antonio Díaz Soto y Gama, Librado Rivera, and others.

In 1905, exiled opponents formed an organizing junta in St. Louis that became the leadership of the Partido Liberal Mexicano (PLM). Its ideology shifted over time from liberal reformism toward anarchism, particularly for Ricardo Flores Magón, though not all members followed. The PLM organized clubs inside Mexico, planned uprisings, and distributed propaganda. Its attempted insurrections in 1906 and 1908, in places such as Jiménez in Coahuila and Las Vacas on the border, were put down.

On 1 July 1906 the PLM published a program and manifesto in Regeneración. Its agenda was much broader than removing Díaz: it advocated shorter presidential terms, nonreelection, civil liberties, curbs on jefes políticos, an eight-hour workday, minimum wages, protections against dangerous labor and abusive payment systems, secular compulsory education, and measures to make idle land productive. Yet the program's progressive demands coexisted with explicit exclusion. Article 16 called for a prohibition on Chinese immigration, and the explanatory language relied on discriminatory assumptions about Chinese workers and wage competition. This anti-Chinese position was part of wider transnational racial and labor politics; it was not a defensible description of Chinese migrants or a universal principle of labor equality. The contradiction matters: the PLM could demand protections for some workers while proposing to deny entry to others on the basis of nationality. Its ideas should be examined as a historical program containing both social reform and racial exclusion.

The program is often described as a forerunner of the social provisions of the Constitution of 1917, and several of its demands do reappear later. But historians caution against a simple line of descent. The program was one of several reform visions, and its authors were exiles with limited ability to organize inside Mexico. Its influence was indirect, working through the circulation of ideas, the examples of its activists, and later political memory. The PLM also differed from Madero's antireelectionists, who were less concerned with social questions, and from socialists and anarchists with other priorities.

The Magonistas thus occupied a distinctive place. They provided a language of rights and social reform that circulated among workers and radicals, and they showed that opposition could be organized. They also revealed limits: dependent on exile networks and subject to repression on both sides of the border, they could not mobilize a mass movement by themselves. Later Magonista efforts, including the 1911 campaign in Baja California, belong to the Revolution proper, and Ricardo Flores Magón's later life is treated in subsequent chapters.

Section 71.15

The Cananea Strike of 1906 and the Conflict over Industrial Labor

Cananea, in northern Sonora near the Arizona border, was a copper-mining town built around the Cananea Consolidated Copper Company, an enterprise headed by the American William C. Greene. Its mines employed thousands of workers, both Mexican and American, and the town grew rapidly in the early 1900s. The workforce was divided by national origin and by pay. Mexican miners earned less than Americans doing comparable or similar work, were paid in Mexican currency while many American workers were paid in dollars, and were excluded from many skilled and supervisory positions. The company also employed Mexican workers in more dangerous tasks.

The strike began on 1 June 1906. Workers, influenced by organizers linked to the Liberal Party and by local labor societies, presented demands that included a higher wage and an eight-hour day for the mines, the dismissal of abusive supervisors, a fixed share of jobs for Mexicans, and equal opportunity for promotion. Leaders such as Manuel M. Diéguez and Esteban Baca Calderón figured prominently in later accounts. The strike started at one shift, spread through the district, and led to a march into town. Confrontation with company officials and armed guards produced shooting, deaths, and the burning of a lumber yard.

Authorities responded forcefully. Sonora governor Rafael Izábal arranged for armed men from Arizona to cross into Cananea on June 2, 1906, after the strike and the initial bloodshed of June 1. Accounts differ in their descriptions of the contingent, which included Arizona Rangers and other volunteers, and the authorization provoked criticism because foreign armed men operated on Mexican territory. Mexican rurales and federal troops were also deployed. Strike leaders were arrested, some received long sentences, and the movement was suppressed within days. The sequence matters: the workers' petition and the first day's confrontation preceded the disputed cross-border intervention, rather than resulting from it.

Accounts differ, and casualty figures are uncertain. Company and government reports tended to blame agitators and to minimize deaths, while labor and opposition accounts emphasized unprovoked shooting and greater casualties. Estimates of the dead vary, from a small number to several dozen, and historians treat all as provisional. The incident was widely reported in Mexico and the United States and became a symbol of the alliance between foreign capital and the Porfirian state. It also shows the complexity of the conflict: it combined wage demands, national resentment over pay discrimination, and political grievance.

Historians have debated whether the strike was spontaneous, organized by the PLM, or a product of both. Liberal Party clubs were present in the district, and organizers were involved, but the demands arose from local conditions and workers' own experience. The distinction matters because it bears on the relationship between political opposition and labor. Cananea suggests that they were connected without being identical, and it demonstrated to workers elsewhere that collective action was possible, and to the regime that such action would be met with force.

Section 71.16

Río Blanco, Textile Labor, and the Escalation of Industrial Conflict, 1906–1907

The textile industry was among Mexico's largest industrial sectors, concentrated in Puebla, Tlaxcala, Veracruz, and the Federal District. Mills, many owned by French and Spanish merchants and by Mexican investors, employed thousands of men, women, and children in long days under strict factory rules. At Río Blanco, near Orizaba in Veracruz, a large mill complex belonged to the Compañía Industrial de Orizaba, with company housing, a company store, and regulations that governed workers' lives, including fines and restrictions on visitors and publications.

Textile workers organized, often as mutual-aid societies that evolved into more assertive groups. In 1906 the Gran Círculo de Obreros Libres, formed in the Orizaba district, brought together workers from many mills and sought improvements in wages and conditions, and its members had some links to Liberal Party ideas. In late 1906 a conflict began in Puebla when workers protested new factory regulations and the employers' response. When the Puebla strike spread, manufacturers in several states announced a lockout, closing mills and leaving workers without wages in the Christmas season.

Díaz issued his arbitration award on January 4, 1907. Its first article ordered mills to reopen and workers to return on Monday, January 7, largely under existing regulations. Other provisions contemplated wage-rate adjustments and limited reforms, but the award also required workers to bring grievances through employer-controlled procedures, restricted labor publications through politically appointed supervision, and committed workers not to initiate strikes. The ruling was communicated to workers on January 6, and opposition to returning under these conditions contributed to the events at Río Blanco on January 7. The difference between the award, its communication, and the confrontation is essential to reconstructing the sequence accurately.

Río Blanco should be distinguished from Cananea. Cananea was a strike in a northern mining enclave under American ownership, with sharp national and wage grievances. Río Blanco was part of a larger textile conflict across several states, involving a long preceding dispute, a lockout, and presidential arbitration, with Mexican and French owners and a largely Mexican workforce. Both ended in repression, and both were used by opponents to argue that the state sided with capital, but the contexts, actors, and demands differed. Women and workers' families took part prominently in Río Blanco, in demonstrations and in the attack on the store, and company-store practices that tied household budgets to employers fueled resentment.

The consequences were considerable. Labor organization was set back, leaders were jailed or driven out, and employers tightened control. But the memory of Río Blanco endured and was later invoked in labor movements and in revolutionary politics. Díaz's role as arbiter damaged his claim to stand above social conflict, since his award was seen as pro-employer. In the following years, strikes and labor agitation continued in various places, and the regime's capacity to channel labor conflicts without force was in doubt.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Working Households: Field, Mine, and Mill

A rural laborer's household. On a central estate, a resident worker's family lived in a one-room dwelling, usually of adobe, on estate land. The father worked for a small wage and a maize ration; the mother ground maize, made tortillas, raised small animals, and often worked seasonally in the fields; children herded animals and helped with chores from an early age. Credit at the estate store smoothed shortages but could also deepen debt. Bad harvests and rising maize prices were the greatest threat.

A mineworker's family. In a northern mining town such as Cananea, wages were higher than in most of rural Mexico but were insecure and unequal by nationality. Families lived in company or rented housing, often crowded. Women ran boarding houses, laundries, and small shops, and the household economy depended on their work. Accidents, lung disease, and layoffs were constant risks, and the schooling of children was limited.

A textile worker's day. In an Orizaba-area mill, the day began before dawn and lasted long hours, governed by factory rules and fines. Men, women, and children operated machines, with women prominent in spinning and weaving. Pay at the company store reduced cash wages, and housing was often owned by the firm. After work, mutual-aid societies, churches, and taverns formed community life, and workers' families supported strikes with food and organization.

These descriptions are composites drawn from scholarship, not records of particular individuals. The silences in the sources, especially about women and children, are themselves evidence of whose lives the surviving documents recorded.

Section 71.17

The Economic Disturbances of 1907–1909 and the Weakening of the Porfirian Order

The Porfirian economy was closely tied to international markets, and in 1907 it felt a shock from abroad. A financial panic in the United States in the autumn of that year tightened credit and lowered demand for Mexican exports. Prices of copper and silver fell, mines reduced output or closed, and industrial activity slowed. Banks restricted credit, and commercial firms faced difficulty obtaining loans. The effects were uneven, but they reached mining towns, textile districts, and commercial centers, and the dependence of growth on foreign capital and markets became visible.

Employment became unstable. Mine closures and layoffs sent workers home or toward the border, and returning migrants and the unemployed added to pressure on rural and urban labor markets. Wages, already low, offered little cushion, and prices of staples rose. Poor agricultural conditions made matters worse: frost and drought in 1907 and after reduced maize harvests, and prices of maize and beans climbed, hurting rural and urban consumers alike. Because export crops had expanded faster than food production, the margin for error in subsistence was small.

Economic distress magnified existing inequalities. Haciendas, which could hold grain and bargain with merchants, often profited from rising prices, while villagers and wage workers suffered. Foreign-owned firms could cut costs by laying off workers, and Mexican entrepreneurs dependent on credit faced difficulty. Regional grievances sharpened, in the north where migrants returned from the United States, in the center where food prices rose, and in the south where labor conditions did not improve. Historians stress that economic hardship did not by itself cause a revolution, but that it intensified discontent and weakened confidence in the regime.

The state's response revealed a contradiction. By this time the government had significant fiscal and administrative capacity, and Limantour managed the financial strains with some skill, maintaining the gold-exchange standard and supporting banks. Yet this capacity was directed toward preserving financial stability and the interests of investors, and the regime offered little in the way of relief or reform for workers and rural communities. Its mechanisms for responding to social demands were mainly policing and arbitration, and it had little institutional means for incorporating new groups into politics.

The economic disturbances of 1907–1909 thus did not cause the Revolution but shaped its timing and breadth. They arrived as political uncertainty over succession was rising and as labor conflicts of 1906–1907 remained fresh. They gave an economic dimension to political dissatisfaction, and they widened the range of Mexicans who were prepared to listen to critics. The convergence of these pressures is a theme of the final sections.

The downturn spread unevenly through networks of employment and credit. In districts supplying export markets, a fall in sales might first be expressed as reduced overtime, shorter shifts, or postponed contracts, followed by dismissals that deprived workers' families of cash. Merchants who had advanced supplies to ranchers or small farmers could demand repayment when those producers were least able to sell. A person who lost mining work might return to a village already facing competition for land, while a household with no local subsistence plot had fewer protections against food-price increases. Such mechanisms complicate the idea of a single national crisis beginning on one date. Some enterprises and regions adjusted more successfully than others, and political reactions depended on preexisting grievances, alternative employment, and relations with officials. For historians, the challenge is to connect prices and bank balances with the everyday sequence by which economic insecurity became political discontent.

Section 71.18

The Díaz–Creelman Interview, the Succession Question, and the Crisis of Political Legitimacy, 1908–1909

In March 1908, Pearson's Magazine published an interview by James Creelman, an American journalist, with Porfirio Díaz. Creelman reported Díaz as saying that Mexico had reached a level of development at which democracy could be tolerated, that he would welcome the formation of an opposition party, and that he intended to retire at the end of his term in 1910. The interview was translated and discussed in Mexico, where it caused a sensation. Díaz's government and friendly newspapers soon sought to place it in context, and the president's later statements were more ambiguous.

Interpreting the interview requires attention to audience. It was given to a foreign journalist writing for a U.S. magazine and was intended in part to reassure foreign investors and opinion that Mexico was a stable, progressive country with a path to representative government. It was not an unconditional promise to give up power. Díaz's actual intentions are debated: some historians see a genuine, if hesitant, consideration of retirement; others see an exercise in public relations that he did not expect to be taken so literally. What is clear is that Mexicans took it as a signal and that the regime could not easily retract it.

The succession question was real. Díaz was seventy-seven in 1908. The vice president, Ramón Corral, a former governor of Sonora, was unpopular and associated with the repression of the Yaqui, and few expected him to hold power if Díaz died. General Bernardo Reyes, governor of Nuevo León and former minister of war, had a significant following among military men and regional politicians, and his supporters formed a Democratic Party in 1909 to promote his candidacy. Within the regime, rivalries between Reyes's circle and the Limantour group, between military and civilian elites, and among regional factions intensified. The president's careful balancing of rivals, once a source of stability, now made it hard to prepare a successor.

Díaz moved to neutralize challenges. He sent Reyes on a mission to Europe in late 1909, effectively removing him from the field, and he accepted Corral as his running mate again. These decisions disappointed Reyistas, some of whom drifted toward other opposition. The regime's inability to manage succession through its customary bargaining showed the limits of a political system built around one man. The generation of politicians, journalists, and reformers who expected change, and who had been educated within the regime's own institutions, found the paths closing.

Together these events created a crisis of legitimacy that was different from the grievances of workers or villagers. It was a crisis among the politically attentive about the future of the system itself. This is a significant point: the movement that ended the Porfiriato drew strength from the fact that the opposition was no longer confined to marginalized radicals. It included landowners, professionals, and former officials who believed that the regime could not renew itself.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Antireelectionism in 1876 and in 1908–1910

What Changed

  • In 1876 antireelectionism was the banner of an army-led rebellion; in 1908–1910 it was the platform of a civilian campaign of clubs, a book, and a presidential candidacy, though it ended in armed revolt
  • The target changed: Lerdo, a president seeking a second term, was replaced by Díaz, a president in a seventh term
  • The base broadened to include landowners, professionals, and workers, and the movement operated in a Mexico knitted together by railways and telegraphs

What Stayed the Same

  • The slogan of effective suffrage and nonreelection, and the use of a published plan to justify rebellion against an incumbent
  • The conviction that the constitutional order was being violated by the executive
  • The importance of regional leaders, local grievances, and the border

Who experienced change most directly: Politically ambitious provincial elites and professionals, who in 1876 were allies of power and in 1910 were excluded from it. Greater continuity: Rural villagers and workers, whose grievances over land and wages were little addressed by either slogan. Why the pattern matters: The same principle that brought Díaz to power gave his opponents their language, which shows how political ideas can outlast the interests of those who first used them.

Section 71.19

Francisco I. Madero, Antireelectionism, and the Presidential Election of 1910

Francisco I. Madero was born in 1873 into one of the wealthiest families of northern Mexico, with interests in agriculture, cotton, mining, and industry in Coahuila. He was educated in France and the United States, practiced business, and was known for philanthropic and spiritist interests. He was not a revolutionary by temperament. In the early 1900s he took part in local opposition politics in Coahuila, and his experience of the state's electoral manipulation and the regime's treatment of provincial elites led him toward national politics. His wealth and family connections gave him resources and a measure of protection that other critics lacked.

In January 1909 Madero published La sucesión presidencial en 1910, a book arguing that the country needed free elections and that the central question was whether Mexico would remain subject to the rule of one man. It accepted Díaz's past achievements while insisting that the system had become dangerous because it rested on one person, and it called for the formation of an antireelectionist party. The book sold widely and made him a national figure. In 1909 he helped organize antireelectionist clubs and in May founded a Centro Antirreeleccionista in Mexico City, with a program of effective suffrage and no reelection.

Madero campaigned across the country in 1909 and 1910, traveling by train, speaking in towns and cities, and drawing large crowds. The Partido Nacional Antirreeleccionista held a convention in Mexico City in April 1910 that nominated Madero for president and Francisco Vázquez Gómez for vice president. Díaz and Corral were again nominated by the regime's supporters. The campaign meant that for the first time in a generation a credible candidate was challenging the incumbent publicly, and the response of the regime was to harass the opposition, with arrests of local clubs' leaders and disruption of meetings.

In June 1910, shortly before the election, Madero was arrested in Monterrey and later held in San Luis Potosí, charged in connection with alleged insulting of authorities and incitement. The elections of late June and July 1910 were held with Madero in custody, and Díaz and Corral were declared reelected by large margins, amid widespread allegations of manipulation. Because the regime controlled the electoral machinery, historians generally regard the official results as unreliable, though the degree of manipulation varied by place. For many antireelectionists, the elections confirmed that the path of peaceful reform was closed.

The movement's social base extended beyond Madero's own class. It included professionals, small-town merchants, urban workers, rancheros and former soldiers in the north, and some villagers who saw in Madero a hope of redress for local grievances. Madero's own program was largely political and gave little attention to land or labor, so the breadth of support reflected a convergence of hopes more than agreement on aims. Leaders such as Abraham González in Chihuahua helped build regional networks, and later figures including Pascual Orozco and Francisco Villa would emerge in the revolutionary struggle. Their activities belong to Chapter 72.

The campaign's geography helps explain its unusually broad appeal. Railways allowed Madero to carry a message between state capitals and provincial towns, while newspapers, correspondence, clubs, and local intermediaries circulated it beyond those who attended his speeches. In Coahuila and Chihuahua, opponents of local governing networks could interpret antireelectionism as a route to greater regional influence. Elsewhere, lawyers, merchants, teachers, and independent journalists associated the slogan with access to public office and protection from arbitrary administration. Rural supporters might expect help with land or water disputes even though Madero's platform did not promise a sweeping agrarian revolution. These different expectations made the alliance formidable but also unstable. They help explain why the overthrow of Díaz later failed to settle the competing visions of what political change should achieve.

Section 71.20

The Plan of San Luis Potosí and the Collapse of Porfirian Legitimacy, 1910–1911

Released on bail in early October 1910, Madero left San Luis Potosí and crossed into the United States, reaching San Antonio, Texas, around 6 October. There he and his collaborators completed the Plan of San Luis Potosí. The document carries the date 5 October 1910 and the place name San Luis Potosí, but historians place completion of its text somewhat later in October in Texas, an example of how the dating of a document may differ from its production. The choice of date and place gave the plan a claim to have been issued from within Mexico.

The plan declared the elections of 1910 null and void, denounced the regime's violation of effective suffrage and its repeated reelection, and called for the Constitution to be restored with the principle of no reelection. Madero declared himself provisional president pending elections. A provision on land, a short clause, promised that small proprietors, particularly Indigenous people dispossessed under the surveying and vacant-land laws, would have their lands restored or be compensated. The plan called for an uprising on 20 November 1910, at six in the evening. It was a political program, with limited agrarian content, but it was written in a way that allowed groups with different grievances to find something in it.

Response to the call was uneven at first. A planned uprising in Puebla was discovered, and Aquiles Serdán, an antireelectionist leader, was killed in a confrontation on 18 November. On the appointed day, little happened in many places. But in the following weeks risings spread, notably in Chihuahua, where local leaders and veterans of frontier warfare took up arms, and in other northern and some southern districts. In Morelos, Guerrero, and elsewhere local movements joined or associated themselves with the plan, some pursuing local aims under its banner.

The interaction of electoral opposition with existing rural, regional, and labor grievances gave the movement its force. Villagers who had lost land, miners and railwaymen who had been laid off, small ranchers who resented the Terrazas network, and professionals excluded from office found in the plan a common vehicle, even though their aims differed. The regime's army, though well equipped, was dispersed and underprepared for a multi-front guerrilla campaign, and its political strategy of balancing loyalties was poorly suited to a challenge that cut across regions and classes. Elite fragmentation weakened the regime's cohesion, and the aged president was slow to adapt.

The revolutionary challenge grew through the winter and spring of 1911. In May, insurgent forces captured Ciudad Juárez on the border, and negotiations led to the Treaty of Ciudad Juárez of 21 May. Díaz resigned on 25 May and left the country at the end of the month. Chapter 72 takes up the campaigns, Madero's government, and the wars that followed. The fall of Díaz was not inevitable. His government had endured for decades, and the revolutionary crisis emerged from the convergence of structural grievances, changing political expectations, elite division, and contingent events. Its outcome, as the next chapter shows, would be far from what any of the participants in 1910 expected.

The collapse of the old order should be understood as the breakdown of governing relationships rather than the overnight disappearance of the state. Provincial commanders, local bosses, landowners, and business interests did not respond uniformly to armed rebellion; some stood by Díaz, some sought accommodation, and others shifted allegiance. Opposition networks that could not previously win national elections found new leverage when armed movements made ordinary administration more difficult. Conversely, the revolutionary coalition's shared objection to reelection did not resolve its disagreements over land restitution, labor protections, local autonomy, or federal authority. Those tensions are the starting point, not the conclusion, of the next chapter. The Porfiriato's enduring paradox was that its modernization had helped create the communications, social groups, and aspirations through which its own limitations could be challenged.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how a regime that brought stability and modernization to Mexico also generated the opposition and crisis that ended it. Porfirio Díaz came to power in 1876 through an antireelectionist rebellion, defeating Lerdo at Tecoac, and he spent the following decades building a political order that kept the forms of the Constitution of 1857 while limiting competition. His rule was not continuous from 1877: he governed until 1880, Manuel González from 1880 to 1884, and Díaz from 1884 to 1911.

The governing system rested on networks as well as force. Governors, jefes políticos, army commanders, and the rurales linked local power to the national executive, and Díaz bargained with former enemies and rivals while balancing them against one another. Constitutional amendments in 1887, 1890, and 1904 removed limits on reelection and lengthened the term, and the regime's ideology of order and progress, drawn from positivism and associated with intellectuals and officials such as Justo Sierra and José Yves Limantour, justified the arrangement. The científicos were an influential network, not a unified faction.

Economic modernization was real. Railways grew from a few hundred kilometers to nearly twenty thousand, telegraphs integrated markets, public finance was reorganized under Limantour, banking and a gold-exchange standard were established, and mining, agriculture, and industry expanded. Foreign investment took the forms of ownership, lending, and concessions, and Mexicans also participated. The state created Ferrocarriles Nacionales de México in 1908 with a majority holding. Cities, especially Mexico City, were transformed.

The benefits were unevenly distributed. Infrastructure favored export regions, real wages lagged, literacy remained low, and urban workers lived in crowded neighborhoods. Regional economies diverged, with northern mining and ranching, Yucatán's henequen, Morelos's sugar, and the central plateau's staples each following distinct paths. Historians debate whether the Porfirian transformation provided a foundation for later development or was constrained by its own exclusions, and most now emphasize both achievements and structural limits.

Land policy produced some of the deepest grievances. The Lerdo Law of 1856, the colonization law of 1883, and the vacant-lands law of 1894 each had distinct effects, together enabling surveying companies and large landowners to accumulate territory and exposing villages that lacked formal titles. Dispossession varied by region, and many communities defended their lands. Rural labor ranged from relatively free wage work to debt-bound servitude, with the harshest forms concentrated in the south, including Yucatán's henequen plantations.

Indigenous resistance and state coercion intersected, most dramatically among the Yaqui of Sonora, who fought for decades for their valley and, after 1900 and especially from 1904, were subjected to deportation. Evidence for the scale of these events comes from government records, testimony, and critical journalism such as Turner's, which historians use carefully. In Morelos, Yucatán, Oaxaca, and the south, rural grievances took different forms, and the local dispute in Anenecuilco in which Emiliano Zapata took part illustrates how documented village rights became a source of conflict.

Opposition grew from press criticism and liberal clubs into organized movements. The Flores Magón brothers and the Partido Liberal Mexicano published a broad reform program on 1 July 1906, and labor conflicts at Cananea on 1 June 1906 and in the textile districts from late 1906 to Río Blanco on 7 January 1907 exposed the state's alliance with employers. Cananea and Río Blanco were distinct events with different contexts, actors, and demands, though both ended in repression and became symbols.

The financial disturbance of 1907 and poor harvests deepened discontent, and in March 1908 the Díaz–Creelman interview opened the succession question. Díaz's age, Corral's unpopularity, Reyes's following, and elite rivalries combined to produce a crisis of legitimacy within the political class. Francisco I. Madero's book of 1909 and antireelectionist campaign gave the crisis a focus, but his arrest and Díaz's reelection in 1910 persuaded many that elections would not bring change.

The Plan of San Luis Potosí, dated 5 October 1910 and completed later in Texas, declared the election void and called for armed action on 20 November. The uprising connected electoral opposition with rural, regional, and labor grievances, and within months it forced Díaz's resignation in May 1911. The fall of the regime was contingent, not inevitable, and its outcome, treated in Chapter 72, was not what the participants expected. Students should also take away a method: official, opposition, and foreign sources each served purposes, and historians must compare them.

Chapter Reflection

The Porfiriato matters because it shows how success and fragility can grow together. The regime built railways, balanced budgets, and created a sense of national order, and it did so through arrangements that excluded most Mexicans from political voice and left many exposed to the costs of change. The achievements were real and so were the exclusions, and the history of the period lies in the relationship between them.

The chapter also reveals how varied Mexico was. The grievances of Yaqui families, Morelos villagers, Yucatán plantation workers, Cananea miners, Orizaba textile workers, and provincial professionals were not the same, and the Revolution would draw on all of them without unifying them. Understanding this variety is a precondition for understanding why the revolutionary years brought not one movement but many, and why the settlement that followed took so long to establish.

Chapter 72 begins where this chapter stops, with the revolutionary challenge of November 1910 and Díaz's resignation in May 1911. It follows Madero's government, the coup of Victoriano Huerta, and the armies led by figures such as Villa and Zapata. Students should carry forward the view that the Revolution grew from structures built under Díaz, and that the regime's fall, though shaped by long-term pressures, depended on contingent events and on choices made by many Mexicans.

Later chapters will return to the questions raised here: land and village rights in Chapters 73 and 74, labor and the state, and the problem of constructing a legitimate political order after a revolution. In that sense the Porfiriato is both a self-contained period and the opening of the twentieth-century history of Mexico and of Latin America's first major social revolution.
