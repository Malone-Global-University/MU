The 2016 Colombian Peace Accord and the Search for Postwar Peace

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Ending a War Is Not the Same as Building a Peace

Colombia's peace agreement was signed twice, ratified without the public vote its authors had promised, and by 2026 had been implemented in part, resisted in part, and overtaken in some regions by other armed groups. It ended one of the longest guerrilla wars in the hemisphere, but it did not end Colombian violence. Understanding the difference between those two statements is the purpose of this chapter.

On 26 September 2016, in the Caribbean port of Cartagena, President Juan Manuel Santos and Rodrigo Londoño, the commander of the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia–People's Army (FARC-EP) known by the war name Timochenko, signed an agreement meant to end a confrontation that had lasted for more than half a century. Dignitaries, foreign leaders, and survivors of the conflict attended. Six days later, on 2 October, Colombian voters were asked to approve the text in a plebiscite and narrowly said no. The result stunned participants and pollsters alike. A revised agreement was negotiated, signed in Bogotá on 24 November, and approved by the Colombian Congress on 29 and 30 November. The sequence is central to the chapter: a ceremony is not a ratification, a ratification is not an implementation, and implementation is not yet a durable peace.

This chapter concludes the four-chapter Colombian sequence of Part XXII. Chapter 97 traced the cocaine economy and the Medellín and Cali organizations, Chapter 98 followed the confrontation between Escobar and the Colombian state, and Chapter 99 explained how guerrillas, paramilitaries, and the state, assisted by Plan Colombia, fought the war until the military balance shifted in the early 2010s. Those chapters supply the violence and its causes. This chapter asks a different set of questions. Why did a government and a weakened but still lethal insurgency agree to negotiate in 2012? How were the issues of land, political participation, drugs, victims, and disarmament made part of one settlement? Why did voters reject it, and what did the revisions change? What institutions did the settlement create, and what have they accomplished?

The chapter's chronology runs from 2012 to 14 November 2026, the editorial cutoff of this edition. Events after that date, including the tenth anniversary of the revised agreement on 24 November 2026, are future developments and are not narrated here. The reader should treat the closing sections as contemporary history. The record through 2026 is incomplete, many of its measures are contested, and some outcomes, including the fate of the transitional-justice system under a new government that took office in August 2026, remain genuinely open.

The chapter pays attention to who made peace as well as who signed it. Negotiators, guerrilla commanders, and presidents appear, but so do the delegations of victims who traveled to Havana, women's organizations that pressed for gender provisions, Indigenous and Afro-Colombian authorities who secured an Ethnic Chapter, rural families who signed coca-substitution agreements, former combatants who tried to farm, study, and organize cooperatives, and the municipal officials, teachers, and community leaders who tried to carry out a national agreement in places where the state had rarely been present. It also attends to those who opposed the settlement, for reasons that included moral objections to the treatment of crimes, doubts about the guerrillas' sincerity, and partisan opposition to the government that negotiated it.

Evidence in this chapter differs from that in earlier volumes. Instead of codices and chronicles, historians use the texts of agreements, constitutional and legal decisions, certified election returns, United Nations verification reports, judgments of a transitional court, a truth commission's final report, independent monitoring by the Kroc Institute of the University of Notre Dame, journalism, and the testimony of participants. Each source has a purpose. Government reports tend to emphasize programs launched, monitoring bodies emphasize obligations unmet, former combatants and victims' organizations emphasize lived experience, and political actors on every side use statistics to support their positions. The chapter tries to name these purposes and to avoid collapsing different measures into a single verdict.

The chapter's argument can be stated simply. The 2016 agreement was a historic achievement: it transformed a major guerrilla army into a disarmed organization and a legal political party, and it created institutions for truth, justice, reparation, and the search for the disappeared. But its authors designed it as a fifteen-year program of rural transformation and state-building as well as a ceasefire, and that second part depended on security, resources, administrative capacity, and political will that were unevenly supplied. Where they were absent, other armed organizations and illicit economies filled the space. The chapter keeps three things apart throughout: signing an agreement, implementing it, and building a peace that lasts.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Colombian government and the FARC-EP agreed to negotiate in 2012, distinguishing the incentives to negotiate from any claim of complete military defeat.
  2. Describe the structure of the Havana process, including the exploratory phase, the defined agenda, the guarantor and accompanying states, and the principle that nothing was agreed until everything was agreed.
  3. Identify the six substantive parts of the revised Final Agreement and explain how they combined disarmament with political, rural, drug-policy, and victims' provisions.
  4. Analyze the 2 October 2016 plebiscite using turnout, margin, and regional patterns, and explain why totals cannot reveal individual voters' motives.
  5. Compare the initial agreement with the revised agreement of 24 November 2016 and evaluate the debate over congressional ratification.
  6. Trace the laying down of FARC weapons under United Nations verification and distinguish the successive weapons totals.
  7. Distinguish the Special Jurisdiction for Peace, the Truth Commission, the search unit for the disappeared, and ordinary criminal courts by mandate and limits.
  8. Assess rural reform, the PDET and PNIS programs, and the reparation of victims by separating legal enactment, budget, initiation, and completion.
  9. Evaluate the roles of Indigenous peoples, Afro-Colombian communities, and women as political participants in the peace process.
  10. Differentiate FARC dissident organizations, the ELN, and the Gulf Clan from the original FARC-EP and from former combatants who honored the agreement.
  11. Interpret independent and official measures of implementation through November 2026, including the Kroc Institute's categories and the narrowed mandate of the UN Verification Mission.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XXII's argument that ending a major armed confrontation and building a durable peace are related but distinct achievements.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did negotiations succeed in 2012–2016 after earlier processes in the 1980s and 1990s had failed, and what does that comparison show about the conditions for a settlement?
  2. How did the decision to negotiate a comprehensive agenda, rather than only a ceasefire, shape both the achievements and the vulnerabilities of the final text?
  3. What can a narrow referendum result tell historians about public opinion, and what can it not tell them?
  4. Was it legitimate to ratify a revised agreement through Congress after voters had rejected the first? What arguments support each answer?
  5. How do truth-seeking, restorative justice, and conventional criminal justice differ, and why did the agreement combine them?
  6. Why did the rural and ethnic provisions prove harder to implement than the disarmament of the principal guerrilla organization?
  7. How should historians describe armed groups that emerged after 2016 without implying that the original FARC-EP simply continued?
  8. What makes a peace agreement "successful," and why do different measures yield different answers?

Historical Context

The FARC emerged from rural communities and Communist-influenced peasant self-defense groups in the 1960s, after La Violencia and the power-sharing National Front of 1958–1974 described in Chapter 83. Alongside it grew the ELN, founded in 1964 under the influence of the Cuban Revolution, and other insurgencies, some of which, such as the M-19, demobilized in 1990 and took part in writing the Constitution of 1991. The FARC remained at war. During the 1980s a negotiation under President Belisario Betancur produced the Unión Patriótica, a political party whose members were killed in very large numbers by paramilitaries, state agents, and criminal actors, a catastrophe that guerrilla leaders remembered for decades as proof that legal politics could be fatal. Chapter 99 described the expansion of the FARC and the paramilitaries in the 1990s, the failed talks of 1999–2002 at Caguán, and the military offensive of the Uribe years, which with Plan Colombia assistance killed or captured several senior commanders and pushed the guerrillas away from major population centers.

Colombia is a country of regions, and the war was unevenly distributed. The Andean cities of Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, and Bucaramanga lived with the consequences of kidnapping, bombings, and displacement, but the heaviest burdens fell on rural zones: the Pacific coast of Chocó, Cauca, and Nariño, the Caribbean hinterland of Montes de María, the eastern plains of Arauca, the lowland departments of Caquetá, Putumayo, and Guaviare, the northeastern frontier of Catatumbo, and the Magdalena valley. These were regions where state presence was thin, land titling was unclear, roads were poor, and illicit economies, above all coca, provided income where legal markets did not. The Indigenous and Afro-Colombian peoples recognized in the Constitution of 1991 lived disproportionately in such regions.

Colombia also had unusually strong institutions for a country at war. It held regular elections, sustained a professional civil service in many areas, and had an independent Constitutional Court that became a decisive actor in this chapter. The Victims and Land Restitution Law of 2011, enacted under Santos before negotiations opened, established the principle that the state would recognize and repair victims of the conflict while the conflict continued. In 2012, Congress passed a Legal Framework for Peace, a constitutional amendment authorizing transitional justice mechanisms. These measures meant that the peace process occurred in a state with legal tools for it, but a state that did not control many of the territories where the agreement had to be carried out.

Foreign actors mattered but did not dominate. Cuba and Norway served as guarantors of the negotiations, Venezuela and Chile as accompanying countries, and the United Nations, the United States, and the European Union played later roles. The United States, which had invested heavily in the military campaign through Plan Colombia, supported the process diplomatically and financially under President Barack Obama while keeping the FARC on its terrorism list until 2021. Cuba hosted the talks. Venezuelan governments had their own relationships with Colombian armed groups, which would matter after 2016.

Finally, readers should note the terminology. This chapter uses "the peace agreement" or "Final Agreement" for the settlement signed in 2016, "the FARC-EP" for the insurgent organization that signed it, "former combatants" for those who disarmed, and "dissidents" for armed groups that rejected or later abandoned it. Terms such as "peace," "success," and "implementation" are used with care, because the chapter argues that they refer to different things.

Section 100.1

Colombia in 2012: Military Pressure, Political Change, and the Opening for Negotiation

When Juan Manuel Santos took office on 7 August 2010, he inherited a conflict that had changed shape. As defense minister under Álvaro Uribe, he had overseen the offensive that killed the FARC commander Raúl Reyes in a 2008 raid across the Ecuadorian border and the rescue of hostages, including Ingrid Betancourt, in Operation Jaque. After becoming president, his government oversaw the killing of senior commanders, among them Mono Jojoy in 2010 and Alfonso Cano, the FARC's top leader, in November 2011. Cano was succeeded by Rodrigo Londoño, who had a reputation as a more pragmatic figure. The guerrillas had lost territory, commanders, and manpower compared with the late 1990s, and they had been pushed from many areas near the major cities.

But the FARC-EP had not been defeated. It retained thousands of fighters, adapted to smaller mobile units, relied on mines, ambushes, and harassment, and continued to draw income from the coca economy, illegal mining, and extortion. It remained dangerous in the Pacific lowlands, the southern departments, Catatumbo, and the eastern plains. Historians therefore describe the situation less as victory than as a mutually hurting stalemate: the state had gained the upper hand but could not quickly end the insurgency, while the insurgency could no longer plausibly hope to take power by force. The distinction matters. A negotiation between an insurgency that has surrendered and a government that has won would look quite different from the one that took place, in which both sides bargained from positions of relative strength and relative weakness.

Santos's calculations combined security, political, and economic motives that remain debated. Politically, he had been elected as Uribe's chosen successor, and his decision to negotiate soon placed him in open conflict with his predecessor, who regarded negotiation with the FARC as capitulation. Santos and his advisers believed the military pressure created a window that might not return and that continued war was costly to rural development, investment, and the state's legitimacy. They also drew lessons from earlier failures. The 1984 talks under Betancur had collapsed amid the violence against the Unión Patriótica. The Caracas and Tlaxcala talks of 1991–1992 under César Gaviria had ended without agreement. The Caguán process of 1998–2002 under Andrés Pastrana had given the FARC a large demilitarized zone, which it used to rearm, and had ended in public disillusion. Santos's team resolved to negotiate under a tighter structure, with a limited agenda, no demilitarized zone, and no ceasefire until an agreement was reached.

The government also prepared the legal and political ground. The Victims and Land Restitution Law of June 2011 signaled that the state would address victims' rights regardless of the outcome of any talks. The Legal Framework for Peace of 2012 amended the Constitution to permit special treatment of those who had committed serious crimes, within limits. Exploratory contacts began in secret in Havana in early 2012, conducted by a small group of officials and FARC representatives with Cuban and Norwegian facilitation. The FARC, for its part, had reasons to hear them: its leadership had suffered successive losses, it faced an aging cadre, and it understood that its political project had been blocked for decades by armed struggle itself. Some commanders probably also saw a negotiation as a way of preserving something of the organization's political identity.

Evidence for these calculations is incomplete. Memoirs of negotiators, including those of Santos, De la Calle, and Jaramillo, and interviews with guerrilla commanders, document the reasoning, but they were written to explain and defend choices. The text of the agreements is firm evidence about what was agreed, less so about why. Opponents, in turn, argued that the Santos government gave too much, while some on the left argued that it conceded too little on land and economic policy. Historians use these competing accounts as sources about perceptions, not as final judgments.

Section 100.2

The Havana Negotiations: Santos, the FARC, Cuba, and Norway

The secret exploratory talks produced the General Agreement for the Termination of the Conflict and the Construction of a Stable and Lasting Peace, signed in Havana on 26 August 2012. Santos announced the process publicly on 4 September 2012. The talks opened formally in Oslo in October 2012, and the main negotiations moved to Havana in November. The choice of venues reflected the structure: Cuba, which had its own history with Colombian insurgents and good relations with the FARC, and Norway, a country with long experience in conflict mediation and acceptable to the Colombian government, served as guarantors. Venezuela and Chile were named as accompanying countries. The United Nations and later the Colombian public were given roles at stages that were agreed on over time.

Context: The document was drafted after months of confidential exploration. It set the agenda and rules of the talks before the public learned of them, and it was intended to prevent a repetition of Caguán.

Editorial paraphrase: The parties agree to negotiate an agenda of five points, namely comprehensive rural development, political participation, the end of the conflict, the solution to the problem of illicit drugs, and victims, with a further mechanism for implementation, verification, and endorsement. The talks are to proceed in Cuba and Norway, with Venezuela and Chile accompanying, without suspending military operations, and under a principle often summarized as "nothing is agreed until everything is agreed." Mechanisms are to be created for citizen participation.

Analysis: The document reveals a deliberate design. Fixing the agenda in advance limited the government's exposure to demands such as changes to the economic model, while the commitment to the structure of "a final agreement" gave both parties assurance that concessions in one area would not be banked and ignored. It shows that negotiations would take place amid continuing conflict. It does not show how the parties understood each item, which was settled in later texts, and it says nothing about the opinions of Colombians outside the table.

  1. What does the document say about the agenda, location, and conduct of the talks?
  2. Who created it, and what past negotiation failures was it designed to avoid?
  3. What can historians learn about the parties' priorities from what is included and omitted?

The delegations reflected the parties' choices. The government's chief negotiator was Humberto de la Calle, a former vice president and constitutional lawyer who had helped write the 1991 Constitution. High Commissioner for Peace Sergio Jaramillo shaped the strategy, and the team included retired generals Jorge Enrique Mora and Óscar Naranjo, representing the military and police and giving the process legitimacy with the security forces. Business leaders and other officials took part at various times. The FARC delegation was led by Iván Márquez and included leaders such as Pablo Catatumbo and Jesús Santrich. Timochenko attended at key moments. Women's organizations criticized the near absence of women among the negotiators at the outset, a complaint that led to a gender subcommission and to the later inclusion of women in the delegations, among them the Dutch-born guerrilla Tanja Nijmeijer.

Negotiating during the conflict had consequences. The FARC declared unilateral ceasefires, including one at the end of 2012 and another in 2015, and the government suspended aerial bombing of FARC camps in 2015 after a de-escalation agreement, but the Colombian military continued operations for most of the process and violent incidents occurred. In April 2015, an attack on soldiers in Cauca led Santos to order the resumption of bombings, and the talks continued. These episodes showed both the fragility of the process and its resilience; they also fed the argument of critics that the guerrillas had negotiated while still killing.

Process mattered as much as substance. Sessions were conducted privately, with delegations working in Havana for weeks at a time and issuing joint communiqués. Subcommissions handled technical subjects, including gender, the end of the conflict, and, later, a technical group on victims. Citizen participation took the form of forums organized with the United Nations and the National University, and tens of thousands of proposals from civil society were submitted. Critics doubted that many were reflected in the text, but the process allowed organizations such as women's groups and ethnic authorities to press specific demands, as later sections show.

Section 100.3

The Negotiating Agenda: Rural Reform, Political Participation, and Illicit Drugs

The agenda's first point, comprehensive rural reform, addressed the oldest grievance behind the insurgency: the concentration of land and the absence of the state in rural Colombia. The partial agreement of May 2013 committed the state to create a Land Fund of three million hectares to be distributed to landless and land-poor peasants over twelve years, to formalize the titles of millions of additional hectares, to build rural roads, schools, and health services, to expand credit and technical assistance, and to prioritize the regions most affected by the conflict. The text was shaped by the Colombian reality that title was often uncertain or contested, that large estates coexisted with small plots, and that decades of war, including paramilitary land seizures and displacement, had transferred land to new owners by violence. Critics on the right worried that the provisions threatened private property, and the government insisted that the reform would not expropriate legitimate owners. Critics on the left argued that the agreement left the agrarian model untouched, since large-scale agribusiness was not addressed.

The second point, political participation, was equally rooted in history. The experience of the Unión Patriótica made security guarantees for former combatants and for opposition politics essential for the guerrillas. The partial agreement of November 2013 and subsequent texts committed the government to a statute for opposition parties, to measures protecting social leaders, to reforms to expand political participation in remote areas, and to the transformation of the FARC into a legal party. The final text provided for the party to receive ten seats in Congress, five in the Senate and five in the House, for two electoral periods regardless of votes, and it created sixteen temporary special districts for the House of Representatives in conflict-affected regions, reserved for candidates from those communities. These seats proved controversial. Supporters saw them as a minimal guarantee for a group that would otherwise be shut out or killed, and critics saw them as political rewards for those who had committed crimes.

The fourth point, illicit drugs, brought the cocaine economy described in Chapters 97 and 98 into the negotiation. The partial agreement of May 2014 acknowledged that coca cultivation and trafficking had become entangled with the conflict and with rural poverty. It committed the parties to a program of voluntary crop substitution with support for the households that grew coca, to treat growers as vulnerable rural families rather than criminals when possible, to continue the fight against trafficking organizations, to address consumption as a public-health issue, and to seek international discussion of drug policy. The FARC committed to cooperating in ending its involvement in the drug economy. The text did not legalize coca or drugs, and the government kept the option of forced eradication where voluntary substitution failed. This structure, which treated coca farmers as participants in a development program, set up one of the most difficult implementation problems, as Section 100.15 shows.

Taken together, these three points tied the settlement to the grievances traced in Chapters 83 and 99. The agenda was not a program of national transformation. It did not change the constitutional order, the structure of the economy, or the role of foreign investment, and the FARC dropped demands it had once made on these subjects. It nonetheless committed the state to build institutions in the regions where the conflict had been fought, and to treat the war's causes in the countryside as part of ending it. Whether that commitment could be carried out was the question that implementation would answer.

Section 100.4

Victims, Ceasefire, Disarmament, and the Design of a Comprehensive Agreement

The agenda's hardest point was victims. For more than a year negotiators found it difficult to reconcile the guerrillas' insistence that they were political actors with the government's insistence that serious crimes could not go unaddressed. A turning point came in 2014, when the delegations invited victims to Havana. Five delegations, comprising a total of sixty victims of guerrillas, paramilitaries, state agents, and other actors, traveled to Cuba between August and December 2014 and met the negotiators. Their testimony, including accounts of massacres, disappearances, kidnappings, and displacement, shifted the tone of the talks and made it harder to treat victims as an abstraction. The FARC later apologized in public for specific events, including the Bojayá massacre of 2002, in a ceremony in the community in December 2015.

The agreement on victims, announced in December 2015, established what the text called a Comprehensive System of Truth, Justice, Reparation and Non-Repetition. It comprised a truth commission, a unit to search for missing persons, a special jurisdiction for the crimes committed in the conflict, and measures of reparation. The system's central bargain was that those who acknowledged responsibility and told the truth would receive restorative sanctions, with restrictions on liberty but without ordinary prison, while those who did not would face conventional trials and long sentences. Amnesty was available for political crimes, but not for crimes against humanity and serious war crimes. The bargain tried to reconcile the demands of the Rome Statute and human-rights law with the practical need to induce combatants to demobilize, and it remained the most debated element of the settlement.

Gender was a separate negotiating issue. After criticism of the all-male delegations, a gender subcommission was established in September 2014 with women from both sides and civil society, and it reviewed the draft agreements. Women's organizations obtained more than a hundred gender-related provisions across the final text, including attention to women in land access, to sexual violence as a crime to be addressed by transitional justice, and to recognition of the rights of LGBTI people, an unusual feature in a peace agreement. The Ethnic Chapter, treated in Section 100.16, was added late in the process after pressure from Indigenous and Afro-Colombian organizations.

The agreement on a definitive bilateral ceasefire and the laying down of arms was signed in Havana on 23 June 2016, in the presence of Santos, Timochenko, Raúl Castro, UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon, and other figures. It provided for a tripartite verification mechanism of the government, the FARC, and an unarmed United Nations mission, for concentration of FARC fighters in designated zones, and for the surrender of weapons to the United Nations over a period of months. The ceasefire took effect when the final agreement was announced in August. The text on the end of conflict also addressed security guarantees for former combatants and the dismantling of criminal successor organizations, including a commission for security guarantees and a special investigative unit within the Attorney General's office.

Historical Vocabulary I
Peace agreement, ceasefire, demobilization, and reintegration

A peace agreement is a negotiated settlement between parties to an armed conflict, often a package of commitments rather than a single act. A ceasefire halts hostilities, either temporarily or definitively, and can be unilateral or bilateral. Demobilization is the formal disbanding of armed organizations, including disarmament, in which weapons are surrendered, and the assembly of members. Reintegration (in Spanish, reincorporación, a term the agreement preferred) is the longer process by which former combatants acquire civilian livelihoods, documentation, education, and political rights.

These words describe different stages that are often confused. A ceasefire can occur without disarmament, and disarmament can occur without successful reintegration. In Colombia, the agreement used reincorporación to emphasize a collective process rather than a purely individual return, and the distinction mattered when assessing results.

Section 100.5

The Cartagena Signing and the Promise of Peace

On 24 August 2016 the delegations announced from Havana that they had concluded the Final Agreement. Santos had promised repeatedly that the public would endorse the result, and the legal route for that endorsement was a plebiscite regulated by a statute of 2016. The Constitutional Court, in a decision issued in July 2016, approved the plebiscite but lowered the approval threshold to a majority of valid votes provided that the YES vote exceeded 13 percent of the registered electorate. The FARC held its Tenth National Conference in September and endorsed the agreement. The date was set for 2 October.

The signing in Cartagena on 26 September was designed as a national and international ceremony. Santos and Londoño signed the text in front of hundreds of guests, many dressed in white, including foreign presidents and officials such as UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon, Cuban President Raúl Castro, and US Secretary of State John Kerry. Press accounts widely reported that the pen used was made from a bullet casing, a gesture meant to show that weapons would become instruments of peace. Londoño asked forgiveness for the pain the FARC had caused. The event was broadcast widely, and for many Colombians, particularly those in conflict zones, it carried deep emotional weight.

The ceremony was not a legal ratification. Under the arrangement then in force, the signed text was to be submitted to voters, and only after endorsement would it enter into force through implementing legislation and constitutional changes. Historians distinguish three stages that the ceremony tended to compress: signing, which expressed the parties' consent; endorsement, which was meant to supply popular legitimacy; and implementation, which required laws, budgets, and administration. The symbolism of Cartagena may have encouraged the impression, shared by some supporters and by many foreign observers, that the war was over. In practice, the polls favored YES, and the campaign for approval was relatively short and, according to later analyses, poorly organized in the interior, where distrust of the FARC and of Santos was strong.

The Cartagena setting also helps explain why the referendum produced such a jarring contrast. A signing ceremony is a deliberately curated political event: the invited guests, white clothing, international presence, and language of reconciliation communicated a shared aspiration. The electorate, by contrast, had to decide whether to authorize a complicated institutional settlement involving special judicial arrangements, guaranteed political participation, rural development commitments, and implementation over many years. Photographs of dignitaries signing establish how leaders presented the settlement, not whether the country agreed. After the referendum, the more restrained Bogotá ceremony of November offered an entirely different political message: negotiation had continued, but legitimacy now had to be defended through a revised text, legislative debate, and contested constitutional rules. These two settings therefore form useful paired sources for studying the difference between public symbolism and enforceable institutions.

Section 100.6

October 2, 2016: The Referendum Defeat

The plebiscite asked voters whether they supported the Final Agreement for ending the conflict and building a stable and lasting peace. According to the Registraduría's subsequently published final departmental results, NO received 6,438,552 votes (approximately 50.22 percent of valid votes) and YES 6,382,901 (49.78 percent). The final margin was 55,651 votes, approximately 0.43 percentage points of valid votes; 13,062,917 people cast ballots, about 37.43 percent of the registered electorate. Election-night Bulletin 53 had reported a slightly different preliminary NO–YES margin of 53,894. Turnout remained exceptionally low, one of the lowest for a major national vote in recent Colombian history. Surveys before the vote had pointed to a comfortable YES victory, and the result surprised the government, the guerrillas, foreign observers, and the NO campaign itself.

The regional pattern was striking and has been widely commented on. YES prevailed in much of the Pacific coast and the south and in regions that had suffered most directly from the armed conflict: Chocó, Cauca, Nariño, Putumayo, Vaupés, Guainía, Amazonas, and Guaviare, along with La Guajira and Bogotá, where it won with a substantial margin. In some of the municipalities devastated by the war, including Bojayá in Chocó, the YES vote was overwhelming. NO prevailed in many departments of the Andean interior, such as Antioquia, Santander, Norte de Santander, and the coffee region, as well as in the eastern plains. These patterns, reported in the electoral returns, suggest that the experience of war was not simply correlated with support for the peace agreement in the way outside observers had assumed, and that the vote in the interior reflected political and cultural alignments as much as direct exposure to violence. Students should consult certified departmental results in Quantitative Study I.

Hurricane Matthew moved along the Colombian Caribbean coast around the time of the vote, bringing heavy rain to regions that had been expected to vote strongly YES. Analysts debated how much it reduced the YES vote. The effect cannot be precisely measured, and the margin was small enough that many factors could have been decisive. Historians therefore avoid attributing the result to any single cause.

The legal and political consequences were immediate. Under the plebiscite law, the government was obligated to treat the result as binding in some sense, and Santos acknowledged the defeat that evening. The agreement could not be implemented as drafted. The ceasefire, which was bilateral and by then in force, did not collapse: both sides said they would respect it, the FARC leadership announced it would continue to seek peace by words and the government extended the ceasefire. On 7 October the Nobel Committee awarded Santos the Peace Prize, announced five days after the referendum, which was read by many as an international appeal to keep the process alive. The prize was not shared with the FARC.

Not every voter who opposed the agreement opposed peace. Polls before and after the vote found that many NO voters said they wanted an end to the conflict but objected to specific provisions, particularly those on justice and political representation, while others distrusted the guerrillas' intentions. Historians should avoid attributing a single motivation to either camp. A NO vote was a rejection of a particular text, offered in a particular political context, and it shaped the content of the revised agreement.

Section 100.7

Opposition, Polarization, and the Debate over Justice and Political Representation

The NO campaign was led by former president Álvaro Uribe and his Centro Democrático party, with support from former president Andrés Pastrana, the Inspector General Alejandro Ordóñez, and a range of civic and religious voices. Uribe's argument, repeated in public statements, was that the agreement rewarded those responsible for kidnapping, massacres, and recruitment of minors with seats in Congress and light sanctions, that it did not require them to be imprisoned, and that it offered inadequate guarantees against the guerrillas' continued involvement in drug trafficking. He did not oppose negotiation as such and called for a different agreement. For his supporters, the issue was justice and the rule of law; for his critics, it was a partisan campaign by a leader whose own security policy had been repudiated by his successor.

Other arguments were more diffuse. Some opponents feared that the agreement's provisions on land would weaken property rights or move Colombia toward a Venezuelan-style socialist model, a claim that circulated widely on social media and in rural networks, though the text did not provide for such a model. Some religious leaders, including prominent Catholic and Evangelical figures, objected to the agreement's gender approach, which they described in terms of "gender ideology." The controversy overlapped with a dispute over a Ministry of Education guide on sexual diversity in schools, which was unrelated to the peace process but entered the campaign. The agreement's gender provisions concerned women's access to land and protection from violence, recognition of sexual violence, and nondiscrimination, rather than the educational content that became the focus of the debate.

Misinformation played a role, and one NO campaign manager gave an interview to a Colombian newspaper a few days after the vote, in which he acknowledged that the campaign had sought to generate indignation and tailored its messages to different regions. He later apologized for the remarks. These statements are documented, but they show the strategy of one campaign and do not establish the extent to which messages affected voters. The YES campaign also faced criticism for relying on the government's authority and for being slow to organize in the regions where NO prevailed. Historians should distinguish documented campaign claims, which are matters of record, from broader interpretations of voter motivation, which remain inferences.

The debate also reflected deeper tensions in Colombian society. Many urban and rural Colombians had suffered from guerrilla violence, including kidnapping and extortion, and could not accept the prospect that senior commanders would serve restorative sanctions rather than ordinary prison. Others had suffered primarily from paramilitary or state violence and saw justice for the guerrillas as only one part of a larger accounting. Santos himself had deep adversaries on both sides: some on the right considered him a traitor to his political family, and some on the left distrusted his government's record. The vote therefore combined questions about justice, political representation, personal and partisan loyalty, trust in institutions, and the memory of earlier failures such as Caguán.

After the vote, the FARC's public statements expressed regret and a commitment to continue the process. Santos began meetings with leaders of the NO campaign, civil society groups, religious leaders, and victims' organizations, collecting proposals for changes. The opposition's proposals were numerous: on justice, they called for sentences that included effective deprivation of liberty and for the exclusion of drug trafficking from political crimes; on politics, they asked that those convicted of serious crimes be barred from office; and on other issues, they asked for guarantees for property and for changes in the text on gender. These proposals shaped the next phase.

Section 100.8

The Revised Agreement of November 24 and Congressional Ratification

Negotiations resumed in Havana in October and continued for several weeks. The government relayed hundreds of proposals gathered from opponents, and the FARC agreed to a series of changes, though it resisted some. The revised agreement was announced on 12 November and signed on 24 November 2016 in Bogotá, in a more modest ceremony at the Teatro Colón. It incorporated changes in several areas, including the following. The revised text clarified that the special jurisdiction's sanctions would involve effective restrictions on freedom of movement and residence, monitored by an independent mechanism; it limited the tribunal's duration; it removed provisions allowing foreign magistrates to sit on the tribunal; it required the FARC to disclose assets for use in reparations; it strengthened statements on private property; and it reworded passages on gender and family to address objections. It also dropped language that would have incorporated the agreement into the constitutional bloc as a special agreement under international humanitarian law, so that its status would be set by Colombian legislation instead.

The government did not submit the revised agreement to a second plebiscite. Santos argued that the question had been answered by the earlier vote's demand for changes and that holding another vote would be risky and divisive. The Colombian Congress approved the revised agreement on 29 and 30 November 2016, after debates in which the Centro Democrático declined to take part in the vote. The agreement was then implemented through constitutional reforms and legislation, using a fast-track legislative procedure approved by Congress and reviewed by the Constitutional Court. The text was transmitted to the United Nations Security Council in a letter dated late November 2016, document S/2016/1063.

The legal framework supporting implementation was strengthened by later steps. The Constitutional Court approved the fast-track procedure and, in 2017, reviewed the constitutional amendment creating the Special Jurisdiction for Peace. A further amendment adopted in 2017 required state institutions to comply in good faith with the content of the agreement during the three presidential terms following its adoption, an arrangement intended to protect it from reversal by future governments, though its interpretation and the scope of the obligation have been debated.

The changes were not merely cosmetic. The revised text included more explicit rules on the Special Jurisdiction for Peace, the disclosure of FARC assets for reparations, respect for lawful private property, and interpretations of gender-related language, while retaining the principal rural, political, and victims' commitments. A historian must therefore compare the signed versions directly rather than summarize all objections as having been accepted. Ratification also differed from implementation: congressional endorsement did not automatically fund rural services, establish every judicial office, or guarantee local protection. In 2017 the Constitutional Court's decision C-332 limited parts of the fast-track procedure by protecting Congress's power to deliberate and amend government-submitted measures. That judgment shows why implementation remained subject to constitutional review and the ordinary separation of powers, notwithstanding the agreement's special procedures.

Historical Debate I

Was Congressional Ratification Legitimate after the Referendum Defeat?

The question: Did the rejection of the first agreement require a second popular vote on the revised text, or was ratification by Congress a lawful and legitimate democratic mechanism?

Interpretation A — A new popular vote was required: Critics, including leaders of the NO campaign, argued that the plebiscite was the form of endorsement the government had promised, that voters had rejected the agreement, and that the modified text was still substantially the same. On this view, bypassing a second vote undermined democratic legitimacy and weakened the agreement's authority among those who had voted against it.
Interpretation B — Congressional approval was lawful and legitimate: Supporters argued that the plebiscite was a political mechanism whose result obliged the government to renegotiate, which it did, that the revised text incorporated numerous opposition proposals, that Congress is the elected representative body, and that the country could not wait for another campaign while the ceasefire remained fragile. The Constitutional Court reviewed aspects of the legal route and found the procedure to be constitutional.

Evidence: The debate depends on the plebiscite law and the constitutional provisions on peace agreements, the specific changes in the revised text, the Constitutional Court's decisions, polling on public attitudes, and the political context of November 2016.

Why it matters: If the agreement lacked democratic legitimacy, later governments might claim a mandate to modify it, which is what critics did. If it was legitimate, challenges to it could be treated as challenges to a lawful settlement. Legitimacy also affects implementation by influencing how many Colombians viewed the agreement as theirs.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, using the specific changes in the revised text, and identify what evidence about public opinion would help decide whether another plebiscite would have produced a different outcome.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How the Agreement Came About

Long-term causes

Decades of rural inequality, weak state presence, the failures of earlier negotiations, the Unión Patriótica experience, and the war's rising human cost.

Short-term causes

Military pressure on the FARC-EP after 2008, the loss of senior commanders, Santos's decision to negotiate, and the FARC leadership's reassessment.

Event or transformation

The Havana negotiations of 2012–2016 and the Final Agreement.

Immediate effects

A bilateral ceasefire, the initial signing, a referendum defeat, and a revised agreement approved by Congress.

Long-term consequences

Disarmament of the principal FARC-EP, new institutions of justice and truth, an incomplete rural program, and continued violence by other groups.

Historical complexity

The agreement was produced by interaction among military pressure, domestic politics, international facilitation, and public opinion. No single factor explains it.

Section 100.9

The United Nations Mission and the Laying Down of FARC Weapons

After the revised agreement took effect, FARC fighters moved to designated sites in the first months of 2017. The sites were initially called transitional village zones and transit points, nineteen of the former and seven of the latter, scattered across rural departments, many in places that had been strongholds of the insurgency. The fighters lived there in temporary camps, often in difficult conditions, with delays in construction, supplies, and services that frustrated both the guerrillas and the government. In August 2017 the zones were converted into Territorial Spaces for Training and Reincorporation, which were no longer designed as places of concentration but as sites for the first stage of civilian transition.

The United Nations played a distinctive role. Colombia asked the Security Council for a political mission composed of unarmed international observers, which was authorized in 2016 and in July 2017 was succeeded by a Verification Mission with a broader mandate. The mission monitored the ceasefire, the registration and storage of weapons, and, later, the reintegration of former combatants and security guarantees. It worked through a tripartite mechanism that also included the government and the FARC. The arrangement was unusual because the disarming party and the state jointly supervised the process with an international body as arbiter. It gave both sides a way to dispute facts without returning to violence, and it gave the world a credible account of what had been surrendered.

The laying down of arms occurred in stages. Fighters registered their individual weapons, which the United Nations stored in containers and then removed. The mission also took charge of caches that the FARC had hidden in remote areas, which were located, inventoried, and emptied over the following months. On 27 June 2017, at a ceremony in Mesetas, Meta, attended by Santos, Londoño, and UN officials, the organization's final individual weapons were handed over. The UN's records distinguish stages. In the phase completed in June 2017, the mission reported 7,132 registered individual and collective weapons under its control. Its later consolidated report, which included additional operations involving stored arms and caches, counted 8,994 weapons. These are different reporting stages, not contradictory totals.

Disarmament did not automatically guarantee local security. Weapons surrendered to the United Nations were those of an organization that had agreed to demobilize. Armed groups that did not join the agreement, including dissident fronts and the ELN, kept their arms, and successor criminal organizations expanded into territories the FARC vacated, as Section 100.18 explains. The disappearance of a dominant armed actor in a region created a vacuum that the state frequently did not fill. In some areas, communities that had lived under FARC control for decades found themselves under new armed authorities or caught between competing groups. The distinction between demobilizing one organization and securing a territory is central to the rest of this chapter.

Section 100.10

From Guerrilla Organization to Political Party: Reintegration and Citizenship

Reintegration was both individual and collective. Individuals received civilian identity documents, enrollment in the health and pension systems, and a monthly allowance for a period, and they were offered education and training programs. The agreement also encouraged collective projects, and many former combatants formed cooperatives, including those linked to a national solidarity organization, to farm, produce goods, or operate small enterprises. A state agency, now called the Agency for Reincorporation and Normalization, coordinated these programs. In practice, the former territorial spaces became the sites of many projects: coffee, cattle, ecotourism, bakeries, and sewing workshops. Results varied widely by place. Some projects obtained land and markets, and others struggled with isolation, insufficient funding, lack of title to the land on which they were located, and security threats.

Some former combatants returned to home regions, where they faced social stigma and sometimes hostility, while others moved to cities. Many of the former fighters were from poor rural families, and many had spent most of their adult lives in the organization. Women made up a substantial minority, and they raised specific problems of childcare, reproductive health, and the status of children born during the conflict. The reintegration of those who had been recruited as minors posed distinct legal and psychological questions. Studies by the UN and by research organizations documented high levels of participation in education, rising employment in the informal sector, and persistent problems of land access and housing, though the numbers differed by source and date.

The transformation of the FARC into a political party was a symbolic and legal watershed. At a founding congress in Bogotá in late August 2017, the organization launched the Revolutionary Alternative Force of the Common, using the initials FARC. In March 2018 the party contested legislative elections and won a very small share of the vote, around half of one percent in the Senate, but under the agreement received its ten guaranteed seats. Its presidential candidate, Londoño, withdrew from the campaign in early 2018 after hostile public protests at events and a health crisis. In 2021 the party changed its name to Comunes ("the commons"), dropping the acronym that had come to symbolize the war. Its electoral weakness over the following years illustrated a point central to this section: the successful demobilization of an organization is distinct from its political popularity. Many Colombians continued to view the former guerrillas with deep hostility.

Threats to former combatants cast a shadow over the entire process. The United Nations repeatedly reported killings of former FARC members who had demobilized, as well as threats and forced displacement. Some were killed by dissident groups, others by criminal organizations, and others by unidentified perpetrators, and the circumstances were often unclear. The security guarantees promised in the agreement, including a special police unit and a national commission, were slow to deliver protection for people living in remote areas. The killing of former combatants undermined confidence in the process and encouraged some, though a minority, to return to armed groups. Historians should distinguish those who honored the agreement, those who stayed in civilian life under threat, and those who left it. Section 100.18 returns to the last group.

The distinction between registration and continuing participation matters in any account of reincorporation. Approximately thirteen thousand people were initially accredited in the transition, a group that included different categories of former members; by October 2026 UN reporting described more than eleven thousand still pursuing civilian reintegration. These figures represent different points in time and should not be interpreted as a single cohort's dropout calculation. The same mission documented 501 verified killings, 185 attempted killings, and 56 enforced disappearances since the accord, with distinct verification criteria for each category. The persistence of such threats challenged the practical credibility of the promised security guarantees, even where collective economic projects produced genuine gains.

Section 100.11

The Special Jurisdiction for Peace: Accountability, Crimes, and Restorative Sanctions

The Special Jurisdiction for Peace (Jurisdicción Especial para la Paz, JEP) was established by a constitutional reform in 2017 and began operating in January 2018. It is a transitional judicial body with several chambers and a Tribunal for Peace, and it has jurisdiction over serious crimes committed in the armed conflict before the agreement's cutoff, whether by former FARC members, members of the security forces, or other state agents and civilians who voluntarily submit. Its mandate is limited in time and by the nature of the crimes. It does not replace ordinary courts for most offenses. It was designed to apply a model different from conventional retributive justice, and understanding the model is essential to evaluating it.

The JEP's system rests on conditionality. Participants receive benefits, including amnesty for political crimes, special treatment, and restorative sanctions in lieu of prison, only if they provide full, detailed, and exhaustive truth, acknowledge responsibility, contribute to reparation, and commit to non-repetition. Those who do not may be tried in an adversarial process and face prison sentences of up to twenty years. Amnesty is not available for crimes against humanity, genocide, or serious war crimes, such as extrajudicial killings, forced disappearance, kidnapping, torture, and sexual violence. A law passed at the end of 2016 regulated amnesties. Victims have a right to participate in the process, to be heard, and to comment on sanctions. The JEP organizes its work around "macro-cases" that examine patterns of crimes and the roles of those most responsible, and it has opened cases on kidnapping by the FARC, extrajudicial killings by the army presented as combat deaths, the recruitment of children, crimes against the Unión Patriótica, and others.

The first major restorative sentences were issued on 16 September 2025. In the case on kidnapping and other deprivations of liberty by the FARC, the JEP imposed sanctions on seven former members of the organization's leadership, including Rodrigo Londoño. The decision examined the organization's kidnapping policy, which the JEP's own investigations had found affected more than twenty thousand people, a count that is the court's and is subject to refinement. The sanctions consisted of eight years of restorative work and activities, accompanied by restrictions on movement and monitoring, rather than prison. The judgment drew praise from supporters of the process as a demonstration that senior leaders could be held accountable, and sharp criticism from opponents, including victims' organizations and political figures, who argued that the sanctions were too light for crimes of this gravity.

The September 2025 decision did not end the judicial process. On 26 June 2026, the JEP Appeals Section issued judgment TP-SA-SP-620 on appeals brought against the kidnapping-case sentence by participants including victims, the Public Ministry, and the former FARC leadership. The court's published case record subsequently describes a second-instance conviction and an eight-year sanction framework. That appellate stage matters because the existence of a first-instance ruling, appellate review, and the actual performance of restorative work are three separate procedural events. The court's underlying investigations identified at least 21,396 victimizing incidents in the kidnapping and detention case; incidents should not be described without qualification as a count of unique persons.

Historical Vocabulary II
Transitional justice, restorative sanctions, reparations, and non-repetition

Transitional justice refers to judicial and non-judicial mechanisms used by societies emerging from war or dictatorship to address mass abuses, including trials, truth commissions, reparations, and institutional reform. Restorative sanctions are penalties designed to repair harm, such as work for victims' communities, combined with restrictions on liberty, as opposed to retributive punishment centered on imprisonment. Reparations are measures, including compensation, restitution, rehabilitation, and symbolic acts, intended to acknowledge and redress harm. Non-repetition refers to guarantees that abuses will not recur, including institutional changes.

These terms do not mean impunity or ordinary punishment. The Colombian system tried to balance competing demands: international law that limits amnesty for the gravest crimes, the practical need to give combatants reasons to demobilize, and victims' claims to truth and repair. Whether the balance was well struck is debated.

Section 100.12

The Truth Commission, the Search for the Disappeared, and the Historical Record

The Commission for the Clarification of Truth, Coexistence and Non-Repetition was created in 2017 and led by the Jesuit priest Francisco de Roux, with ten other commissioners. Its mandate was to clarify the patterns and causes of the conflict, to recognize victims, to promote coexistence, and to recommend measures to avoid repetition. It was not a court and could not determine individual criminal liability. It collected thousands of testimonies in Colombia and among exiles abroad, held public hearings of acknowledgment where perpetrators of different kinds and victims met, and worked with regional organizations and ethnic authorities. It published its Final Report in June 2022 under a title that translated as "There Is a Future if There Is Truth."

The Unit for the Search for Persons Deemed Missing (UBPD) was created at the same time. Its mission is humanitarian: to locate and, where possible, recover the remains of those who disappeared in the conflict and to return them to their families. It differs from the JEP, which determines responsibility, and from the Truth Commission, which seeks understanding. The scale of the problem is large, with well over a hundred thousand people reported missing by various counts, and the work of exhumation, identification, and communication with families is slow, technical, and dependent on information from perpetrators. Families, often led by women, have been prominent in demands for the search, in a pattern similar to that of the Argentine and Chilean movements described in Chapters 85 and 88.

The search unit's humanitarian purpose also shapes its evidentiary work. A family may supply a photograph, a remembered route, or information from a former combatant that could lead investigators to a burial site; forensic specialists must then establish whether remains correspond to the missing person before the family can receive them. Such information may overlap with evidence examined in criminal proceedings, but the unit's principal obligation is to search and respond to families rather than to prosecute suspects. The Truth Commission, by contrast, had a time-limited mandate to synthesize testimony and publish a narrative about causes, patterns, and responsibilities, while the JEP makes binding judicial findings in particular cases. Comparing their results without recognizing the difference among humanitarian search, nonjudicial truth-seeking, and adjudication would create a false appearance of contradiction.

These three bodies were designed to work together while remaining distinct. Truth-seeking aims at a broad historical account, criminal judgment determines individual responsibility under law, and humanitarian search locates the missing without assigning blame. A confession before the JEP may help the search unit, and the Truth Commission's findings may inform JEP cases. The differences also create tensions: victims may wish for punishment as well as truth, perpetrators may offer partial truths, and political controversy over the Commission's interpretations, including how it described state, paramilitary, and guerrilla responsibility, has continued. Historians treat the report as a major source and a contested one.

Section 100.13

Victims' Rights, Reparations, and the Difficulties of Return

Colombia had one of the largest displaced populations in the world, and its official registry of victims included more than nine million people, the great majority of them displaced persons. The Victims and Land Restitution Law of 2011, which the peace agreement incorporated and extended, set up a Victims Unit to register and compensate victims, a Land Restitution Unit to process claims by those who had been dispossessed, and specialized judges to decide such claims. The law's term was later extended by legislation to 2031. The agreement added commitments to collective reparation, to psychosocial care, and to the participation of victims in the transitional justice institutions.

Recognition as a victim is distinct from actual receipt of restitution or reparation. Registration was a legal and administrative status that opened the way to benefits, but the number of registered persons was far larger than the state's capacity to compensate them, and individual payments were modest relative to the harm suffered. Land restitution has proceeded slowly. Claims required proof of ownership or possession in regions where titles had never been clear, and judicial decisions in favor of claimants did not always lead to actual return. Many returning families faced renewed threats from armed groups that had benefited from the dispossession, and some community leaders who pursued restitution were threatened or killed.

Return to a place of violence involved more than property. Families that had fled massacres or forced recruitment weighed the memory of loss against the need for land and community. Women, who had often become household heads through the death or disappearance of male relatives, played central roles in organizing returns and demanding services. Children raised in cities had little connection to rural land. Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities faced collective displacement from territories to which they held collective titles, and their return was complicated by mining concessions, plantations, and armed actors. Psychological harm, including trauma, grief for the disappeared, and mistrust, was widespread, and the state's mental-health services in conflict regions were limited.

The reparations system also faced the question of who paid. The FARC's declared assets, which the agreement required for reparation, turned out to be small relative to the scale of harm, and implementation of the asset transfer was slow and contested. The principal burden fell on the state budget, which competed with other priorities. Victims' organizations differed in their views. Some regarded the process as the first time that victims were heard in a national negotiation, and others judged that promises of reparation had been made that the state could not keep.

Section 100.14

Comprehensive Rural Reform, Land Ownership, and the PDET Programs

Rural reform was meant to address the conditions that the agreement described as underlying the conflict. Its main instruments were a Land Fund, to be fed by land recovered from illegal occupation, expired concessions, donated land, and purchases, and from which three million hectares were to be distributed free to peasants without land or with too little; a program to formalize the titles of seven million hectares of small and medium rural property; a cadastre, or land registry, to be updated to reflect actual ownership and support taxation; and a package of public goods that included rural roads, electrification, irrigation, schools, health posts, housing, and technical assistance. The agreement also called for a special agrarian jurisdiction to resolve land conflicts, which Congress later authorized through a constitutional reform in 2023.

The vehicle for territorial transformation was the Development Programs with a Territorial Focus, known by their Spanish initials PDET. Presidential Decree 893 of 28 May 2017 established them in 170 municipalities grouped into sixteen subregions, selected through criteria that included poverty, the incidence of conflict, weak institutional capacity, and the presence of illicit crops. The subregions include the Alto Patía and northern Cauca, Arauca, the lower Cauca and northeastern Antioquia, Catatumbo, Chocó, the Caguán basin and Caquetá piedmont, Macarena-Guaviare, Montes de María, the middle Pacific, the Pacific and frontier of Nariño, Putumayo, the Sierra Nevada and Perijá and banana zone, southern Bolívar, southern Córdoba, southern Tolima, and the Urabá region of Antioquia. Each subregion was to develop a participatory plan, drawn up through community assemblies at the village, municipal, and regional levels, which would then be financed by national and local funds and international cooperation.

Historical Vocabulary III
Rural reform, PDET, PNIS, dissident groups, and implementation verification

Rural reform in the agreement means a package of land, infrastructure, and services for rural areas, not only redistribution of land. PDET (Programas de Desarrollo con Enfoque Territorial) are territorial plans for 170 prioritized municipalities. PNIS (Programa Nacional Integral de Sustitución de Cultivos de Uso Ilícito) is the voluntary crop-substitution program created in 2017. Dissident groups are armed organizations that rejected the agreement or abandoned it after signing, as distinct from the FARC-EP that demobilized. Implementation verification is the monitoring of how far commitments have been carried out, by international bodies such as the UN Mission and independent institutions such as the Kroc Institute.

Care is needed because the word "implementation" can mean a law passed, a budget assigned, a project begun, or a result achieved. Different monitors measure different things.

Section 100.15

Coca Cultivation, Voluntary Substitution, and Rural Economic Alternatives

The agreement's fourth point defined coca growers as rural families in need of alternatives. Decree 896 of 29 May 2017 created the National Comprehensive Program for the Substitution of Illicit Crops, the PNIS. Families that signed collective agreements committed to eradicate their coca voluntarily and not to replant, and the state committed to pay a subsidy for a defined period, to provide technical assistance and food security assistance, and to finance productive projects that would give the household a legal income. Nearly one hundred thousand families enrolled in the program in its first years, a sign that many farmers preferred an alternative if it were credible. The economic logic was that farmers grew coca because it was the only crop with reliable buyers, roads were poor, and legal crops could not compete.

The most recent comparable national cultivation observation available by the chapter's November 2026 cutoff was the UNODC–Colombian government survey for 2024, published in June 2026. It estimated 261,000 hectares at 31 December 2024, compared with 253,000 in 2023, an increase of approximately 3.5 percent. Regional trends diverged: the Pacific region reached 121,612 hectares, while Putumayo–Caquetá fell to 49,190. These are estimates of cultivated area, not measures of total cocaine produced, and the study expressly noted that estimating potential cocaine production required additional methodological work. A farmer's continued coca cultivation may reflect price, insecurity, debt, or failure of promised substitution support; area statistics alone cannot establish individual motives.

Monitoring by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime found that the rate of replanting among participating households was very low, which indicated that farmers who signed generally kept their commitment. The principal weakness was on the state's side. Payments and technical assistance were delayed, long-term productive projects were slow to materialize, and many households that had eradicated coca did not receive the full package. In some regions, farmers who had joined the program were threatened by armed groups or stigmatized, and community leaders who promoted substitution were targeted. The Atlantic Council and other organizations reported that the program's budget was only partly spent in some years. The failure to deliver also created distrust: families that had kept their word felt abandoned, and others concluded that substitution was not worth attempting.

Meanwhile, coca cultivation expanded. The UNODC reported roughly 146,000 hectares in Colombia in 2016, and its later surveys showed a rise to approximately 230,000 hectares in 2022 and about 253,000 in 2023. Estimates by the United States government have differed in level, though they show a similar broad trend. The cocaine economy described in Chapter 97 had adapted: demand in North America and Europe remained high, trafficking organizations continued to buy coca paste, and armed groups competed to control cultivation areas, laboratories, and export routes. The demobilization of the FARC removed one actor that had taxed and regulated coca production in some regions but left markets intact, and dissidents, the ELN, and the Gulf Clan stepped in. The expansion coincided with implementation of the agreement and was a major reason that critics described the drug provisions as a failure.

The debate over policy remained live. Aerial fumigation with glyphosate, suspended in 2015, was proposed again by some politicians, while others emphasized voluntary substitution and the crop-substitution approach. Governments under Duque emphasized forced eradication and sought to restore spraying, and the Petro government criticized the militarized approach and supported substitution and, internationally, a change in drug policy, while also facing criticism that its programs were underfunded. Historians should note that the number of hectares eradicated is a poor measure of rural development: eradication that is not followed by income does not remove the incentive to replant. A balanced assessment would examine household incomes, delivery of promised support, security, and market access, not hectares alone.

Section 100.16

Indigenous Peoples, Afro-Colombian Communities, Women, and Local Peacebuilding

The Ethnic Chapter of the agreement was the result of pressure by Indigenous and Afro-Colombian organizations, who argued that they had borne a disproportionate burden of the war and that a peace that ignored their collective rights would fail. The chapter, which forms part of the implementation section, states that the agreement must be implemented with respect for ethnic and cultural integrity, the right to self-government, prior consultation, collective territorial rights, and participation in the design and implementation of programs. It establishes a Special High-Level Body with ethnic representation to oversee the application of the chapter and provides for ethnic perspectives within the rural reform, political participation, drugs, and victims provisions, including collective reparation and protection of territories.

The communities were active participants in the process, not simply categories of victims. In the Cauca, the Indigenous Regional Council and its unarmed guard, which had resisted both guerrilla and army incursions into its territories, played an important role as political organizers and as defenders of community authority. Afro-Colombian councils in Chocó and the Pacific region, including those of Bojayá, were central to the demand for truth and for participation in the Havana process through delegations of victims. Their organizations engaged with the Truth Commission and the JEP, which opened a macro-case on harms to ethnic peoples and their territories. Their experience demonstrated how ethnic authorities used constitutional rights recognized in 1991 to claim a place in the transition.

Implementation of the Ethnic Chapter was uneven. Independent monitors found that its provisions advanced more slowly than others and that consultation requirements were sometimes not respected. Indigenous and Afro-Colombian territories continued to experience confinement, forced recruitment, assassinations of leaders, and displacement. In the Pacific region and in Cauca, armed groups competed for control of coca areas, mining zones, and corridors to the sea. Ethnic authorities often reported that they were the first to be targeted when they resisted, and the guards in Cauca suffered casualties. Where ethnic communities had strong organization and territorial titles, they could sometimes negotiate with armed actors or press the state, but organization could not substitute for protection.

Women's organizations were similarly active. They contributed to the gender subcommission, participated in victims' delegations, formed networks for local reconciliation, and pressed for the implementation of gender provisions. The Kroc Institute has identified 130 stipulations in the agreement as gender-related and 80 as ethnic-related, which makes the agreement notable internationally, though its monitoring found that gender and ethnic provisions generally lagged behind the average. Women leaders and former women combatants reported specific risks, including sexual violence and threats. Women leaders were among those threatened.

Local peacebuilding went beyond national institutions. Communities organized reconciliation events, memory projects, and shared use of spaces once divided by armed actors. Former combatants and neighbors in some places collaborated in cooperative enterprises, rebuilt schools and roads, and created local committees. These efforts often succeeded where there was local leadership and some security, but they depended on conditions that no community could supply by itself.

Everyday Life and Social History

Returning Home, Rebuilding, and Living Under Threat

For many people the agreement's meaning was local and concrete. A former combatant leaving a transitional zone faced the first civilian bureaucracy of his or her adult life: obtaining an identity card, registering for health insurance, opening a bank account, and negotiating with a landowner for access to a plot. Families that had been displaced for years considered whether to return, often sending one member first to assess safety. Teachers and health workers in remote municipalities sometimes found that the school or clinic promised in a plan had not been built, while neighbors who had been guerrillas, soldiers, or paramilitary collaborators lived in the same village, and everyone knew who had been which.

In regions where armed groups remained, daily life involved restrictions on movement, curfews imposed by armed actors, extortion of shopkeepers and farmers, and the risk of being accused of collaboration. Communities developed ways of coping, including collective meetings, humanitarian zones, unarmed guards, church networks, and women's committees. Reconciliation events brought former adversaries together to share meals or rebuild a bridge. The sources for such experiences include oral histories, testimony before the Truth Commission, local journalism, and community records, which are vivid but uneven, and they often give more voice to those with organized representation than to isolated families.

Section 100.17

Political Contestation and Implementation under Santos, Duque, and Petro

Implementation unfolded under four governments between 2016 and 2026, each with a different relationship to the agreement. Under Santos, the final year and a half after ratification was devoted to the legislative and administrative architecture: constitutional reforms, an amnesty law, the establishment of the JEP, the Truth Commission, and the search unit, the creation of the PDET and PNIS, and the arrangements for disarmament. Many of the laws were passed through a fast-track procedure that limited debate, which critics found objectionable, and implementation in the field lagged behind the legislation. The government faced a short time between the agreement and the end of its term in August 2018.

Iván Duque, elected in 2018 as the candidate of the Centro Democrático, had campaigned for changes in the agreement, particularly its justice provisions. He took office pledging to respect the agreement while modifying parts. In 2019 he objected to the statutory law governing the JEP, and the dispute among the presidency, Congress, and the Constitutional Court dragged on for months before the law was enacted with its central features intact. His administration emphasized a policy of "peace with legality," maintained the reintegration program and the PDET, expanded forced eradication, and was criticized by monitoring institutions for slow progress on rural reform and for inadequate protection of social leaders and former combatants. In January 2019, after a car bomb at a police academy in Bogotá killed twenty-two people, Duque ended the negotiations that had begun with the ELN under Santos. In August 2019 Iván Márquez, who had been the FARC's chief negotiator, announced from an undisclosed location that he and others were returning to arms, citing the state's failure to comply and prosecutions of former comrades, and founded the group known as Segunda Marquetalia.

Gustavo Petro, a former member of the M-19 guerrilla movement that had demobilized in 1990, won the presidency in 2022, the first leftist elected in Colombia's history. He pledged full implementation, including greater emphasis on land reform, and his government increased purchases and distribution of land and sought to recognize ethnic territories. It also launched a distinct project known as "Paz Total" (Total Peace), established by Law 2272 of November 2022. The policy authorized the government to pursue negotiations with the ELN and dissident groups, with legal tools to talk to armed groups with political status and to seek surrender arrangements with criminal organizations, including the Gulf Clan and urban gangs. Critics argued that Paz Total diverted attention and resources from the 2016 agreement, gave armed groups time to expand, and lacked clear legal and security frameworks. Its defenders argued that the 2016 agreement had not ended the war and that a negotiated approach to other groups was necessary. Talks with the ELN were suspended after the group's offensive in Catatumbo in January 2025, and ceasefires with dissident factions lapsed or fractured.

The relationship between the 2016 agreement and Paz Total is easily misunderstood. The 2016 agreement was a signed settlement with a single organization that included disarmament, institutions, and a long-term program, while Paz Total was a policy of parallel and diverse negotiations that produced no comparable settlement. Critics in 2026 often treated the two as a single failed project, while former president Santos argued that Paz Total had pushed the implementation of the agreement aside. The two should be kept analytically separate.

The 2026 election added a new stage. De la Espriella, a lawyer and political outsider, won the runoff of 21 June 2026 by a narrow margin, according to published post-election reports. The outgoing president refused for a time to recognize the result and alleged irregularities, which international observers and Colombia's electoral authorities rejected, and the transition between governments broke down in July. De la Espriella took office on 7 August 2026 in a ceremony in Cali. He had campaigned against Paz Total and had criticized the JEP, and according to press reports his government formally ended three dialogue processes with armed groups at the end of August. Former president Santos publicly urged him to implement the 2016 agreement and warned that attempting to dismantle the JEP would be a lost fight. Any modification of the JEP would require constitutional or legislative processes, and the government's actual policy toward the agreement was still taking shape at the editorial cutoff of this edition. The outcome is therefore unresolved, and this chapter does not predict it.

Contemporary verification (2026): Associated Press on the electoral-transition dispute; EFE on the August 7 inauguration in Cali; the August 30 presidential statement ending three particular Paz Total dialogue processes. The last measure did not annul the 2016 Final Agreement.

Section 100.18

FARC Dissidents, the ELN, Successor Criminal Groups, and Continuing Violence

By 2026 Colombia faced violence from several types of armed organizations. They should not be conflated. The first category consists of FARC dissidents, groups of former members or fronts that never joined the process or abandoned it. Some fronts in the south and east rejected the agreement from the start and coalesced as a force often called the Estado Mayor Central, associated with the commander known as Iván Mordisco. A later split produced a rival structure led by Calarcá. The Segunda Marquetalia, founded by Márquez and others in 2019, was a distinct group made up largely of former negotiators and commanders who had left the process. These organizations differ in leadership, ideology, and relation to drug economies, and they often fight one another as well as the state.

The second category is the ELN, the National Liberation Army, which was never part of the Havana talks. It is a distinct insurgency with a federated structure, a long ideological history, and a strong presence in Arauca, Catatumbo, Chocó, and the Venezuelan border region. After the FARC's disarmament it expanded into some territories the FARC left. The third is the Gulf Clan, also called the Gaitanista Self-Defense Forces or AGC by the group itself, a criminal organization descended from paramilitary networks described in Chapter 99, which controls trafficking routes and extortion economies in the Caribbean, Urabá, and other regions. It is not a guerrilla movement with a political program, though it has sought political recognition, and its leadership, origins, and motives differ from those of either the FARC dissidents or the ELN.

These groups are not simply the FARC-EP under another name. Their leaders, territorial interests, and relationships to the 2016 agreement vary. Most of the FARC's former members honored the agreement. A minority returned to armed groups, and the dissident groups recruited new members, including young people with no previous guerrilla experience. Estimates of the numbers of armed group members differ by source, and research organizations such as the Fundación Ideas para la Paz have reported significant growth in the numbers of armed combatants since 2016, a trend that critics used to argue that Paz Total had failed. Such counts are estimates and should be used cautiously.

Violence in these years had clear patterns. Armed groups competed for control of coca zones, illegal gold mining, smuggling routes, and border corridors. They killed social leaders, human-rights defenders, and former combatants, imposed confinement on communities, recruited minors, laid mines, and caused renewed displacement. Organizations that monitor killings of social leaders, including Indepaz, have counted well over a thousand since 2016, and the United Nations has recorded 501 killings of former combatants verified by the UN mission by October 2026. The offensive of the ELN against dissident forces in Catatumbo in January 2025 displaced tens of thousands of people in a few weeks and led the government to declare a state of internal commotion. In Cauca and Nariño, in the Pacific, in Chocó, and in Arauca, communities reported repeated clashes, attacks, and blockades.

Historians should avoid equating armed presence with territorial control. In many areas several groups were active at once, and control shifted with alliances and violence. Some communities negotiated local arrangements with armed groups to survive, others were displaced, and others resisted through unarmed organizing. The presence of armed groups is also not a measure of the success or failure of the agreement itself. It shows that Colombia's violence had many sources, including illicit economies, weak institutions, and regional rivalries, which an agreement with one organization could not remove.

Section 100.19

Measuring Implementation: The JEP, Kroc Institute, and United Nations Verification

How should historians measure implementation? Several bodies monitor it, each with its own method. The United Nations Verification Mission reports to the Security Council every three months and focuses on the matters in its mandate. The Kroc Institute's Peace Accords Matrix, run by researchers at the University of Notre Dame, which was designated by the agreement as technical support for the verification process, tracks 578 stipulations of the text. Government agencies, including the Office of the Presidential Peace Implementation Unit, publish their own indicators. The Comptroller General and the Ombudsman publish reports on spending and rights. The JEP issues decisions, and civil-society organizations such as Indepaz, the Fundación Ideas para la Paz, and PARES produce data and analysis. They often reach different conclusions because they measure different things.

The key analytical point is that legal enactment, budget allocation, project initiation, and completed implementation are distinct. A law may be passed without funds. Funds may be allocated but not spent. A project may begin but remain unfinished. A completed project may not produce the intended result. Governments may report that a program has been "implemented" when it has been legislated, while independent monitors count only stipulations that have reached full effect. The Kroc Institute, whose May 2026 report assessed implementation through November 2025, classifies each stipulation as not initiated, minimally implemented, at an intermediate stage, or completed.

Monitoring illustrates a problem of scale. Kroc's May 2026 assessment rated 578 specific obligations in a document designed for comprehensive implementation, using information gathered through November 2025. The United Nations mission instead reports on particular obligations assigned to its mandate and on risks visible to its monitors. A municipality may have a formally approved development plan yet lack a completed road; a former combatant may be registered in a reintegration program but still lack secure land or protection. These facts can coexist. To evaluate progress, a researcher should record the document or dataset, its observation date, the definition of the unit counted, the stage of execution, and what remains outside the measurement. Kroc also identified 130 gender-related and 80 ethnic-related stipulations; their lower completion rates show why a national aggregate can conceal unequal implementation for different groups.

The October 2026 Security Council resolution provides a second test of interpretation. Resolution 2833 extended the verification mission until October 2027, concentrating its tasks on the social, political, and economic reintegration of former FARC members and security guarantees. Removing rural reform from that particular mission's verification remit does not erase Point 1 of the 2016 agreement, the PDET decrees, or Colombia's constitutional and administrative responsibilities. It changes the international oversight arrangement and the kinds of findings the mission will be positioned to report. Scholars assessing the agreement in 2026 must accordingly distinguish an obligation's continued legal existence from the identity and capacity of the body assigned to monitor it.

Section 100.20

Colombia in 2026: Ten Years after the Peace Agreement

As of the editorial cutoff of 14 November 2026, the revised Final Agreement was nearly ten years old, and its tenth anniversary on 24 November 2026 had not yet occurred. The record permits a careful distinction between what is established and what remains contingent. Several outcomes are established. The FARC-EP as a large armed organization disarmed under United Nations verification, and thousands of former combatants entered civilian life. A legal political movement succeeded it, though with modest electoral support. The Colombian state created a Truth Commission that published a final report, a search unit for the disappeared that continues its work, and a transitional court that has issued sentences against senior former guerrillas and has pursued cases involving state agents. These are consequential historical outcomes.

Other outcomes are incomplete. According to the Kroc Institute's assessment through November 2025, a little more than a third of the agreement's stipulations had been completed, and about as many were at minimal implementation. Rural reform, which was intended to remedy the structural causes of the war, was among the slowest, and the land, titling, road, and service commitments remained far from complete in most priority regions. Substitution of coca crops reached many families but did not stop the expansion of cultivation. Many former combatants lived under threat, and the killings of former combatants and social leaders were an enduring indictment of the state's security guarantees. Violence involving the ELN, the Gulf Clan, and multiple FARC dissident groups persisted in the Pacific, Cauca, Catatumbo, Arauca, and elsewhere, and displacement and confinement continued.

Security and development were also moving on different timetables. The UN Verification Mission had verified 501 killings of former combatants since the 2016 agreement by its October 2026 reporting, while the 2024 coca-monitoring survey published in June 2026 recorded 261,000 hectares under cultivation. Neither figure on its own demonstrates that the settlement was unsuccessful: the first concerns a defined population of peace signatories over nearly a decade, and the second concerns the area of a commodity crop whose market is affected by actors beyond the original FARC. Together they show why disarmament of one organization cannot be equated with territorial security, the dismantling of illicit markets, or delivery of alternatives to rural households.

Politically, the agreement's survival and form depended on a new government that took office in August 2026 and had criticized parts of it, especially the JEP, while ending Petro's dialogue processes. The agreement has legal protections, including constitutional provisions requiring good-faith compliance and the constitutional status of the JEP, and the question of how the new government will use its powers to implement, alter, or neglect the agreement was open when this chapter was completed. On 30 October 2026, the UN Security Council renewed the Verification Mission through October 2027 but narrowed its mandate to the reintegration of former combatants and security guarantees, removing rural-reform verification from the mission's assignments without removing the state's commitments. Students should be careful to describe this accurately: it is a change in what the United Nations will verify, not a repeal of the rural-reform provisions.

Historical Debate II

Has the 2016 Peace Agreement Succeeded?

The question: By the standards of its own goals, and by comparison with other peace processes, has the Colombian agreement succeeded, failed, or something in between?

Interpretation A — A substantial but incomplete success: Supporters point to the disarmament of the largest guerrilla organization in the hemisphere, the end of a major armed confrontation between that army and the state, the integration of thousands of combatants, the creation of transitional-justice institutions that have issued findings and sanctions, and the introduction of victims into national politics. On this view, the agreement succeeded in what a peace agreement can do directly, and its shortcomings reflect incomplete implementation by successive governments rather than flaws in the design.
Interpretation B — A limited or failing peace: Critics point to expanding coca cultivation, the persistence of armed groups, killings of former combatants and social leaders, the slow delivery of rural reform, and the failure of many communities to see change. Some critics from the right add that the sanctions were too lenient and the political concessions undeserved. On this view, the settlement did not deliver security, and a peace that does not protect civilians is not yet a peace.

Evidence: Each side uses real evidence: UN and Kroc reports, JEP decisions, UNODC coca surveys, data on killings, and testimony. They weigh the same facts differently, and they define success differently, as ending one confrontation or as ending violence in general.

Why it matters: The answer affects policy choices, including whether to deepen implementation, renegotiate, or abandon the framework, and it affects how other conflicts are approached. The debate should not be reduced to a choice between complete success and total failure. Success on some dimensions coexists with failure on others.

Student task: Create a table with the dimensions of success named in the chapter plan: ending the principal government–FARC confrontation, disarmament, reintegration, transitional justice, rural reform, civilian protection, and the continuation of organized violence. Rate each as established, partial, or unresolved, with evidence, and explain which dimension you think matters most and why.

Part XXII has followed one country through its cocaine economy, its great trafficking organizations, its guerrilla and paramilitary war, and its negotiated peace. The agreement's central lesson is analytic. Ending a major armed confrontation and building a durable peace are related but distinct achievements. The first depends on negotiated commitments, political will, and disarmament, and in Colombia it was accomplished with respect to the principal guerrilla organization. The second depends on institutions that continue to function, on accountability, on rural opportunities, on the safety of civilians and former combatants, and on years of sustained implementation, and in Colombia it remained unfinished in 2026. The search for peace was not over. That conclusion, rather than a verdict of triumph or collapse, is the one that the evidence supports.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Colombia's government and the FARC-EP negotiated an end to their armed confrontation, why voters rejected the first agreement, how a revised settlement entered into force, and why peace remained incomplete in 2026. Its central argument is that ending a major armed confrontation and building a durable peace are related but distinct achievements, and that the agreement's history shows the difference between signing, implementing, and sustaining a settlement.

Negotiations became possible in 2012 because military pressure after 2008 had weakened the FARC-EP without destroying it, because President Santos judged that a window for talks existed, and because lessons had been drawn from the failures of the 1980s and 1990s, including the Unión Patriótica catastrophe and the Caguán zone. The General Agreement of 26 August 2012 fixed an agenda, a venue in Cuba and Norway with Venezuela and Chile accompanying, and the principle that nothing was agreed until everything was agreed, and it allowed talks to proceed while fighting continued.

The agenda tied the settlement to historical grievances. Rural reform addressed land inequality and the absence of the state, political participation addressed the exclusion and killing of leftist politicians, and the drugs point treated coca growers as participants in a development program. The victims agreement, shaped by delegations of victims who traveled to Havana, created a comprehensive system of truth, justice, reparation, and non-repetition, while the ceasefire and disarmament arrangements provided for United Nations verification. The Final Agreement thus had six parts, and disarmament was only one of them. Women's and ethnic organizations obtained gender and ethnic provisions that were unusual in peace agreements.

The ceremony in Cartagena on 26 September 2016 was a public symbol, not a ratification. On 2 October 2016, voters rejected the agreement by a final margin of 55,651 votes, on turnout of about 37 percent. The regional pattern showed support in many of the areas most affected by the war and opposition in much of the Andean interior. The result could not reveal individual motives, and many who voted NO said they wanted peace but objected to specific provisions, particularly on justice and political representation. Campaign messages, partisan loyalty, weather, and distrust all played roles that historians cannot precisely measure.

A revised agreement was signed on 24 November 2016 and approved by Congress on 29 and 30 November without a second plebiscite. It retained the six-part structure but altered provisions on sanctions, asset disclosure, property, gender language, and legal status. Whether ratification by Congress was legitimate remains debated: critics argued that a second popular vote was required, while supporters argued that the revision incorporated opposition proposals and that Congress and the Constitutional Court provided a lawful route.

Disarmament was completed under United Nations verification in 2017, when thousands of weapons were handed over. The weapons totals reported at successive stages, 7,132 in June 2017 and 8,994 in the consolidated report, count different stages and are not contradictory. Former combatants began a process of reintegration, and the organization became a legal political party, later called Comunes, which won little electoral support but received guaranteed seats. Reintegration was often successful at the level of individuals and cooperatives and was threatened by killings of former combatants, stigma, and insecure land.

The transitional-justice institutions were consequential. The Special Jurisdiction for Peace began operating in 2018 and issued its first major restorative sentences on 16 September 2025, imposing eight-year sanctions on seven former FARC leaders in the kidnapping case. The Truth Commission published its report in 2022 with a retrospective estimate of 450,664 conflict-related homicides between 1985 and 2018, a figure that differs in period and method from other counts. The search unit continued the humanitarian work of locating the disappeared. The three bodies pursue different aims and cannot be treated as a single form of justice.

Rural reform and substitution proved harder than disarmament. The PDET program was established in 170 municipalities in sixteen subregions, and the PNIS enrolled nearly a hundred thousand families, but delivery of land, roads, services, and productive projects lagged, and coca cultivation expanded after 2016. The Ethnic Chapter and gender provisions advanced unevenly. Victims were recognized in large numbers, but recognition was distinct from restitution and reparation, and return to areas where armed groups remained was dangerous.

Armed violence continued, but not as a simple continuation of the FARC-EP. FARC dissident organizations, the ELN, and the Gulf Clan have different leaders, territories, and relationships to the agreement and to illicit economies. Most former FARC members honored the agreement. Violence killed social leaders and former combatants and displaced communities in Catatumbo, Cauca, Chocó, Arauca, Nariño, and elsewhere. Armed presence should not be equated with territorial control, and continuing violence should not be equated with total failure of the agreement.

Implementation was shaped by changing governments. Duque criticized parts of the agreement and objected to the JEP statute, Petro pursued Paz Total alongside the agreement, and De la Espriella, who took office on 7 August 2026, criticized the JEP, ended three dialogue processes, and left unresolved what he would do with the 2016 framework. The Kroc Institute's May 2026 assessment found 36 percent of 578 stipulations completed, 21 percent at intermediate progress, 35 percent at minimal implementation, and 8 percent not initiated. On 30 October 2026 the Security Council renewed the UN mission through October 2027 with a narrower mandate. As of 14 November 2026, the process was neither finished nor failed, and its future depended on decisions not yet made.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what a negotiated end to a long insurgency can and cannot do. The agreement transformed a major guerrilla army into a disarmed organization and a legal party, and it built institutions that give victims a public role and force perpetrators to account for their acts in new ways. Those are real changes. It also shows that a negotiated settlement with one organization does not alter the economic and territorial conditions that sustain other armed groups.

The chapter also reveals the importance of legitimacy and implementation. A signing ceremony, a referendum defeat, and a congressional ratification each had different meanings, and the conflict over how to understand them continued to shape politics for a decade. The decade after 2016 saw different governments treat the same text as a binding settlement, a flawed compromise, or an obstacle, and the difference between legal protection and real delivery was visible in rural regions that waited for roads, land, and security.

Part XXII began with the cocaine economy and its great organizations, followed the escalation of violence and the confrontation between criminal groups and the state, traced guerrillas and paramilitaries through Plan Colombia, and ends here with a peace process whose results remain contested. The unfinished rural transformation of Colombia, the persistence of coca economies, and the hostility of many Colombians to the former guerrillas are continuities that stretch back through the earlier chapters. What changed is that one major armed confrontation ended, with consequences that will take years to assess.

Chapter 101 turns to Mexico and the cartel wars, where trafficking organizations, political change, and the militarization of counternarcotics policy produced a different relationship among crime, the state, and the United States. Students should carry forward the Colombian lessons about distinguishing negotiated settlements from durable peace, and about reading claims of success and failure against the evidence on which they rest.
