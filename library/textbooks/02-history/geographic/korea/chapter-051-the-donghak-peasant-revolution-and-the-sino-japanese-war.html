The Donghak Peasant Revolution and the Sino-Japanese War

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 1893–1894, in Gobu County in Jeolla Province, farmers faced a new reservoir, built with forced labor, and a water tax on fields that had been promised an exemption. Their petitions failed. A few weeks later, hundreds of them attacked the county offices. Within four months, a force of thousands had defeated provincial troops and occupied the provincial capital. Within eight months, Japanese soldiers had occupied the royal palace in Seoul and a war between Japan and China was under way on Korean soil. By the spring of 1895, the peasant army had been crushed, its leaders executed, and the Qing empire had agreed to recognize Korea's independence.

This chapter studies the connection between those events. It treats the Donghak Peasant Revolution as a Korean movement with its own causes and aims, not as a prologue to a war between foreign powers. At the same time, it explains how the revolution's course intersected with the strategic decisions of the Qing and Japanese governments and why the outcome transformed Korea's position in East Asia without resolving the problems that had produced the uprising.

The central question is how a movement for rural justice and political reform became entangled with a war between China and Japan. The answer lies in several layers: agrarian crisis and the weakness of local government; a religious network able to organize protest; a court that, in fear, requested foreign troops; and Japanese and Qing leaders who chose to use the occasion for their own purposes.

The evidence is varied and uneven. Court chronicles and military reports record the government's view. Donghak histories preserve the movement's memory but were compiled later. The interrogation record of Jeon Bong-jun is a rare near-contemporary voice, though given in captivity. Diplomatic papers from Qing, Japanese, and British archives explain the escalation. Ordinary farmers appear mainly through reflection in other people's records.

The chapter builds on earlier chapters: Chapter 44 on rural unrest, Chapter 46 on Donghak, Chapters 48–50 on the opening of Korea and the Imo and Gapsin crises. It stops short of the reform program of the Gabo government and the assassination of Queen Min, which Chapter 52 examines, and of the Korean Empire, treated in Chapter 53.

Its structure follows six divisions: the origins of the revolt; the first uprising; the truce and the experiment in local government; foreign intervention and the outbreak of war; the second uprising and its repression; and the wider war and the peace settlement. Along the way, students will practice distinguishing contemporary evidence from later memory, local grievance from religious organization, and declared independence from effective sovereignty.

Historical Context

By 1894, Joseon was in its fifth century. Its monarchy, under King Gojong (r. 1863–1907), governed through the court, the provinces, and local magistrates; rural society was organized by status (Chapter 29) and by tenure and tax. The country had been forced into treaty relations after 1876 (Chapter 48); Japanese and Chinese merchants and officials competed in the treaty ports; and Qing influence had grown after the military mutiny of 1882 and the failed coup of 1884 (Chapter 50).

Rural conditions had been deteriorating. Unauthorized levies, the grain-loan system, and the sale of offices produced chronic grievances, while the growth of rice exports linked farmers to a market that benefited some more than others. Donghak had grown in this setting, despite persecution, as a network of congregations in the south and center of the peninsula.

The international context was a rivalry between Qing China, which sought to turn customary suzerainty into more direct influence, and Japan, which sought to end that predominance. The 1885 Convention of Tianjin restrained both for nearly a decade. Russia and Britain watched, each with its own interests. Korea's government, led by Gojong and influenced by Queen Min's family, tried to balance among these powers.

Section 51.1

Agrarian Grievances and the Crisis of Rural Government

By the early 1890s, most Koreans lived by farming, and the central fact of rural life was the relationship among the cultivator, the landholder, and the local government. A tenant typically paid a large share of the harvest to the owner; an owner-cultivator paid land tax to the state; and nearly every household was drawn into a layered system of grain loans, labor demands, and local levies. The legal tax burden was fixed by statute, but the sums actually collected depended heavily on the magistrate (suryeong), his clerks (iseo), and the intermediaries who handled collection. Chapter 44 traced how this system produced the Hong Gyeong-nae Rebellion of 1811 and the mass protests of 1862; the problems it described had not been solved by the 1890s.

Several abuses recur in the sources. Officials added unauthorized surcharges to the land tax. The state's grain-loan system (hwanja), originally a form of relief that lent seed grain in spring and collected it with interest in autumn, had in many places become a compulsory loan that functioned as a second tax, sometimes recorded as lent when no grain had changed hands. Local clerks inflated measures, demanded fees for routine services, and manipulated registers. Offices were frequently obtained through payment, which gave appointees a motive to recover their costs from the districts they governed. The region later at the center of the revolt, Jeolla Province, was among the peninsula's richest rice-producing areas, which made it both a prime target for extraction and a place where rural people had much to lose.

Historians distinguish structural from proximate causes. The structural pressures were fiscal weakness in the central state, the commercialization of rice and the growth of rice exports through the treaty ports opened after 1876 (Chapter 48 and Chapter 49), the rise of rural debt, and the unevenness between landlords who could profit from the market and small cultivators who could not. Rising Japanese rice purchases, in particular, are often linked to grain-price pressure on poorer households, although scholars debate how large and how uniform that effect was. The proximate causes were local: in each district, specific officials and specific levies provoked specific protests.

The limits of the government's response matter as much as the abuses. Petitioning was a legitimate, long-established channel, and magistrates were sometimes removed. But punishments were inconsistent, new appointees often repeated their predecessors' practices, and the court in Seoul, as Chapter 43 explained, was preoccupied with factional politics and finance. For many cultivators, the problem was therefore not an absence of law but the failure of existing remedies. That failure, rather than any single tax, created the conditions in which an organized religious network could become a vehicle for rural protest.

Evidence. Historians reconstruct these conditions from tax registers and county records, the court's own chronicles (the Seungjeongwon ilgi and the Gojong sillok), petitions, local scholars' diaries such as Hwang Hyeon's Maecheon yarok, and later testimony. Most of these were written by officials or literati, who tended to describe rural unrest either as misgovernment or as disorder; farmers' own accounts survive mainly through trial records and later memory.

Section 51.2

Donghak's Religious Message and Its Social Appeal

Chapter 46 treated Donghak ("Eastern Learning") as a religious movement and need not be repeated here. Its founder, Choe Je-u, was executed in 1864 on charges of deceiving the people; his successor Choe Si-hyeong rebuilt a clandestine network of local communities over the following three decades. By the 1890s Donghak adherents were organized in smaller congregational units called jeop, led by jeopju; several jeop could be grouped into a larger organization called a po, led by a senior organizer. Their exact regional arrangements were not uniform. Believers were concentrated in Chungcheong, Jeolla, and Gyeongsang provinces.

Three features of Donghak's teaching help explain its social reach. First, it taught that Heaven is not remote but present in every person, a doctrine that Choe Si-hyeong and later leaders expressed in the idea of treating people as one would treat Heaven. Although the movement did not abolish social distinctions in practice, this teaching gave dignity to commoners and, in some congregations, encouraged believers of different status to meet as co-religionists. Second, Donghak offered moral renewal and ritual practice, including incantation and mutual aid, at a time when Neo-Confucian orthodoxy offered little to ordinary farmers and when Western Christianity (Chapter 45) and imported goods were widely felt as threats. Third, its rhetoric condemned both corrupt rule and foreign encroachment, which allowed it to speak to several grievances at once.

None of this made Donghak identical to the peasant uprising. A religious network is a means of communication and trust, and in societies where public assembly was dangerous, such networks mattered. But belief, kinship, village loyalty, and grievance were separate things; many who joined the revolt in 1894 were not initiates. Whether Donghak is best understood as the cause, the organization, or merely one among several vehicles of the revolt is a central historical question, taken up in the later debate on the character of the movement.

Evidence. Our knowledge of Donghak comes from its own scriptures and later compilations of its history, from government prosecutions that depicted it as heterodox, and from foreign observers. Each has a bias: believers' histories celebrate their leaders, and official sources treat the religion as a threat.

Section 51.3

Donghak Mobilization and the Petition Campaigns of 1892–1893

After 1892 Donghak leaders sought to end the persecution of believers and to clear Choe Je-u's name. In late 1892, assemblies at Gongju in Chungcheong and Samnye in Jeolla petitioned provincial governors, asking for relief from the arrest and extortion of adherents. These petitions were framed in the language of loyalty to the king and of lawful appeal. The governors made limited promises and, in practice, persecution continued.

In early 1893 Donghak delegates went to Seoul and, in a ritual form of protest, prostrated themselves before the palace gates to present a memorial to the throne; the court responded with conciliatory words and ordered the dispersal of the petitioners. The most visible mass gathering followed in the spring of 1893 at Boeun in Chungcheong. There, thousands of adherents assembled, and the demands broadened. Some banners and statements at Boeun are recorded as attacking the presence of Japanese and Westerners, indicating that anti-foreign sentiment had joined the call for religious toleration. There were also reports of a parallel gathering at Geumgu in Jeolla led by Jeon Bong-jun and others, who favored more forceful positions.

These campaigns show a split that would reappear in 1894. Choe Si-hyeong and other leaders based in the north favored petitioning and caution, partly because they feared the destruction of the network. More militant regional leaders, particularly in southern Jeolla, were prepared to move toward armed action. Historians often describe a division between the "northern" and "southern" Donghak organizations, though they caution that the labels simplify a complex pattern of personal relations and local interests. The government responded to Boeun by sending an official to investigate and by dispatching troops to the vicinity. The assemblies dispersed without a battle, but the court had seen that Donghak could gather large crowds quickly.

Evidence. These events are documented in government dispatches and in Donghak records, which sometimes disagree about numbers and about who gave which orders. Figures for attendance are rough estimates, not counts.

Section 51.4

Gobu County, Jo Byeong-gap, and the Local Crisis

Gobu was a county in northwestern Jeolla Province whose agricultural wealth depended on irrigation. In 1892 Jo Byeong-gap was appointed magistrate. The accounts that survive, from official investigators and later narratives, agree on the outlines of the complaints against him. He compelled villagers to build a new reservoir below an existing one, using forced labor, and then charged a water tax on fields irrigated from it, reportedly after promising an initial exemption. He is also accused of extorting money from wealthy residents on false charges and of collecting funds to build a memorial for his father.

The county's residents petitioned repeatedly. According to the tradition most often repeated, Jeon Chang-hyeok, the father of Jeon Bong-jun, was among the petitioners, was punished, and died afterward; this account comes mainly from later sources, and historians treat the details cautiously. What is secure is that petitions to the county and provincial offices were rejected or ignored. Jo was briefly reassigned elsewhere in 1893 but reappointed to Gobu, which many residents saw as a sign that their complaints would never be addressed.

Gobu mattered because it combined features that appear across the countryside: a specific, well-documented abuse; a failed legal remedy; local leaders with prior experience in organizing; and a nearby Donghak network. The county was not unique, and similar disputes occurred in other districts. Its significance lies in the fact that here a local protest crossed the line from petition to action.

Evidence. The case is known through investigators' reports to the court, which blamed some parties and minimized others, and through memories recorded decades afterward. Care is needed because the story of Jo's abuses became central to the later memory of the revolution.

Section 51.5

Jeon Bong-jun and the Networks of Peasant Leadership

Jeon Bong-jun (born about 1855) was a village schoolteacher and local figure of modest standing from the Gobu area, later nicknamed Nokdu Janggun, the "Green Pea General," probably for his small stature. He was a Donghak adherent and held a position within its local organization. Other leaders in the south included Son Hwa-jung and Kim Gae-nam, both Donghak leaders with their own followings. Their relationship to Jeon is better described as an alliance among regional leaders than as a chain of command.

The organization of the revolt rested on layered networks. Donghak jeop and the larger po networks provided contacts across villages. Kinship, neighborhood ties, and work relationships carried messages and mobilized recruits. Local literati, small landholders, tenants, laborers, runners, and discharged soldiers participated for varied reasons. Women appear in the records mainly as members of households who supplied food, carried messages, and suffered reprisals, and as victims; evidence of their direct leadership is scarce.

A crucial point is that the revolt cannot be reduced to a Donghak uprising. In his 1895 interrogation, Jeon himself distinguished the small number of Donghak members involved at Gobu from the many aggrieved residents. This testimony is valuable but must be read with the knowledge that he was a prisoner answering questions from officials who sought to establish responsibility and, in some respects, to place it on the movement; he may have had reasons to emphasize some points and to omit others. The revolt began, in other words, without a single national command, as the convergence of local anger and religious organization.

Evidence. Biographical details for these men come from interrogation and trial records and later Donghak and nationalist histories. Their reliability varies, and the later heroization of Jeon makes critical reading essential.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary: Po, Jeop, and Jipgangso

Donghak organizational terms

Definition. Jeop were smaller Donghak congregational units led by jeopju; po brought together multiple jeop under more senior leaders. Jipgangso (also rendered "directorates") were offices set up in 1894 in many southwestern counties to administer local affairs under peasant-army leadership.

Historical context. The terms come from the Donghak organizational tradition and from the movement's own record of 1894, and they appear in later scholarship in varied English translations.

Potential misunderstanding. These were not formal institutions of the Joseon state, and they did not form a uniform hierarchy; arrangements varied by district and by period. Treating all participants in the revolt as registered Donghak adherents would erase the many non-believers who joined.

Section 51.6

The Gobu Uprising of Early 1894

In mid-February 1894 (the tenth day of the first lunar month, in most accounts), several hundred villagers and Donghak adherents under local leaders including Jeon Bong-jun assembled and attacked the Gobu county offices. They seized weapons from the armory, took grain from official stores, released prisoners according to some accounts, and tore down the new reservoir embankment that had been built under forced labor. Magistrate Jo Byeong-gap fled. The rebels refrained, by most accounts, from killing officials or looting private homes, and they withdrew from the town after a few days.

The goals were local and specific: to punish an abusive magistrate, to cancel the unjust water tax, and to recover grain taken through the loan system. The participants were not yet a standing army; they were a crowd mobilized for a particular redress, and many returned to their villages. For this reason historians distinguish the Gobu uprising from the organized peasant revolution that followed in April.

The central government initially responded by replacing Jo, appointing a new magistrate who promised to address grievances, and sending an official to investigate. The pattern, in other words, resembled earlier episodes in which the state conceded local demands while preserving its basic authority. If the story had ended here, Gobu might have been remembered as one more county revolt like those catalogued in Chapter 44.

Evidence. Reports to the court and the later testimony of participants agree on the general sequence but differ on numbers and on the role of Donghak; the 1895 interrogation, cited earlier, suggests that most participants were not believers.

Section 51.7

Government Investigation, Repression, and the Mujang Rising

The investigator dispatched to Gobu, Yi Yong-tae, took a very different line from the new magistrate. Instead of focusing on official abuse, he treated the episode as a Donghak conspiracy. According to Donghak and later accounts, his men arrested suspects, burned houses of people thought to be involved, and extorted money. Whether every claim is accurate is difficult to verify, but the broad pattern, that investigators punished the protesters rather than the officials, is consistent across sources and is crucial for understanding what followed.

The reprisals converted a limited protest into a broader crisis. Leaders who might have accepted compromise now faced arrest, and the villagers whose homes were burned had little to lose. Jeon Bong-jun and other leaders, notably Son Hwa-jung and Kim Gae-nam, issued calls to rise. In late April 1894 (around April 25, the twentieth day of the third lunar month), thousands assembled at Mujang in Jeolla Province and issued a declaration explaining the rising.

The Mujang gathering marks the beginning of the revolution proper. Unlike Gobu, it was planned, multi-county, and led by a coalition of Donghak leaders; it aimed beyond a single official to the broader abuses of government. Importantly, the Mujang participants moved on rather than disbanding.

Evidence. The principal sources are the court's records of its investigators' reports, Donghak compilations, and local diaries. Because the reports served officials who wanted to show they were restoring order, they emphasize rebel violence and downplay official misconduct.

Section 51.8

Baeksan and the Formation of the Peasant Army

From Mujang the assembled force marched to Baeksan, a defensible hill position, around April 30. There it was organized as an army. Jeon Bong-jun was acclaimed commander, with Son Hwa-jung and Kim Gae-nam among the senior leaders, and a staff structure was set up, as recorded in later Donghak sources. The details of titles are reconstructed from texts of uncertain date, so the general point, that a command structure emerged, is firmer than any specific list.

Two slogans from this period became central to the movement's memory: boguk anmin, "protect the country and secure the people," and jepok gumin, "remove tyranny and rescue the people." The first expresses loyalty to the throne and the state; the second condemns abusive officials. Taken together they present the rebels as defenders of the kingdom against its corrupt servants, not as opponents of the dynasty. Whether the rebels all thought in precisely these terms is unknown, and historians caution against assuming a uniform ideology.

Discipline was a theme of the movement's own documents. Rules attributed to the army, reported in sources of varied reliability, forbade killing, looting, and mistreating civilians. The army's reputation for restraint was valuable: it helped to win support from villagers, whose cooperation provided food, intelligence, and recruits. The force marched in columns, relied on local supply, and used signal and messenger systems. It was far from a modern army, however. Many men carried only farm implements or pikes, and firearms were limited, a point that matters for the battles described below.

Evidence. The Baeksan proclamation is preserved in several versions, summarized in the Primary Source Window below.

Primary Source Window I

Primary Source Window: The Baeksan Proclamation, Spring 1894

Creator: issued in the name of the rebel leadership · Date: late April 1894 · Type: political proclamation preserved in Donghak compilations and later histories

Context: The proclamation was issued as the assembled force organized itself in Jeolla Province. The wording survives in later copies, not in an original; versions differ in minor respects, and scholars treat the exact text with caution.

Paraphrase (not a quotation):

The proclamation argues that the people are the foundation of the state and that a ruler's task is to protect them. It charges that officials, forgetting their duty, have enriched themselves and treated the people as prey. It declares that the authors have risen not to harm the state but to remove the tyranny of corrupt officials, protect the realm, and rescue the people, and it appeals to others, including officers, scholars, and the common people, to join or to stand aside.

Analysis: The text frames the rising as an act of loyalty and invokes the standard Confucian principle that government exists for the people's welfare. This language gave the movement legitimacy and made it harder for the court to dismiss the participants as mere bandits.

Limitations: It cannot tell historians what ordinary participants thought, since proclamations speak for leaders and were composed by literate men. Later copying may have altered the wording.

Perspective: The perspective is that of educated Donghak and local leaders. The views of women, landless laborers, and rural people who opposed or avoided the movement are largely missing.

Section 51.9

The Battle of Hwangtohyeon

The government's first response was to send provincial troops from Jeonju. In early May 1894 (April 7 by the lunar calendar, which corresponds to about May 11), the peasant army met them at Hwangtohyeon, a hill pass near Jeongeup. The rebels, who knew the terrain, drew the government force forward and attacked from the surrounding slopes, in some accounts at dawn, scattering it. The government force had firearms but little combat experience; local recruits and untrained levies made up a large share of Joseon's provincial military, which was poorly paid and badly supplied.

The victory was not just tactical. News of it spread rapidly through the countryside, encouraging recruitment and showing that provincial authority could be defeated. It also forced the court to take the revolt seriously as a military threat, and that decision, as later sections explain, ultimately opened the way to foreign intervention.

Historians caution against overstating the "strategy" at Hwangtohyeon. The rebel leaders' ability to exploit terrain probably owed much to local knowledge and to the confidence of a mobilized population, not to sophisticated planning. The same limits would matter at Ugeumchi, where terrain and technology favored the other side.

The setting explains much. Hwangtohyeon lies on the road between Gobu and Jeongeup, and the peasant army arrived there after moving through villages whose residents knew the paths, wells, and ridgelines. Local people supplied food and information about the government column, while the officers marching from Jeonju depended on a provincial establishment whose rolls were padded and whose pay was irregular. Contemporary court reports explain the defeat partly by the troops' fear and desertion, an account that also shielded officers from blame, so the official explanation should not be accepted at face value.

The battle also shows how the revolt's army compensated for weak weapons. Its strength was density, mobility, and local legitimacy; it could bring several thousand people to a point faster than a magistrate could bring a few hundred soldiers. Accounts say the rebels captured weapons and supplies from the dispersed force, though quantities are unrecorded. For the court, the practical lesson was that provincial troops could not be relied on, and this pushed it toward sending a better-equipped force from the capital and, soon afterward, toward considering outside help.

Evidence. Battle accounts come from government dispatches, which downplayed losses, and Donghak sources, which magnified victories; casualty figures are unreliable.

Section 51.10

The Battle of Hwangnyongchon and the Advance toward Jeonju

The court dispatched a force of roughly 800 capital troops under Hong Gye-hun. The troops traveled via Incheon and Gunsan and reached Jeonju, bringing modern rifles and artillery. Hong subsequently sent a detachment under officers including Yi Hak-seung toward Jangseong, where it confronted the peasant army. At Hwangnyongchon, near Jangseong (around May 27 Gregorian, April 23 lunar), the peasant army faced these better-armed troops. According to traditional accounts, the rebels used mobile shields woven from bamboo or straw, loaded on carts, to approach the government line. Whether this device was as effective as legend suggests is uncertain, but the engagement ended in a rebel victory and in the death of a government officer and heavy losses among his troops.

The outcome revealed a pattern. The government's regular troops had better weapons but were few, far from supply, and poorly motivated. The rebel army had numbers, local support, and the ability to move quickly. After Hwangnyongchon, the peasant army moved northward through county towns, recruiting and gathering supplies along the way.

The government commander's position was awkward. His force was small, he had not expected so formidable an opposition, and he could not count on reinforcements. In Seoul, officials began to consider seeking outside help, a decision that would reshape the entire crisis.

The troops from Seoul were a different kind of force. They were drawn from the capital's modernized units, were armed with breech-loading rifles and field guns, and were transported by sea to Gunsan before marching inland to Jeonju. That journey itself reveals a constraint: the government could not move quickly overland through a region it did not control, and it depended on ships, which tied its logistics to the coast. Once these troops reached Jeolla, the rebels shifted from defeating a local garrison to confronting a unit that represented the central state's best available strength.

After Hwangnyongchon the peasant army moved through county towns, gathering recruits and supplies, before approaching Jeonju. A later testimony describes insurgents mingling with marketgoers on the day of entry, but other accounts describe attacks on several city gates beginning in the morning; another record reports that the market operated normally only after the city had fallen. The market-day infiltration story should therefore be presented as a particular retrospective account, not as an established operational explanation. The governor withdrew, while Hong Gye-hun’s troops were outside the city, leaving its defenses vulnerable.

Evidence. The court's chronicle, military reports, and Donghak narratives all describe the battle, with differences over details, and none provides a reliable casualty count.

Section 51.11

The Capture of Jeonju and the Crisis of Provincial Authority

On May 31, 1894 (April 27 by the lunar calendar), the peasant army entered Jeonju, the capital of Jeolla Province and the site of the Joseon royal dynasty's ancestral shrine, which gave the city symbolic weight. The provincial governor had fled. Accounts describe attacks on city gates as well as the withdrawal of officials and defenders; the exact sequence and degree of resistance vary by source. The peasant army secured the city that day.

The capture was a political shock. For the first time a rebel force held a provincial capital, and the dynasty's ancestral city was in the hands of men labeled criminals by the court. Gojong's government faced pressure from court factions and from foreign diplomats in Seoul, who feared disorder. Meanwhile, the occupation raised practical questions for the peasant army. It had to feed itself, maintain discipline among thousands of men in a town of residents who had not chosen its presence, and decide whether to go on to Seoul.

Residents' experiences varied. Some officials and wealthy people had fled; some townspeople, according to later accounts, observed that the army kept discipline and paid for goods, while others feared reprisals. Evidence on these views is thin, however, and since most sources come from officials or from partisans of the movement, they may overstate either rebel restraint or disorder.

The capture of Jeonju marked the peak of the first campaign. It also set the stage for the settlement discussed in Division III and for the intervention discussed in Division IV.

The occupation also exposed the question of purpose. A force of thousands inside a provincial capital must feed itself and decide what to do next. Sources suggest that leaders weighed an advance on Seoul against the risk of leaving their home districts and the likelihood that the capital's troops, and eventually foreign forces, would oppose them. The record does not show a settled plan to overthrow the dynasty; the movement's own slogans pointed toward reforming government in the king's name. Historians therefore treat the idea that the army was marching on the capital as an inference from its momentum and not as a documented intention.

Division IIIThe Jeonju Settlement and Experiments in Peasant Self-Government
Section 51.12

The Siege of Jeonju and the Government's Search for a Settlement

Within days of the occupation, government troops commanded by Hong Gye-hun took positions on the heights outside Jeonju and shelled the city. Rebel counterattacks failed to dislodge them. The balance was now uncertain: the peasant army could not easily defeat artillery in open assault, but the government force was too small to retake the city by storm and feared heavy losses and the destruction of the provincial capital's buildings, including shrine property of the royal house.

Seoul was divided. Some court figures, influential in the government of Queen Min's circle, argued for decisive suppression and for outside help. Others favored negotiation. The court also feared that a prolonged siege might encourage risings elsewhere. Meanwhile conditions inside Jeonju were hard: thousands of fighters needed food, many had been away from their fields during the planting season, and some had already begun to leave.

On both sides there were reasons for a settlement. The rebels sought redress and not necessarily the overthrow of the state, and the commander Hong needed a way out of a stalemate. Both appear to have sent messages. Jeon Bong-jun is reported to have presented a list of grievances to the government commander, the contents of which are preserved in versions of uncertain reliability. The appearance of foreign troops, which occurred while these exchanges were under way (Division IV), added urgency.

Military pressure on Jeonju came from artillery fired from the heights, which damaged parts of the city and alarmed the court because Jeonju contained property associated with the royal house. A counterattack by the peasant army against the government position did not succeed, and the experience seems to have clarified the limits of its weapons. At the same time the besiegers were not strong enough to retake the city by assault. This stalemate is the mechanism that made negotiation attractive to both sides, and it is essential to understanding why the truce did not require a decisive victory for either.

Conditions inside the city also mattered. Planting season was passing, and thousands of fighters were away from their fields; households at home faced labor shortages and the risk of a failed harvest. Government officials worried that a long siege would encourage risings elsewhere, and the court's reports from other provinces show mounting anxiety. Contemporary sources rarely describe the townspeople's own views of the siege, so their experience of fear, hunger, and displacement has to be inferred from the general pattern of such events.

Evidence. The siege is documented in military dispatches, Donghak compilations, and diaries; these do not agree on details of casualties or on the content of the exchanges.

Section 51.13

The Jeonju Truce and the Withdrawal of the Peasant Army

On about June 11, 1894 (May 8 by the lunar calendar), the peasant army withdrew from Jeonju and the government force took possession. The withdrawal followed negotiation between Hong Gye-hun and Jeon Bong-jun. The government is recorded as promising to hear the people's grievances and to address official abuses, and the rebels agreed to disperse to their home districts. Historians generally accept that a settlement of this kind occurred.

What they do not have is a formally preserved text. Later accounts describe a written agreement, sometimes with numbered articles, and some versions attribute a set of terms to it. Because no authenticated original has been located, the terms cannot be treated as a signed treaty. What is secure is the sequence: negotiation, withdrawal, and, in the following weeks, practical arrangements in several counties that allowed local peasant leadership to function (§51.14).

The foreign dimension shaped the settlement. By early June, Qing forces were arriving at Asan on the western coast, and the Japanese government had decided to send troops; they began landing at Incheon around June 12. Some historians argue that fear of foreign intervention encouraged the rebels to accept a truce and that the government's wish to remove any pretext for foreign troops encouraged it to make concessions. Others point out that both sides had other reasons. In any case, the truce came too late to prevent the arrival of foreign soldiers, because the court's appeal for Qing help had already been made.

Several features of the truce deserve emphasis. The negotiators were military men and local leaders, not diplomats, and the government side had an incentive to present the outcome as a dispersal of rebels, while the peasant side had reason to present it as a recognition of their grievances. Both versions appear in later sources. The practical result was that the peasant army left the city without being destroyed, and that many officials in Jeolla, uncertain whether the court would honor any promise, accepted local arrangements that they could not prevent.

The timing created a paradox. The truce removed the stated reason for Qing troops to be in Korea, because the rebels had withdrawn, yet Qing soldiers were already arriving at Asan and Japanese soldiers were landing at Incheon. Some Korean officials argued that foreign troops should leave at once. The fact that they did not, and that Japan chose to remain, shows that the presence of foreign armies had by then acquired its own momentum, one that could not be reversed by the peasant army's withdrawal.

Evidence. Official dispatches, Donghak accounts, and later memoirs mention the truce, but their details differ. Treating the later versions as primary evidence of June 1894 would be a methodological error.

Section 51.14

The Jipgangso: Local Government under Peasant Leadership

After the truce, peasant leaders returned to their districts across Jeolla Province, and in many counties they created offices called jipgangso. These offices, which appear to have operated mainly in the southwest, took on tasks usually reserved for magistrates and clerks: hearing disputes, restraining abusive officials, keeping order, and in some places supervising tax collection and the distribution of grain. Jeon Bong-jun maintained a headquarters at Jeonju, and other leaders such as Kim Gae-nam and Son Hwa-jung exercised influence in their own areas.

The jipgangso must be understood as a practical experiment in local government. Some operated alongside the officially appointed magistrate, with whom they negotiated; in other places magistrates had fled or been intimidated, and the jipgangso effectively governed. They also varied in character. Some, led by disciplined local figures, were orderly; others were criticized by contemporaries for allowing bands to settle old scores or to seize property from landlords. The sources do not allow a precise count of jipgangso or a uniform description of their work.

In July the new provincial governor, Kim Hak-jin, met Jeon Bong-jun and agreed to a cooperative arrangement, in which jipgangso would be tolerated as an administrative partner to the government. This is sometimes described as a form of dual authority, a remarkable development: for several weeks a state that had denounced the rebels shared local authority with them. Whether the arrangement would have lasted is unknown, because the events of July 1894 overtook it.

Several mechanisms made jipgangso workable. First, they relied on existing Donghak networks and village leaders, who could communicate quickly and could claim moral authority among believers. Second, they performed visible services, such as stopping unauthorized collections, resolving disputes, and punishing abuses, that cost little but won support. Third, they benefited from the government's weakness: officials in many places lacked the means to oppose them and accepted accommodation. Historians often describe jipgangso as spread across much of the southwest, though the degree of control differed from county to county and the evidence for specific counties is uneven.

The cooperative arrangement associated with Governor Kim Hak-jin is sometimes summarized by the phrase gwanmin sangwha, roughly harmony between officials and people. The phrase captures what both sides hoped to achieve: Kim sought to restore order without further fighting, and Jeon Bong-jun sought to retain the gains of the spring. It was an unstable compromise, however. It rested on personal understanding between two men, it had no basis in law, and it depended on the court tolerating a dual administration while foreign troops threatened the capital.

Evidence. The principal sources are Donghak histories, official dispatches, and diaries of local observers who sympathized with neither side.

Section 51.15

Reform Demands: Taxes, Status, Debt, Land, and Social Justice

What did the movement want? Historians reconstruct its aims from proclamations, petitions, testimony, and later compilations. Across these sources, several themes recur: an end to unauthorized taxes and abuses in tax collection; punishment of corrupt officials; relief from the grain-loan system and from debts; and a reduction of the privileges of the yangban and wealthy. Most of these concerned the operation of government and are consistent with what earlier rural protests had demanded.

Some demands touched on the structure of society. The best-known document is a twelve-point program preserved in Oh Ji-yeong's Donghaksa, written during the 1920s and published in 1940. In paraphrase, the program calls for ending enmity between Donghak and the government; punishing corrupt officials; punishing violent and unscrupulous wealthy people; burning slave registers and ending hereditary servitude; improving the treatment of the lowest status groups; allowing widows to remarry; abolishing miscellaneous taxes; appointing officials by ability; punishing those who collaborated with the Japanese; cancelling past debts; and distributing land equally.

The reliability of this list is debated (Historical Debate I). The program is not found in surviving documents from 1894, and the wording and ordering vary in different publications. Some items fit well with other evidence, such as tax and debt demands; others, such as the anti-Japanese item and land redistribution, are more contested. Students should therefore treat the program as evidence of how later Donghak adherents remembered the movement and as a possible, but unproven, record of 1894 demands.

A careful summary distinguishes three categories: demands that appear in contemporary sources, proposals that appear only in later compilations, and reforms actually implemented, which were limited and local. The Gabo reform government, treated in Chapter 52, would later abolish status distinctions and slave registers by decree, but it did so under different conditions and by its own authority.

Tax demands show the movement's most documented concerns. Cultivators objected not simply to taxation but to its arbitrary growth: surcharges, inflated measures, collection fees, and forced loans. The demand to end miscellaneous levies, to punish officials who had enriched themselves, and to reduce grain-loan abuses appears across sources of different types, which gives it more weight than demands found in one later compilation. By contrast, demands to alter landholding or abolish status distinctions rest on thinner and later evidence, and they should be presented with that distinction made clear.

The question of status deserves particular care. The hereditary status system was not a minor feature of Joseon society, and an attack on it would have implied a deeper transformation than the correction of tax abuses. Some adherents of Donghak had low social standing, and the religion's teachings on human dignity may have encouraged such demands. Yet leaders also included educated men of local standing who had reasons to preserve some hierarchy. The surviving evidence supports the conclusion that status reform was discussed or hoped for within parts of the movement, without proving that it formed a unified program.

Section 51.16

Rural Administration, Community Conflict, and the Limits of Reform

The actual record of reform in the summer of 1894 is uneven. In some counties, peasant leaders reportedly cancelled certain levies, punished particularly hated officials, and pressed landlords to cancel debts. In others, nothing changed or the peasants' presence became a source of conflict. Historians stress variety: the jipgangso were local, depended on the personalities of their leaders, and varied in strength.

The social groups involved had different stakes. Cultivators wanted lower taxes and release from debt. Some local elites cooperated, either from conviction or from prudence, while others organized armed resistance, forming local militias to defend property. Officials were divided between those who adapted to the new situation and those who awaited government reinforcement. Merchants worried about disruption. Women in rural households carried the burdens of provisioning and, when men were away or killed, of sustaining farms; the sources record this indirectly. Enslaved and low-status people had particular reason to support demands for status reform, although few accounts describe their own views.

The limits of the movement are evident. It had no central bureaucracy, no stable revenue system, and no control over the court. Many leaders envisioned correction of abuses within the existing order and did not seek to replace the dynasty, a point made by the slogan boguk anmin. As a result, what the movement achieved was largely a de facto reduction of official abuse in some areas, not a lasting institutional change. The surviving sources are mostly hostile or retrospective, which makes it difficult to assess how deep the changes ran.

By mid-July, the entire arrangement was threatened from outside, because the politics of the capital and of the region were changing rapidly under the pressure of foreign troops.

The sources allow a few comparisons. In counties where leaders were respected local figures, jipgangso seem to have operated with some restraint and cooperation with magistrates. In counties where leaders were less disciplined or where old disputes were strong, accounts describe seizures of property, forced contributions, and attacks on landlords. These differences matter because opponents of the movement used the worst examples to portray it as lawless, while sympathetic writers emphasized the best. A balanced account has to hold both facts in view without treating either as representative of all districts.

The social composition of the movement also shaped its limits. Cultivators who owned small plots had interests different from tenants and laborers, and both differed from yangban sympathizers. Merchants and market-town residents sought stability. These differences help explain why the movement could unite around tax abuses but would have struggled to agree on land redistribution. They also help explain why many local elites, including some who disliked the magistrates, organized to resist the peasant army when it moved toward more sweeping change.

Interpretation A — The program reflects 1894 aims, with allowance for later wording: Some scholars argue that Oh drew on survivors' memories and Donghak records, and that the themes match contemporary evidence on taxation, corruption, and status.
Interpretation B — The program is substantially a later reconstruction: Others stress that no 1894 original has been found, that Oh wrote after the Gabo reforms and during colonial rule, and that some items, especially land redistribution and anti-Japanese measures, may reflect later concerns.

Division IVChinese and Japanese Intervention and the Outbreak of War
Section 51.17

Gojong's Government, Qing Assistance, and Japanese Intervention

As the peasant army advanced, Gojong's court debated how to respond. Chapter 49 and Chapter 50 described how Qing influence in Seoul had grown after the Imo Mutiny of 1882 and the Gapsin Coup of 1884, and how Yuan Shikai, the Qing resident, acted as an adviser who pressed Korean officials in Beijing's favor. In early June 1894, after the fall of Jeonju, Joseon officials, with Yuan's encouragement, asked the Qing government to send troops. The decision was taken at court and reflected the government's fear that its own forces could not defeat the rebels; historians debate how far Yuan Shikai prompted the request, but the formal appeal came from the Korean side.

The Qing response was prompt. Li Hongzhang, the viceroy responsible for Korean affairs, sent about two thousand men, under Ye Zhichao and Nie Shicheng, to land at Asan Bay in the second week of June, and informed Japan, as required, of the deployment. In his notification, however, he described the troops as sent to protect a tributary state, a phrase that Japan rejected because it implied Qing suzerainty.

The legal context was the 1885 Convention of Tianjin, concluded after the Gapsin Coup, under which China and Japan each agreed to withdraw troops from Korea and to notify the other before sending forces to restore order. Japan's government, led by Prime Minister Ito Hirobumi with Mutsu Munemitsu as foreign minister, used the notification as grounds to send its own forces. The Japanese cabinet had approved a dispatch on June 2, before the Korean court formally requested Qing assistance on June 3, in anticipation of possible Qing intervention. A smaller Japanese party reached Seoul on June 10; a much larger brigade began landing at Incheon on June 12.

The point to stress is that Japan's decision was its own. Japan invoked the Convention's reciprocal notification framework to justify its troop dispatch, but that bilateral agreement did not itself grant Korea's consent, nor require Japan to send a large force, retain troops after the rebels had dispersed, or demand political change. Japanese leaders, facing domestic political pressure and seeing a chance to end Qing predominance, chose to escalate.

The decision to request Qing troops was made under particular pressures. Provincial forces had been beaten, Seoul's force was small, and the fall of Jeonju made the situation look dire. Some Korean officials, including influential members of the queen's circle, are recorded as favoring the request, while other voices warned that bringing foreign troops into the country would invite trouble. The court was thus not passively dragged along; it made a choice, under fear of losing control, that it had reason to regret afterward. Yet the choice did not require Japan to act as it did.

Japanese decision-making unfolded in parallel. The cabinet under Ito Hirobumi decided in early June that if Qing troops were sent, Japan would send its own, and it did so with a force far larger than the Qing contingent, accompanied by marines who moved into Seoul. Army leaders and diplomats on the spot pressed for a firm line, and historians debate how far the government had decided on war in advance and how far it pursued escalation step by step as opportunities arose. What the record establishes is that Japanese officials repeatedly chose options that made withdrawal less likely.

Evidence. Korean court records, Qing telegrams, and Japanese diplomatic documents, including Mutsu's later memoir, provide different views of these decisions. The memoir was written after the war and must be read as a self-justifying account.

Section 51.18

The Failure of Diplomacy and the Struggle over Korean Reform

Once the truce at Jeonju removed the rebellion's immediate threat, the Korean government asked both China and Japan to withdraw their forces. China was prepared to withdraw if Japan did. Japan refused, arguing that the rebellion's causes remained and that Korea needed reform. In mid-June Tokyo proposed that China and Japan jointly oversee reform of the Korean government; Beijing replied that Korea should reform itself and that Japan had no right to interfere. The proposals showed incompatible aims: China wished to maintain its customary role without a new Japanese claim, while Japan sought to establish an equal or greater role.

Gojong's government had limited freedom. Japanese troops occupied key points around Seoul. Ōtori Keisuke, the Japanese minister, pressed the king to adopt reforms and, in late June, presented demands. Korean officials, including conservative ministers and those close to the queen's circle, tried to avoid taking sides and to gain time by promising reform under their own control. Some Koreans, including reform-minded officials, saw Japan's pressure as an opportunity; others regarded it as a threat. The court, in short, was not a united actor.

Third parties did not rescue the situation. Britain, which had large commercial interests in China, attempted mediation, and Russia expressed concern, but neither was prepared to compel Japan. Japan also secured a revised commercial treaty with Britain in mid-July, which signaled that London would not obstruct it. Meanwhile Japan prepared militarily, assembling transport and shipping.

The proposals of mid-June reveal what each side sought. Japan's offer of joint Sino-Japanese supervision of Korean reform would, if accepted, have given it a formal role equal to China's. China's refusal followed from its view that Korea's internal affairs belonged within the traditional framework, in which Beijing had a special role. Neither proposal consulted Korean opinion in any meaningful way. Mediation by Britain and urging from Russia for mutual withdrawal had no effect, partly because Japan secured a new commercial treaty with Britain in mid-July, which reduced the likelihood of British opposition.

On July 19 Japanese minister Ōtori Keisuke pressed the Korean government to authorize a military telegraph connection between Seoul and Busan and provide barracks for Japanese troops. On July 20 he added demands for Qing withdrawal and the cancellation of Korea’s 1882 treaty and subsequent trade arrangements with China, setting July 22 as the deadline. The Korean response insisted on Korean-led reform and the withdrawal of both foreign armies, rather than accepting those demands. The sequence shows a pattern in which Japanese demands were framed as assistance to Korean independence while being backed by troops stationed around the capital, a pattern that Korean officials recognized even as they struggled to respond.

Evidence. The diplomatic record is large, but each government's papers were prepared to justify its policy. Korean voices are less well represented than Qing and Japanese ones.

Section 51.19

The Japanese Seizure of Gyeongbokgung, July 1894

In the early hours of July 23, 1894, Japanese troops attacked the royal palace of Gyeongbokgung in Seoul. After fighting with palace guards that lasted several hours, Japanese troops took control, disarmed Korean troops in the capital, and took Gojong under their authority. The operation followed Korea's refusal to meet Japanese demands, and Japanese officials later described it as a response to Korean resistance and, in some accounts, to firing by Korean soldiers; the details of who fired first remain disputed.

Japan then reshaped the Korean government. It excluded members of the Min family faction, restored the king's father, the Heungseon Daewongun, as a figurehead leader, and arranged for the government to authorize the expulsion of Qing troops, giving Japan a legal pretext for a war on Korean soil. A deliberative body, the Office for Deliberation on State Affairs (Gunguk gimucheo), was soon set up to enact reforms. This is the beginning of the Gabo reform government, treated in Chapter 52, and its measures are not narrated here.

The significance for Korea was immense. The government that proclaimed its independence from China was now operating under Japanese military pressure. The king, who had been the nominal source of authority, was in effect a prisoner of one of the foreign powers that had claimed to defend his country. Korean officials, army officers, and ordinary people responded in different ways: some accepted the new order, some withdrew from office, some resisted. The event became a major reason for the second peasant uprising described in Division V.

The operation began before dawn and involved Japanese units from the garrison near the capital moving on the palace gates. Korean guards resisted briefly, and there was gunfire; accounts differ on who started it. By morning, Korean units in the capital had been disarmed or dispersed, and Japanese soldiers controlled the palace grounds. Gojong's circumstances after the attack are often described as detention, though the king retained the formal functions of monarch. A new set of officials was installed under the leadership of the Daewongun, who had long opposed the queen's faction and agreed, whether willingly or under pressure, to lend his name.

Source problems are significant. The principal Korean court chronicle for the reign, the Gojong sillok, was compiled between 1927 and 1932 by the colonial Governor-General’s office, and its treatment of the palace seizure and the Gabo period needs to be read with that in mind. Japanese military and diplomatic reports provide detail but present the event as defensive. Foreign diplomats' accounts add an outside view. Comparing them is essential, and the outline of what happened is secure even though motive and responsibility for the first shots remain disputed.

Evidence. Japanese military records, Korean court sources, and foreign diplomats' reports describe the incident. Japanese records emphasize provocation and Korean accounts emphasize violation of sovereignty.

Section 51.20

The Battles of Pungdo and Seonghwan: War Begins

On July 25, 1894, off Pungdo (Feng Island) near Asan Bay, ships of the Japanese Combined Fleet encountered Qing warships escorting transports. Japanese cruisers attacked, damaging or destroying Qing escort vessels. The Japanese also sank the Kowshing, a British-registered steamer chartered to carry about 1,100 Qing troops. Most of the soldiers and others aboard died, although estimates differ. Some European crew members, including the British captain, were rescued by the Japanese cruiser Naniwa, while other ships rescued surviving Chinese soldiers. The incident caused a diplomatic uproar in Britain, though London ultimately accepted that Japan had acted within the laws of war.

On July 29, on land, Japanese forces under Major General Ōshima Yoshimasa attacked Qing positions at Seonghwan, east of Asan. The Qing troops were defeated and withdrew northward, joining other Qing forces moving to Pyongyang. The Japanese had by now gained the upper hand in the Seoul-Asan region.

Formal declarations of war were issued by both governments on August 1, 1894. The distinction between hostilities and declarations is important because fighting had begun a week earlier, and because the legal rationale for the Japanese actions was disputed. Japan presented the war as a campaign for Korean independence and civilization, while China presented it as a response to Japanese aggression. The Korean government under Japanese control concluded agreements that gave Japan military privileges, among them a provisional alliance in August that required Korea to assist Japanese operations.

The Pungdo engagement was fought by cruisers of the Japanese fleet that included ships under future admirals who would become prominent in later wars. The Qing escorts were outgunned or poorly handled, and one was destroyed. The sinking of the Kowshing was a distinct incident with larger international consequences: the British ship carried Qing troops who, according to testimony collected after the incident, prevented its captain from complying with Japanese orders to follow the Naniwa. Accounts differ about the negotiations, the firing, and rescue operations. Many Chinese soldiers died; a minority were rescued by vessels arriving later, whereas the Naniwa took aboard some of the European survivors. The legality of the sinking was debated in Britain, but the government ultimately declined to treat it as a cause for confrontation.

At Seonghwan, Japanese infantry attacked Qing positions near a hill and swamp area and drove the defenders out. The Qing commander withdrew his remaining forces toward Pyongyang by a roundabout route. The effect was to clear the Seoul-Asan region of organized Qing resistance within a week of the first shot. It also meant that the war's early fighting was conducted on Korean soil among Korean villages, whose residents were not consulted and whose crops and property were affected.

Evidence. The military record comes from Japanese and Qing reports, foreign observers, and press accounts. Details of the Pungdo engagement and of casualties at Seonghwan differ.

Section 51.21

Pyongyang, the Yellow Sea, and the Destruction of Qing Power in Korea

After Seonghwan, Qing commanders concentrated forces at Pyongyang, the major city of the northwest. In mid-September 1894 Japanese columns converged on the city. On September 15 the Japanese assaulted from several directions, and by the following day the Qing army had abandoned Pyongyang, retreating north with heavy losses. Estimates of casualties vary widely and are uncertain. The Qing army never again held ground in Korea.

On September 17, off the mouth of the Yalu, the Japanese Combined Fleet engaged the Qing Beiyang Fleet in the Battle of the Yellow Sea. The Qing lost several ships, while the Japanese lost none, and Japan gained effective control of the sea. The battle shaped the rest of the war: it secured Japanese transport and supply, and it left the Qing fleet on the defensive.

Why did Qing power collapse so quickly in Korea? Historians point to several factors: the Qing forces in Korea were few and divided, command was fragmented among regional armies loyal to different leaders, supply lines were long, and Li Hongzhang tried to preserve his own Beiyang forces. Japan had a unified command, a modern conscript army, and a prepared logistical system. Historians also caution against portraying the outcome as inevitable. Qing officers fought bravely in places, and the war was shaped by decisions made in Tokyo and Beijing.

For Korean civilians, the fighting was devastating. Pyongyang and its surroundings suffered looting, requisitioning, and destruction; observers who visited the region afterward described extensive devastation and displacement. The Korean peasants did not decide any of these events, but they bore many of the costs. These experiences are explored further in §51.32.

At Pyongyang, published force estimates vary. The Japanese archival exhibition gives approximately 13,000 Qing defenders according to Japanese military estimates; Japan assembled several converging formations, but precise strength figures vary across accounts. The Qing force was divided among commanders, and its defenses were improvised. Japanese columns attacked from several directions, and the defenders' position collapsed after a day of fighting; Qing troops retreated north under cover of darkness and rain, suffering heavy losses on the road. Japan's success reflected coordination and logistics at least as much as courage, and historians caution against explanations that rely on national character.

The Battle of the Yellow Sea involved fleets of roughly similar numbers. The Beiyang fleet, which had modern armored ships, lost several vessels, while the Japanese ships, which were faster and had more quick-firing guns, suffered damage but lost none. The result gave Japan the ability to land armies on the coast of China and secured its sea lanes to Korea. Debates about whether Qing naval funds had been diverted to other projects continue, and the evidence is contested; the safer conclusion is that organization, doctrine, and supply all mattered.

Competition between Qing and Japanese influence since 1876; Joseon's fiscal and political weakness; rural grievances; the Convention of Tianjin.

The spread of the peasant army; the court's fear of defeat; the Qing deployment; Japan's decision to send a larger force.

Division VThe Second Donghak Uprising, Military Defeat, and Repression
Section 51.22

From Rural Reform to Renewed Resistance against Japan

By the late summer of 1894, the peasant movement in Jeolla was coexisting uneasily with a provincial government that had accepted jipgangso. The Japanese palace seizure, the war, and the flow of Japanese troops through the country changed its meaning. Reports reached the countryside that Japanese soldiers had occupied the palace, that the king was under foreign control, and that Japanese military telegraph lines and supply stations were being built through Korea. For a movement whose slogans stressed protecting the country, these events offered a new target.

The motives for renewed mobilization were mixed. The local grievances that had produced the first uprising had not been resolved. Government forces, now acting under a Japanese-influenced government, were beginning to press on the jipgangso and to arrest Donghak adherents. Japanese requisitioning and the effects of war on grain prices added to hardship. And a strand of anti-foreign feeling that had been present since the 1893 petitions was now directed at a concrete occupying army. Historians differ on how to weigh these factors, and the question of whether the second uprising was primarily anti-Japanese or a continuation of the earlier movement is discussed in Historical Debate II.

Not everyone agreed. Some Donghak leaders, particularly in the north, doubted that an armed rising could succeed. Some local elites and officials in the south organized militias against the rebels. The peasant movement's decision to resume fighting was therefore contested, not automatic.

Evidence. Interrogation records, Donghak accounts, and Japanese and Korean military records all bear on motives. Interrogation records in particular were created under duress.

Section 51.23

Jeon Bong-jun and the Southern Peasant Leadership

In the autumn of 1894 Jeon Bong-jun began to rebuild his forces at Samnye, a town that had been a Donghak assembly site in 1892. He sent messages through Donghak networks, and in October his army began to assemble. Son Hwa-jung and Kim Gae-nam, among other leaders, brought their followers. Accounts give widely varying figures for the size of the force, ranging from several thousand to far more, and none is reliable.

Their objectives were evolving. Jeon later stated in custody that the second uprising was provoked by the Japanese entry into the palace and the threat to the country, and that he aimed to drive out the Japanese and remove corrupt officials; the testimony needs source criticism, but it is consistent with the movement's slogans. The southern leaders also hoped to link with northern Donghak groups in order to march on Seoul, where, according to several accounts, they hoped to call on the Daewongun or to influence the court.

The southern force had advantages and limits. It had local support and experienced commanders, and many fighters had been through the earlier campaign. But the army was unevenly armed, supply was a constant problem, and its leaders disagreed over strategy. Moving north into Chungcheong meant leaving the home districts that provided its base of support.

Evidence. The most direct source is Jeon's interrogation, discussed in the Primary Source Window below.

Section 51.24

Choe Si-hyeong, Son Byeong-hui, and the Northern Donghak Movement

The Donghak organization in Chungcheong and the north was led by Choe Si-hyeong, the religion's second leader, with Son Byeong-hui as a major commander. Choe had opposed the earlier uprising and had urged believers to avoid armed action. After the Japanese seizure of the palace, and under pressure from believers and from the southern leaders, he changed course. In mid-October 1894 he issued a general order to mobilize, and Son Byeong-hui led forces from Chungcheong, assembling around Boeun and Cheongju.

The northern and southern wings differed in organization and temperament. The northern leadership was more cautious and more bound to the religious institution; the southern leadership was shaped by local rural leaders and by the experience of the first uprising. The cooperation between them was a tactical alliance rather than a merger, and their relations were sometimes tense. Regional networks also mattered: northern forces drew on communities that had not experienced the jipgangso, and some of these communities had suffered earlier persecution.

Choe Si-hyeong himself escaped after the defeat and lived in hiding until his arrest and execution in 1898. Son Byeong-hui survived, fled, and later became a leader of the religion, which was renamed Cheondogyo in 1905.

Evidence. Donghak histories dominate the record for the northern movement, and they tend to emphasize leaders' roles and justify choices.

Section 51.25

The Coalition Campaign toward Gongju

Southern and northern Donghak forces advanced through Chungcheong in the autumn and attempted coordinated operations toward Gongju, the provincial capital and a gateway on the road to Seoul. Nonsan was an important southern assembly area, but historians debate the exact timing and location of the two wings’ first joint operations; research has emphasized fighting around Hyopo rather than treating a single meeting at Nonsan as certain. The coalition faced three problems.

First was supply. An army of several thousand or more needed food daily, and as the force moved north into districts where it lacked the support network of the south, it relied on requisition, voluntary contributions, and plunder, and cases of violence against civilians are recorded. Second was communication: orders traveled by messenger, and coordination among columns was imperfect. Third was strategy. Leaders disagreed about whether to attack Gongju directly or to bypass it, and the court's forces had had time to prepare.

Gongju mattered because of geography. The city sat on the Geum River, and the roads from the south converged on it. A government and Japanese force there could block the way north. The Japanese, who had begun to regard the countryside as a threat to their lines of communication between Seoul and the south, had sent forces under Minami Koshiro to support the Korean government troops, and the pro-Japanese Korean government deployed its own newly trained units.

The campaign also depended on diplomacy among Donghak leaders. Messages passed between Jeon Bong-jun and the northern leadership, and local leaders in Chungcheong had to decide whether to join. Some joined from conviction, some because their communities were being pressured, and some stayed away. The result was a coalition of uneven discipline that grew as it moved. Contemporary Japanese reports, written by officers who watched the countryside through the lens of their communication lines, sometimes exaggerate the coordination of these forces, while Donghak memoirs may exaggerate their unity.

The government and Japanese side had advantages beyond weapons. Telegraph lines and military roads allowed them to share information quickly, and the pro-Japanese government in Seoul could draw on forces trained with Japanese help. Japanese officers also regarded the interior of Korea as a threat to the lines running from the port of Busan to Seoul and north toward the front, which explains why a relatively small Japanese contingent was assigned to the suppression. Their presence turned a Korean domestic confrontation into one in which a foreign army again took part.

Evidence. Japanese military reports are detailed about troop movements; Donghak sources are more informative about rebel motives; each has gaps.

Section 51.26

The Battles of Gongju and Ugeumchi

The fighting around Gongju occurred in more than one phase, from the late tenth lunar month into the eleventh. The decisive clash at Ugeumchi, a hill pass south of the city, took place on the ninth day of the eleventh lunar month, corresponding to December 5, 1894, as recorded in the National Institute of Korean History’s event-by-event chronology. Other secondary chronologies vary by a day in nearby conversions. Thus the familiar dates of November 8–9 in many Korean accounts should not be read as unconverted Gregorian dates. The defenders, composed of Korean government troops, local militias, and a small Japanese contingent, occupied the high ground. They carried modern breech-loading rifles and some machine weapons and artillery.

The attackers had numbers and determination, but few firearms, many of them old matchlocks, and they attacked uphill. Accounts say that they charged repeatedly, dozens of times over several days, and were driven back with heavy loss. Traditional Korean accounts claim that only a few hundred of some ten thousand survived intact; the numbers are uncertain, but the scale of the defeat is not in doubt.

Why did the offensive fail? Historians cite the decisive disparity in weapons, the defenders' control of the terrain, the lack of coordination between northern and southern forces, shortages of ammunition and food, and the fact that Japanese commanders could use the telegraph and roads to concentrate reserves. The failure also reflects the limits of a movement built on local mobilization: it could produce large crowds but not a sustained, supplied field army. Ugeumchi became a lasting symbol in Korean memory of sacrifice against superior arms.

The first Gongju fighting, in the lunar tenth month, involved clashes at several approaches to the city, during which the peasant forces probed the defenses and were repulsed. These engagements gave the defenders time to prepare and gave the peasant leaders a chance to reconsider. The decisive clash at Ugeumchi, which the National Institute of Korean History places on the ninth day of the eleventh lunar month, corresponding to December 5, 1894, was therefore the culmination of an extended struggle and not a single sudden battle.

At Ugeumchi the defenders held the heights and the attackers climbed repeatedly under fire. Japanese soldiers carried single-shot breech-loading rifles, and Korean government troops trained along modern lines supported them. Accounts speak of dozens of assaults across a few days, ending with the peasant forces withdrawing in disorder. The exact numbers on each side and the losses are uncertain, and the larger figures in later memory serve commemorative purposes. A fair statement is that the attackers outnumbered the defenders but lacked firepower, supply, and unified command.

The aftermath mattered for the whole movement. The coalition broke apart, and the leaders had to decide whether to continue or disperse. Some chose to withdraw south, while others dispersed to their home regions. The defeat did not end resistance, but it ended the possibility of a coordinated march on the capital and left the survivors exposed to pursuit.

Evidence. The battle is described in Japanese and Korean government reports and in Donghak accounts. Casualty figures vary by source, and none is verifiable.

Section 51.27

Retreat, Regional Resistance, and the Suppression Campaign

After Ugeumchi the surviving forces retreated south through Nonsan toward Jeonju and fought further rearguard actions, including at Wonpyeong and Taein in the following weeks, where they were again defeated. Government and Japanese troops pursued them, and the army dissolved into smaller groups. Resistance continued locally in many parts of the southwest and elsewhere, including coastal and island districts in Jeolla and areas in Gyeongsang and Hwanghae, where separate Donghak groups had risen.

The suppression was severe. Japanese military orders cited in modern scholarship have been read as calling for the thorough destruction of the rebels, and Korean government units, local militias, and Japanese detachments carried out sweeps. Villages suspected of sheltering rebels were searched, and houses were burned. Local landlords and officials took part in reprisals, settling older scores. Fleeing rebels sought refuge in the hills or hid their identity, and many returned to farming under changed names.

The effects on communities were deep. Fields went untended, families lost members, and neighbors became informers or victims. For women and children, flight and loss of male relatives meant new insecurity, though these experiences are poorly recorded. The suppression took place at the same time as the broader war, so that the rural population faced both rebel and counterinsurgent violence along with the demands of foreign armies.

The pursuit unfolded over weeks. After the retreat from Gongju, the surviving columns fell back through Nonsan and into Jeolla, fighting rearguard actions at places such as Wonpyeong and Taein, where they were again defeated. As the army dissolved, many fighters hid their weapons and returned to villages, where they were vulnerable to denunciation by neighbors or to searches by militia. Local militias organized by landlords and officials played an important role in these sweeps, which shows that the suppression drew on divisions within rural society and not only on foreign or capital forces.

Elsewhere in the country, separate Donghak groups rose or were suppressed, and the pattern of events varied by region. In coastal and island areas of the southwest, hunting down rebels sometimes involved naval and military cooperation, and arrests and executions continued into early 1895. The scale of violence in these months is hard to measure, and the available records are fragmentary, which is why historians prefer ranges and qualitative description to specific totals.

Evidence. Official and military reports describe operations but minimize atrocities; later memory and local oral accounts emphasize them. Neither source type is complete.

Section 51.28

Arrest, Interrogation, Execution, and the Human Cost of Defeat

Jeon Bong-jun disbanded his remaining troops and tried to hide. He was betrayed by an acquaintance and arrested at Sunchang in early December 1894. He was taken to Seoul, where he was interrogated by Korean and Japanese officials, and executed on March 30, 1895, together with other leaders including Son Hwa-jung. Kim Gae-nam was captured and executed in December 1894.

His interrogation record is among the most important sources on the revolution, but it is not a memoir. The questions were chosen by officials, the setting was coercive, and the answers were recorded and edited by others. He is recorded as explaining the origins of the first uprising in Gobu's local grievances, as distinguishing the few Donghak adherents from the larger group of aggrieved residents, and as saying that the second uprising was provoked by the Japanese entry into the palace. He is also reported to have declined to renounce his aims. Historians read the text for both content and context.

The total human cost is unknown. Estimates of those killed in the suppression range from tens of thousands to figures above one hundred thousand; the lower estimates are based on military reports, the higher on later nationalist and religious histories. No registry exists, and many deaths occurred in scattered reprisals and from flight and hunger. Responsible writing acknowledges the large scale of the loss without claiming false precision.

The consequences for the movement were severe. The jipgangso were dismantled, leaders were killed or went underground, and the Donghak religion survived mainly in secret until its later revival. The memory of the revolution, however, endured and later became a central reference point for modern Korean historical consciousness.

The arrest of Jeon Bong-jun illustrates the vulnerability of leaders. He was reportedly betrayed by an acquaintance, and his capture was celebrated by officials as a decisive step. His transfer to Seoul meant that he came under the authority of the new government and, in practice, under Japanese scrutiny as well. His interrogation, which dealt with the origins of the first uprising, the aims of the second, and his relationship to Donghak leaders, was an official inquiry meant to establish responsibility, and its record is valuable for exactly that reason and limited for the same reason.

The scale of punishment extended beyond leaders. Many ordinary fighters were killed in battle or in reprisals, others were imprisoned, and some fled to mountains or other provinces. The long-term effects on rural society included orphaned households, burned villages, and years of fear. Memory of these events was suppressed under colonial rule and surfaced later in commemoration, and the numbers cited in memory have often exceeded those in contemporaneous records. A responsible account keeps both the documented acts and the uncertainty about totals in view.

Historical Debate II

Historical Debate: What Kind of Movement Was the Donghak Peasant Revolution?

The question: Was the revolution primarily religious, agrarian, social, political, or anti-imperialist, and does the label "revolution," "war," or "movement" best describe it?

Interpretation A — An agrarian and social movement: Emphasizes taxation, debt, local abuse, and demands for status reform. The first uprising, on this view, is the core of the story.
Interpretation B — A religious movement: Emphasizes Donghak teachings and networks, and the persecution of believers, as the motive and the organizational basis.
Interpretation C — An anti-imperial or national movement: Emphasizes the second uprising, anti-Japanese slogans, and the movement's later memory as a precursor of Korean nationalism.

Evidence: Proclamations, testimony, official records, and Donghak histories. Terms such as "revolution" (hyeongmyeong), "war" (jeonjaeng), and "movement" (undong) each stress different features: scale and transformation, military struggle, or social mobilization.

Points of disagreement: Interpretation, terminology, and the weight given to the two phases. Many historians combine the views, noting that the movement changed over time and that participants had different motives.

Why it matters: How the revolt is classified affects how it is connected to later Korean history, including nationalism, democracy, and social reform, and warns against reading modern ideologies back into 1894.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Rural Household in 1893–1895

Consider a tenant household in a Jeolla village. Its members rise before dawn for field work, and after the harvest it owes a share of the crop to the landowner, grain-loan repayments to the county, and various fees to clerks. In the spring of 1894 the household faces difficult choices. A son may join a gathering, with the risk of reprisal; the family may supply rice to marchers, whether willingly or under pressure; or it may stay quiet to protect its fields.

In the summer, a local jipgangso may arbitrate a debt dispute or restrain a clerk, giving the household some relief. In the autumn, planting and harvest conflict with mobilization. After the defeat, the household may suffer burned buildings, arrests of kin, informing neighbors, and losses from the movement of armies. The war adds foreign requisitioning, forced transport labor, and rising prices. These patterns are reconstructed from varied sources and varied by village; they should be treated as typical possibilities, not a documented family.

Because almost all written sources were produced by officials and educated men, historians infer much of ordinary households' experience from trial records, local diaries, and later oral memory.

Division VIThe Wider Sino-Japanese War, Diplomatic Settlement, and Historical Synthesis
Section 51.29

The War Crosses the Yalu and Enters Manchuria

After Pyongyang, Japanese forces pushed the remaining Qing troops north. In late October 1894 the Japanese First Army, under Yamagata Aritomo, crossed the Yalu River near Jiuliancheng and Hushan against weak Qing resistance, moving the war from Korean to Chinese territory. A second army under Ōyama Iwao landed on the Liaodong Peninsula at about the same time.

The shift mattered for Korea. The peninsula ceased to be the main battleground, but it remained the logistical base for Japanese operations: roads, ports, and telegraph lines were used for supply, and Korean laborers were pressed into transport service. The Korean government, under Japanese influence, formally remained a party to the war effort through the August agreement.

Qing weaknesses were evident. Defenses were improvised, forces were divided among regional commands, and equipment varied in quality. Japan, by contrast, had unified command, a modern supply system, and control of the sea. Historians caution that the war was not a simple contest between a modern and a stagnant state; China had undertaken military reforms, and the outcome reflected organization, command, and political choices as well as technology.

The campaign beyond the Yalu also reflected political developments in Japan. The imperial headquarters had moved to Hiroshima in September, the Diet met there in October, and opposition parties that had challenged the government earlier closed ranks behind the war effort. Popular enthusiasm, fed by newspapers and by war reporting, strengthened the government's position. These domestic conditions help explain why Japan was prepared to press the war beyond Korea once it had achieved its initial goals, whereas the Qing court remained divided over strategy and leadership.

For the Qing, the crossing exposed the weakness of the northern defenses. Forces along the river were scattered, many were poorly supplied, and commanders disagreed over where to make a stand. Japanese units, supported by artillery and engineers, forced the crossing and pushed on toward Manchuria. The speed of the advance meant that the war now threatened the Qing homeland and its dynastic heartland, which increased pressure to seek a settlement.

Evidence. Japanese military histories, Qing memorials, and foreign press reports form the main sources; each tends to emphasize its own side's views.

Section 51.30

The Liaodong Campaign and the Capture of Port Arthur

The Japanese Second Army took Jinzhou and Dalian in early November 1894 and attacked Port Arthur (Lüshun), the Qing's major naval base on the tip of Liaodong, which fell on November 21. Port Arthur was strategically important because it served as the Beiyang Fleet's principal dockyard, and its loss weakened Qing naval power.

The capture was followed by killing of Chinese civilians and prisoners over several days. Contemporary Western correspondents reported widespread massacre, and their accounts caused controversy; Japanese authorities denied or minimized the reports, arguing that soldiers had reacted to mutilated Japanese bodies and to resistance by men who had discarded uniforms. Estimates of the number killed vary widely, from a few hundred in Japanese official claims to thousands in other accounts, and the highest numbers circulated in the press are generally regarded as uncertain. Historians agree that large-scale killing of noncombatants and prisoners occurred and that it was a significant episode in the war's conduct.

The incident should be understood in context. The same Japanese government that presented the war as a campaign of civilization sought to demonstrate compliance with international law, and the reports tested that claim. The episode also reflects the dynamics of an army in a victorious assault, but historians avoid reducing it to a single cause.

The Second Army landed on the Liaodong coast in late October and moved against Jinzhou and Dalian in early November before turning toward Port Arthur. The base had been built up with modern forts and docks, but its garrison was divided and its commanders did not agree on defense. The Japanese attack on November 21 overran the forts within a day. The speed of the fall surprised foreign observers who had regarded the base as a strong position.

The killings that followed became an international issue because foreign correspondents were present. Their reports described the killing of civilians and of prisoners over several days, and they provoked public criticism in Western newspapers. Japanese officials, worried about their country's reputation and the revision of unequal treaties, issued explanations and denied some claims; they argued that soldiers had been enraged by mutilated Japanese bodies they found. These explanations do not erase the evidence of widespread killing of people who were not fighting. The dispute over numbers reflects the difficulty of counting the dead of a chaotic event rather than doubt about the killings themselves.

Evidence. Western press accounts, diplomatic correspondence, Japanese official reports, and later memoirs provide conflicting accounts; the disputes concern scale and responsibility and not whether the killings occurred.

Section 51.31

The Naval War and the Defeat at Weihaiwei

With Port Arthur lost, the remains of the Beiyang Fleet withdrew to Weihaiwei, on the Shandong coast. In late January 1895 Japanese forces landed in Shandong and attacked the fortified harbor from the land side, while the Combined Fleet blockaded the sea approach. After a siege that included torpedo boat attacks on the Qing fleet, Admiral Ding Ruchang authorized a surrender proposal dated February 12, 1895, and died by suicide before the final capitulation was signed on February 17.

The loss of the fleet ended Qing naval capability for the war. It also exposed Qing coastal defenses to Japanese pressure and strengthened the pressure on Beijing to seek peace. The Qing government, which had been divided about whether to continue, now faced the possibility of Japanese operations toward the capital region.

For Korea, these battles were distant. But they determined the eventual terms of the settlement, and they confirmed that Qing influence on the peninsula could not be restored by arms.

Weihaiwei on the Shandong coast was fortified with shore batteries and island forts, and it sheltered the surviving ships of the Beiyang fleet. Rather than assault the harbor directly from the sea, Japanese forces landed to the east in January 1895 and took the shore batteries from the landward side, turning the guns against the ships. The campaign was fought in severe winter cold, which caused hardship for both sides. These conditions help explain why the operation took weeks and not days, and why the Japanese preferred a combined attack to a frontal assault.

In early February Japanese torpedo boats attacked the Qing ships in the harbor, while shore batteries captured by Japanese land forces bombarded the fleet. The flagship Dingyuan sustained major damage and was scuttled by its own crew to prevent capture; describing it simply as a ship sunk by torpedoes obscures the sequence. With the harbor defenses lost and morale collapsing, Admiral Ding Ruchang sent surrender terms dated February 12 and died by suicide. The final surrender was signed on February 17, after which the Japanese accepted the remaining ships and fortifications. The resulting loss of Qing naval strength was decisive in the northern Yellow Sea; claims about the precise comparative size of the two entire national navies would require a separate documented inventory.

The strategic result was that the Qing no longer had a fleet capable of contesting the northern seas. Debates continue over how much the failure owed to material shortages, command rivalry, or Li Hongzhang's preference for preserving his forces. Whatever the proportions, the loss ended any realistic prospect of changing the war's outcome by naval means and made negotiation unavoidable.

Evidence. Naval reports, foreign observers' accounts, and later histories provide detailed information; casualty numbers vary.

Section 51.32

Korean Society during the Sino-Japanese War

For ordinary Koreans, the war was an event they experienced but did not choose. Armies moving through the peninsula requisitioned grain, livestock, and labor. Korean porters and carters were drafted to move supplies, often under harsh conditions and with promised payments that did not always arrive. Prices rose in some places and goods were scarce in others. Telegraph and military roads cut through farmland and villages.

Civilians faced violence from multiple sides. In the northwest, battles and occupation devastated Pyongyang and surrounding villages, and many residents fled. In the south, the suppression of the peasant army overlapped with the war and added reprisals. Some communities were burned; others escaped direct violence but suffered from the demands on resources. Many families were displaced, either temporarily or permanently, and the dislocation of the agricultural cycle contributed to hardship.

Koreans responded in different ways. Some officials and elites cooperated with Japan, some from conviction about reform and some from calculation. Others resisted quietly or openly. Some villagers saw Japanese troops as intruders and others sought to profit from trade with them. Missionaries and foreign travelers recorded some of these scenes, though their accounts reflect their own concerns and frequently centered on the cities where they lived.

The experiences cannot be separated from the reform program that Japan was pushing at the same time (Chapter 52). The peasants who had demanded relief from taxes and corruption now faced a new government dominated by officials who depended on foreign support.

Labor was among the heaviest burdens. Armies needed porters and carters, and Korean laborers were hired or pressed into service to move supplies along the road north. Pay was promised but often irregular, and refusals could bring punishment. Japanese forces installed and seized military telegraph lines without Korean authorization and imposed burdens on local communities for communications and supply. Korean historical studies document the cutting of lines, attacks on telegraph workers or military communications, and resistance by Donghak forces and other local opponents; the participants cannot always be identified as unaffiliated villagers. These actions disrupted Japanese operations and sometimes brought punitive responses.

Civilians in different regions experienced the war differently. Northwestern communities suffered the direct effects of battle and occupation, with many residents of Pyongyang fleeing the city before the fighting and returning to find property damaged. In the south, villages faced both suppression of the peasant army and Japanese demands for supplies. Foreign missionaries and travelers described scenes of ruin and fear, though their accounts focus on towns where they lived and on their own concerns. Women, children, and the elderly, who left few records, bore the burdens of flight, hunger, and loss, and historians infer much of their experience from the broader pattern.

Section 51.33

The Treaty of Shimonoseki, April 17, 1895

With military defeat clear, the Qing government sent Li Hongzhang to negotiate in Japan. Talks began in March 1895 at Shimonoseki. During the negotiations, Li was shot and wounded by a Japanese assailant, an incident that embarrassed Tokyo and led it to grant an armistice. The treaty was signed on April 17, 1895.

Article I recognized the full and complete independence and autonomy of Korea and ended the payment of tribute and the performance of ceremonies by Korea to China. This formally ended the traditional tributary relationship, which had in the preceding decade been given new, more assertive form by Qing officials such as Yuan Shikai. Other provisions ceded to Japan the Liaodong Peninsula, Taiwan, and the Penghu Islands, required a large indemnity, and opened additional Chinese ports to Japanese commerce.

The distinction between declared independence and effective independence is central. The treaty was an agreement between Japan and China; Korea was not a party. It ended Qing claims, but it did not bind Japan to respect Korean sovereignty, and Japan was at that time the dominant foreign power in Seoul, with troops and advisors. Many Koreans, including those who favored reform, hoped that independence would be real; the events of the following decade (Chapter 53 and Chapter 54) showed the limits of that hope.

The negotiations show how far Japan's position had improved. Li Hongzhang, who came to Japan as the Qing's senior negotiator, faced demands for territory, indemnity, and commercial privileges. The attack on him by a Japanese assailant caused an international stir and led to a ceasefire, but did not change the substance of Japan's aims. The treaty's financial terms were severe, and the cession of territory represented a major shift in the regional balance.

For Korea, Article I formalized the end of a framework that had shaped diplomacy for centuries. Yet the Korean government had no seat at the negotiations, and many officials were uncertain what independence would mean in practice. Reformers who had hoped to free Korea from Qing predominance saw an opportunity, while conservatives worried that Japan would replace China as the dominant power. Both groups were right in different ways: the treaty ended one form of dependence and left open the question of what would replace it.

Evidence. The treaty text is preserved in the diplomatic records of both countries.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary: Tributary Relationship and Unequal Treaty

Tributary relationship · Unequal treaty

Definition. A tributary relationship was a diplomatic framework in which Joseon sent missions and observed ceremonial forms toward the Chinese court, in return for recognition and a measure of protection; an unequal treaty is a treaty imposed under pressure that grants one party privileges not reciprocated.

Historical context. The tributary framework governed Joseon's relations with China for centuries and was reinterpreted by Qing officials in the 1880s; the treaties of the Ganghwa period (Chapter 48) are the usual examples of unequal treaties in Korea.

Why it matters. Shimonoseki ended the first and replaced it with a new international setting.

Potential misunderstanding. Tributary status did not necessarily mean Chinese control of Joseon's domestic government, though Qing practice after 1882 moved closer to that.

Section 51.34

The Collapse of Qing Predominance and Korea's Uncertain Future

The war ended Qing predominance in Korea, which had been reasserted after 1882. The tributary relationship, which for centuries had defined Korea's formal place in East Asia, was formally ended. Japan's position was transformed from one of several competing powers to the leading foreign influence in the peninsula.

The settlement was immediately complicated. Within days, Russia, Germany, and France pressed Japan to return the Liaodong Peninsula, the Triple Intervention, and Japan complied in exchange for additional indemnity. This episode showed that other powers were prepared to limit Japan and encouraged some Korean leaders to look to Russia, with consequences taken up in Chapter 52.

Compare the peasant revolution's demands with the settlement. The peasant movement had sought relief from abusive taxation, an end to corruption, and perhaps broader social reform. The international settlement addressed none of these. Korea's formal independence was a diplomatic outcome of great-power conflict, not a result of the aims of Korean rural communities. The suppression of the peasant army removed a major force that had demanded reform from below, while the reform program that followed was enacted from above under foreign pressure.

The revolution demonstrated that Korean rural communities could organize at scale and demand systemic change. The war changed the international conditions in which any such change might occur, and it did so without resolving the problems that had produced the uprising.

The Triple Intervention showed that Japan's gains were subject to challenge by other great powers. Within days of the treaty, Russia, Germany, and France urged Japan to give up the Liaodong Peninsula, and Japan, unable to resist all three, agreed in exchange for a larger indemnity. Korean observers, including officials in the court, noted that Japan could be checked, and some drew the conclusion that Russia might serve as a counterweight. The consequences of that reasoning belong to Chapter 52, but the intervention's immediate effect was to make the post-war settlement less stable than it had first appeared.

The contrast between the revolution's aims and the outcome is the chapter's central irony. Peasants had demanded accountable local government and relief from abusive burdens, and their movement was crushed by a government assisted by foreign troops. Meanwhile the war that followed the revolt redefined Korea's international status in a settlement negotiated without Korean participation. The two outcomes were produced by different forces, but they were linked by events: the rebellion's crisis created the occasion for intervention, and intervention determined whether Korean demands would be addressed by Koreans or by others.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Rural Authority and Korea's Diplomatic Position, 1893–1895

What changed? Korea's formal relationship with China ended; Japan became the leading foreign power; the court lost control over the palace and the army; and a peasant movement briefly exercised local authority.

What remained continuous? Rural tax burdens and landlord-tenant relations; the weakness of the central state; Korea's vulnerability to great powers.

When did change occur? Most change was compressed into 1894, with the summer and autumn marking the sharpest breaks.

Gradual or abrupt? Diplomatic change was abrupt, while rural conditions changed only unevenly.

Who experienced change most directly? Court officials, peasant participants, and residents of battle zones; many villagers experienced more continuity in tax and tenure burdens.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that a transformation in international status did not necessarily improve conditions for most Koreans.

Chapter Summary

The Donghak Peasant Revolution arose from agrarian hardship and the failure of local government. Unauthorized taxes, grain-loan abuses, debt, and corruption pressed on cultivators, and the established remedy of petition proved ineffective. Donghak provided networks and moral language, but many participants were not believers.

The Gobu uprising of February 1894 was a local protest against a specific magistrate. Government reprisals under an investigator who blamed Donghak turned it into a larger movement. In April, Jeon Bong-jun, Son Hwa-jung, and Kim Gae-nam mobilized thousands at Mujang and Baeksan, organized an army, and adopted the slogans boguk anmin and jepok gumin.

The peasant army defeated provincial and Seoul troops at Hwangtohyeon and Hwangnyongchon and took Jeonju on May 31. Its successes owed much to local knowledge, numbers, and community support, against poorly motivated government forces.

The Jeonju truce of about June 11 ended the first campaign. Details of the agreement are uncertain, and no authenticated written text is known. In the following weeks, jipgangso exercised local authority in parts of the southwest, in some places alongside officials, a short-lived experiment whose extent is difficult to document.

The famous twelve-point reform program comes from a later source, and its reliability is debated. A careful reading distinguishes demands documented in 1894, proposals recorded later, and reforms actually implemented, which were limited and local.

Gojong's court requested Qing troops; Japan decided independently to send a larger force. Japan's refusal to withdraw, the failure of mediation, and the seizure of Gyeongbokgung on July 23 transformed a domestic crisis into war. The battles of Pungdo and Seonghwan and the declarations of August 1 began the war, and Pyongyang and the Yellow Sea in September ended Qing power in Korea.

The Japanese occupation and the war provoked a second uprising. Southern and northern Donghak forces attempted to coordinate, though the precise timing and location of their first combined actions remain disputed. Southern forces under Jeon and northern forces under Son Byeong-hui, after Choe Si-hyeong's mobilization order, converged on Gongju, where they were defeated at Ugeumchi by better-armed Korean government and Japanese forces. Suppression followed, with burned villages, arrests, and executions, including Jeon's on March 30, 1895. Casualty totals are unknown but were very large.

The war moved into Manchuria and to Port Arthur and Weihaiwei. Japanese forces landed on the Shandong coast in January 1895 and combined assaults on the land defenses and the remaining Qing fleet; Admiral Ding Ruchang died by suicide after offering surrender terms, and the capitulation at Weihaiwei was completed on February 17. The Treaty of Shimonoseki of April 17, 1895 ended the tributary relationship and recognized Korean independence, but Korea was not a party and Japan's dominance in Seoul meant that independence was more formal than effective.

The chapter's argument is that the revolution and the war were connected by decisions as well as by geography. The revolt gave occasion for foreign troops, but the war was a product of Qing and Japanese choices. The war's outcome changed Korea's international position without resolving rural grievances, a gap that shaped the reforms of the Gabo period and the search for sovereignty of the Korean Empire.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows two histories moving together without being the same history. One is a Korean story of rural people who organized, governed locally, and demanded change. The other is an interstate war that used Korea as a stage. Keeping both in view guards against treating Koreans as an opening act in another country's drama.

The revolution's defeat did not end the questions it raised about taxation, status, and legitimate authority. Several appear again in the Gabo reforms and the debates of the Korean Empire, though under different political conditions.

The war ended the tributary order and began a period in which Japan became the principal foreign power in Korea. The declared independence of 1895 was the start of a contested process, whose course Chapters 52 to 54 follow.

The memory of 1894 also continued to matter, as nationalists, religious communities, and later governments in both Korean states interpreted it in different ways. Reading that memory critically is part of studying the revolution itself.
