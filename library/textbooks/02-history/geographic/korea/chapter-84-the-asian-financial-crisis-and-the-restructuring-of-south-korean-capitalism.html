The Asian Financial Crisis and the Restructuring of South Korean Capitalism

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In December 1996 South Korea joined the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, a club of wealthy industrial democracies. Eleven months later, in November 1997, its government asked the International Monetary Fund for emergency assistance. How could an economy celebrated for decades of rapid industrialization become vulnerable so quickly, and what did the crisis permanently change?

The speed of the reversal is the first fact that any account of the 1997 crisis must explain. Through the early 1990s South Korea had been one of the most frequently cited examples of late industrialization. Its exporters sold ships, automobiles, steel, electronics, and semiconductors to markets on several continents, its per capita income had risen to a level that, by official measures, passed ten thousand US dollars in the mid-1990s, and its government under President Kim Young-sam promoted a policy of segyehwa, or globalization, which included entry into the OECD. Yet by late 1997 the country's usable foreign-exchange reserves had nearly run out, the won had lost much of its value against the dollar, and one large industrial group after another was seeking protection from creditors. In the weeks after the government's request for assistance, a national election brought an opposition candidate, Kim Dae-jung, to the presidency during a financial emergency.

This chapter treats the crisis as a problem of financial structure rather than of lost productive capacity. South Korea's factories, ports, universities, and skilled workforce did not disappear in 1997. What failed was the financing arrangement that had supported expansion: large industrial groups had borrowed heavily from banks, banks and newly expanded merchant banks had funded themselves in part with short-term foreign loans, and when foreign lenders declined to renew those loans the country faced a shortage of the foreign currency needed to repay them. This distinction separates the South Korean experience from the economic breakdown that North Korea suffered in the same decade, examined in Chapter 83, where production, energy supply, and the food distribution system themselves collapsed. The two crises shared a decade and little else.

The chapter also asks how the crisis was explained at the time and since. Officials, creditors, economists, executives, and union leaders reached different conclusions about its causes and about who should bear the costs of recovery. Some stressed the weaknesses of Korea's own institutions: debt-heavy conglomerates, politically influenced lending, and weak supervision. Others stressed the volatility of international capital and the contagion that spread across Southeast and East Asia in 1997. Historians now generally treat these explanations as complementary rather than mutually exclusive, but the weight placed on each remains debated, as the Historical Debate features in this chapter show.

The evidence is unusually rich for a recent event, and it varies in kind. It includes the government's Letters of Intent to the IMF, IMF staff and board documents, Bank of Korea and ministry statistics, records of the Tripartite Commission, corporate filings, newspaper reporting, and photographs and footage preserved by the National Archives of Korea. Each source speaks from a position: official documents present the problem as policymakers wished creditors to see it, statistical series simplify the unevenness of experience, and contemporary reporting often captured the mood of a moment without knowing how events would end. The chapter relies on documentary and statistical sources and does not construct imagined individual witnesses.

The chapter's argument is that South Korea emerged from the crisis with its industrial economy and its newly consolidated democratic political system intact, but with the rules governing finance, corporate responsibility, employment security, and economic risk substantially altered. Large conglomerates remained central, yet the way they were financed and governed changed. The state remained influential, yet it exercised power through different institutions. Aggregate growth returned within two years, yet the sense of secure lifetime employment that many regular workers had expected did not return in the same form. These contrasts between institutional recovery and unequal social cost recur throughout the chapter.

The chapter sits at a hinge in Volume IV. It builds on Chapter 75's account of the developmental state and Chapter 76's account of the chaebol, on Chapter 81's description of the industrial-era society that the crisis disrupted, and on Chapter 82's account of the democratic institutions within which crisis management took place. It prepares the ground for Chapter 87's account of South Korea as a technology power and for Chapter 91's analysis of inequality, employment, and household pressures in the twenty-first century. Kim Dae-jung's policy of inter-Korean engagement is reserved for Chapter 85.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how corporate debt, weak financial supervision, and short-term foreign borrowing combined to produce South Korea's 1997 financial crisis.
  2. Distinguish among corporate insolvency, bank weakness, and a foreign-exchange liquidity crisis, and explain why the distinction matters for policy.
  3. Trace the sequence from the Hanbo and Kia failures of 1997 through regional contagion, capital withdrawal, and the request for IMF assistance.
  4. Describe the IMF arrangement approved in December 1997, distinguishing the IMF's own stand-by arrangement from the larger announced international support package.
  5. Analyze how banking reform, chaebol restructuring, and changes in corporate governance altered the relationships among the state, banks, and conglomerates.
  6. Explain the 1998 tripartite negotiations and the trade-offs among employment flexibility, worker protection, and social welfare.
  7. Assess the social consequences of the crisis for regular workers, nonregular workers, women, young people, and households, without assuming that all were affected equally.
  8. Evaluate competing interpretations of the crisis's causes and of the IMF program's design.
  9. Interpret documentary evidence, including a Letter of Intent, as a source shaped by its authors' purposes and audience.
  10. Distinguish macroeconomic recovery from the recovery of employment security and household welfare.
  11. Identify what changed and what remained continuous in South Korean capitalism after 1997, including the continuing role of the chaebol.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could an economy regarded as a success appear stable while accumulating serious financial vulnerabilities?
  2. Why did short-term foreign borrowing convert corporate and banking weakness into a national currency emergency?
  3. To what extent was the crisis a failure of Korean institutions, and to what extent a product of international capital movements and regional contagion?
  4. Why do historians distinguish a liquidity crisis from an insolvency crisis, and why did both matter in 1997?
  5. How did a newly elected democratic government negotiate among creditors, chaebol, banks, and organized labor while implementing a program set under external conditionality?
  6. Who bore the costs of restructuring, and by what mechanisms were losses distributed among shareholders, taxpayers, workers, and households?
  7. What evidence supports the claim that chaebol reform succeeded, and what evidence suggests that chaebol influence persisted?
  8. How should historians weigh the evidence for rapid macroeconomic recovery against evidence of lasting employment insecurity?
  9. What did the 1997 crisis change about Koreans' expectations of the state, the firm, and the family as sources of security?

Historical Context

South Korea's industrialization after the early 1960s, examined in Chapters 75 and 76, relied on a distinctive partnership between the state and private business groups. Under Park Chung-hee the government set export targets, directed credit toward favored industries, and rewarded firms that met its goals. The banking system was largely under state influence, and lending often followed policy priorities rather than independent assessments of risk. The large family-controlled groups known as chaebol, including Hyundai, Samsung, LG, and Daewoo, grew by borrowing heavily to expand into steel, shipbuilding, automobiles, electronics, and other heavy and chemical industries. Lenders came to assume that the state would not allow a very large group to fail, because its collapse would threaten banks, employment, and the export record on which the regime's legitimacy partly rested.

This model produced remarkable growth, but it also built habits of financing that were later called into question. Firms were encouraged to expand rapidly, often measured by market share and sales rather than profitability, and to finance expansion with borrowing rather than with equity. During the 1980s the government gradually reduced direct control over credit and encouraged a larger role for private financial institutions, while political democratization, examined in Chapter 82, shifted the balance of power. Presidents after 1987 could no longer discipline business groups, or direct banks, in the manner of the authoritarian era. Businesses gained autonomy faster than the financial system developed independent credit assessment, and supervisory institutions were slower to adapt than corporate borrowing.

In the 1990s the Kim Young-sam government pursued financial liberalization and international integration. Interest rates were gradually deregulated, new financial institutions were licensed, and controls on foreign borrowing and on foreign participation in domestic markets were partly relaxed, in part in connection with OECD accession. The effect was to give banks, merchant banks, and firms new access to international funds before supervisory capacity, accounting standards, and risk management had caught up. At the same time, the economy was less secure than headline growth suggested: in 1996 world prices for semiconductors, one of Korea's most important exports, fell sharply, and the current-account deficit widened.

The crisis therefore occurred within a particular conjunction: a democratic but not yet financially reformed state, powerful and indebted business groups, international lenders eager to place funds in Asian emerging markets, and a regional environment in which confidence could reverse quickly. Understanding the crisis requires holding these together, and requires remembering that the same economy had both genuine industrial strengths and genuine financial weaknesses.

Section 84.1 · Part I: Vulnerabilities beneath success

South Korea on the Eve of the Financial Crisis

By the mid-1990s South Korea looked like a finished success story. The economy had grown at very high rates for more than three decades, with growth in the early 1990s commonly in the range of seven to nine percent a year. Manufacturing had moved from textiles and light goods to automobiles, ships, steel, consumer electronics, and memory chips. Cities had grown and incomes had risen, as Chapter 81 describes, and the political system had moved from military-backed rule to competitive democracy, with Kim Young-sam, the first president in three decades without a military background, taking office in 1993. Admission to the OECD in December 1996 was widely read, inside and outside the country, as a certificate of arrival.

Admission to the OECD also reflected the policy direction of the Kim Young-sam government, which presented segyehwa, or globalization, as a national goal. Joining the organization involved commitments to liberalize aspects of financial markets and capital movements. Policymakers, business leaders, and much of the press believed that integration would discipline firms and open new sources of capital. Few of them anticipated that the same openness would expose the country to abrupt reversals of international credit.

Several warning signs were present but were interpreted in different ways. The current-account deficit rose sharply in 1996, to a level commonly estimated at around four to five percent of gross domestic product, partly because semiconductor prices fell and partly because investment and imports had grown rapidly. Corporate profitability was weak by comparison with the scale of investment. Many large groups were expanding into new industries, including automobiles and steel, at a time when export prices were under pressure. But the government's own budget was close to balance and public debt was low, inflation was modest, and the country had a long record of meeting external obligations. These conditions made the country look sound to conventional indicators. The vulnerability lay not in the government's accounts but in private-sector balance sheets, the structure of foreign liabilities, and the opacity of reporting, which made problems hard to see until confidence broke.

For historians, the interpretive question is why these weaknesses were underestimated. The explanation involves institutional habits as much as individual misjudgment. A history of successful rescues of troubled firms encouraged lenders to assume that the state would stand behind major borrowers. Rapid growth made past borrowing look justified. International investors, enthusiastic about Asian emerging markets, supplied funds readily. And information about group-wide finances, including cross-guarantees among affiliates, was not fully disclosed. It is easier to identify weakness in retrospect than it was for contemporaries, but the chapter's task is to explain why weaknesses visible in the structure of the economy did not reduce confidence until 1997.

The international recognition embodied in OECD membership also produced a change in how Korean economic performance was judged. Policymakers were no longer comparing their country only with developing industrial competitors; they were also being assessed by foreign creditors, credit-rating agencies, and securities investors. Those audiences paid attention to financial disclosure, the soundness of banks, and the credibility of currency policy. A factory could remain competitive in exports while the firm that owned it had a balance sheet exposed to refinancing risk. That distinction became decisive when external lenders began to judge Korean borrowers differently from the way they had during the boom.

A broader historical comparison makes the paradox clear. Earlier phases of industrialization had used directed finance to overcome an absence of domestic capital, and the government could sometimes coordinate creditors behind selected strategic industries. By the 1990s the number of firms, financial products, and international transactions had outgrown the institutions that originally coordinated this expansion. Industrial skill, public education, infrastructure, and export markets remained genuine assets. They were not substitutes for a system able to monitor short-term foreign liabilities and require firms to recognize losses. The crisis would test the financing of an advanced industrial economy rather than erase the achievements of industrialization.

Section 84.2

The Chaebol, Corporate Debt, and Overexpansion

The chaebol, whose development is traced in Chapter 76, were the center of the vulnerability. Groups such as Hyundai, Samsung, LG, Daewoo, and many smaller ones comprised dozens of affiliated firms in industries ranging from construction and finance to shipbuilding and electronics, typically controlled by a founding family that held modest direct equity but exercised control through cross-shareholdings among affiliates. Their growth had been financed overwhelmingly by borrowing from banks, supported by the assumption that large groups would be rescued if they fell into difficulty. By the mid-1990s the largest groups reported debt far exceeding their equity. Estimates of the average debt-equity ratio of the thirty largest groups at the time of the crisis place it on the order of four to five hundred percent, with some groups far above that level, in contrast to the much lower ratios typical of large firms in the United States.

High leverage was not itself a sign of weakness while firms earned enough to service debt and banks were willing to lend. The problem was the pattern of investment. In the mid-1990s several groups launched large expansions into capital-intensive industries such as steel, automobiles, petrochemicals, and semiconductors. These projects often sought market share and group prestige, and some expansions were politically supported. Competition among domestic groups for position in the same industries produced overcapacity. When export prices fell and borrowing costs rose, many projects could not generate enough income to service the debt. Rates of return on assets were, by many measures, low, and a significant number of the largest groups did not earn enough from operations to cover interest payments.

Cross-guarantees among affiliates compounded the problem. When one firm borrowed with the guarantee of others in the same group, a failure in one part could transmit losses to all, so that a weak affiliate could drag down healthy ones. They also allowed groups to borrow more than any single firm's balance sheet would have supported. This structure made lenders more willing to extend credit, but it concentrated risk. The combination of implicit state support, family control, cross-guarantees, and limited disclosure created what economists describe as moral hazard: when lenders believe a borrower will be rescued, both lenders and borrowers have an incentive to take greater risks.

It would be a mistake to describe the chaebol as uniformly reckless or to regard growth through debt as an inherent flaw of Korean capitalism. Debt-financed expansion had helped build globally competitive industries. The most heavily indebted groups, however, were those that had grown fastest into new fields, and their fragility meant that a shock to credit could threaten banks, employment, and suppliers across the economy.

A firm's financing choice mattered differently from the point of view of its controlling family, minority investors, and creditors. A family could preserve control over a large group by borrowing instead of issuing shares that might dilute ownership. An affiliated lender might value the group's reputation more than a project's expected cash flow, while an outside shareholder had limited information about obligations buried in other affiliates. These incentives did not require every executive or banker to behave irresponsibly. They produced a system in which growth was rewarded, risks were difficult to isolate, and an unsuccessful project could create losses far beyond the subsidiary that undertook it.

The categories of debt also need separation. Debt denominated in won could be hard to service when domestic interest rates rose; debt denominated in dollars became more expensive in won terms when the currency depreciated; and a loan due within a few months posed an immediate refinancing problem even if the underlying investment could become profitable over several years. Some conglomerate affiliates faced all three exposures, but the vulnerabilities were not identical. This is why a policy that stabilized the won, a policy that recapitalized a bank, and a policy that reorganized a business group addressed different parts of the same crisis.

Historical Vocabulary
Debt-Equity Ratio and Cross-Guarantee

Definition. The debt-equity ratio compares a firm's borrowing with its owners' capital; a ratio of 400 percent means four units of debt for every unit of equity. A cross-guarantee is a promise by one affiliate to cover another's debt.

Historical context. During Korea's high-growth decades, banks lent on the basis of collateral, group size, and expected state support, and groups used cross-guarantees to raise funds for new ventures. After 1997 the government set a target for reducing the largest groups' debt-equity ratios to 200 percent and prohibited new cross-guarantees.

Why it matters. These measures explain how weakness at one affiliate could threaten a whole group, and why reformers regarded cross-guarantees and high leverage as central to the crisis.

Potential misunderstanding. A high debt ratio alone does not prove irrational management. In a period of rapid growth and abundant credit, leverage could be profitable. The vulnerability lay in the combination of high debt, low returns, short maturities, and opaque reporting.

Section 84.3

Banks, Merchant Banks, and Short-Term Foreign Borrowing

Korean commercial banks had long been influenced by the state, and their lending decisions had often followed government priorities. By the 1990s they carried large exposures to the chaebol and to heavy industry, and their credit assessment skills were weaker than the scale of their lending required. Since lending was often secured by collateral, notably real estate, and backed by implicit assurance that major borrowers would not be allowed to fail, banks had limited incentive to scrutinize firms' prospects.

A second set of institutions, the merchant banks, played a distinctive role. Beginning in the mid-1990s a number of short-term finance companies were converted into merchant banks, with permission to deal in foreign currency and to borrow abroad. By 1997 there were around thirty. They sought high returns: many borrowed short-term funds in international markets and lent to Korean firms or invested in higher-yielding foreign securities, including bonds issued by governments and firms in other emerging markets. Their supervision was uneven, and their expansion coincided with international lenders' appetite for exposure to Asia.

The central structural weakness was the mismatch between the maturity of foreign borrowing and the use of the money. Long-term foreign borrowing was more tightly regulated than short-term borrowing, which left short-term channels the easier route. As a result, Korean banks and merchant banks borrowed abroad for periods of a year or less and lent the money into investments that would take years to pay off, or into domestic loans that could not easily be repaid on demand. This arrangement works as long as foreign creditors renew their loans, but it depends on confidence. By international banking statistics, roughly two-thirds of foreign banks' claims on Korea at the end of mid-1997 were due within a year, and short-term external obligations exceeded the usable reserves held by the central bank. Japanese banks were among the largest creditors, followed by European and American institutions.

This explains why it is more accurate to describe the crisis as one of liquidity and balance-sheet structure than as an absolute shortage of national wealth. Korea owned productive assets of great value, but its foreign obligations came due faster than it could convert those assets into foreign currency.

The international statistics require careful reading. The often-cited figure that roughly two-thirds of bank claims on Korea were short-term concerns the maturity of claims reported by international banks at mid-1997, not a claim that two-thirds of all Korean public and private debt was owed by banks. A second, distinct statistic concerns how much of those claims was owed by Korean financial institutions rather than by nonfinancial corporations. Confusing the two ratios would hide the particular function of banks as intermediaries between foreign creditors and Korean borrowers. It was their need to renew debts repeatedly that gave the reversal of confidence its speed.

The mismatch could be transmitted even without a direct loan from a foreign bank to a Korean factory. A merchant bank might borrow dollars for three months, convert or invest the proceeds, and finance a Korean borrower whose project took years to produce revenue. The factory would experience a credit shortage when the intermediary could no longer obtain fresh dollars; the merchant bank would experience a liquidity shortage even before its underlying loans were formally in default. This explains why the currency emergency could spread beyond the chaebol most visibly associated with overinvestment.

Section 84.4

Financial Liberalization and Regulatory Weaknesses

Financial liberalization in the 1990s was not simply a retreat of the state. It changed the way the state influenced finance. The Kim Young-sam government reduced controls on interest rates, expanded the types of financial institutions, and eased some restrictions on foreign borrowing. In the same period it reorganized the economic bureaucracy: the Economic Planning Board, a powerful coordinating body of the developmental era, was merged with the Ministry of Finance in 1994 into a new ministry. Many analysts have argued that the removal of the planning body weakened the older coordinating capacity of the state without replacing it with a modern regulatory framework.

Supervision was fragmented and often lacked independence. Responsibility for overseeing banks was divided among the finance ministry and the central bank's supervisory office, and the Bank of Korea did not have the formal independence that its counterparts in some other countries enjoyed. Supervisors had limited ability to assess consolidated risk across affiliated companies, and accounting standards allowed groups to present the financial position of individual firms without a clear picture of the whole. Nonperforming loans were not always recognized or reported in a way that allowed outsiders to judge the true condition of banks.

Liberalization thus produced new opportunities and new exposures at the same time. Firms and financial institutions could borrow more easily abroad, but the institutions that were expected to monitor their risk had not yet developed the necessary tools. It is important to avoid two simplified readings. One holds that liberalization itself caused the crisis; the other holds that the crisis proved the dangers of relying on markets at all. The evidence points rather to a mismatch of pace and sequencing: capital movements were opened more quickly than prudential supervision, disclosure, and corporate governance were strengthened.

The asymmetry in liberalization was concrete. Short-term foreign borrowing by financial intermediaries was relatively easy, while longer-term borrowing and several forms of direct investment remained more tightly controlled. Supervisors therefore faced an expanding volume of transactions that could reverse quickly. Merchant banks did not face a uniform supervisory regime equivalent to commercial banks, despite undertaking risky international activities. A foreign creditor might see a familiar institutional label but could not necessarily assess the consolidated risks of that intermediary or its affiliated borrowers. Reform had opened a channel for capital without adequately governing its maturity, currency exposure, or concentration.

The problem was compounded by organizational fragmentation. Decisions about banking, nonbank finance, foreign borrowing, monetary operations, and financial disclosure sat in overlapping bureaucratic jurisdictions. When problems emerged in one industrial group, no single agency necessarily possessed a timely, comprehensive picture of the exposures of its banks, insurers, and affiliates. Public statements could therefore underestimate liabilities even without deliberate misrepresentation. The credibility of regulation depended not only on having formal rules, but on enforcement, consolidated accounting, timely loss recognition, and the ability to close weak institutions before their losses threatened the system.

Financial opening should also be placed within domestic political choices. Korean policymakers sought the prestige of OECD accession, broader international investment, and a more competitive financial sector; financial institutions and large borrowers expected advantages from access to cheaper capital. They were acting within the prevailing international enthusiasm for liberalization rather than under a single directive. The lesson historians can draw is not that openness inevitably causes instability. It is that the order of reforms matters: opening borrowing channels without preparing supervision and resolution mechanisms changes the risks imposed on the wider economy.

Section 84.5

The Warning Signs: Corporate Failures in 1997

The first major corporate failure of 1997 came on January 23, when Hanbo Steel, the flagship of the Hanbo group, was unable to meet its obligations. Hanbo had borrowed several trillion won, much of it from state-influenced banks, to build a large steelworks at Dangjin, and its collapse was a shock because it was the first failure of a major group in more than a decade. The affair quickly became a political scandal. Prosecutors investigated allegations that Hanbo's founder had obtained loans through payments to officials and politicians, and the scandal reached into circles near the president's family; a presidential son was later convicted on bribery and tax charges. For the economy, Hanbo mattered in two ways. It exposed how lending had followed connections as well as credit assessment, and it increased banks' nonperforming loans.

Further failures followed through the year. Sammi Steel in March, Jinro in April, and a series of other firms entered court protection or creditor management. In July the Kia group, a large automobile and industrial group, asked for creditor support, and its problems dragged through the autumn as the government and banks debated how to handle a failure of that size. Kia was eventually placed under creditor control and was acquired by Hyundai Motor in 1998 after a contested process. The sequence of failures, one after another, signaled that the weakness in corporate balance sheets was not confined to a single firm, and it reduced confidence among both domestic and foreign lenders.

It is useful to distinguish these domestic warning signs from the later liquidity emergency. Corporate failures weakened banks, raised nonperforming loans, and made lenders more cautious. But they did not by themselves exhaust the country's reserves. That transformation took place when the regional crisis of the second half of 1997 combined with the domestic fragility described above.

Hanbo's failure revealed a specific problem of collective exposure. When a major borrower could not pay, its creditors had to classify loans as impaired, set aside capital against potential losses, and reconsider their treatment of other heavily indebted groups. Banks that had appeared stable because their borrowers were still paying interest suddenly faced questions about the quality of whole portfolios. The scandal also made it harder for officials to claim that credit decisions had followed purely commercial criteria. A failure in one industrial project thus became a problem of confidence in governance and disclosure.

The Kia episode extended this uncertainty into an industry with large workforces and supplier networks. Automobile manufacturing connected assembly plants to parts makers, shipping firms, dealers, and local service businesses. Delayed restructuring kept employees and creditors uncertain about whether production would continue and under whose control. A company could be economically important without being financially solvent; the case forced policymakers to choose between preserving productive capacity, protecting creditors, and ending the expectation of automatic rescue. That unresolved tension foreshadowed the decisions of 1998 and the Daewoo workout in 1999.

Section 84.6 · Part II: From regional turmoil to the IMF rescue

The Asian Financial Crisis Reaches South Korea

The regional crisis began in Thailand. On July 2, 1997 the Thai government, having exhausted its efforts to defend its currency, allowed the baht to float, and the currency fell sharply. Pressure spread to the Philippines, Malaysia, and Indonesia over the following months, and then to other markets. Investors who had seen Asian economies as a single attractive category began to reassess risk across the region. In October the Hong Kong stock market fell dramatically and Taiwan allowed its currency to depreciate, raising doubts about whether regional currency arrangements could hold.

The word contagion is used for this process, and it helps to explain the timing. But contagion alone cannot explain why Korea, a larger and more industrially advanced economy than Thailand, was caught. The mechanism of transmission was the credit decisions of international banks and investors, who reduced exposure to economies that looked similar to those in trouble: heavy corporate borrowing, rapid financial opening, and short-term foreign debt. Korea shared these features, and the failures of Hanbo and Kia had already reduced confidence. Credit-rating agencies lowered Korea's sovereign ratings in steps through the autumn and, in December, below investment grade, which raised borrowing costs and restricted the pool of investors who could hold Korean debt. The distress of Japanese financial institutions in November, with several failures, made Japanese banks, Korea's largest foreign creditors, more cautious about renewing loans.

This is why historians generally avoid both extremes. Korea was not a passive victim of a regional panic, because its own financial structure made it vulnerable. But neither was the crisis a purely domestic event: had the regional crisis not occurred, the weaknesses might have been addressed more gradually, or might have surfaced in a different and perhaps less abrupt form.

The chronology of contagion was not a single wave that washed over every Asian economy in the same way. Thailand's currency problems drew attention to foreign liabilities and exchange-rate promises elsewhere. Financial turbulence in Indonesia, Malaysia, and other markets then made lenders reconsider what they knew about regional bank exposures. The late-October deterioration in Hong Kong's financial markets increased anxiety further. At that stage foreign institutions with obligations of their own had an incentive to cut exposure even to borrowers whose immediate operating businesses remained viable. Decisions made by many separate lenders could collectively create the shortage each lender feared.

For Korea the channel was especially direct because so much foreign bank financing matured in the near term. A country with mostly long-term external loans might have endured a deterioration of confidence while continuing to service scheduled payments. Korea's short-maturity profile meant that refinancing decisions made in Tokyo, Europe, or the United States could change the available dollars within days or weeks. The same feature explains why a successful creditor agreement became nearly as important as the initial multilateral financing: the authorities had to change not simply expectations but the timetable on which debts were falling due.

The distinction between common regional pressures and country-specific institutions helps avoid hindsight. Foreign banks reassessed Asia as a category, but Korean merchant banks, highly leveraged corporations, and weak disclosure gave them substantive reasons to worry. The crisis spread through shared investor behavior, while its depth depended on national institutional arrangements. This combination of external pressure and internal vulnerability is more historically precise than describing an innocent victim of panic or a country that failed solely because its companies borrowed too much.

Section 84.7

Capital Flight, the Won's Collapse, and the Credit Emergency

The transition from vulnerability to emergency took place through a series of linked steps in the autumn of 1997. Foreign banks declined to renew short-term loans to Korean banks and merchant banks as they fell due. Because the loans were short, the need for refinancing was constant, and a refusal to roll over meant the borrower had to find foreign currency elsewhere. Korean financial institutions, unable to borrow abroad, turned to the domestic market and then to the central bank, which lent foreign currency from its reserves, and the reserves began to decline.

At the same time pressure fell on the won. The exchange rate had been managed within a narrow daily band, and the central bank used reserves to defend it. In mid-November the authorities abandoned efforts to hold the rate; they widened the permitted daily movement on November 20, and on December 16 abolished the daily fluctuation limit. The currency fell from roughly 900 to the dollar early in 1997 to approach 2,000 to the dollar in late December. Depreciation raised the won cost of dollar-denominated debt for firms and banks that had borrowed abroad without hedging, so the more the currency fell, the heavier the debts became. Every step in this sequence reinforced the next: weaker banks encouraged creditors to withdraw, withdrawal pressured the won, and a weaker won increased the burden of foreign debt.

Domestic credit also tightened. Banks, uncertain about their own solvency and about the value of their loans, reduced lending, and firms with sound prospects but dependent on short-term finance struggled to roll over their debts. A number of merchant banks suspended operations in December, and many were later closed. The crisis thus turned from a corporate and financial problem into a general credit shortage affecting firms that had not themselves been reckless.

A currency can depreciate even when a country possesses valuable factories and land because foreign creditors require payment in a currency that those assets do not immediately produce. An exporter receiving dollars might eventually benefit from a cheaper won, but those gains could arrive months after a bank's dollar loan matured. Meanwhile an unhedged borrower had to find more won to purchase each dollar required for repayment. The timing of cash flows therefore mattered as much as the final competitiveness of the export sector. Currency depreciation and banking weakness amplified one another precisely because debts and earnings were denominated in different currencies.

The Bank of Korea's chronology places the widening of the daily exchange-rate fluctuation band to ten percent on November 20, 1997; the daily limit was removed on December 16. The near-2,000-won quotations belong to the late-December crisis, not to the annual average exchange rate. The distinction is essential for interpreting charts: an annual average conceals the speed and severity of a short-lived exchange-rate collapse, while quoting an extreme daily price as the year's typical rate exaggerates the cost borne throughout 1997.

Historical Debate

Institutional Failure or Liquidity Panic?

The question: Was the 1997 crisis primarily the consequence of weaknesses in South Korea's own economic institutions, or primarily a panic produced by sudden withdrawal of international credit from economies that were fundamentally sound?

Interpretation A — Structural weakness: Many economists, including the IMF in its contemporary assessments, emphasized debt-heavy conglomerates, weakly supervised banks, politically influenced lending, and opaque accounting. On this view, the crisis exposed real weaknesses that would have required correction in any case, and the speed of the collapse reflected their extent.
Interpretation B — Liquidity panic: Other economists argued that Korea's fundamentals, including modest public debt, balanced budgets, high saving, and strong export industries, did not justify the scale of the collapse. In this view, the withdrawal of short-term credit created a self-reinforcing panic, in which each creditor withdrew because others were expected to, and the crisis was larger than domestic weaknesses alone warranted.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same evidence: corporate balance-sheet data, bank loan quality, the structure of short-term foreign debt, the timing of capital withdrawals, and the rapid recovery of exports after depreciation. Structural readings point to the failures of 1997 and to the later necessity of reform; liquidity readings point to the speed of reversal and to the fact that Korea's external position recovered quickly once short-term debts were rescheduled.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and weighting rather than the basic chronology. It also concerns policy: if the crisis was largely structural, thorough restructuring was necessary; if it was largely a panic, emphasis belongs on stabilizing credit and avoiding unnecessary damage.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these explanations shapes judgments about whether subsequent reforms were necessary corrections, or an overcorrection that imposed avoidable costs.

Section 84.8

Exhausted Foreign Reserves and the Decision to Seek IMF Assistance

By November 1997 the government faced a stark arithmetic. Headline reserves overstated what was available, because a portion consisted of deposits placed with overseas branches of Korean banks and could not easily be used. Usable reserves fell to a level far below the short-term external obligations coming due, and below an amount that could sustain defense of the exchange rate. Officials tried a variety of measures, including appeals to foreign governments and banks, but support from major governments was limited without an IMF program. Some officials were initially reluctant to turn to the IMF, partly because the political and symbolic costs of external supervision were great in a country that regarded its economic achievements as a matter of national pride.

On November 21, 1997 the government announced that it would request IMF assistance. A new deputy prime minister and minister of finance and economy, Lim Chang-yuel, who had replaced Kang Kyung-shik shortly before, negotiated with an IMF team in Seoul during the following days. The talks were conducted under extreme time pressure, with the won falling and reserves dwindling. They occurred, moreover, in the middle of a presidential campaign, in which the leading candidates were asked to commit to honoring the agreement.

Understanding the decision requires distinguishing three kinds of financial problem. An external liquidity crisis occurs when a country cannot obtain the foreign currency to meet payments falling due. A banking crisis occurs when banks hold so many bad loans that depositors and creditors lose confidence in them. A sovereign default risk would exist if the government itself could not service its debts. Korea in late 1997 had a severe liquidity crisis and a developing banking crisis, but the government's own finances were sound; the public debt was small, and the budget was in balance. This is one reason many critics later questioned whether the IMF's initial emphasis on fiscal tightening, a standard remedy for governments that have overspent, was appropriate.

Foreign-exchange reserves were especially difficult to judge because the published total was not identical to immediately usable cash. Some reserves had been placed with overseas branches of domestic banks, and using them to meet other payments could further weaken those institutions. As liabilities matured, the government had to consider the simultaneous needs of the central bank, commercial banks, merchant banks, and corporate importers. A nominal reserve figure that looked adequate against several months of imports could become dangerously small when compared with foreign-bank claims due in the next few weeks.

The decision to call upon the IMF involved more than a calculation of money available. Korean policymakers feared that an official request would damage national prestige and indicate that economic sovereignty had been constrained. Delay, however, could worsen the panic if creditors concluded that the authorities were concealing the reserve problem. The eventual appeal reflected the narrowing of alternatives. Emergency assistance offered foreign currency and a signal of policy commitment, but neither could replace negotiation with the private banks holding immediately maturing claims.

Historical Vocabulary
Liquidity and Insolvency

Definition. Liquidity concerns the ability to meet obligations as they fall due; a firm or country with valuable assets can still be illiquid if it cannot convert them to cash quickly. Insolvency concerns whether liabilities exceed assets.

Historical context. In 1997 both problems existed in different parts of the economy. Several chaebol and financial institutions were insolvent, with debts beyond any realistic income. At the same time, many solvent firms and banks were illiquid because foreign credit had been withdrawn.

Why it matters. The two problems require different responses: emergency financing may bridge a liquidity gap, while insolvency requires restructuring, write-offs, or closure.

Potential misunderstanding. The distinction can be hard to make in a crisis. An illiquid institution may become insolvent if forced to sell assets at distressed prices, and a solvent one may appear insolvent to frightened creditors.

Section 84.9

The December 1997 IMF Agreement and Economic Conditionality

On December 3, 1997 the Korean government signed a Letter of Intent setting out its economic program, and on December 4 the IMF's Executive Board approved a stand-by arrangement for Korea. The arrangement was for about 15.5 billion Special Drawing Rights, equivalent to approximately 21 billion US dollars, and was generally described as the largest IMF arrangement ever approved up to that time. It is essential to distinguish this from the broader international support package. Alongside the IMF's financing, the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank pledged additional funds, and a group of governments, including the United States and Japan, indicated that they would provide further resources if needed, a so-called second line of defense. Together the announced commitments were approximately 58 billion US dollars (commonly cited as 58.4 billion). That total was a statement of the potential support available, not the amount actually disbursed; the IMF provided funds in tranches over the following period, and a significant part of the second-line commitments was never drawn.

The agreement carried conditions. Monetary policy was to be tightened to stabilize the won and contain inflation, and the initial program also called for fiscal restraint, including a target for a small budget surplus. The financial sector was to be restructured: insolvent institutions were to be closed or recapitalized, supervision strengthened, and the central bank given greater independence and a mandate focused on price stability. Corporations were to improve transparency, reduce cross-guarantees, and strengthen their financial structures. The program also included trade and capital-market commitments, such as eliminating trade-related subsidies and widening foreign participation in Korean financial markets, including raising the ceilings on foreign ownership of listed stocks. A further commitment concerned labor-market flexibility.

The agreement thus reached far beyond emergency lending. It was a program for the redesign of Korea's financial and corporate system, which is why it was experienced domestically as an intrusion on national economic sovereignty. At the same time, its reforms coincided with the preferences of some Korean reformers, who had long argued that the older system of state-directed credit and chaebol debt needed change. The program was therefore both externally imposed and, in important respects, internally welcomed, a duality that shaped how it was later remembered.

The financing commitments had different legal and economic meanings. The IMF stand-by facility provided access to resources subject to program reviews; the development banks supported particular reform and financing efforts; bilateral second-line pledges were contingent rather than an unconditional transfer of cash. Adding all these commitments and describing the result as money handed to Korea would be misleading. Likewise, the value of the agreement was partly psychological: it was intended to persuade private creditors that Korea could meet obligations while reforms restored confidence. The short interval in which the won continued to fall after approval shows that this signal initially proved insufficient.

The original program also illustrates the limitations of forecasting under crisis conditions. The December letter projected real GDP growth of about three percent in 1998, but later historical series record a contraction of 6.7 percent. This was not merely a small revision to an economic forecast. It meant that the income, tax revenue, credit losses, and unemployment consequences on which the initial program rested were profoundly underestimated. Subsequent adjustments to fiscal policy and social provision have to be understood in that context: decisions were made using projections that rapidly became obsolete.

Historical Vocabulary
Conditionality

Definition. The policy commitments a government makes to the IMF in return for financial support, normally set out in a Letter of Intent and reviewed periodically.

Historical context. The IMF's Korea program went beyond macroeconomic stabilization to include financial, corporate, trade, and labor policies. Funds were released in installments, subject to reviews, and the program was revised several times during 1998.

Why it matters. Conditionality explains both the leverage of external lenders in 1997–98 and the controversy over whether some conditions were necessary.

Potential misunderstanding. Conditionality does not mean the IMF wrote policy alone. Korean officials drafted much of the program in negotiation, and the program's text records Korean commitments rather than IMF orders, even though the bargaining power was uneven.

Section 84.10

Stabilization, High Interest Rates, and Foreign Debt Rescheduling

The initial approval of the IMF arrangement did not end the emergency. In the second half of December the won continued to fall, and foreign creditors continued to withdraw. The program's first disbursements were insufficient to restore confidence, and credit-rating cuts continued. Interest rates rose to extraordinary levels; short-term market rates exceeded 25 percent, and in some segments rose above 30 percent, as the authorities tried to stem capital outflows and defend the currency. Such rates were intended to make holding won assets more attractive and to discourage speculation against the currency.

The central bank's high interest rates came at a heavy cost to domestic firms. Companies already carrying large debts faced sharply higher interest costs, and even profitable firms that depended on short-term finance found it hard to roll over debt. Company failures rose into the thousands, and the credit shortage cut into output and employment. This is the key tension in the stabilization program: measures designed to restore financial confidence deepened the recession and raised the number of bankruptcies. The question of whether the balance was right is a major subject of historical debate, taken up below.

Confidence began to recover only after a second set of measures. In late December the major governments agreed to speed up the release of their second-line support, and in January 1998 Korea and its foreign creditor banks negotiated the rescheduling of short-term bank debt. An agreement in principle was reached on January 28, 1998; the definitive exchange, signed on March 31, covered approximately 21.8 billion dollars of eligible short-term obligations exchanged for government-guaranteed loans with maturities of one to three years and revised interest terms. This operation removed the immediate threat of mass withdrawals and was crucial to stabilizing the external position. It is sometimes forgotten in popular accounts that emphasize other episodes of the crisis. The won recovered from its December low during 1998, and interest rates fell back over the course of the year.

A private-bank agreement was indispensable because the foreign-creditor problem involved collective action. Each lender preferred to recover its claim immediately if it feared other lenders would withdraw, yet simultaneous repayment demands would exhaust the reserves on which all depended. Negotiations therefore had to assemble a sufficiently broad group of creditors, coordinate maturity extensions, and give assurances that the exchange would be honored. Government guarantees were important to securing acceptance, while IMF monitoring helped demonstrate that the proposed solution was being implemented. The agreement changed the timing of the crisis and prevented a disorderly scramble for scarce dollars; it did not eliminate the underlying banking losses.

Rescheduling did not make debts disappear. It changed their maturity and guarantee structure: creditors received claims with later due dates and promised interest payments, while the Korean state assumed contingent responsibility for the restructured obligations. That bargain bought time for banks and firms to reorganize and for official reserves to recover. It also placed future costs on the public sector if borrowers failed. In the crisis narrative, debt rescheduling therefore belongs alongside, not beneath, IMF financing, interest-rate policy, and domestic banking reform.

Monetary tightening also needs to be judged in sequence. Very high short-term rates could make won-denominated assets more attractive and help stabilize the exchange rate, but the same rates increased financing costs for solvent firms and could turn troubled borrowers into bankrupt ones. Once confidence and the currency stabilized, rates declined and fiscal policy became more supportive of demand. The relevant historical debate concerns the speed and calibration of the initial response, not the inaccurate proposition that high rates persisted unchanged throughout the recovery.

Historical Debate

Did IMF Intervention Produce Necessary Stabilization, Unnecessarily Severe Adjustment, or Both?

The question: Was the program's combination of high interest rates, fiscal restraint, and structural reform an appropriate response to the crisis, or did it intensify the recession and the social costs unnecessarily?

Interpretation A — A necessary and ultimately successful stabilization: Defenders point out that confidence was restored within months, that the external position improved quickly, that the won stabilized, that growth resumed in 1999, and that Korea repaid the IMF early. They argue that the structural reforms addressed real weaknesses and that Korea emerged with a sounder financial system.
Interpretation B — An unnecessarily severe adjustment: Critics argue that early fiscal tightening was inappropriate for a country with a balanced budget, that very high interest rates drove otherwise viable firms to bankruptcy, and that a faster rescheduling of short-term debt could have reduced the severity of the downturn. They also point to the program's optimistic growth projection and to its heavy social costs.
Interpretation C — Mixed assessment: Many scholars hold that the program combined elements that worked, notably the large financing and the debt rescheduling, with elements that were later adjusted. The IMF and the Korean government relaxed fiscal targets and lowered interest rates during 1998, and later IMF reviews acknowledged that some initial assumptions had been too tight.

Evidence: The evidence includes the program documents, the actual path of output, interest rates, and bankruptcies, the speed of recovery, and comparisons with other Asian crisis countries that followed different strategies.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns counterfactuals: what would have happened with different policies cannot be observed directly. It also concerns the weight placed on structural reform relative to short-term output loss.

Why it matters: The debate shaped later international financial policy and Korean attitudes toward the IMF, and it affects how the social costs of 1998 are judged.

Section 84.11 · Part III: Restructuring the state, finance, and conglomerates

Kim Dae-jung, Democratic Government, and Crisis Management

The crisis coincided with a presidential election. On December 18, 1997, two weeks after the IMF agreement, Kim Dae-jung of the National Congress for New Politics narrowly defeated Lee Hoi-chang of the ruling Grand National Party, with the margin under two percentage points. He had built an electoral alliance with Kim Jong-pil of the United Liberal Democrats. The result was the first transfer of the presidency to an opposition candidate in Korea's modern democratic period, an event of political significance in its own right, discussed in the context of democratization in Chapter 82. Kim Dae-jung, long an opposition figure who had survived imprisonment and attempts on his life under earlier governments, now had to manage the country's most serious economic emergency.

Between the election and his inauguration on February 25, 1998 the president-elect worked with the outgoing government on crisis measures and made clear that he would honor the IMF agreement. His government described its approach as pursuing democracy and the market economy together, a formula that treated reform of the chaebol and the financial system as part of democratization. In January 1998 he established a Tripartite Commission bringing together government, labor, and employers, which produced a social agreement in February, discussed in Section 84.15.

The new government faced constraints. It lacked a majority in the National Assembly, which was controlled by the opposition, and had to negotiate legislation. It had to maintain credibility with foreign creditors while managing popular anger about unemployment and about the perceived loss of national autonomy. And it had to impose reforms on powerful business groups without provoking a collapse of employment. The management of the crisis was therefore a political as well as an economic task, which required balancing creditors, chaebol, ministries, and organized labor. The government's engagement with North Korea, an important part of Kim Dae-jung's presidency, is treated separately in Chapter 85.

The transition between administrations mattered for the credibility of the emergency program. International lenders had to consider whether measures negotiated by an outgoing president would survive the election. Kim Dae-jung's public commitment to honor the agreement reduced one source of uncertainty. Within Korea, however, it also made an opposition politician responsible for enforcing decisions that imposed immediate costs on workers and borrowers. Electoral victory gave democratic legitimacy to the new government but not a blank mandate for every measure required by creditors.

Kim's approach depended partly on changing the distribution of political participation. Organized labor, whose influence had expanded after democratization, was invited into talks with employers and the government instead of being treated simply as an obstacle. This institutional inclusion did not dissolve conflict; union confederations disagreed about concessions, and implementation remained contentious. It nonetheless marked a different method of economic governance from the coercive labor relations associated with earlier developmental regimes. The February 1998 social pact demonstrates how the democratic state attempted to negotiate the costs of restructuring.

Kim also relied on the state's inherited administrative capacity. Ministries, regulators, the Bank of Korea, and public financial agencies were essential to executing bank takeovers, creditor negotiations, and corporate restructuring. The state did not disappear as the market was liberalized. Rather, it changed its policy instruments, using supervision, recapitalization, legal restructuring, and negotiation with international institutions in place of much of the earlier system of subsidized directed credit.

Section 84.12

Banking Reform, Insolvency, and Financial Supervision

The crisis left the banking system with enormous volumes of nonperforming loans. The government's response combined closure, merger, recapitalization, and the purchase of bad loans. In December 1997 over a dozen merchant banks were suspended or ordered closed, and later many more. In June 1998 the authorities ordered five commercial banks closed and their assets and liabilities taken over by stronger banks. Others were merged, and two large banks, Korea First Bank and Seoul Bank, were nationalized and later sold, Korea First to an American investment group.

New institutions were created. A unified Financial Supervisory Commission was launched in April 1998, bringing together supervisory functions formerly divided among several bodies, with a supervisory service established subsequently. The Bank of Korea Act was revised to give the central bank greater independence and a focus on price stability. Public bodies, notably the deposit insurance corporation and the asset management corporation, issued government-guaranteed bonds to recapitalize banks, to repay depositors, and to purchase nonperforming loans from financial institutions. The total public funds committed to financial restructuring were very large, well over 100 trillion won, by commonly cited estimates.

This process involved a fundamental transfer: losses that had accumulated in private banks and firms were absorbed in large part by the public sector, through government-guaranteed debt that taxpayers would ultimately service. Shareholders in failed banks lost their capital, depositors were protected, and workers in closed institutions lost their jobs. The reform strengthened supervision and banking, but it also raised questions about fairness: those who had made lending decisions or taken risks were not always those who bore the costs. The reform also changed how banks behaved. Facing market discipline and supervisors' stricter standards, banks turned toward lending to households and to smaller firms, a shift that had longer-term consequences for household debt, discussed in Section 84.20.

The first challenge was to distinguish financial institutions that lacked immediate cash from those whose assets were worth less than their liabilities. Temporary liquidity could be supplied to a fundamentally solvent bank, but persistently insolvent institutions needed closure, fresh capital, or a transfer of their viable business. Korea First Bank and Seoul Bank were taken under public control, while other banks faced plans for recapitalization and mergers. The closure of five commercial banks in June 1998 was executed through transfers of assets and liabilities to stronger institutions, designed to preserve many deposit relationships while removing weak banking licenses.

A second challenge was recognizing and disposing of bad loans. When an impaired loan remained on a bank's balance sheet at an unrealistic value, the bank had little capacity or incentive to make new credit available. Asset-management institutions could purchase or manage problem claims, while deposit-insurance and recapitalization funds supported reorganized banks. These mechanisms converted opaque private losses into identifiable claims and public obligations. Their cost cannot be understood by looking only at the initial expenditure: recovery from disposed assets, guarantees, interest payments, and later sales affect the final public burden.

Regulatory reform involved both institutions and incentives. A more unified supervisory commission could establish comparable capital adequacy tests and intervention rules across institutions; stronger disclosure and risk controls made it harder to hide dangerous exposures. Yet a new regulator cannot instantly create reliable accounting, capable examiners, or an independent lending culture. The change was institutional rather than magical. The Korean financial sector had to learn to evaluate borrowers without assuming that a large industrial name or a political connection would always guarantee repayment.

Section 84.13

Chaebol Restructuring, the Big Deals, and Daewoo's Collapse

In January 1998 President-elect Kim Dae-jung met leaders of the major groups and secured agreement to a set of principles for restructuring: greater transparency, an end to cross-guarantees among affiliates, improved financial structures, concentration on core businesses, and greater accountability of controlling shareholders. The government set a target for the largest groups to reduce their debt-equity ratios to 200 percent by the end of 1999. Banks were expected to push groups toward these targets through creditor-led restructuring and workouts, though the process was marked by tension between the government's role and the formal independence of creditors.

In December 1998 the government and the five largest groups, Hyundai, Samsung, Daewoo, LG, and SK, agreed to a set of exchanges and mergers known as the Big Deals, intended to reduce overcapacity in industries such as semiconductors, petrochemicals, aircraft, rail vehicles, power-generation equipment, and ship engines. Critics objected that these were government-pressured arrangements that contradicted the principle of market-led restructuring, and several of the planned exchanges did not proceed as announced. Some had lasting effects: in the semiconductor sector, Hyundai acquired the chip business of LG, forming what later became a separate major firm. Samsung's ambitious entry into automobiles collapsed and its operations were later sold to a foreign carmaker.

The most dramatic episode was the fall of Daewoo, then one of the largest groups. Under its founder Kim Woo-choong, Daewoo had expanded aggressively overseas, financing its growth with large borrowing, and it continued to expand even as other groups retrenched. In July 1999 the government acknowledged that the group could not meet its obligations, and in August creditors placed Daewoo's main companies in workout. The group's liabilities were enormous, commonly estimated at approximately 89 trillion won in liabilities for twelve principal workout affiliates at end-1999 in a later due-diligence study. It was dismantled, with its affiliates sold, merged, or liquidated. The founder left the country and later faced prosecution. Daewoo's collapse showed that even a leading group could fail, but also revealed the dependence of banks and public funds on the cost of its debts.

The overall outcome was uneven. Of the thirty largest groups of 1997, a substantial number failed or entered creditor control by the early 2000s, including Hanbo, Jinro, Haitai, Kia, Halla, and Daewoo. Others, notably Samsung, Hyundai Motor, LG, and SK, survived and strengthened, reduced their debt ratios substantially, and in some cases expanded their international position. The restructuring thus reshuffled the ranks of the chaebol more than it eliminated them.

Not all restructuring took the same form. Creditors sometimes negotiated voluntary workouts that extended maturities, wrote down claims, or exchanged debt for ownership; in other cases courts or public agencies intervened. The Big Deals attempted to reduce duplication by reallocating business lines among major groups, but arrangements that made industrial sense on paper could run into incompatible ownership interests, creditor claims, and practical difficulties of combining factories or management systems. Some proposed transactions were modified or never delivered the hoped-for efficiency gains. State coordination persisted, but the state could no longer simply command every private group to accept its preferred outcome.

Daewoo demonstrates why a debt number must carry a date and a definition. Its obligations expanded as the group struggled to finance overseas operations and its extensive network of affiliates. A contemporary Korean ministry statement reported borrowings and group-level financial liabilities, while a later due-diligence study presented total liabilities for a narrower set of principal workout affiliates. The different measures are not interchangeable: bank borrowing, bond claims, groupwide obligations, and amounts under creditor workout may cover different institutions and dates. What is historically secure is the enormous scale of the obligations, the collapse of confidence, and the transition to creditor-controlled restructuring. Students should identify the entity coverage and accounting date before comparing any Daewoo totals.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Should Bear the Cost of Restructuring?

Government officials: Officials had to restore creditor confidence under severe time pressure, and they generally argued that reform of banks and corporations was essential to avoid a repetition. They accepted unpopular measures as the price of stabilizing the currency, while seeking to limit the damage to employment and to protect depositors.
Chaebol executives and owning families: Executives of groups that survived stressed the need for flexibility to restructure and cut costs, and many resisted measures that threatened family control. Some argued that the government's Big Deals interfered with business decisions, while others sought state help to manage debts.
Foreign creditors and investors: Creditors wanted repayment, transparency, and reforms that would reduce the risk of future losses. Some had lent without close scrutiny and were reluctant to accept losses, while others bought distressed Korean assets at low prices after the crisis.
Organized workers: Union leaders argued that workers had not caused the crisis and should not bear its costs through dismissals, and they demanded stronger protection and a say in restructuring. Their position was divided between those willing to negotiate in the Tripartite Commission and those who rejected the agreement.
Small-business owners: Small firms, often suppliers to large groups or dependent on bank credit, were exposed to the credit shortage and to the failure of their customers. They had less access to government support than large groups, and many were driven into bankruptcy.
Households: Households experienced the crisis through job loss, reduced wages, falling asset values, and higher interest costs. Many responded with participation in national campaigns, while also questioning why those who had mismanaged firms were not held more responsible.

Section 84.14

Corporate Governance, Foreign Investment, and Ownership Reform

A major strand of reform aimed at changing how firms were governed. The government required listed firms to appoint outside directors, strengthened the rights of minority shareholders by lowering thresholds for shareholder actions, and moved toward international accounting standards, including requirements for combined financial statements for groups. New cross-guarantees among affiliates were prohibited from 1998 and existing ones were required to be eliminated within a few years. These changes were meant to increase transparency and to reduce the capacity of controlling families to direct group finances without scrutiny. They fell short of ending family control, and the effectiveness of outside directors and shareholder rights was uneven in practice.

Foreign investment was opened widely. Ceilings on foreign ownership of listed stocks were raised and then removed, restrictions on foreign acquisition of Korean firms, including hostile takeovers, were relaxed, and new legislation promoted foreign direct investment. Foreign investment inflows grew several-fold from precrisis levels. Foreign investors bought stakes in banks and manufacturers, and the foreign share of the stock market's capitalization rose to roughly a third or more by the early 2000s. Domestic assets were cheap after the depreciation, and some foreign buyers acquired firms at low prices, producing a public debate about the sale of national assets.

Yet increased foreign participation should not be confused with foreign control of the economy. Foreign investors became important shareholders in leading firms, and foreign-owned banks became significant, but the largest groups remained under the control of founding families and domestic management. Foreign ownership brought pressure for shareholder value and transparency, but it also coexisted with continuing domestic dominance in manufacturing and exports. The change was real, but its scope should be described precisely: Korean capitalism became more open to global finance without becoming a foreign-owned economy.

The reform of disclosure had concrete consequences for both companies and investors. Consolidated accounts made it harder for a group to conceal weak subsidiaries behind apparently profitable affiliates. Outside directors and audit committees were intended to introduce review from beyond controlling families. Minority-shareholder protections created channels for challenging transactions that transferred value within a group at the expense of outside owners. These requirements did not automatically end family control, but they changed the information that owners and regulators could demand and the legal risks of opaque transactions.

Foreign capital had distinct functions in different sectors. Portfolio investors could buy minority shares and influence share prices or governance without operating the business. Strategic investors could acquire control of particular banks or distressed firms and introduce capital, technology, or management practices. Foreign banks and private-equity investors also had their own objectives and could be criticized for buying assets at distressed valuations. The historian should distinguish these forms of investment instead of describing all foreign ownership as a single transfer of national economic decision-making.

Reform also changed the relationship between financial markets and corporate strategy. Firms were encouraged to improve profitability, sell peripheral businesses, limit mutual guarantees, and present credible plans to creditors. But short-term shareholder pressure could conflict with continued research investment and employment stability. A successful assessment must weigh measurable improvements in solvency and disclosure against the persistence of concentrated ownership and the social consequences of restructuring.

Section 84.15

Labor Reform, Tripartite Negotiations, and the Social Safety Net

Labor was the most contested element of restructuring. Employers and the IMF program argued that Korea's labor market was rigid and that firms needed freedom to dismiss workers in order to restructure. Existing labor law and court practice made dismissal of regular workers difficult, and the practice of lifetime employment in large firms, though never universal, had been a central expectation of regular employees. Unions, which had grown stronger since democratization, argued that dismissals would shift the burden of the crisis onto workers.

In January 1998 the government created the Tripartite Commission, bringing together representatives of the government, employers, and the two union confederations, the Federation of Korean Trade Unions and the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions, the latter having been a legally unrecognized organization until it was legalized in the following period. On February 6, 1998 the commission concluded a social agreement. Labor accepted legislation allowing dismissals for urgent managerial reasons, along with the legalization of temporary-work agencies, while obtaining expanded unemployment benefits, the legalization of a teachers' union and of union political activity, and commitments on employment protection and welfare. The legislature adopted the labor-law changes soon afterwards.

The agreement was contested within the labor movement. The leadership of the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions, which had agreed to the compact, faced rejection by delegates who considered dismissals a betrayal, and the leadership resigned. The confederation subsequently withdrew from the commission, and strikes and demonstrations took place during 1998, including a major dispute at Hyundai Motor in Ulsan over layoffs. The agreement nonetheless shaped the post-crisis labor regime.

The social safety net was expanded in parallel, though not enough to match the scale of unemployment. Employment insurance, introduced in 1995 for larger firms, was extended in stages to all workplaces, including the smallest, in 1998; public works programs and training were created for the unemployed; and the National Basic Livelihood Security Act, passed in 1999 and implemented in 2000, established an entitlement to minimum livelihood support. These changes marked an expansion of the Korean welfare state, built in the midst of crisis. They did not fully compensate for the loss of employment security, and many unemployed workers did not qualify for benefits or exhausted them.

The tripartite pact brought together policy commitments that affected groups very differently. Employers obtained clearer legal routes to dismiss workers for urgent managerial reasons, while labor representatives sought collective consultation, safeguards against arbitrary dismissals, and a broader public system of protection. The agreement of February 6, 1998, also addressed wider corporate and public-sector reform. It should not be reduced to a simple exchange in which all unions willingly accepted layoffs for benefits. The negotiating organizations possessed different mandates, and parts of the labor movement subsequently challenged the deal.

Employment insurance and public-works provision became more important because earlier protections had assumed comparatively stable formal employment. Workers in small enterprises, family businesses, or irregular jobs often had incomplete coverage or little access to accumulated benefits. An expanded system could reduce hardship without instantly compensating those groups most exposed to restructuring. The crisis accelerated a move toward public social insurance, but the state had to build institutions capable of identifying beneficiaries and financing support during a recession.

Section 84.16 · Part IV: Social consequences, recovery, and remaking capitalism

Mass Unemployment, Layoffs, and the Expansion of Precarious Work

Unemployment, which had averaged under three percent in 1997, rose rapidly in 1998. The annual average for 1998 was about 7 percent, and the monthly rate reached roughly 8.7 percent in February 1999, with well over one and a half million people counted as unemployed. These figures understate the problem, because many people who lost jobs left the labor force or took short-term or informal work and so were not counted as unemployed. The number of employed people fell by more than a million in 1998, and real wages fell significantly, as firms cut overtime, bonuses, and pay as well as jobs.

The experience of job loss varied. Large firms reduced employment through voluntary and early retirement programs, reduced hiring of new graduates, and in some cases dismissals. Small firms, suppliers, and construction businesses, more dependent on credit and on the demand of larger firms, closed in large numbers. Daily-wage and temporary workers were dismissed first, since they had the least legal protection. Surveys and press reports at the time indicated that women, older workers, and young entrants to the labor market were disproportionately affected, though the extent differed by sector and the evidence is uneven.

One of the most enduring consequences was the growth of nonregular employment. Firms seeking flexibility increasingly relied on temporary, part-time, dispatched, and subcontracted workers, who were cheaper and easier to dismiss. The legalization of temporary-agency work in 1998 facilitated this shift. Estimates of the nonregular share of the workforce vary widely depending on definitions, with government and union figures differing markedly, but there is wide agreement that such work expanded and became a lasting feature of the labor market. A dual structure emerged in which workers in large firms retained relatively strong protections while a growing group worked with limited job security, lower pay, and weaker benefits. This division is examined further, in its twenty-first-century form, in Chapter 91.

Official unemployment rates were only one measure of the shock. A worker who accepted an early-retirement package or ceased searching for work might not appear as unemployed, while a household that retained a wage earner could still lose overtime, bonuses, or the contributions of a second worker. Underemployment, discouraged job seeking, and movement into low-paid self-employment complicate a simple before-and-after comparison. The annual unemployment average and the monthly peak also represent different statistics; the rate near nine percent in February 1999 should not be substituted for the annual average.

Regional conditions mattered. When a major automobile or steel producer reduced production, local parts suppliers and shops often suffered even if the headquarters remained open in Seoul. A worker in a diversified metropolitan labor market had different opportunities from someone in a city whose employment depended on one large industrial complex. Family savings, severance payments, access to credit, and kinship support influenced who could search for comparable employment and who had to accept the first available position. These distinctions make the social history of the crisis more precise than a single national unemployment figure.

The longer-run division between regular and nonregular employment did not originate overnight in 1997. Small firms and service occupations had long used insecure work, and labor-market segmentation predated the crisis. What changed was the legal, economic, and managerial environment in which flexibility was pursued. Subcontracting, dispatch arrangements, and fixed-term hiring became more visible strategies for limiting future liabilities. A history of precarity should therefore distinguish an acceleration and institutionalization of existing tendencies from the inaccurate claim that all job insecurity was created by the IMF program.

Section 84.17

Families, Women, Youth, and Household Insecurity

The crisis became a social crisis through the household. In an economy in which the male breadwinner's regular job was the main source of family income and security, job loss or wage cuts could affect a whole household. Private consumption fell sharply in 1998, as families cut spending, delayed purchases, and drew down savings. Households with debts, including mortgages and informal loans, were exposed to higher interest costs, and housing prices fell in 1998 before recovering. Families also depended on informal support from relatives, which could be strained when several members were affected.

Official statistics recorded increases in some indicators of social stress during and after the crisis, including rates of suicide and divorce, and news reports described a rise in homelessness, particularly in the vicinity of major railway stations in Seoul, as well as an increase in children placed in institutional care. Such statistics must be interpreted with caution, since the causes of any single event are complex and since some trends had begun before 1997, but historians generally treat them as evidence of the strain placed on households.

Women's experience was mixed. Many women, particularly those in nonregular or lower-paid jobs, lost employment early, and reports of dismissal practices that treated women as the first to be released led to protest. At the same time, many women entered or returned to paid work to support households, often in nonregular positions, and public-works and training programs offered some employment. Young people faced a labor market in which large firms had cut entry-level hiring; graduates who had expected stable corporate jobs found the path uncertain, and competition for positions in large firms and the civil service intensified. Parents continued, and often increased, spending on education as a hedge against insecurity, which fed the educational competition discussed in Chapter 91. In these ways the crisis altered the assumptions about life paths that Chapter 81 describes for the industrial generation.

Household exposure depended on the interaction of earnings, debt, assets, and family obligations. A household paying rent or servicing loans could become vulnerable as soon as income fell, even if its former wage had been above the national average. Families with savings or several earners had more time to adjust; those dependent on one job or on a small business faced immediate pressure. Spending cuts could affect children's tutoring, transportation, medical care, or support for older relatives. These are plausible pathways documented in aggregate evidence, not a license to invent what any particular family purchased or felt.

The gendered pattern was not simply that men lost formal employment and women took their places. Women were concentrated in several lower-paid and nonregular occupations and could face early dismissal, but some households also relied more heavily on women's earnings or small-scale enterprise after a male wage earner lost his position. The consequence was contradictory: new responsibilities and opportunities for some women, intensified unpaid care and insecurity for others. Youth entering the labor market faced recruitment freezes and greater competition for regular jobs, while older displaced workers encountered age barriers to reemployment. These experiences would shape later debates about welfare and employment, examined in Chapter 91.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Restructuring Reached the Household, and How Historians Know

Historians reconstruct the household experience of 1997–99 from several kinds of evidence, each with limits. Government labor statistics provide aggregate unemployment, wage, and consumption trends but conceal differences among workers and regions. Union and civic-group surveys provide perspectives from affected groups but may reflect the concerns of those who organized them. Contemporary newspapers report local stories and public mood but select dramatic cases. Interviews and oral-history projects, where they exist and are properly sourced, record how people interpreted their experience but may be shaped by later memory. The National Archives of Korea holds photographs and footage of crisis-era events, including the gold-collection drives and public works, which show how the state and media wished the crisis to be seen.

Taken together, this evidence indicates several patterns without requiring invented individuals: families reduced spending and postponed major purchases; some households shifted to informal or self-employed work, including small shops and food businesses; older workers who left firms in early retirement frequently had few alternatives; and public programs offered temporary employment on a limited scale. The effects differed by occupation, region, age, and sex. Because many sources were produced by officials, journalists, and organizations rather than by affected individuals, the voices of the most vulnerable, including nonregular workers, small-business employees, and recent graduates, are underrepresented, and students should treat summaries of "the Korean household" as generalizations.

Section 84.18

Protest, the Gold-Collection Campaign, and National Solidarity

One of the best-known episodes of the crisis was the gold-collection campaign that began in January 1998. Citizens were urged to donate or sell household gold, such as rings, jewelry, and keepsakes, to help the country earn foreign currency and repay its debts. The campaign was promoted by the media, civic groups, and the government, and banks served as collection points. By commonly cited figures, about 3.5 million people participated and roughly 227 tonnes of gold were collected, valued at around two billion US dollars. The gold was sold abroad, adding to foreign-exchange earnings.

The campaign was a powerful symbol of national solidarity, comparable in its rhetoric to earlier campaigns that mobilized Koreans around national goals, and it appealed to a sense of collective sacrifice. Photographs and footage preserved in the archives capture its public character. It should not, however, be presented as the principal mechanism that resolved the crisis. Compared with the tens of billions of dollars at issue, the amount raised was small. The decisive measures were the debt-rescheduling agreement, external financing, exchange-rate adjustment, and reform. The campaign's importance lies in what it reveals about public mood and the moral framing of the crisis, as a national ordeal to be borne by ordinary citizens.

Solidarity coexisted with conflict. Unions staged protests and strikes over dismissals and the Tripartite agreement, and citizens questioned why ordinary households were asked to give up gold while the owners and managers of failed groups had not been held fully accountable. A bitter colloquial use of the abbreviation "IMF" circulated to describe the personal experience of job loss, reflecting how the institution's name became a shorthand for the period. The competing interpretations of sacrifice, who should give, who should bear costs, and who was responsible, remained part of the politics of the following years.

The gold campaign's mechanism is important. Many participants brought rings, necklaces, or household gold to collection points where the metal was valued, purchased or deposited for later payment, gathered for export, and converted into foreign currency. Treating every piece as an outright charitable gift erases the actual transactions and exaggerates the fiscal meaning of the campaign. The resulting exports helped mobilize foreign exchange and demonstrated public solidarity, but their scale was small relative to immediately maturing foreign-bank liabilities and the larger international support package. Its political and commemorative significance therefore exceeded the direct share it contributed to financial stabilization.

Public participation did not imply agreement on the allocation of blame. People could contribute family gold and still resent the perceived privileges of conglomerate owners or the pressure placed on employees. Workers protested dismissals; employers complained about financing shortages; opposition groups questioned the government's policy choices. The crisis generated a vocabulary of national sacrifice, yet the ability to sacrifice differed sharply according to wealth, job security, and access to public assistance. A historically useful treatment juxtaposes campaign imagery with labor records and the chronology of creditor negotiations instead of presenting gold collection as the event that single-handedly rescued the economy.

The campaign also formed part of a longer memory of national mobilization. Appeals to collective recovery resonated with traditions of public fundraising and campaigns linked to national sovereignty. In the democratic conditions of 1998, however, volunteers, unions, the media, and private organizations were not identical with the state. Their participation reveals how national identity could mobilize support for recovery while leaving room for public disagreement about the costs of restructuring.

Section 84.19

Economic Recovery, IMF Repayment, and the Return of Growth

The recession of 1998 was severe. Real GDP contracted by 6.7 percent, the first annual decline in many years, and investment and consumption fell sharply. Yet the external position improved quickly. The depreciated won, together with weak domestic demand that reduced imports, produced a large current-account surplus in 1998, in contrast to the deficits of 1996 and 1997. Foreign reserves recovered, the won stabilized, and interest rates fell. Credit ratings were restored to investment grade during 1999.

Growth returned strongly: on the revised historical series, real GDP rose 10.9 percent in 1999 and 8.8 percent in 2000. Competitive exporters benefited from recovered trade finance and increased demand, including rising international orders for electronics and semiconductors. The restoration of credit markets and renewed investment helped spread recovery beyond immediate exporters, although restructuring continued to eliminate unsuccessful businesses. These rates describe annual changes in economy-wide output, not the recovery of each individual workplace. Their interpretation requires distinguishing faster production from more secure employment, as well as recognizing that growth after a sharp decline begins from a reduced base.

The IMF stand-by arrangement expired in December 2000, and Korea completed repayment of the remaining IMF credit in August 2001, ahead of the scheduled date. In the national memory this repayment became a symbol of recovery and the end of external supervision. But macroeconomic recovery must be distinguished from social recovery. Unemployment fell to about 4 percent by 2000, but employment security did not return in the same form, since many new jobs were nonregular, and many households that had lost savings, housing wealth, or income did not regain their previous position. The recovery was real, but it did not restore the conditions that the industrial generation had come to expect.

The rebound was driven by several distinguishable mechanisms. A cheaper won eventually improved price competitiveness for exporters, while restored international trade finance allowed firms to purchase imported components and deliver orders. The current-account balance swung strongly into surplus, although part of this improvement initially reflected the collapse of imports and domestic demand rather than an export miracle. Foreign reserves were rebuilt and market access gradually returned. These developments helped banks and firms refinance obligations, making recovery self-reinforcing once the immediate foreign-currency shortage eased.

Interpreting the statistical record requires consistency. The selected historical series shows a real-GDP fall of 6.7 percent in 1998, while some earlier IMF releases published a smaller estimate before data were revised. The rebound in subsequent years should be read against that deep initial contraction rather than presented as evidence that the downturn was slight. Investment fell especially sharply during the recession, and the recovery of production and external balances did not eliminate the debt or employment problems of distressed firms. The Daewoo workout during the apparent recovery illustrates why aggregate growth, company solvency, and household welfare must be compared rather than conflated.

The recovery was not experienced uniformly across industries. Competitive exporters and their suppliers could benefit from restored sales, while heavily indebted domestic firms remained dependent on workouts and creditor forbearance. Workers rehired at lower wages or on insecure contracts did not necessarily recover the employment conditions they had lost. Early repayment of IMF credit was symbolically important because it marked the restoration of external financing capacity, but it did not prove that the economic and social settlement had become uncontested.

Section 84.20

After the Crisis: A Transformed South Korean Capitalist Economy

The longer-term consequences can be summarized as a change in the rules rather than in the basic structure. Corporate governance was reformed, with outside directors, minority shareholder rights, consolidated accounts, and a ban on cross-guarantees. The financial system was supervised by a stronger and more unified regulator, with a more independent central bank, and with banks that were more attentive to risk. Foreign capital became a significant presence in finance and in corporate ownership. The labor market became more flexible, with legal provisions for dismissal and agency work and a larger nonregular workforce. A broader welfare system, including employment insurance and minimum livelihood support, became part of the state's role.

At the same time, important continuities remained. The chaebol did not disappear. Surviving groups, including Samsung, Hyundai Motor, LG, and SK, emerged leaner, financially stronger, and in some cases more global, and they continued to dominate exports, investment, and the stock market. Family control persisted through ownership structures and succession, and debates about the concentration of economic power, sometimes described in terms of the dominance of particular groups, continued in the following decades. The state remained an influential actor, though it exercised influence through regulation, supervision, and, at times, informal pressure rather than through the credit allocation of the developmental era.

The reforms also had side effects. As banks turned from corporate to household lending, household debt grew, and a credit-card lending boom in the early 2000s was followed by a crisis in 2003 that required further intervention. Many firms accumulated large cash reserves and invested cautiously, and the expectation that large firms and the civil service offered the only secure careers became stronger. Precautionary savings and educational competition were reinforced. When global finance again became turbulent in 2008, South Korea experienced currency depreciation and capital outflows, and relied on a currency swap arrangement with the United States, showing that the vulnerability to international capital flows had been reduced but not eliminated.

The chapter's central point can now be stated precisely. South Korea emerged from 1997 with its industrial base and democratic institutions intact and with a recovered macroeconomy, but with a changed set of rules for finance, corporate behavior, employment, and economic risk. The costs of the transformation, however, were unevenly distributed: shareholders and some owners lost, taxpayers assumed financial-sector losses, and workers and households, especially those in nonregular work and small businesses, bore much of the adjustment. The institutional recovery and the unequal distribution of its costs are two sides of the same history, and both shaped the society examined in Chapters 87 and 91.

The change in the state's role was therefore neither straightforward withdrawal nor a return to the old developmental system. Government agencies underwrote bank restructuring and managed distressed assets, while regulators placed new demands on firms and financial institutions. Foreign investors gained greater access, but surviving chaebol retained considerable bargaining power and remained responsible for large shares of exports and investment. The historical outcome was a hybrid order: more market-oriented finance within an economy still characterized by concentrated private business groups and an active state.

Later controversies are best treated as consequences that can be traced, not as events to narrate in full here. Household credit expansion and the 2003 credit-card difficulties, the global turbulence of 2008, and later disputes over corporate power illustrate the persistence of risk after formal recovery. Their detailed histories belong elsewhere in the textbook. Within Chapter 84, the central lesson is that the 1997 emergency transformed expectations of what the state, an employer, and a household would guarantee, while leaving unequal capacity to manage risk across the population.

What changed

  • Banking supervision was unified, central-bank independence was strengthened, and the state moved from directing credit to regulating it
  • Foreign investors acquired a substantial presence in banks, stock ownership, and acquisitions
  • Employment became more flexible, with dismissal for managerial reasons, agency work, and a larger nonregular workforce

What remained continuous

  • A small number of family-controlled groups continued to dominate exports and investment
  • The export-oriented industrial base and its skilled workforce remained intact
  • The state remained a major economic actor through regulation and policy
  • Democratic political institutions continued to operate under economic stress

When and how change occurred: Change was concentrated in 1998–2000 but unfolded unevenly: financial reform was fast and state-led, corporate reform was partial and contested, and labor-market change was gradual and cumulative. Who experienced change most directly: Nonregular workers, small-business owners, and entrants to the labor market experienced the greatest change, while shareholders and employees of surviving groups experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: The mixture of institutional reform and persistent concentration helps explain why debates about chaebol power, job security, and inequality continued long after the IMF was repaid.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the 1997 financial crisis exposed structural weaknesses in South Korea's developmental economic model and transformed the relationships among government, banks, conglomerates, workers, and households. Its central question was how an economy celebrated for rapid industrialization became vulnerable to financial collapse. The answer lay in the financing of growth rather than in any loss of productive capacity: heavily indebted chaebol, lightly supervised banks and merchant banks, and large short-term foreign liabilities left the economy exposed to a sudden loss of confidence.

The vulnerabilities developed in a specific context. The developmental state had directed credit and rewarded expansion, creating expectations that major borrowers would be rescued. Democratization and liberalization reduced the state's capacity to discipline business, while capital movements were opened before supervision, disclosure, and corporate governance were strengthened. Cross-guarantees concentrated risk within groups, and expansion into crowded industries produced weak returns.

The crisis unfolded through a sequence of domestic and external events. Hanbo's failure in January 1997 and the troubles of Kia and other groups weakened banks and confidence. The regional crisis beginning in Thailand in July, together with credit-rating downgrades and the caution of Japanese and other foreign lenders, led creditors to withhold short-term loans. Reserves were drained, the won collapsed, and the government sought IMF assistance in November. The crisis was neither purely domestic nor purely a matter of contagion; it arose from the interaction of the two.

The IMF arrangement approved on December 4, 1997, of about 21 billion US dollars, was part of a larger announced package of about 58 billion, and it carried conditions concerning monetary and fiscal policy, financial reform, corporate restructuring, trade, capital markets, and labor flexibility. Historians have debated whether it was a necessary stabilization, an unnecessarily severe adjustment, or a combination. The evidence supports a mixed assessment: the financing and the rescheduling of short-term bank debt restored external stability, while elements of the early policy mix were later relaxed.

Restructuring proceeded through democratic politics. Kim Dae-jung's election in December 1997 and inauguration in February 1998 placed an opposition leader in charge of crisis management. Banks were closed, merged, and recapitalized with public funds, a unified supervisor was created, and the central bank became more independent. The chaebol were pressed to reduce debt and cross-guarantees, a group of Big Deals was negotiated, and Daewoo collapsed. Some groups failed; others emerged stronger, so that the chaebol were reshuffled rather than abolished.

Corporate governance and ownership changed. Outside directors, minority shareholder rights, and consolidated accounting were introduced, and foreign investment was liberalized. Foreign participation increased substantially, but this is distinct from foreign control, since the major groups remained under domestic family management.

The social effects were large and unequal. The Tripartite Commission's agreement of February 1998 combined dismissal rights and agency work with expanded unemployment insurance and welfare, but the agreement divided the labor movement. Unemployment rose to a peak near 9 percent in early 1999, real wages fell, and nonregular employment grew. Households cut consumption, and indicators of social stress rose. The effects varied by status, sex, age, and firm size.

The gold-collection campaign symbolized national solidarity and public participation, but its financial contribution was small relative to the size of the problem, and it coexisted with protest and disputes about who should bear the costs. The recovery that followed was rapid: GDP fell 6.7 percent in 1998 and then grew strongly in 1999 and 2000, supported by exchange-rate adjustment, restructuring, and global demand, and the IMF was repaid in 2001.

The chapter's defining insight is that South Korea emerged with its industrial economy and democracy intact but with altered rules for finance, corporate responsibility, employment security, and risk. Chaebol influence persisted, governance and supervision changed, foreign capital became more important, and labor became more flexible and more divided. Macroeconomic recovery did not mean universal social recovery, a distinction that is essential for understanding the society examined in later chapters.

Key Terms

Asian financial crisis
The 1997–98 sequence of currency and financial crises that began in Thailand and affected several Asian economies, including Indonesia and South Korea.
Chaebol
Family-controlled business groups whose borrowing and expansion were central to Korean industrialization and to the 1997 crisis.

A measure of borrowing relative to owners' capital; the largest groups' average ratios were several hundred percent before the crisis.
Cross-guarantee
An affiliate's guarantee of another affiliate's debt, which spread risk across groups and was prohibited for new borrowing after 1998.
Merchant bank
A financial institution permitted to deal in foreign currency, many of which borrowed short-term abroad and were suspended or closed in 1997–98.
Maturity mismatch
Funding long-term uses with short-term borrowing, creating dependence on creditors' willingness to renew loans.
Liquidity crisis
A shortage of funds to meet obligations falling due, distinct from a condition in which liabilities exceed assets.
Insolvency
A condition in which liabilities exceed assets, requiring restructuring or closure rather than temporary financing.
Foreign-exchange reserves
Foreign assets held by the central bank; usable reserves were far smaller than headline totals in late 1997.
Contagion
The spread of financial distress among countries through investor behavior and financial linkages.
Hanbo Steel
Steel firm whose January 1997 bankruptcy opened a year of corporate failures and a lending scandal.

The IMF facility of about 21 billion US dollars approved on December 4, 1997.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that economic success is not a permanent condition and that the rules governing finance, ownership, and employment are as historically important as the factories they support. South Korea's crisis arose from the same arrangements that had produced its rapid growth: close ties among the state, banks, and large firms, and a willingness to borrow for expansion. The crisis did not erase the achievements of the previous decades, but it revealed their financial foundations and forced a reconsideration of them.

The chapter also reveals how democratic institutions function under stress. A newly consolidated democracy, with an opposition leader taking office in the midst of an emergency, negotiated with international creditors, powerful business groups, and organized labor, and it did so while citizens debated sacrifice and responsibility. That the political system absorbed the shock and continued to operate is a significant fact about the period examined in Chapter 82, and it distinguishes the South Korean experience from those of other countries in which crises produced prolonged political turmoil.

The chapter shaped later developments in several ways. The reforms of banks and firms prepared the ground for the technological expansion discussed in Chapter 87. The growth of nonregular employment, the intensification of educational competition, household debt, and the concentration of security in a few kinds of jobs contributed to the inequalities and demographic pressures examined in Chapter 91. And the memory of 1997, as a national ordeal, remained a reference point in later debates about economic policy and risk.

A final continuity concerns the relationship between aggregate recovery and social experience. The story of 1997 is often told as a story of national resilience, and the evidence supports a story of institutional recovery. But the chapter has also insisted that the costs were unequally distributed and that recovery for the economy was not identical to recovery for the households within it. Holding these two accounts together is one of the central tasks of historical analysis, and it is a habit of mind that applies well beyond this chapter.
