Trade, Travel, and the Maritime World

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 838, a Japanese Buddhist monk named Ennin boarded a ship bound for Tang China as part of an official embassy. When his own mission stalled and later fell apart, it was not Japanese officials or Chinese authorities who rescued his plans to travel and study, but a broad, largely autonomous network of Sillan sailors, interpreters, ship captains, and monastery residents scattered along the Chinese coast, a network within which a Sillan maritime commander named Jang Bogo held a distinctive and, at points, directly documented place. Ennin's diary, one of the most detailed travel accounts to survive from ninth-century East Asia, describes a world that royal chronicles rarely show: a world organized not primarily around courts and capitals, but around ports, ships, monasteries, and the people who moved between them.

The preceding three chapters have examined Silla and Balhae chiefly as states: as governments with kings, aristocracies, administrative systems, military institutions, and religious establishments centered on fixed capitals at Gyeongju and, in Balhae's case, a succession of northern centers. That approach is necessary, but it is also incomplete. Eighth- and ninth-century Korea did not exist only as territory bounded by borders and administered from palaces. It existed simultaneously as a set of nodes within a much larger and more fluid East Asian world, a world held together by sea lanes, seasonal winds, merchant ships, Buddhist pilgrimage routes, diplomatic missions, and communities of Koreans living, working, and worshipping far from the peninsula itself.

This chapter asks students to look at the same period covered in Chapters 14 through 16 from a different vantage point: not from inside the royal capital, but from the deck of a merchant ship crossing the Yellow Sea, from a Sillan monastery on the Shandong coast, from the wharves of a Japanese port receiving Balhae envoys after a dangerous crossing of the East Sea, or from the maritime garrison of Cheonghaejin on the southwestern coast of the peninsula. Viewed this way, Silla and Balhae appear less like sealed containers and more like anchoring points within networks that extended across the Yellow Sea to the Shandong Peninsula and the lower Yangzi coast, across the Korea Strait and the East Sea to Kyushu and the Japanese heartland, and, through Buddhist pilgrimage routes, as far as Central and South Asia.

These networks were built and sustained by identifiable people whose activities left traces in the historical record even though they rarely appear in dynastic chronicles focused on kings and battles. Merchants carried textiles, metals, ceramics, medicines, books, and religious objects between Korean, Chinese, and Japanese ports. Buddhist monks such as the celebrated pilgrim Hyecho traveled thousands of kilometers by sea and overland route in pursuit of religious knowledge, carrying manuscripts, artistic styles, and geographic information back with them. Diplomatic missions from Balhae braved one of the most dangerous stretches of open water in the region to maintain relations with Japan. And expatriate Sillan communities settled along the Chinese coast, particularly around the Shandong Peninsula, where they built monasteries, operated lodging houses, and provided the interpreters, ships, and local knowledge that made further travel possible for Koreans, Chinese, and even Japanese travelers such as Ennin.

At the center of this chapter stands Jang Bogo, a figure whose career illustrates how commercial power, military force, religious patronage, and political influence could become intertwined at sea in ways that had no exact equivalent on land. Jang Bogo spent years in Tang China, rising to military prominence in the Wuning Army, and returned to Silla with firsthand experience of Tang military organization and, through his subsequent exposure to overseas Sillan communities and shipping around the Shandong coast, direct knowledge of the vulnerabilities created by piracy and the slave trade that preyed on Sillan communities and travelers. In 828 he persuaded the Silla court to grant him authority to establish a garrisoned base at Cheonghaejin, centered on the island of Jangdo in the Wando area off Korea's southwestern coast. From this base, Jang Bogo built an organization that protected shipping lanes, suppressed raiding, and positioned him as an indispensable broker in the flow of goods, people, and diplomatic contact between Silla, Tang China, and Japan. His story does not fit comfortably into either a purely commercial or a purely political framework. It shows instead how thoroughly maritime power blended the two.

This chapter also introduces one of the most valuable primary sources available for understanding Korea's place in this network: the diary kept by Ennin himself, who observed Sillan communities, ships, and institutions directly and recorded what he saw in detail rare for this period. His testimony allows historians to move beyond the formal language of tribute missions and royal edicts to see how ordinary and semi-official cooperation actually functioned along the coasts of the Yellow Sea.

None of this displaces the political history developed in earlier chapters. Silla and Balhae remained real states with real governments, and Chapter 18 will show how court politics, aristocratic conflict, and regional rebellion eventually broke Silla apart. But understanding that political history fully requires first understanding the maritime world within which it took place. Kings and aristocrats governed territory, but merchants, monks, sailors, and migrants moved constantly across and beyond that territory's edges, and the wealth, ideas, religious knowledge, and even the political dangers that shaped Silla's final century arrived, in no small part, by sea.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Yellow Sea, Korea Strait, and East Sea should be understood as connective maritime spaces rather than as barriers separating Silla and Balhae from their neighbors.
  2. Describe the practical realities of premodern sea travel in this region, including seasonal winds, coastal versus open-water routes, and the risks travelers faced.
  3. Distinguish government-organized tribute exchange from private and semi-private merchant commerce, and explain how the two interacted.
  4. Analyze the significance of Sillan expatriate communities in Tang China, including their role in supporting travelers, trade, and religious exchange.
  5. Trace the travels of Buddhist pilgrims such as Hyecho and explain what their journeys reveal about the scale of premodern religious networks.
  6. Explain the character of Silla's and Balhae's maritime connections with Japan, including both diplomatic missions and less formal contact.
  7. Describe Jang Bogo's career, including his experience in Tang China, his founding of Cheonghaejin in 828, and the sources of his power.
  8. Evaluate competing interpretations of how extensive and centralized Jang Bogo's control over regional maritime trade actually was.
  9. Use Ennin's travel diary as historical evidence, identifying what it reveals and what limitations it carries as a source.
  10. Explain how goods, people, and ideas moved together along the same maritime routes, rather than treating trade, migration, and religious exchange as separate phenomena.
  11. Assess why studying Silla and Balhae through their maritime connections modifies, without overturning, the state-centered narrative developed in earlier chapters.
  12. Contextualize ninth-century Korean maritime activity within the wider connected world of Tang-era East Asia.

Historical Context

Chapters 14 and 15 examined Unified Silla's government, aristocratic society, and Buddhist cultural achievements. Chapter 16 examined Balhae's founding, territorial expansion, and relations with Tang China. Both chapters necessarily focused on institutions centered on fixed capitals: Gyeongju in Silla's case, and a series of northern capitals in Balhae's case. Both chapters also mentioned, without developing at length, the diplomatic and commercial contacts that connected these states to their neighbors.

This chapter develops those connections fully. By the eighth and ninth centuries, the Tang dynasty's vast territory, wealth, and cosmopolitan cities made it the central hub of an East Asian world that included Silla, Balhae, Japan, and, through Tang's own extensive connections, regions as distant as Central Asia, the Iranian world, and South Asia. Tang capitals and coastal cities hosted merchants, monks, and diplomats from across this world, and Korean travelers formed one significant part of this cosmopolitan mixture. At the same time, the waters separating the Korean peninsula, the Chinese coast, and the Japanese archipelago were neither empty nor impassable. They were used constantly, by government envoys carrying formal tribute and receiving investiture titles, discussed in Chapter 14 for Silla and Chapter 16 for Balhae, and by private merchants, pilgrims, and migrants whose movements official chronicles record only incompletely.

Understanding this maritime world requires combining several different kinds of evidence: Chinese and Korean official histories, which record embassies and formal exchanges; Buddhist monastic records and pilgrim accounts, which describe religious travel; archaeological evidence of ports, ships, and trade goods; and, most valuably for this chapter, the detailed firsthand travel diary of the Japanese monk Ennin, who observed Sillan maritime communities directly during his years in Tang China. No single source provides a complete picture, but together they allow historians to reconstruct a world of movement that existed alongside, and often independently of, the formal diplomatic relationships examined in earlier chapters.

Section 17.1

Korea in a Maritime East Asian World

A map that shows only the land boundaries of Silla and Balhae, with the sea drawn as an empty blue margin, misrepresents how people living along Korea's coasts in the eighth and ninth centuries actually understood their world. For merchants, monks, sailors, and diplomats, the Yellow Sea, the Korea Strait, and the East Sea were not empty spaces at the edge of the map. They were populated, navigable, and economically vital corridors linking the Korean peninsula to the Shandong Peninsula and the lower Yangzi coast of China, to Kyushu and the Japanese heartland, and, through further connections, to a wider world of Buddhist pilgrimage sites stretching toward Central and South Asia.

This chapter asks students to redraw that mental map. Rather than picturing Silla and Balhae as land-bound territories with the sea beginning where the land ends, students should picture a network of ports, sea lanes, and communities in which the peninsula formed one node among several, connected by regular, if often dangerous, movement. Sillan merchants sailed regularly to Tang ports, sustaining commercial relationships across the sea in much the way inland traders sustained them along overland roads. Buddhist monks treated the sea crossing to China as a normal, if hazardous, stage in a much longer religious journey. Balhae envoys crossed the East Sea to Japan as a matter of established diplomatic practice, even though that crossing was among the most dangerous regularly undertaken in the region.

Several features shaped this maritime world. Prevailing winds and ocean currents varied by season, making certain months far safer for particular crossings than others, and ships often waited weeks or months in port for favorable conditions. Coastal geography offered natural harbors, sheltered anchorages, and observable landmarks that guided navigation before reliable long-distance instruments existed. Political geography mattered as well: the presence of friendly ports, expatriate communities, and diplomatic relationships could make an otherwise perilous journey survivable, while their absence could leave travelers stranded or vulnerable.

It is important not to romanticize this connectivity. Sea travel in this period remained slow, uncertain, and frequently dangerous. Ships were lost to storms, ran aground on unfamiliar coasts, or fell prey to pirates and slave-raiders who operated along poorly defended stretches of coastline. The very dangers of maritime travel, examined further in Section 17.8, created the conditions that made a figure like Jang Bogo, who could offer protection and organization at sea, so valuable to merchants, travelers, and eventually to the Silla court itself.

Section 17.2

Ports, Ships, and Maritime Routes

Understanding why particular routes were used, and why particular months were favored for particular crossings, requires attention to the physical realities of premodern sailing rather than treating maritime geography as mere background scenery.

Three broad kinds of voyages structured this maritime world. The first, coastal sailing, hugged the shoreline, allowing ships to make frequent stops, take shelter from sudden storms, and navigate using visible landmarks. Such routes connected Silla's own coastal regions to one another and extended northward along the Chinese coast toward Shandong. The second, open-water crossing, required ships to leave sight of land for extended periods, relying on wind, current, and accumulated navigational experience rather than continuous visual reference. The most direct routes between the southwestern Korean coast and the Shandong Peninsula, and the crossing of the East Sea between Balhae's eastern coast or the Korea Strait and the Japanese archipelago, both required significant open-water sailing. The third, a hybrid pattern common on longer voyages, combined coastal legs with shorter open-water crossings at the narrowest or most favorable points, minimizing time spent out of sight of land.

Seasonal wind patterns, generally described in modern terms as a monsoon system affecting much of maritime East Asia, shaped which months were considered safe for particular crossings. Ships bound from Korea toward China generally sought conditions that would carry them westward and southward without excessive risk of being blown off course into open ocean, while return voyages depended on different seasonal wind patterns. Missions and merchant voyages accordingly clustered in particular months, and official Chinese and Korean records of embassy departures and arrivals reflect this seasonal rhythm, even when the records themselves do not explain the underlying meteorological reasoning.

Ships of this period were constructed primarily of wood, using shipbuilding traditions that combined Korean, Chinese, and, in some cases, adapted foreign techniques, though very little direct archaeological evidence of actual Silla-period seagoing vessels survives, and much of what historians know about ship construction depends on later textual descriptions, comparative evidence from neighboring regions, and cautious archaeological inference. Provisioning a ship for a multi-week voyage required substantial stores of food and fresh water, and successful voyages depended heavily on the accumulated experience of captains and crews who had learned particular routes, hazards, and safe harbors through repeated travel rather than through formal charted knowledge in the modern sense.

Shipwreck remained a constant risk. Storms, navigational error, and unfamiliar coastlines could destroy a vessel and its cargo, and surviving accounts, including portions of Ennin's diary examined later in this chapter, describe travelers delayed for months by unfavorable winds, damaged ships, or the need to wait for a safer season. Understanding this uncertainty is essential to understanding why organizations capable of providing security, local knowledge, and logistical support, such as the network Jang Bogo eventually built around Cheonghaejin, became so valuable to travelers and merchants alike.

Section 17.3

Sillan Merchants and Commercial Networks

Earlier chapters described Silla's diplomatic relationship with Tang China largely in terms of tribute missions, investiture titles, and formal exchange between courts. That relationship was real and important, but it did not exhaust the commercial contact between Silla and its neighbors. Alongside official exchange, private and semi-private merchants carried on a substantial trade that appears only unevenly in official chronicles, more often through archaeological evidence, incidental references in religious and administrative records, and later observers such as Ennin.

Unusually concrete evidence for the actual content of this trade survives in a set of mid-eighth-century Japanese administrative records known as the Buy Silla Goods Documents (Mai Shilla-motsuge), compiled around 752 in connection with a large Sillan trading mission to Japan. These documents list specific commodities offered for purchase, including metalwares, woven textiles and carpets, incense, medicines, dyes and pigments, ink, Buddhist ritual objects, and books. Rather than functioning merely as an inventory of Korean-made export goods, the list is significant because a number of the commodities it records, including certain incense, dyes, and other aromatic and medicinal substances, originated not in Korea itself but in South China, Southeast Asia, India, Arabia, or Persia. This detail demonstrates that Sillan merchants were not simply exporting domestic products but were operating as intermediaries within a much larger continental and maritime trading system, gathering goods from across Asia and redistributing them onward to Japan.

It would therefore be a mistake to imagine this commerce as consisting only of exotic luxury items moving directly from Korean producers to aristocratic consumers abroad. Some goods served everyday and institutional needs: provisioning ships, supplying monasteries, and meeting the practical requirements of communities living far from their place of origin. Other goods, as the 752 documents show, had already passed through multiple hands and multiple regions before reaching Sillan merchants at all. Trade also connected regional Korean products, drawn from agriculture, fishing, and craft production in Silla's own provinces, to distant markets, giving some regional economies a stake in maritime commerce beyond what a purely land-based picture of Silla's economy would suggest.

The boundary between government-organized tribute exchange and private commercial activity was not always sharp. Officially sanctioned embassies often carried substantial quantities of goods intended for exchange or sale as well as items designated as tribute or gifts, and merchants sometimes traveled under the practical protection or cover of official missions. Understanding Silla's commercial world therefore requires resisting a simple division between "diplomacy," treated as political and prestige-driven, and "trade," treated as purely economic. In practice, the two activities frequently traveled on the same ships and depended on the same networks of contacts, interpreters, and local knowledge.

Section 17.4

Sillan Communities in Tang China

One of the most historically significant, and for a long time underappreciated, features of this maritime world was the presence of substantial Sillan expatriate communities along the Chinese coast, particularly in and around the Shandong Peninsula, as well as further south at points such as Chuzhou and Lianshui. These communities took several institutional forms. A Sillabang, meaning a Sillan ward or quarter, was a residential community of Sillan settlers within or attached to a Chinese town. Alongside such wards, other institutions, including Sillan-run hostels sometimes called Sillawon and Sillan monasteries, served overlapping but distinct functions, from lodging travelers to conducting religious life. This textbook uses "Sillabang" for the residential wards specifically and the broader phrase "overseas Sillan communities and institutions" when referring to this fuller range of settlements together. Ennin's diary, examined in Section 17.10, describes Sillan residents at several of these locations engaged in shipping, trade, salt production, and the hospitality that supported travelers, indicating that these were functioning, economically integrated communities rather than temporary encampments of transient travelers.

These communities developed because Shandong's coastal towns sat at a natural convergence point for ships crossing the Yellow Sea from the Korean peninsula, making the region a logical place for merchants, sailors, and eventually settled families to establish themselves. Over time, some of these communities grew large enough to support their own institutions, including monasteries built and maintained by and for Sillan residents, which served both religious and practical functions. A Sillan monastery on the Chinese coast could provide lodging for newly arrived travelers, connect visitors to established merchants and interpreters, offer religious services in a familiar tradition, and serve as a hub of information about shipping schedules, political conditions, and safe routes.

The practical value of these communities to travelers cannot be overstated. A Sillan or, as Ennin's diary demonstrates, even a Japanese traveler arriving at a Chinese port without local connections faced serious obstacles: unfamiliar language, uncertain lodging, no established relationships with local officials or ship captains, and no reliable way to arrange onward travel. Sillan expatriate communities addressed each of these obstacles. They offered lodging and food to new arrivals, provided interpreters fluent in both Korean and Chinese and, in some cases, familiar with Japanese as well, connected travelers with ship captains and merchants who could arrange further passage, and shared accumulated knowledge of routes, dangers, and local conditions built up over years of continuous settlement.

These communities also functioned as economic actors in their own right, not merely as service providers for passing travelers. Sillan merchants based in these coastal settlements conducted business with Chinese counterparts, participated in regional trade networks, and, as Section 17.8 will show, formed part of the broader commercial world with which Jang Bogo's organization at Cheonghaejin was closely connected. The existence of these communities helps explain how a figure based on the Korean peninsula could nonetheless exercise substantial influence over trade and travel occurring hundreds of kilometers away on the Chinese coast: through relationships with merchants and monastic institutions already established within these expatriate settlements.

Historical Vocabulary
Sillabang and Overseas Communities

Sillabang, sometimes rendered in English as Sillan wards or Sillan settlements, refers to expatriate Sillan communities established in Tang Chinese coastal towns, particularly around the Shandong Peninsula, during the eighth and ninth centuries. Related institutions, including Sillan-run hostels and monasteries, served overlapping functions. Together these communities included merchants, sailors, and settled families, and in some cases supported their own religious institutions, including monasteries built and staffed by Sillan residents.

Historical context: Such communities developed as a natural consequence of sustained maritime contact between Silla and Tang China. Once enough Sillan travelers, merchants, and religious figures were passing through or settling in particular coastal towns, it became practical and profitable to establish more permanent institutions serving that population.

Why it matters: These communities provided the practical infrastructure, lodging, interpretation, local contacts, and accumulated knowledge, that made sustained long-distance travel and trade possible. They also demonstrate that Korean historical experience extended well beyond the peninsula's physical borders, a theme this textbook returns to in later centuries when discussing Korean communities in Manchuria, Japan, and beyond.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not imagine these communities as isolated ethnic enclaves cut off from their Chinese surroundings. Evidence, including Ennin's observations, suggests these communities interacted extensively with Chinese officials, merchants, and religious institutions, operating as an integrated, if distinct, part of the coastal Chinese economy and society.

Section 17.5

Buddhist Pilgrims as Travelers

Chapter 15 examined Buddhism's cultural and institutional role within Silla, including monastic scholarship, temple architecture, and royal patronage. This section returns to Buddhism from a different angle: not as a body of doctrine or a set of institutions fixed in place, but as a network that moved constantly across enormous distances, carried by monks whose religious commitments required, or at least strongly encouraged, extensive travel.

Buddhist pilgrimage in this period operated on a genuinely transregional scale. Monks traveled to study with recognized teachers, to visit sites associated with the Buddha's life and with important events in Buddhist history, to obtain manuscripts and texts not yet available in their home regions, and to participate in the broader intellectual and religious life of Buddhist centers across Asia. For a Sillan monk, this often meant a journey that began with a sea crossing to Tang China, continued through Chinese Buddhist centers, and in some cases extended much further along overland and maritime routes connecting East Asia to Central and South Asia.

The Sillan monk Hyecho provides the most striking example available from this period. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that Hyecho was a Sillan monk active in Tang China who, around 723 to 727, undertook an extended journey through India and Central Asia, regions associated with the origins of Buddhism and with important centers of Buddhist learning. His birth and death dates are not securely documented, and neither is the precise route by which he traveled between China and India, whether primarily by sea, overland, or some combination of the two; students should not assume a fully documented Korea-to-China-to-India itinerary. His travel account, which survives only in fragmentary form, nonetheless describes places, distances, and conditions across an extraordinary geographic range, offering historians a rare firsthand Sillan perspective on regions far beyond East Asia. The existence of such a journey demonstrates how far Buddhist pilgrimage networks could extend and how thoroughly a Sillan monk could participate in a genuinely transregional religious world.

Monks like Hyecho did not travel merely to satisfy personal religious devotion in isolation from broader historical consequences. Pilgrims carried manuscripts, ritual objects, artistic models, and geographic and cultural information back to their home regions, contributing to the religious, artistic, and intellectual developments examined in Chapter 15. They also relied on the same practical infrastructure described in Section 17.4: expatriate communities, monastery networks, and merchant contacts that made long-distance travel survivable. In this sense, Buddhist pilgrimage and commercial networks were not separate systems but overlapping uses of the same routes, ports, and communities.

Students should also note that pilgrimage carried real risk and uncertainty, comparable to the risks facing merchants and diplomats. Monks faced shipwreck, illness, banditry, language barriers, and the uncertainty of whether teachers, texts, or resources they sought would actually be available upon arrival. Religious motivation did not exempt travelers from the same physical dangers that shaped all long-distance movement in this period.

Section 17.6

Japan and the Sea Routes East

Silla's relationship with Japan across this period was neither uniformly hostile nor uniformly cooperative, and it operated through several different channels simultaneously: formal diplomatic missions between courts, private merchant activity, religious exchange, and, at various points, periods of tension connected to broader regional politics and to specific incidents at sea, including piracy and disputes over trade.

Official relations between the Silla and Japanese courts fluctuated over the course of the seventh through ninth centuries, shaped by changing political priorities on both sides, memories of earlier conflict connected to the seventh-century wars examined in Chapter 13, and shifting assessments of relative status and precedence in diplomatic protocol. At various points, official embassies traveled in both directions, carrying formal gifts, exchanging correspondence, and observing the ceremonial forms expected of interstate diplomacy in this period.

Beneath this official layer, however, private contact continued in ways that did not depend entirely on the state of court relations. Sillan merchants traded with Japanese counterparts, sometimes through intermediary ports and communities, and Buddhist exchange continued as monks and religious objects moved between Silla, or communities connected to Silla, and Japanese institutions. Japanese sources, including later court records and travel accounts, occasionally reference Sillan ships, sailors, and traders operating along Japan's coasts and in its ports, suggesting a persistent undercurrent of contact that did not always require, or wait for, formal diplomatic sanction.

This pattern illustrates a broader point relevant to the entire chapter: formal diplomatic relations between courts and the actual movement of people, goods, and ideas did not always move in lockstep. Periods of diplomatic coolness between Silla and Japan did not necessarily halt merchant activity or religious contact entirely, just as periods of active diplomacy did not guarantee that private commerce flowed smoothly or safely. Students should therefore be cautious about inferring the full extent of Silla-Japan connection solely from the tone of formal diplomatic exchanges recorded in court chronicles.

Section 17.7

Balhae's Maritime Connections

Chapter 16 established that Balhae maintained sustained diplomatic relations with Japan alongside its relationship with Tang China, sending numerous missions across the East Sea over the course of its history. This section returns to that relationship to examine the physical and logistical realities behind it, rather than its diplomatic titles and protocol, which Chapter 16 has already addressed.

The crossing between Balhae's eastern coastal territory and Japan required navigating one of the most exposed and dangerous stretches of water regularly traveled in this period. Unlike the Yellow Sea crossings available to Silla, which could sometimes follow more coastal or intermediate routes, direct crossings of the East Sea from Balhae's coast toward Japan often required extended open-water sailing with limited opportunity for shelter. Historical records of Balhae missions to Japan include references to ships lost, delayed, or blown off course, underscoring the genuine hazards involved.

Despite these dangers, Balhae maintained this connection consistently over an extended period, suggesting that the diplomatic, economic, and strategic benefits of the relationship were considered to outweigh the risks. Missions carried official correspondence and gifts consistent with the diplomatic patterns examined in Chapter 16, but they also provided opportunities for exchange of goods, including furs, ginseng, and other products associated with Balhae's northern and Manchurian territories, items that were often highly valued in Japan. Japanese court records document the reception of Balhae envoys, including ceremonial protocols, the exchange of goods, and, in some periods, extended negotiations over the frequency and scale of missions, since hosting foreign embassies carried real costs for the receiving court.

The logistics of these missions required careful planning: selecting favorable seasons, provisioning ships for an uncertain and potentially lengthy voyage, and preparing for the possibility of delay or disaster. That Balhae persisted in sending missions across such a hazardous crossing, over multiple generations of rulers, indicates that this maritime connection to Japan was considered a valuable and durable component of Balhae's broader international position, a position that, as Chapter 16 explained, also depended heavily on Balhae's relationship with Tang China and its Manchurian frontier.

Section 17.8

Jang Bogo and the Rise of Cheonghaejin

No individual illustrates the intersection of commerce, military force, and political influence within this maritime world more clearly than Jang Bogo. His career cannot be reduced to any single modern category. He was not simply a merchant, not simply a military officer, and not simply a court political figure, but someone whose power arose precisely from his ability to combine all three roles within the distinctive conditions of maritime East Asia.

Jang Bogo's early life is known only imperfectly, and later accounts, compiled after his death and shaped by the political interests of those who recorded them, should be treated with appropriate caution. What can be said with reasonable confidence, based on the surviving tradition, is that he and a companion, Jeong Nyeon, served as junior officers in the Tang Wuning Army at Xuzhou, an inland military command associated in the sources with cavalry and spear combat rather than with any specifically naval or coastal-defense role. Historical reconstruction also connects Jang Bogo's Tang military service with campaigns against the regional warlord Li Shidao. It would therefore overstate the evidence to claim that his military post itself trained him in naval organization or coastal fortification. What his years in Tang China more defensibly provided was firsthand experience of Tang military organization and, through his subsequent activity around the Shandong coast where many Sillan communities were concentrated, direct exposure to those communities, to regional shipping, and to the persistent problem of piracy and slave-raiding affecting travelers and merchants in the area.

Jang Bogo returned to Silla around the late 820s with this accumulated experience and, according to traditional accounts, was troubled by the vulnerability of Sillan communities and travelers to raiding, including the seizure of Sillan people for sale into slavery, an especially disturbing symptom of the same maritime disorder that made trade routes dangerous more generally. He approached the Silla court with a proposal: grant him authority and resources to establish a fortified base capable of suppressing this raiding and securing regional shipping lanes. In 828, the Silla court granted this request, and Jang Bogo established his garrison at Cheonghaejin, centered on the small island of Jangdo in the Wando area off Silla's southwestern coast.

The location was strategically significant. The Wando area sat along routes connecting Silla's southwestern coast to both the Yellow Sea crossing toward Shandong and coastal routes running further south and east, making Cheonghaejin well positioned to monitor and influence traffic moving in multiple directions. The surviving literary tradition credits Jang Bogo with command of some ten thousand troops at Cheonghaejin, although historians debate whether this figure should be taken literally or understood instead as a conventional way of indicating a large and formidable force, given the limitations of the surviving sources.

Students should resist two tempting but historically inaccurate framings of Jang Bogo's rise. He should not be understood as a modern-style entrepreneur who built a private trading empire through market competition alone, since his authority rested fundamentally on royal sanction and military force as much as on commercial acumen. Nor should he be reduced to a simple folk-heroic image of an uncomplicated "king of the seas" who single-handedly pacified the region, an image that later popular memory has sometimes encouraged but that outruns what the evidence can support. The more historically accurate picture presents Jang Bogo as a figure whose power depended on a specific and unusual convergence of military capability, commercial connections, and political sanction, operating within conditions, maritime insecurity and the profitability of regional trade, that made such a combination unusually effective.

Section 17.9

Cheonghaejin as a Maritime Hub

Once established, Cheonghaejin functioned as considerably more than a simple military outpost. Its activities illustrate how thoroughly military and commercial functions could merge in a maritime context, in ways that had no exact parallel within land-based Silla institutions examined in Chapter 14.

The garrison's most direct function was suppressing piracy and slave-raiding along the shipping routes it could reach, a task that directly served both humanitarian and commercial interests: protecting Sillan communities and travelers from capture and enslavement while simultaneously making the surrounding waters safer, and therefore more attractive, for merchant shipping. This security function gave Jang Bogo substantial leverage. Merchants operating in the region had strong incentives to cooperate with, or at least avoid antagonizing, the organization that determined how safe their voyages would be.

Beyond direct military suppression of raiding, Cheonghaejin appears to have functioned as a hub connecting merchants, ships, and, through relationships with Sillan communities described in Section 17.4, contacts along the Chinese coast. Jang Bogo's organization could provide logistical support, including provisioning, safe harbor, and connections to trusted trading partners, to merchants willing to operate within or alongside his network. This positioned Jang Bogo not merely as a military commander protecting a fixed territory, but as an organizer and broker within a much wider commercial system extending from the southwestern Korean coast to Shandong and, through further connections, toward Japan.

This combination of military and commercial power inevitably carried political implications. A figure who controlled significant maritime force, commanded substantial resources through trade, and possessed direct relationships with communities and networks beyond Silla's formal territorial reach became, almost unavoidably, a political actor whose favor or opposition could matter within Silla's own court politics. Traditional accounts describe Jang Bogo's later involvement in a succession dispute within the Silla royal court, in which his military support reportedly helped determine the outcome, and his subsequent political prominence, including, according to some traditional accounts, a proposed marriage alliance connecting his family to the royal house. Whether every detail of these accounts can be verified with confidence, the broader pattern they describe, that maritime power at Cheonghaejin translated into real influence within Silla's central politics, is consistent with the overall picture developed throughout this section.

This growing political influence, however, ultimately proved dangerous to Jang Bogo himself. According to traditional accounts, resistance among segments of the Silla aristocracy to his rising prominence, possibly connected to the proposed marriage alliance and to broader anxieties about a figure whose power rested outside conventional aristocratic and bureaucratic channels, culminated in his assassination. The exact date of his death is itself a matter of source conflict: Japanese evidence indicates that Jang Bogo had already died by 841, while the Korean chronicle Samguk Sagi places his assassination in 846. A substantial body of modern historical scholarship favors the earlier date of 841, but the textbook presents both because the disagreement illustrates, in miniature, the kind of chronological uncertainty this textbook asks students to recognize rather than smooth over. Cheonghaejin did not disappear immediately upon Jang Bogo's death; the Silla court did not formally abolish the garrison and relocate its population, to a location called Byeokgol-gun, until 851, several years after his assassination under either dating. This episode should be understood here chiefly as evidence of how thoroughly maritime power had become entangled with central political power by this point in Silla's history. The fuller political context of aristocratic conflict and succession disputes into which this episode fits belongs to Chapter 18, which addresses the broader breakdown of late Silla politics in detail.

Section 17.10

Ennin and the Human Network Behind Maritime Exchange

Much of what historians can say with confidence about how this maritime network actually functioned in daily practice depends heavily on a single remarkable source: the travel diary kept by the Japanese Buddhist monk Ennin during his years in Tang China between 838 and 847. Ennin's account is examined in full in this chapter's Primary Source Window, but its significance deserves introduction here as well, since it directly illustrates several of the patterns described in previous sections.

Ennin traveled to China as part of an official Japanese embassy, but when his original travel plans encountered official obstacles, he found himself dependent on precisely the kind of informal, Sillan-organized network described in Section 17.4. His diary describes encounters with Sillan sailors who could provide ships and navigational knowledge, Sillan interpreters who could bridge the language barriers separating him from Chinese officials and local communities, and Sillan monastic communities along the Chinese coast, at locations including Chuzhou, Lianshui, and the Shandong coast, that offered him lodging, support, and connections he could not have obtained through official Japanese channels alone.

Ennin's connection to Jang Bogo specifically is documented rather than merely inferred. His diary records that he stayed at Chishan Fahua Monastery on the Shandong coast, an institution he identifies as founded by Jang Bogo for the benefit of Sillan residents and travelers, and that he received support there from members of the local Sillan community. Ennin also corresponded directly with Jang Bogo by letter, and, at the end of his years in China, he made use of connections associated with Cheonghaejin to arrange his passage back to Japan. This combination of a named monastery, direct correspondence, and practical assistance in arranging return travel provides unusually concrete, independent evidence, from a source with no institutional stake in the Silla court's own political narratives, that Jang Bogo's network was real, active, and recognized by travelers with no prior connection to Silla. This does not mean Ennin's account is free of its own purposes and perspective, a point examined further in the Primary Source Window; it means his testimony is independent and roughly contemporary, which makes it valuable evidence even though it must still be read critically rather than taken as an unfiltered record of fact.

Ennin's diary also illustrates the everyday texture of maritime travel in this period in a way official chronicles rarely capture: the frustration of waiting for favorable winds, the difficulty of navigating Chinese bureaucratic requirements for foreign travelers, the practical necessity of finding trustworthy local guides and interpreters, and the genuine gratitude he expressed toward Sillan individuals who assisted him at critical moments. This human, personal dimension of the historical record is examined further in the Primary Source Window and in the Everyday Life feature later in this chapter.

Section 17.11

Goods, People, and Ideas in Motion

The preceding sections have examined merchants, pilgrims, diplomats, and maritime organizations somewhat separately, but the ships and routes that carried them did not sort their cargo and passengers into neatly separate categories. A single voyage across the Yellow Sea might carry commercial goods, a traveling monk, official correspondence, and ordinary migrants relocating for economic or family reasons, all aboard the same vessel, dependent on the same captain's skill, the same favorable winds, and, potentially, the same protective network organized around Cheonghaejin.

Understanding exchange as a social process, rather than as a simple list of imported and exported commodities, means recognizing that goods traveled embedded within human relationships and institutional structures. A shipment of Buddhist manuscripts did not simply appear in a Silla monastery; it arrived because a specific monk had traveled to obtain it, because ship captains and interpreters had made the journey possible, and because monastic networks on both ends of the voyage had established the relationships of trust that allowed valuable and fragile materials to be entrusted to a long and uncertain sea crossing. Similarly, ceramic techniques, artistic styles, and even architectural ideas did not move as abstract information but traveled with the craftspeople, monks, and merchants who had learned them and carried that knowledge, sometimes literally in the form of sample objects, across the sea.

This chapter's evidence also requires acknowledging that not all movement was voluntary or benign. The same maritime routes that carried merchants, monks, and diplomats also, as Section 17.8 noted, carried captives seized by pirates and slave-raiders, a grim reminder that the same conditions of connectivity and mobility that enabled trade and religious exchange also created opportunities for exploitation and violence. Jang Bogo's suppression of this raiding was not merely a matter of protecting commercial profit but of addressing a real humanitarian crisis affecting Sillan communities and travelers.

Migration, too, formed part of this network's human traffic. Some individuals who appear in the historical record as merchants or monks had, in fact, relocated more permanently, becoming part of the expatriate Sillan communities described in Section 17.4 rather than returning to the peninsula. Others moved temporarily for specific purposes, whether commercial, religious, or diplomatic, and returned home afterward. This range of mobility, from permanent migration to brief diplomatic visits, demonstrates that "Korea's" relationship to this maritime world cannot be reduced to a single pattern of contact but instead included a spectrum of temporary, seasonal, and permanent movement.

Section 17.12

A Connected East Asia

This chapter has examined Silla and Balhae not as sealed territorial units but as anchoring points within a broader East Asian maritime world connected by trade, pilgrimage, diplomacy, and migration. This perspective does not overturn the state-centered narrative developed in Chapters 14 through 16; kings, aristocracies, and formal institutions remained real and important, and the following chapter will show how thoroughly domestic political conflict, rather than external maritime pressure, ultimately destabilized Silla's government. But the maritime perspective developed in this chapter modifies that narrative in an essential way, showing that Silla's and Balhae's histories cannot be fully understood by looking only within their own borders.

Jang Bogo's career offers the clearest illustration of why this matters. His rise depended on Tang military experience, his organization depended on relationships with expatriate Sillan communities along the Chinese coast, and his eventual political influence within Silla depended on resources and networks that existed largely beyond the reach of conventional aristocratic and bureaucratic institutions examined in Chapter 14. A history of Silla that considered only its internal government would struggle to explain how such a figure could emerge, accumulate power, and eventually threaten the stability of the royal court itself. Understanding Jang Bogo requires understanding the maritime network this chapter has described.

The same is true, in different ways, of Balhae's persistent willingness to send missions across the dangerous East Sea crossing to Japan, of Hyecho's extraordinary journey toward Central and South Asia, and of Ennin's dependence on Sillan communities and networks he encountered as a foreign traveler in Tang China. In each case, formal state boundaries mattered less than the actual routes, relationships, and institutions that made movement across those boundaries possible.

Chapter 18 returns to a more state-centered narrative, examining the aristocratic conflict, peasant rebellion, and regional fragmentation that eventually broke Unified Silla apart in the century after Jang Bogo's death. Readers should carry this chapter's maritime perspective forward into that narrative, recognizing that the wealth, ideas, and even some of the political dangers that shaped Silla's final decades arrived, in significant part, along the sea routes and through the networks this chapter has examined.

Historical Vocabulary
Cheonghaejin and Maritime Garrisons

Cheonghaejin refers to the fortified maritime base established on Wando Island in 828 under Jang Bogo's command, combining military garrison functions with organizational control over regional shipping and trade.

Historical context: The base emerged as a direct response to documented problems of piracy and slave-raiding affecting Sillan shipping and coastal communities, and to the broader vulnerability of maritime commerce to disruption without organized protection.

Why it matters: Cheonghaejin illustrates a distinctively maritime form of power, combining military force, commercial organization, and eventually political influence, that had no precise land-based equivalent within Silla's aristocratic and bureaucratic institutions examined in Chapter 14.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid describing Cheonghaejin as simply a "trading company" or "naval base" in a narrowly modern sense. Its functions combined military, commercial, and diplomatic elements in ways that resist easy translation into single modern institutional categories.

Primary Source Window

Ennin's Travel Diary on Sillan Communities in Tang China, 9th Century CE

Creator: Ennin, a Japanese Tendai Buddhist monk · Date: composed during his travels in Tang China between 838 and 847 CE · Type: Personal travel diary recording daily observations, encounters, and difficulties

Context: Ennin traveled to Tang China as part of an official Japanese embassy, intending to study Buddhist teachings at recognized centers of learning. When his official travel plans encountered bureaucratic obstacles, he found himself relying on informal assistance from Sillan communities and individuals along the Chinese coast, whose ships, interpreters, and local knowledge proved essential to his ability to continue traveling and eventually return home.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the diary's relevant content.

The diary describes Ennin's repeated reliance on Sillan sailors and ship captains to arrange passage when official channels proved unavailable, his use of Sillan interpreters to communicate with Chinese officials and local residents, and his stays at monastic and communal institutions maintained by Sillan residents at several coastal locations, including Chuzhou, Lianshui, and the Shandong coast. At Chishan Fahua Monastery on the Shandong coast, an institution he identifies as founded by Jang Bogo for Sillan residents and travelers, Ennin received lodging and support from the local Sillan community. He also exchanged correspondence directly with Jang Bogo and later relied on connections associated with Cheonghaejin to arrange his return voyage to Japan.

Analysis: This source is valuable because it offers an outside, non-Sillan perspective on the practical functioning of Sillan maritime networks, providing independent and roughly contemporary confirmation of details, including the existence and reputation of Jang Bogo's organization, that would otherwise depend solely on later Korean accounts compiled after his death. This does not mean Ennin's account is free of its own purposes and perspective; as a Buddhist monk seeking support for his own religious mission, he had reason to record the connections and institutions that served him well, and his diary should be read as valuable independent testimony rather than as a neutral, disinterested survey of the entire Sillan maritime world.

Limitations: As a personal diary, Ennin's account reflects his own individual experiences and cannot establish the full scale or organization of Jang Bogo's network beyond what Ennin himself directly witnessed. His perspective is also that of an educated Buddhist monk traveling under at least partial official sponsorship, which may have shaped which aspects of Sillan society he encountered and recorded, and his account should not be assumed to represent the experience of all travelers equally.

Perspective: The diary represents the perspective of a foreign religious traveler dependent on local assistance, not the perspective of the Sillan merchants, sailors, or officials themselves, whose own accounts of these same events, had they survived, might emphasize different details or motivations.

Historical Debate

How Much Did Jang Bogo Actually Control?

The question: How extensive and centralized was Jang Bogo's control over maritime trade between Silla, Tang China, and Japan? Was Cheonghaejin the coordinating center of a genuinely unified regional trading and security network, or does the surviving evidence, much of it fragmentary, later, or filtered through sources with their own interests, support only a more modest picture of localized coastal influence?

Interpretation A — Jang Bogo commanded a coordinated regional network: Some historians emphasize the convergence of evidence, including traditional Korean accounts, Chinese records, and Ennin's independent corroboration, to argue that Jang Bogo built a genuinely coordinated network linking Cheonghaejin, Sillan communities in Tang China, and trade routes toward Japan, functioning as something close to a unified maritime commercial and security system under his effective control.
Interpretation B — Evidence supports a more limited and localized picture: Other historians caution that surviving sources, particularly the more dramatic traditional Korean accounts compiled after Jang Bogo's death, may exaggerate the scale and coordination of his influence, whether through later admiration, political usage of his memory, or simple embellishment over time. On this view, Jang Bogo controlled Cheonghaejin and exercised significant regional influence, but the image of him personally dominating all Northeast Asian maritime trade outruns what the fragmentary contemporary evidence, including Ennin's necessarily limited firsthand observations, can actually support.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same limited body of evidence: traditional Korean historical accounts compiled after Jang Bogo's death, incidental references in Chinese sources, and Ennin's diary, which documents specific personal encounters rather than a comprehensive survey of the entire network's scale.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily the scale and degree of centralized coordination attributed to Jang Bogo's organization, rather than whether Cheonghaejin existed or whether Jang Bogo held real regional influence, both of which are well established.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes broader assessments of how much individual maritime figures could achieve within premodern East Asian political and commercial systems, and how much weight to place on traditional accounts compiled well after the events they describe.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 17.8 through 17.10 and the Primary Source Window, evaluate which interpretation the available evidence better supports, and identify what additional evidence, if it existed, could help resolve the disagreement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Maritime Connection over Time

What Changed

  • Korea–China–Japan maritime interaction had already been substantial for centuries before this period; what changed by the eighth and ninth centuries was the emergence of durable, institutionally developed overseas Sillan communities, more visible and better-documented private commerce, and, in Cheonghaejin, a particularly powerful maritime organization combining military and commercial functions
  • The emergence of Cheonghaejin under Jang Bogo introduced a new, more centralized form of maritime organization combining military, commercial, and logistical functions that had not existed in the same form previously
  • The balance between government-controlled tribute exchange and private merchant commerce shifted over time, with private commercial activity becoming increasingly significant and visible in the historical record by the ninth century

What Stayed the Same

  • The fundamental geography of winds, currents, and coastlines continued to shape which routes and seasons were viable for travel throughout this entire period
  • Sea travel remained genuinely dangerous throughout, with shipwreck, piracy, and unpredictable weather posing constant risks regardless of organizational improvements
  • Buddhist pilgrimage, diplomatic exchange, and commercial trade continued to overlap and depend on shared infrastructure, including ports, ships, and expatriate communities, across the entire period examined in this chapter

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real growth in organization and scale, exemplified by Cheonghaejin, and the persistent underlying dangers and geographic constraints, helps students avoid treating this maritime world as either primitive and disconnected or, at the opposite extreme, as safe and fully modernized in its organization.

Cause and Effect

The Rise and Fall of Cheonghaejin

Long-term causes

Sustained growth of Sillan maritime trade and expatriate communities in Tang China created a valuable but poorly protected commercial world vulnerable to piracy and slave-raiding.

Short-term causes

Jang Bogo's military service in the Tang Wuning Army and his subsequent firsthand exposure to Sillan communities and shipping around Shandong gave him both the motivation and practical connections to propose a solution to the Silla court.

Event

The Silla court's grant of authority in 828 allowed Jang Bogo to establish Cheonghaejin at Jangdo in the Wando area, combining military garrison, commercial hub, and logistical network functions.

Effects

Regional shipping became substantially safer, Jang Bogo accumulated significant commercial and military power, and this power eventually translated into political influence within the Silla court, provoking aristocratic resistance that led to his assassination (dated to either 841 or 846, depending on the source) and, in 851, the formal abolition of Cheonghaejin.

Historical complexity: The same qualities that made Jang Bogo an effective solution to the problem of maritime insecurity, independent military force and extensive networks beyond direct court control, also made him a destabilizing and ultimately dangerous figure within Silla's aristocratic political system, a tension that anticipates the broader political fragmentation examined in Chapter 18.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life at Sea and in Port

Behind the diplomatic titles, commercial goods, and religious manuscripts examined throughout this chapter lay the ordinary physical experience of travel that merchants, monks, sailors, and migrants actually endured. A multi-week voyage required careful provisioning of food and fresh water, since running short at sea could prove fatal even without encountering storms or hostile vessels. Sailors and passengers alike faced seasickness, cramped conditions, and the psychological strain of extended open-water crossings with no guarantee of a safe arrival.

Language posed a constant practical challenge for travelers moving among Korean, Chinese, and Japanese communities, making interpreters, often themselves members of expatriate Sillan communities, indispensable rather than merely convenient. Lodging upon arrival depended heavily on existing personal and institutional connections; a traveler without contacts in a Sillan community or monastery might find securing food, shelter, and onward transportation difficult or impossible, a vulnerability Ennin's diary illustrates repeatedly. Port markets brought together goods, languages, and people from across the maritime network described in this chapter, creating cosmopolitan spaces quite different from the more socially stratified and locally rooted world of an inland agricultural village.

Disease also traveled with ships and their passengers, and extended voyages could expose travelers to unfamiliar illnesses upon arrival in distant ports. Storms and shipwreck remained the most dramatic dangers, but the everyday realities of delay, uncertainty, and dependence on the goodwill and competence of others, ship captains, interpreters, and hosts in unfamiliar communities, shaped the daily experience of maritime travel as much as any single catastrophic event. Understanding these everyday realities helps explain why organized networks like those built around Cheonghaejin and the Sillan communities of the Chinese coast were so highly valued by travelers of every kind, from merchants to monks to diplomats.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Silla and Balhae not merely as territorial states governed from fixed capitals, but as anchoring points within a broader and genuinely connected maritime East Asian world. The Yellow Sea, Korea Strait, and East Sea, rather than functioning as barriers separating the Korean peninsula from its neighbors, served as navigable corridors along which merchants, Buddhist pilgrims, diplomatic envoys, and migrants moved with real, if never entirely safe, regularity.

The chapter began by reorienting students' understanding of Korean geography during this period, emphasizing seasonal winds, coastal and open-water sailing patterns, and the practical realities of shipbuilding and navigation that shaped which routes and months travelers actually used. It then turned to the commercial dimension of this maritime world, distinguishing formal tribute exchange from the substantial private merchant activity that moved textiles, metals, ceramics, medicines, books, and religious objects across these routes, while noting that the boundary between official and private exchange was often blurred in practice.

A central focus of the chapter has been the expatriate Sillan communities established along the Chinese coast, particularly around the Shandong Peninsula, which functioned as durable settlements providing lodging, interpretation, local knowledge, and commercial connections to travelers of many backgrounds, including, as the Primary Source Window demonstrated, foreign travelers such as the Japanese monk Ennin. The chapter also examined Buddhist pilgrimage as a form of transregional mobility, using the example of the Sillan monk Hyecho, whose travels extended remarkably far beyond East Asia, to illustrate the geographic scale of premodern religious networks in which Korean monks actively participated.

Turning to Korea's relationship with Japan, the chapter distinguished formal diplomatic exchange, which fluctuated over time, from persistent private merchant and religious contact that did not always depend on the state of official relations. It then examined Balhae's maritime connections to Japan in physical and logistical terms, emphasizing the genuine danger of the East Sea crossing and the sustained commitment Balhae's rulers showed in maintaining this connection across multiple generations.

The chapter's central case study has been Jang Bogo, whose career from Tang military service through the founding of Cheonghaejin in 828 to his eventual political prominence and assassination illustrates how thoroughly commercial, military, and political power could intertwine within this maritime context. Cheonghaejin functioned simultaneously as a garrison suppressing piracy and slave-raiding, a commercial hub connecting merchants and Sillan communities across the Yellow Sea, and, eventually, a base of political influence that reached into the highest levels of the Silla court, provoking the aristocratic resistance that ultimately led to Jang Bogo's downfall.

Ennin's travel diary provided this chapter with an unusually valuable primary source, offering independent, outside corroboration of the Sillan maritime network's reach and reputation while also illustrating the everyday practical realities, delay, dependence on local assistance, and genuine danger, that shaped the lived experience of maritime travel in this period. The Historical Debate feature used this same evidence to explore genuine scholarly disagreement over how extensive and centralized Jang Bogo's actual control over regional trade may have been.

Finally, the chapter emphasized that goods, people, and ideas moved together along the same routes and through the same networks, rather than as separate categories of exchange, and that this same mobility carried real dangers, including the persistent threat of piracy and enslavement that Jang Bogo's organization was specifically created to address. This perspective modifies, without replacing, the state-centered narrative developed in Chapters 14 through 16, showing that Silla's and Balhae's internal political histories cannot be fully understood without accounting for the maritime world in which both states were embedded.

Chapter 18 returns to Silla's internal political history, examining how aristocratic conflict, peasant rebellion, and regional fragmentation eventually broke Unified Silla apart in the decades following Jang Bogo's death. Students should carry forward from this chapter an understanding that the wealth, ideas, and political dangers shaping that breakdown arrived, in significant part, through the maritime networks examined here.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the history of Silla and Balhae cannot be told solely from within their own borders. Kings, aristocracies, and administrative institutions, examined in the preceding three chapters, governed real territory and exercised real authority, but they did so within a world already crossed by merchant ships, pilgrim monks, diplomatic envoys, and migrant communities whose movements connected the Korean peninsula to Tang China, to Japan, and, through Buddhist pilgrimage networks, to regions far beyond East Asia.

Jang Bogo's career stands as this chapter's clearest illustration of why the maritime perspective matters. A figure whose power arose from military experience gained abroad, commercial networks built through expatriate communities, and organizational control over shipping lanes could not have existed, let alone risen to influence within the Silla court itself, without the maritime world this chapter has described. His story also foreshadows a theme central to Chapter 18: that power accumulated outside conventional aristocratic channels, however useful it proved for solving real problems like piracy and slave-raiding, could prove destabilizing to a political system built around inherited status and centralized royal authority.

The evidence examined in this chapter, particularly Ennin's travel diary, reminds students that history is not only recorded in royal chronicles and official diplomatic correspondence. Some of the richest evidence for how societies actually functioned survives in the personal accounts of travelers who depended on ordinary people, sailors, interpreters, and monastic hosts, to survive difficult journeys. Reading such sources carefully, while remaining aware of their limitations, allows historians to reconstruct dimensions of the past that official records alone would leave largely invisible.

As the textbook moves into Chapter 18 and the political fragmentation of late Silla, students should carry forward an understanding that maritime connection was not a peripheral curiosity but an integral part of how Silla and Balhae related to their neighbors, generated wealth, exchanged ideas, and, ultimately, experienced some of the political tensions that would help reshape the Korean peninsula in the century to come.
