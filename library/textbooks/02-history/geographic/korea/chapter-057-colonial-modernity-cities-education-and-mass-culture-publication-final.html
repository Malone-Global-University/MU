Colonial Modernity, Cities, Education, and Mass Culture

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In October 1926, two very different buildings became public in the colonial capital. At the front of Gyeongbokgung, the Government-General headquarters was completed, a massive granite structure whose position hid the old royal palace behind it. A short distance away, at the Danseongsa theater, a Korean-produced silent film called Arirang began its run. One building made colonial authority visible in stone; the other became, in later memory, one of the best-known symbols of Korean cultural feeling under that authority. Both were products of the same city, the same decade, and the same colonial system.

The coincidence is useful because it captures the problem of this chapter. Between 1920 and the mid-1930s, Korean society acquired institutions that most observers would call modern: daily newspapers in Korean, mass-circulation magazines, a university, expanded primary and secondary schooling, regular radio broadcasting, commercial cinema, recorded popular music, department stores, cafés, sports stadiums, and new public arguments about women, children, and youth. Koreans founded, edited, financed, wrote for, performed in, and consumed many of these institutions. Yet every one of them operated under a government that denied Koreans sovereignty, policed what could be printed or screened, and distributed opportunities unequally among Japanese settlers, Korean elites, and the Korean majority.

Historians describe this combination with the term colonial modernity. The phrase does not mean that colonial rule was beneficial, and it does not mean that Korean culture was merely imposed from outside. It names a historical situation in which new technologies, institutions, and social practices spread under conditions of political subordination and economic inequality, and in which Koreans used, adapted, criticized, and sometimes turned those institutions against the regime that regulated them. This chapter keeps three concepts distinct: modernization, meaning changes in technology, institutions, education, infrastructure, and everyday practice; colonial modernity, meaning the particular, unequal forms those changes took under Japanese rule; and agency, meaning the capacity of Koreans to create, adapt, contest, and reinterpret them.

The chapter must therefore resist two inadequate stories. One holds that Japanese colonialism brought only stagnation and cultural destruction, leaving Korean society static or merely victimized. The other holds that railways, schools, and cinemas justify the colonial relationship. The first erases Korean creativity and the real social changes of the period; the second confuses material and institutional change with political legitimacy, and ignores who paid for change, who benefited, and who was excluded. Evidence for the period supports neither simplification.

The evidence is rich but selective. Newspapers, magazines, advertisements, government statistical yearbooks, school registers, film reviews, memoirs, photographs, and literary texts survive in large quantities, but each was produced by someone with a purpose: editors operating under censorship, officials presenting progress, advertisers selling goods, novelists shaping fiction. Many films are lost, radio broadcasts were not recorded, and the voices of rural farmers, domestic servants, and factory women appear mostly through others' descriptions. Throughout, the chapter asks whose experience a source captures and whose it omits.

The chapter is organized by social and cultural process rather than strictly by year. It begins with the policy of cultural rule and the meaning of modernity, then examines the colonial city, education, print culture, performance and mass media, consumer life, and the changing positions of women, youth, and children, before assessing inequality and interpretation. Its principal period is 1920 to 1936, with essential context from 1919 and a transition toward 1937. The detailed history of colonial administration, the 1919 uprising, the colonial economy, political resistance, and wartime assimilation belongs to Chapters 55, 56, 58, 59, and 60 respectively; this chapter takes up those subjects only where needed to explain the conditions of cultural life.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the policy of cultural rule after 1919 changed the conditions for Korean publishing, education, and association, and distinguish those changes from political freedom.
  2. Define modernization, colonial modernity, and agency, and apply the distinctions to specific institutions.
  3. Analyze the colonial city, especially Gyeongseong, as a spatial hierarchy of power, residence, commerce, and public services.
  4. Trace the development of primary, secondary, vocational, and university education from the 1922 ordinance to the mid-1930s, using exact chronology.
  5. Assess how newspapers, magazines, literature, and language standardization created a Korean reading public under censorship.
  6. Describe the institutional development of theater, cinema, recorded music, and radio, and explain the significance of Arirang without overstating what surviving evidence supports.
  7. Interpret department stores, fashion, cafés, advertising, and sports as evidence of commercial modernity and its class, gender, and ethnic limits.
  8. Compare the experiences of the New Woman, working women, children, and youth, avoiding a single account of emancipation.
  9. Evaluate who actually had access to modern cultural goods, using prices, tuition, and audience evidence.
  10. Analyze why cultural activity cannot be sorted neatly into collaborating and resisting categories.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations, including debates over cultural rule, mass culture, and the New Woman.
  12. Connect the cultural landscape of the 1920s and early 1930s to the intensified controls beginning in 1936–1937 without treating the earlier period as merely a prelude to them.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Was cultural rule a genuine loosening of colonial control, a new method of managing Korean society, or both?
  2. What distinguishes modernization from colonial modernity, and why does the distinction matter for judging the period?
  3. How did the colonial city organize space by ethnicity, class, and function, and how did residents live within or across those boundaries?
  4. How did education both open pathways for Koreans and channel them into unequal structures?
  5. How could a Korean-language public sphere grow under licensing, censorship, and surveillance?
  6. What can film, radio, and recorded music reveal about audiences, and what limits does the loss or silence of the evidence impose?
  7. Why does an advertisement show what was marketed rather than what ordinary households could buy?
  8. Who counted as a New Woman, who wrote about her, and whose experience did the label leave out?
  9. How should historians describe Korean cultural activity that was commercially successful, politically constrained, and socially significant all at once?

Historical Context

The cultural world of the 1920s did not begin from nothing. Before annexation in 1910, the late Joseon and Korean Empire periods had already produced modern schools, privately published newspapers, Christian mission institutions, electric trams, and public debate about civilization and enlightenment, as Chapters 49, 53, and 54 explain. The Government-General established in 1910, examined in Chapter 55, then suppressed most Korean-language newspapers and associations, regulated private schools, and governed through a military-police apparatus that left little room for open public life. Economic change in the 1910s, including land surveys, railways, and expanded Japanese commercial presence, laid physical and legal foundations for later urban growth, a subject treated in Chapter 58.

The March First Movement of 1919, examined in Chapter 56, altered the terms of colonial governance. The uprising demonstrated broad popular rejection of Japanese rule and drew international attention, and Japanese authorities responded with repression and, soon afterward, with revisions of policy. The new Governor-General, Admiral Saitō Makoto, announced what became known as cultural rule (bunka seiji). The policy permitted limited Korean-language newspapers, magazines, and associations, expanded some educational provision, and reorganized policing, while preserving Japanese sovereignty and extensive surveillance. This chapter begins with how that framework operated, not with the uprising itself.

Other forces beyond colonial policy shaped the period. Japan was experiencing its own urban growth, mass publishing, and consumer culture, and Korean students, writers, and entrepreneurs moved between Tokyo, Seoul, Shanghai, Manchuria, and the United States. Christian missions ran influential schools and hospitals. Global cinema, recorded music, radio technology, and fashion arrived through Japanese and international channels. The Russian Revolution and international socialist ideas also reached Korean intellectuals, mostly via Japan and China. Korean cultural life in the 1920s was therefore a meeting point of colonial administration, Japanese commercial networks, global cultural flows, and Korean initiative.

Finally, the people who experienced these changes were unevenly distributed. In about 1920, most Koreans lived in villages and worked in agriculture. Urban residents were a minority, and those with regular access to schools, newspapers, theaters, and electricity were a smaller one still. Tracing the spread of modern culture means constantly asking who lived in cities, who could afford tuition and tickets, who could read, and who could not.

Section 57.1

From Military-Police Rule to the Policy of Cultural Rule after 1919

After the March First Movement, Japanese leaders reconsidered the methods of colonial rule. In August 1919 Saitō Makoto became Governor-General, and the administration announced reforms that later writers grouped under the label cultural rule. The most visible changes were organizational. The gendarmerie, a military force that had performed police functions throughout the 1910s, was replaced by a civilian-style police system; the practice of requiring schoolteachers and some civil officials to wear swords was ended; and the Government-General allowed a limited number of Korean-owned newspapers, magazines, and associations to be licensed. The official presentation emphasized harmony, gradual improvement, and respect for Korean customs.

These were real changes in the conditions of public life, but they were not a transfer of political power. Sovereignty stayed with Japan. The ordinary police expanded in personnel, stations, and budget, so that surveillance in many localities became denser rather than lighter even as its uniforms changed. Political offenses were still prosecuted under harsh laws, and the Peace Preservation Law, applied to Korea in 1925, gave authorities a powerful tool against organizations advocating changes to the national polity or private property. Licensing meant that the government decided which publications existed, and censors could delete, confiscate, or suspend. Koreans had no elected legislature with real authority, and the highest offices remained in Japanese hands.

Cultural rule also had a strategic dimension. Officials sought to cultivate cooperative Korean elites, encourage moderate reformist organizations, and divide Korean opinion between those willing to work within the system and those who rejected it. Historians continue to debate how much of the policy was deliberate manipulation and how much was a pragmatic response to unrest, a question taken up in the first Historical Debate below. What is not disputed is that the new framework created a limited legal space in which Korean-language cultural institutions could operate, and that this space was permitted, bounded, and revocable.

This section explains how the framework worked after 1919. The uprising itself, the repression of 1919, and the creation of the Provisional Government belong to Chapter 56, and the apparatus of the early colonial state belongs to Chapter 55. The key analytical point is that the cultural openings of the 1920s depended on colonial permission, and that Koreans used them for purposes the permit-granting authorities did not control.

The distinction between policy reform and policing capacity is the key to reading these years. Several changes were announced together in 1919: replacement of the gendarmerie by an ordinary police force, the end of the sword-bearing requirement for teachers, expanded rules for Korean-language publication, and promises of "equal" treatment under a policy of Japan–Korea harmony. Each was accompanied by a counterweight. The new police system placed officers in far more localities than the old gendarmerie had covered, and a dedicated higher-police (kōtō keisatsu) apparatus watched political and ideological activity. The 1919 Political Offense Ordinance, enacted in the weeks after the uprising, remained available against protest, and in 1925 the Peace Preservation Law was extended to Korea, allowing prosecution for organizing to alter the "national polity" or abolish private property. A reader of the Dong-A Ilbo in the early 1920s could therefore purchase a Korean-language newspaper and know that its editor, its printer, and its readers were all within reach of a police bureaucracy more extensive than before.

Associations show the same pattern. Korean youth associations, educational societies, religious bodies, and local improvement groups multiplied after 1919, and many registered with the authorities. Registration was both permission and surveillance: meetings could be attended by police, programs submitted in advance, and speakers cut off mid-sentence. Historians who have worked in the surviving police reports find that the same organizations that appear in newspapers as civic institutions appear in police files as objects of classification, sorted by perceived political tendency. This archival doubleness is why the Chapter 57 narrative rests on both newspapers and police and censorship records.

Cultural rule also redistributed risk. A newspaper that stayed within the bounds of acceptable criticism could survive for years; one that crossed them might be seized within hours. A magazine editor learned to anticipate the censor, and writers learned to compose with deletion in mind. The policy created a market for self-censorship that operated even where no formal ban existed. For students, the practical consequence is an interpretive rule: the absence of an open challenge to colonial rule in a licensed publication is not evidence of agreement, and the presence of criticism is not evidence of freedom.

Section 57.2

The Meaning of Modernity in Colonial Korea

Koreans of the 1920s debated modernity using several overlapping words. Geundae (近代), the later standard term for modernity, described the contemporary age as distinct from the past. Munmyeong (文明), usually translated civilization, carried the nineteenth-century belief that societies advanced along a path whose markers were schools, railways, hygiene, and constitutional government. Munhwa (文化), culture, became a favored word in the 1920s, partly because the Government-General itself used the phrase cultural rule, and partly because many Korean reformers believed that cultural improvement and education could build national strength where direct political action was blocked.

Two simplifications need to be avoided. Modernity was not simply Westernization, because most new technologies, textbooks, and commercial goods reached Korea through Japanese intermediaries, and Korean students often encountered European and American ideas in Japanese translation. Nor was it simply Japanization, because Koreans also drew on missionary schools, Chinese reform writing, Soviet and international socialist literature, and Hollywood films. Korean intellectuals asked whether electric lights, bobbed hair, and schools were signs of national progress or of dependence, and whether a person could be modern without being a subject of an empire that denied national sovereignty.

This chapter uses three analytical terms. Modernization refers to observable changes in technology, institutions, education, infrastructure, and daily practice. Colonial modernity refers to the form those changes took under unequal political and economic relations: a new school system that restricted opportunity by language and admissions, a railway built to move goods and troops, a newspaper operating under censorship. Agency refers to the capacity of Koreans to create, adapt, contest, and reinterpret institutions, and it applies to editors, filmmakers, students, women, and consumers alike. The three terms are not interchangeable, and using them together prevents both a story of pure victimhood and a story of imposed benefits.

The vocabulary of the age was itself an object of struggle. Writers in Gaebyeok and the daily papers used munhwa (culture) to name a program of national self-strengthening through education, industry, and moral reform, an approach often called the cultural movement (munhwa undong). Critics on the left argued that culturalism evaded the political and economic conditions that made reform impossible, while critics on the right and in religious circles argued that the nation first needed education and character. The same word could therefore describe a government policy (bunka seiji), a Korean nationalist strategy, and a commercial lifestyle. Students reading sources from the period should note which sense a writer intends.

The shift in the meaning of modernity can be traced across two generations. The reformers of the 1890s and 1900s, discussed in Chapters 49 and 53, treated civilization as an external standard to be met in order to preserve independence. After annexation that argument lost its political object, and by the 1920s the question had become how to be modern without sovereignty, and whether doing so strengthened or weakened the nation. The assumption that technology and nationhood advanced together, strong in the Korean Empire period, was now open to doubt, because the most visible technologies were operated by a colonial state.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Colonial Modernity, Cultural Rule, and Urbanization

Definition. Colonial modernity is a scholarly term for the particular forms of modern institutions, technologies, and social practices that developed under colonial rule. Cultural rule (bunka seiji) is the label for the policy shift after 1919 that permitted limited Korean publishing, association, and education within a framework of continued Japanese control. Urbanization is the growth of cities and the share of people living in them.

Historical context. The three ideas overlap. Colonial cities grew as administrative, commercial, and educational centers; cultural rule gave some Koreans legal space to publish and organize in those cities; and the resulting modern institutions bore the marks of unequal colonial arrangements.

Why it matters. The terms let students describe change without endorsing colonial rule and without denying Korean creativity.

Potential misunderstanding. Colonial modernity does not mean that colonialism produced modernity or that Koreans were passive. Cultural rule did not mean political freedom. Urbanization in the 1920s and 1930s involved only a minority of Koreans, and cities such as Gyeongseong were not representative of the whole peninsula.

Section 57.3

Cities, Mass Communication, and the Expansion of Cultural Participation

Modern cultural institutions fed on one another. Urban population growth created concentrations of readers, students, shoppers, and spectators. Rail, tram, and road networks let them gather and let newspapers and goods circulate. Schools produced literate readers, who in turn supported newspapers, magazines, and commercial publishing; newspapers carried advertisements for schools, books, films, and goods; electricity extended the hours of leisure and made cinemas and cafés viable; and radio, recorded music, and film created audiences that overlapped with those of print.

These networks created overlapping publics, but they did not create a single national audience. A reader of the Dong-A Ilbo in Gyeongseong, a cinema patron in Pyongyang, a tenant farmer who attended a village night school, and a Japanese settler who listened to JODK all participated in modern culture, but in different languages, at different prices, and with different possibilities. Newspapers could be read aloud, shared, or posted in public reading rooms, so exposure exceeded circulation. Films reached beyond literate audiences through narrators and images. Radio required receivers costing more than many households could afford. Participation, in short, expanded without becoming universal.

The geography was concentrated. Seoul, Busan, Pyongyang, Incheon, Daegu, and a few other cities held the theaters, schools, newspaper branch offices, and department stores. Smaller towns had fewer facilities and villages fewer still. The following sections trace these institutions one at a time, always asking where they stood and who could use them.

Concrete connections can be traced. A student who left a village school for a provincial higher common school learned Japanese and some Korean, travelled by rail to Gyeongseong for further study, and encountered the daily papers. A newspaper such as the Dong-A Ilbo organized lectures, sporting events, and contests, including, according to the National Institute of Korean History, a national women's tennis tournament that began on June 30, 1923, and later swimming and marathon competitions. Branch offices and local agents distributed copies by rail and post, so that a story printed in Gyeongseong could reach provincial towns the next day. Radio, introduced in 1927, extended the network further but, as Section 57.28 shows, only for those who could pay.

The result was a set of overlapping audiences with different gateways: schools for the young, newspapers for the literate, cinema for the urban crowd, radio for the affluent, and traveling lecturers and night schools for the rest. A single event, such as the 1936 Berlin Olympic marathon, could be experienced in several of these ways at once, but the number of people reached by each channel differed enormously, and no single statistic captures the whole.

Section 57.4

Continuity, Transformation, and the Unequal Geography of Modern Life

The same year could look very different from different positions. A Korean student in a Gyeongseong school might read a Korean daily, attend a film on Saturday, and use a tram. A Japanese colonial official might live in a Western-style residence in the southern city, send children to a Japanese school, and listen to radio news in Japanese. A rural farmer, living many hours from a rail station, might never see a film, keep no clock, and obtain news by word of mouth and occasional visits to a market town. A Korean merchant might read newspapers for business and advertise in them, while a working woman might spend most of her day in a factory, shop, or household, with limited time and money for leisure.

These differences matter for method. Urban photographs, magazines, and official reports are selective evidence. A photograph of a busy Gyeongseong street shows a real street but not the villages that lacked one. A government yearbook of school construction records buildings, not who could afford tuition. A newspaper celebrates a new department store but does not report who could shop there. The chapter therefore treats the highly visible modernity of Gyeongseong as one dimension of colonial Korea, alongside the less visible conditions of smaller towns and countryside.

Continuity is also visible. Village schools of traditional learning (seodang), local markets, shamanic and Buddhist practice, extended-family obligation, and agricultural calendars persisted alongside the new institutions. Even in cities, many residents combined modern and older practices, dressing in Korean clothes, observing lunar festivals, and using traditional medicine alongside new pharmaceuticals. Modernity did not replace older life so much as layer itself over it, unevenly.

The urban–rural contrast is the most important structural fact for evaluating claims about modernization. In the early 1920s the great majority of Koreans lived in villages and worked in agriculture, and most were not enrolled in school, did not subscribe to newspapers, and lived beyond the reach of electricity and tram lines. For many of them the most tangible experiences of colonial economic change were rent, taxes, debt, and, increasingly, migration, subjects treated in Chapter 58. The point is not that villagers were untouched by modern culture; village night schools, itinerant lecturers, market-day news, shared newspapers, and returning migrants all carried it. The point is that its intensity and its costs were distributed very unevenly.

Evidence reflects this inequality. Newspapers and magazines were written in cities and for readers who could pay; government yearbooks counted institutions that existed, not those that did not; photographers favored new buildings. A reader who encounters a dense, optimistic urban record should therefore ask what a parallel village record would show, and which archives, memoirs, oral histories, and surveys might supply it.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Cultural Rule Meaningful Liberalization or a Revised Method of Colonial Control?

The question: Did the policy announced after 1919 meaningfully widen Korean public life, or did it mainly adapt colonial control to a changed political environment?

Interpretation A — Real if limited opening: Some historians stress that the licensing of newspapers, magazines, and associations enabled a genuine expansion of Korean-language public discussion, education, and organization that had been largely impossible in the 1910s, and that Koreans used the space in ways the authorities could not fully direct.
Interpretation B — Revised method of control: Others emphasize that policing, surveillance, and prosecutions expanded, that licensing gave the state leverage over every publication, and that cultural rule aimed to divide Korean opinion and absorb moderate leaders while preserving Japanese sovereignty.
Interpretation C — Both at once: A third approach holds that the two descriptions are not exclusive: the policy was designed as control, but it created conditions whose consequences exceeded its designers' intentions.

Evidence: Evidence includes government announcements, police records, newspaper content and suspension records, the numbers of licensed publications, memoirs of editors and journalists, and prosecutions under security laws.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than basic facts: historians largely agree on what was permitted and what was prohibited, but weigh intent, consequences, and the importance of agency differently.

Why it matters: How the policy is classified shapes how the cultural achievements of the 1920s are understood, either as concessions extracted by Korean pressure, as products of colonial strategy, or as the unintended result of both.

Section 57.5

Gyeongseong/Keijō: The Colonial Capital as a Divided Urban Landscape

After annexation, Seoul was renamed Gyeongseong (Japanese: Keijō) and designated the seat of the Government-General. By colonial municipal counts, its population grew from roughly a quarter of a million around 1920 toward about four hundred thousand by 1930 within the older city limits, and a boundary expansion in 1936 pushed the official total well beyond seven hundred thousand; the figures are approximate and the boundaries changed. Japanese residents formed a large minority, on the order of a quarter of the city by about 1930, a far higher share than in most of the peninsula.

The city developed an informal but powerful spatial hierarchy. The Korean commercial and residential core lay north of the Cheonggyecheon stream, around Jongno, the traditional main avenue, and the older neighborhoods surrounding the royal palaces. The southern districts around Namsan, including Honmachi (today's Chungmuro) and Meiji-machi (today's Myeong-dong), became the Japanese commercial and residential center, with department stores, banks, theaters, and the Shinto shrine on Namsan, and municipal institutions nearby. Namdaemun-ro, the avenue running from the old south gate toward the main railway station, linked the two zones and carried the colonial government's financial and commercial buildings.

Residential boundaries were not absolute. Korean families lived in the south, Japanese in the north, and wealthy Koreans built Western-style or hybrid houses in new districts; shops, schools, and offices drew people across the stream every day. Even so, the distribution of public investment, shops serving different clienteles, and the common description of the city as having a Korean "north village" and a Japanese "south village" made the divide a real feature of urban experience. Students should treat the divide as a tendency and a social perception, not an invisible wall.

The north–south divide had a physical axis. The Cheonggyecheon, a stream running east–west through the old city, separated the northern district (bukchon) of Korean residences and Jongno shops from the southern district (namchon) of Japanese settlement, which had grown from a nineteenth-century settlement near Namsan. Honmachi, the main shopping street of the southern district (modern Chungmuro), housed Japanese-run department stores and banks; Namdaemun-ro led from the old south gate to the new railway station. Jongno, by contrast, remained the center of Korean commerce, with the Jongno Y.M.C.A., theaters such as Danseongsa, many Korean newspaper, magazine, and publishing offices in the northern city, and, from 1931, Hwashin.

The pattern was never absolute. Elite Korean families lived in the south and Japanese officials lived in the north; tradesmen, students, and clerks crossed the stream daily; and the Government-General headquarters, Gyeongbokgung, and the Joseon-era royal precincts in the north placed colonial institutions in the heart of the Korean city. What the geography created was unequal visibility and investment: the southern district was lit, paved, and served earlier, while many northern lanes and the fringe settlements remained poorly served. Historians therefore describe a tendency to segregation and an asymmetric hierarchy, not a wall.

Section 57.6

Urban Planning, Monumental Architecture, and the Reconstruction of the Capital

Colonial authorities reshaped the capital through street widening, zoning, municipal buildings, and the repurposing of royal and imperial sites. Roads were straightened and widened to accommodate trams and automobiles, and new public buildings, including Gyeongseong Station (completed in 1925) and the city hall (completed in 1926), provided monumental institutions in European-derived styles. The Joseon Urban Planning Ordinance, promulgated on June 20, 1934 and effective June 28, provided a comprehensive legal framework for planning urban districts, land readjustment, and restrictions on construction; the city's 1936 expansion belongs to the same broad transformation.

The most consequential building was the Government-General headquarters. Construction had begun in 1916 and the building was completed in October 1926. It stood directly in front of Gyeongbokgung, the principal Joseon palace, on a site formerly occupied by the palace's outer precincts, and its large neo-Renaissance granite mass, with a central dome, dominated the axis of the old royal avenue. Its design is associated with the German architect Georg de Lalande, who died in 1914, with Japanese architects carrying the project forward. The building's scale and location meant that anyone approaching the palace from the south confronted colonial administration first. Gwanghwamun, the palace's main gate, began to be dismantled and relocated eastward in 1926; the physical relocation was completed in September 1927. The earlier proposal to demolish the gate had been modified in the face of objections.

The symbolism was deliberate in the minds of many observers and became a central element of later Korean memory of the colonial period, though the project's justification at the time was administrative need and the age of the former headquarters building. Gyeongbokgung itself suffered extensive demolition and sale of buildings during the colonial period, and its grounds were used for exhibitions, including the 1929 Joseon Exposition. Planning, then, was not only technical but political: it made colonial authority a permanent physical presence at the center of the capital and rewrote the use of sites tied to the former dynasty. The building was removed in the 1990s, a later decision about historical memory beyond this chapter's scope.

Other buildings reinforced the same message. Gyeongseong Station, completed in 1925 and designed in a European-derived style with a central dome, became the arrival point for travelers from Busan, Manchuria, and Japan by ferry-and-rail connection. City Hall (completed 1926), the Bank of Chōsen, the Oriental Development Company, and the Mitsukoshi store (new building opened October 24, 1930) formed a corridor of institutional power in the southern and central districts. Korean-built landmarks existed too: the Jongno Y.M.C.A. and Korean-owned shops, schools, and churches expressed alternative claims on urban space.

The Government-General headquarters deserves close analysis. Work began in 1916 and the building was completed in October 1926; its granite exterior, central tower, and imposing facade made it among the largest government buildings in East Asia at that date, though comparative size claims should be made cautiously. It occupied ground in front of Gyeongbokgung and thus interposed colonial government between the visitor and the palace.

Planning law mattered socially as well as legally. The 1934 ordinance and the 1936 expansion had large social consequences, because land readjustment could displace poor residents while raising the value of land held by others, an issue relevant to the tomak settlements discussed below.

Section 57.7

Housing, Neighborhoods, Water Supply, Sanitation, and Urban Inequality

Modern municipal services arrived unevenly. Piped water, electric lighting, drainage, refuse collection, and public-health inspection expanded over the 1920s and 1930s, and officials publicized them as signs of progress. The distribution of these services followed the city's hierarchy. Japanese districts in the south, and wealthier or more central Korean neighborhoods, were more likely to receive piped water, paved streets, and electric service early; peripheral, low-lying, and poor districts waited longer. Public-health enforcement, including inspections, quarantines during epidemics, and cleanliness regulations, fell on Korean households more heavily than on Japanese ones, and was sometimes resented as intrusive policing.

Housing reflected class. Wealthy Korean families built or bought new hybrid houses, sometimes combining Korean layouts with Western or Japanese features. Many middle-income families rented rooms or small houses. At the bottom, migrants from villages built or rented crowded shelters on the edges of the city, including along stream banks and on hillsides. Contemporary writers called these residents tomak (earth-hut or dugout) dwellers, a term that entered literature and drama, including a 1930s Korean play that took the word as its title. The existence of such neighborhoods shows that the modern capital absorbed migrants faster than it housed them.

Evidence for these conditions comes from municipal reports, journalism, social surveys conducted by both government and Korean organizations, and literature. Each has limits. Official reports record service extensions but rarely who benefited; journalism highlights scandal; literature dramatizes. Together they suggest that the improvement of infrastructure and the persistence of severe inequality were two features of the same urban transformation.

Water and sanitation illustrate the unevenness. Seoul's modern water system dated from the early twentieth century, with a waterworks at Ttukseom completed in 1908, and expanded through the colonial period. Extensions followed the density of paying customers, which meant the commercial center and Japanese residential districts first. Households without private taps relied on public standpipes, wells, and water carriers, and municipal reports of the 1920s and 1930s repeatedly noted the scale of unserved neighborhoods. Epidemic disease, including cholera and typhoid, provoked inspections and quarantines, which affected Korean households more intrusively.

Housing diversified. In the 1920s and 1930s developers built compact urban hanok on subdivided lots in northern districts, a form associated with the small-lot housing built for a growing middle class. Suburban "culture houses" (munhwa jutaek) with Western-style reception rooms signaled middle-class aspiration. At the other end of the scale, migrants constructed makeshift tomak dwellings on stream banks, hillsides, and the city periphery. Contemporary newspapers, municipal surveys, and the 1930s writers who gave the settlements literary prominence all treated them as a visible social problem, though policy responses were limited and planning measures, including land readjustment, sometimes displaced residents without rehousing them. For these communities, "modern city" meant exposure to demolition orders and public-health enforcement without corresponding services.

Section 57.8

Streetcars, Railways, Roads, and Everyday Urban Mobility

Transport reorganized the experience of time and distance. Seoul's electric tram system, begun in 1899 and expanded under Japanese-controlled ownership after 1909, carried commuters and shoppers along major streets, while the railway, with Gyeongseong Station as its hub after 1925, connected the city to Busan, Pyongyang, and Manchuria, and city bus service began in the late 1920s. Automobiles, taxis, and bicycles became more common, though still marks of status or specialized work. Pedestrians in the central districts shared streets with trams, rickshaws, and carts, and traffic accidents became a recurring subject of newspaper reporting.

Travel was class-differentiated. Fares, classes of rail carriage, and the cost of owning a bicycle or paying for a taxi determined who could move freely. Students and clerks could commute from boarding houses; porters and day laborers walked long distances; wealthy families rode in cars. Schedules and timetables, and the need to arrive at school or work at set hours, spread a new sense of clock time, a theme developed in Section 57.32.

The history of railway financing, construction, and the movement of goods and troops belongs to Chapter 58. Here the focus is on how ordinary people experienced new mobility: as an opportunity for study, employment, and leisure, as a source of new risk and expense, and as a visible reminder of whose economy the networks primarily served.

Tram lines ran along the main arteries, including Jongno, Namdaemun-ro, and routes toward Dongdaemun and the Han River district, and by the late 1920s a city bus service supplemented them. Fares were modest per ride but significant for daily commuters on low wages, and rail travel was divided into classes whose price differences mirrored social difference. Rickshaws and carts remained common, and the ownership of bicycles and motorcars marked status. Accidents became a staple of newspaper coverage, and traffic regulation, including rules on speed and right of way, introduced new official intervention in street life.

Mobility reorganized time. Timetables, the rail clock, and the tram schedule taught new habits of punctuality, and the standardization of Korean time to Japanese Standard Time in 1912 meant that city clocks were set by a distant capital. For students, clerks, and shop workers, daily commuting made the city's spatial hierarchy visible: a Korean clerk might live in the north and work in the south, crossing from one world to another every day. The financing and construction of the rail network, and its role in moving goods and troops, belongs to Chapter 58.

Section 57.9

Beyond Seoul: Busan, Pyongyang, Incheon, Daegu, and Provincial Urban Centers

Gyeongseong was the largest city, but other centers developed their own characters. Busan, the principal port facing Japan, had a large Japanese settler community, a rail and ferry link to Shimonoseki, and a commercial life tied to trade and fisheries. Incheon, a treaty port since the nineteenth century, retained a large Japanese and Chinese presence and a role in trade and early industry. Pyongyang was a major center of Korean Protestant Christianity, with mission schools, churches, and a strong Korean commercial and educational elite, and it developed a notable local press and manufacturing. Daegu, an inland commercial hub, was known for textile trade and a regional marketplace. Kaesong, the former Goryeo capital, retained a distinctive merchant tradition associated with ginseng and commerce, and Wonsan, Gwangju, Jeonju, and others had their own schools, markets, and newspapers.

Japanese settler concentrations varied by city, shaping spatial segregation, commercial competition, and schooling. Regional trade connections, including those with Manchuria and Japan, influenced local cultural life. Local newspapers and branch offices of the major dailies extended the reach of print, while theaters and, later, cinemas appeared in larger towns, as did stadiums and schools. Yet urban modernity diffused unevenly, and smaller towns often had a single cinema or none.

Comparing cities is a corrective to Seoul-centered narratives. It shows that modern cultural institutions grew from different local histories, religious networks, and commercial structures, and that colonial urban hierarchy involved a network of centers of differing size and character.

Specific institutions illustrate the differences. In Pyongyang, Protestant missionary schools and churches had been established since the late nineteenth century, including Soongsil, and the city developed a Korean commercial class that supported local newspapers and cultural associations; Christian networks gave the city a distinctive civic life that later chapters revisit, notably around the shrine controversy of the late 1930s. Busan, the ferry gateway to Japan, grew around its port and a large Japanese settler community, and Busan was also the base of the Joseon Kinema company that produced Arirang in 1926. Incheon, the old treaty port, combined shipping, rice milling, and a mixed Japanese, Chinese, and Korean population. Daegu served as a commercial hub for the southeast, with a major traditional market. Kaesong's merchants, linked to ginseng and long-distance trade, maintained an established Korean commercial tradition.

These differences affected culture. Each city had its own mix of schools, churches, newspapers' branch offices, and theaters, and Korean associations often reflected local elites. A history of colonial culture written only from Gyeongseong would miss the roles of Pyongyang's Christian networks and Busan's port and settler economy, and would overstate the capital's representativeness.

Section 57.10

Urban Migration, New Occupations, and the Social Experience of the Modern City

The city offered new kinds of work. Students and aspiring students filled boarding houses and rented rooms. Office clerks, bank employees, teachers, nurses, journalists, and shop workers staffed an expanding service and administrative sector. Domestic servants, including live-in maids and cooks, worked in both Korean and Japanese households. Street vendors, rickshaw pullers, porters, and day laborers made a precarious living, and entertainers and workers in cafés, theaters, and gisaeng houses served expanding leisure markets. Young women worked as telephone operators, shop assistants, and, by the 1930s, bus conductors.

Urban life brought new social forms. Boardinghouses, student dormitories, regional associations (organizations linking migrants from the same home area), neighborhood groups, and religious congregations offered community in unfamiliar surroundings. Writers of the period described both the anonymity of the city and the persistence of ties of kin and place. Tensions arose between the freedom the city permitted, especially to young people, and the expectations of family, village, and community.

Many migrants were not students or clerks but poor villagers pushed from the countryside by tenancy and debt, a story told in Chapter 58, which also systematically treats wages, labor conflict, and migration statistics. Here the focus is on the lived social texture of urban life, remembering that the visible modern city depended on a large population of precarious workers.

The city absorbed many kinds of newcomers. Students came for secondary schools and colleges; clerks, teachers, and bank employees formed a new salaried group; shop assistants, waitresses, telephone operators, and by the 1930s bus conductors held jobs that had not existed a generation earlier; domestic servants, rickshaw pullers, A-frame porters (jigekkun), street vendors, and day laborers filled the informal economy. Regional associations and neighborhood networks helped migrants find work and housing, and boardinghouses were crucial institutions for students and unmarried workers.

Migration was also a social drama. Literature of the 1920s and 1930s depicts the arrival of provincial youths, the loneliness of the boarding room, the compromises of clerical work, and the poverty of the unemployed educated, a type that became a stock figure of 1930s fiction. These portrayals are literary and selective, but they correspond to a real social problem: the school system produced more graduates than the colonial economy employed on equal terms. Detailed statistics on wages, employment, and migration are treated in Chapter 58.

Section 57.11

The Second Chōsen Education Ordinance of 1922

The First Chōsen Education Ordinance of 1911, discussed in Chapter 55, had created a differentiated, restrictive system with short Korean schooling and an emphasis on obedience and basic Japanese-language ability. After 1919, authorities revised the framework. The Second Chōsen Education Ordinance, issued in February 1922 and effective from April, extended the length of Korean common school (botong hakgyo) to six years, lengthened higher common school (go-deung botong hakgyo) to five years for boys and four for girls, and provided a pathway toward normal schools, professional schools, and, eventually, university. It also stated a principle of common education for Koreans and Japanese.

The principle was nominal rather than complete. The ordinance divided schools by the language habitually used by pupils: elementary schools for Japanese-speaking children, and common schools for those who spoke Korean. Curriculum, textbooks, resources, and teacher qualifications differed, and Japanese pupils often had better-funded facilities. Korean children could in principle attend Japanese schools through examination, but competition was intense, and admissions favored Japanese applicants. Korean language remained a subject in common schools but at limited hours, with Japanese as the language of instruction for most subjects. Korean history and geography were not taught as national subjects in the way Koreans wished.

Korean educators and the Korean press welcomed the extended years and wider pathways while criticizing the persistence of unequal treatment. Historians describe the 1922 ordinance as both an extension of opportunity and a retention of institutional inequality, and the Primary Source Window below invites students to examine the text.

The ordinance, issued as Imperial Ordinance No. 19 in February 1922, was the product of a preparatory review begun under Governor-General Saitō in 1921. It lengthened Korean common school from four years to six, higher common school from four years to five for boys, girls' higher common school from three years to four (with some variation), and restored Korean as a required subject in schools, where it had been reduced or dropped under the earlier arrangements. It set the length of normal-school courses, created pathways from common school to higher common school to professional and normal schools, and made legal provision for universities, which became the basis for Gyeongseong Imperial University. The principle announced was that the Korean school system would follow Japan's system "in common" (naisen kyōtsū).

The ordinance's practical structure depended on the distinction between those who "habitually used the national language" (Japanese) and those who did not. The former attended elementary and middle schools on the Japanese model; the latter attended common and higher common schools. This formula avoided naming ethnicity while producing a largely ethnic division, with exceptions for Koreans able to enter Japanese schools by examination, and some Japanese children in Korean-language schools. Resources, teacher pay, facilities, and curricula differed. The ordinance also retained the principle that education should foster loyal imperial subjects and promote Japanese-language competence, which meant that Japanese was the language of instruction even where Korean was required as a subject, and that Korean history and geography were taught within a Japan-centered curriculum.

Korean reactions were mixed and active. Newspapers welcomed the longer course and the revived Korean-language subject while criticizing inequality; families seized the new openings; and educators debated whether to work within the system or build parallel private institutions. The ordinance thus created both opportunities and grievances, and the school strikes and student protests of the 1920s grew out of the gap between promise and practice. The distinction is not between "colonial" and "Korean" education, since Koreans filled many classroom roles, but between a state-directed framework and the uses made of it.

It declares that education in Korea follows the Japanese education system in general, with provisions suited to local conditions. It divides schooling into common, vocational, and professional education. It applies the Japanese elementary and middle school system to those who habitually use Japanese and the common and higher common school system to those who do not. Section 57.12

Primary Education, Enrollment, Language, and Unequal Access

Primary schooling expanded significantly after 1920, driven by Korean demand, Government-General programs, and local fundraising. Enrollment of Korean children grew several-fold between 1919 and the mid-1930s, from a very low base: only a small share of school-age Korean children were enrolled around 1919, and most estimates place the rate well below half until the late 1930s, though figures vary with year and denominator. The Government-General's plans of the late 1920s and 1930s, including a program to provide roughly one school per township (myeon), aimed to widen access, partly in response to Korean pressure and partly to extend Japanese-language reach.

Access remained unequal. Common schools charged tuition and required families to pay for books, clothing, and sometimes local school association fees, and many peasant households could not afford them or needed children's labor. School construction followed administrative priorities and local financial capacity, leaving remote areas underserved. Girls attended at lower rates than boys, reflecting both cost and family expectations. Teachers were increasingly Korean in common schools but supervised by a Japanese-dominated hierarchy, and the teaching of Japanese, a national language in the eyes of the state, was a central purpose of primary education. Korean-language instruction and traditional village academies (seodang) persisted, and many parents who could not or would not use government schools sent children to private night schools or traditional schools, which authorities monitored.

Statistical classifications require care. Official enrollment counts differ from attendance, completion, and literacy; an increase in enrolled pupils does not mean universal access, and categories such as "literate" could mean different things in different surveys. The following evidence comparison illustrates the problem.

The shortage of places was the crucial fact of the 1920s. Common schools were not free or compulsory, and many charged an entrance examination and tuition in addition to costs of books and clothing; the demand for places regularly exceeded supply. Korean communities responded with local school-building movements, raising money for new schools or extending existing ones, and the Government-General in turn announced expansion plans in the late 1920s and 1930s that aimed at roughly one school per township. The effect was a steady increase in enrollment, but statistics from the 1920s and 1930s show that most school-age Korean children were still outside primary school at the start of the 1930s, and attendance climbed above half only late in the decade. Estimates depend on the denominator used, such as all children of school age or only those living in districts with schools, so any single percentage should be cited with its year and definition.

Gender and region affected access. Boys were enrolled in far greater numbers than girls, and urban and southern districts with older schools had higher rates than remote or mountainous areas. The proportion of Korean teachers grew, but they worked under a hierarchy of Japanese principals and inspectors. Language instruction mattered: Japanese occupied the majority of weekly hours in Korean common schools, and Korean was a limited subject. Many students who completed six years aspired to continue; a scholarly survey of 1920s graduate destinations shows, for example, that large shares of graduates of some provincial schools, such as Miryang in 1923, applied for higher common schools, normal schools, and commercial and agricultural schools, and that competitive entrance examinations then filtered them. The school thus aroused aspiration more widely than it could satisfy it.

Alongside the state schools, older village schools (seodang) continued to operate, and authorities periodically regulated them. Their persistence indicates that many families distrusted or could not afford the new system, and reminds us that literacy in Chinese characters and in Hangeul was transmitted outside the official statistics.

What statistics show

Numbers of schools, enrolled pupils, teachers, and sometimes graduates by year and region; trends in school construction and enrollment growth; differences between Japanese and Korean schooling.

What statistics cannot show

Whether children could afford to attend, whether they completed their studies, how well they learned, what language they used at home, who was excluded by tuition or distance, and how families made decisions.

Section 57.13

Secondary Schools, Vocational Education, and the Formation of an Educated Population

Beyond primary school, Korean students entered higher common schools, girls' higher common schools, normal schools for teacher training, commercial and technical schools, and professional colleges. Mission and private institutions, such as Pai Chai, Ewha, Soongsil, Yonhi, and Boseong, formed a distinctive network, some of which gained recognition as professional or college-level institutions in the 1910s and 1920s. Specialized schools offered training in medicine, law, commerce, agriculture, and engineering at limited scale.

Educational credentials created opportunities. Graduates became teachers, clerks, bank employees, journalists, doctors, and merchants, and many of the writers, editors, and organizers discussed in this chapter were products of the system. Yet credentials also channeled Korean graduates into unequal structures. Korean candidates faced competition from Japanese applicants for limited positions, higher wages for Japanese staff in many workplaces, and limits on promotion. Many educated Koreans experienced frustration, as the system produced more graduates than good jobs. Some of that frustration fed into cultural and political activism, while some led to emigration, study in Japan, or work in commerce.

Study in Japan was an important alternative. Thousands of Korean students attended Japanese schools and universities, where they encountered new ideas and Korean student communities, then returned to Korea as writers, teachers, and activists. The educated population that formed in these years was therefore shaped by colonial schools, mission institutions, and study abroad, and it was a small and relatively privileged slice of Korean society.

Secondary and vocational education created the educated Koreans who staffed many of the institutions discussed in this chapter. Government higher common schools in Gyeongseong and provincial capitals, private and mission schools such as Pai Chai, Boseong, Jungang, Yangjeong, and Soongsil, and girls' schools such as Ewha, Sookmyung, and the government girls' higher common schools prepared students for further study or employment. Agricultural, commercial, and industrial schools trained technicians and clerks, though with places limited and with Japanese applicants often favored. Normal schools produced the teachers for the primary expansion. Professional colleges included Yonhi (Chosen Christian College), Ewha, Boseong, Severance medical school, and government professional schools of law, medicine, and commerce.

Credential and employment did not align neatly. Korean graduates often found that equal credentials did not bring equal pay, rank, or promotion, since Japanese employees in government and large firms received additional allowances and filled senior posts. The "unemployed intellectual" became a recognizable social figure in 1920s and 1930s journalism. Some graduates turned to journalism, teaching, private business, social movements, or further study in Japan. Thousands of Koreans studied in Tokyo and other Japanese cities, and returning students such as writers of Gaebyeok and organizers of cultural associations brought back ideas from Japanese universities, the Christian student movement, and socialist circles. Educational credentials thus opened pathways while also generating frustration with the limits of colonial opportunity.

Section 57.14

The Private University Movement and Gyeongseong Imperial University

Korea had no university in 1920. Korean leaders, many associated with educational nationalism, argued that higher education should be provided and controlled by Koreans. In 1922–1923 organizers launched a campaign to establish a Korean private university (Minnip Daehak), forming a promotion committee and then an association, with a fundraising effort supported by the Korean press, notably the Dong-A Ilbo. The campaign sought donations nationwide, including from modest households, and presented the university as a national project.

The campaign faced obstacles. Government permission was difficult to secure, funds raised fell short of targets, regional natural disasters and economic hardship in the mid-1920s reduced giving, and organizers disagreed over strategy and leadership. The Government-General meanwhile announced its own university. Gyeongseong Imperial University was authorized in 1924: a two-year preparatory course (yeka) opened in that year, and the university's first faculties, Law and Literature, and Medicine, opened in 1926. A faculty of science and engineering was not added until the early 1940s. Historians generally treat the establishment of the imperial university as a government response that undercut the private movement, though officials presented it as part of a planned educational expansion.

Access was unequal. Japanese students formed a majority, especially in the law and literature faculty, and Korean students, though present, were a minority, entering through competitive examinations in a system whose preparatory course and admissions favored those schooled in Japanese. Faculty were overwhelmingly Japanese, and research agendas included colonial studies of Korean history, language, and society, scholarship that later generations of Koreans used, criticized, and transformed. The failure of the private campaign left Koreans without a national university of their own until after liberation, though missions and private colleges, including Yonhi and Boseong, continued to offer higher education.

The Korean university campaign deserves detailed attention because it joined fundraising, press mobilization, and a contest over who would control higher education. Preparatory groups formed in late 1922, and the Association for Establishing a Korean Private University (Joseon Minnip Daehak Gisŏnghoe) was launched in March 1923, with leaders from educational, religious, and civic circles, and with the Dong-A Ilbo prominent in publicizing it. The campaign set a large fundraising goal and organized local branches that collected donations from households and associations. It rested on the idea that a people without a university could not train its own scholars, professionals, and teachers.

The campaign encountered several difficulties. The Government-General had to approve any university under the new ordinance and did not grant permission; fundraising was hampered by natural disasters and economic distress in several regions in the mid-1920s; leaders disagreed about strategy; and the Kanto earthquake of September 1923, which killed many Koreans in Japan in massacres of Korean residents, disrupted student and community networks and diverted attention. By 1924–1925 the movement had faded, and it is generally judged to have failed to establish a university.

The government's own university answered the same demand on different terms. Gyeongseong Imperial University was authorized in 1924: its preparatory course (yoka) opened that year and its law-and-literature and medical faculties opened in 1926; a science-and-engineering faculty was added only in the early 1940s. Japanese students were a majority, Korean students a minority admitted through competitive examinations, and the faculty was overwhelmingly Japanese, though some Korean scholars held lower positions or worked in related research. The university also hosted colonial scholarship on Korean history, language, and society, a body of research that later Korean scholars have both used and criticized. Historians generally describe the university as a government response that preempted the private movement, although officials presented it as part of a planned educational expansion, and the question of motive remains one of interpretation.

Section 57.15

Private Education, Mission Schools, Literacy Campaigns, and Rural Enlightenment

Outside the government system, a broad world of private and voluntary education operated. Mission schools, including Protestant and Catholic institutions, educated large numbers of students, particularly girls, and combined general education with religious instruction, though regulations constrained religious teaching in the 1910s and were later partly relaxed. Evening and night schools (yahak) taught working people, including laborers and domestic servants, basic literacy and arithmetic. Local associations, Cheondogyo and Christian organizations, youth groups, and village committees ran reading rooms and classes.

In the early 1930s, the major Korean newspapers organized large literacy and enlightenment campaigns. The Chosun Ilbo ran a character-spreading movement (munja bogeup undong) beginning in 1929, and the Dong-A Ilbo launched a "to the people" campaign modeled on the Russian V Narod movement beginning in 1931. Each summer, students from schools and colleges traveled to villages to teach Hangul, arithmetic, and hygiene, using primers prepared for the campaigns. Similar efforts spread through other organizations. The campaigns linked urban students with rural communities and fostered an ideal of the educated youth serving the nation, and they inspired fiction, including a 1935 novel serialized in the Dong-A Ilbo about a young man and woman teaching in a village.

Police restrictions limited the campaigns. Authorities required permission, monitored the content of teaching, and curtailed activities seen as political or as promoting nationalism, and by the mid-1930s the large campaigns had been narrowed or stopped. The campaigns illustrate how mass literacy connected cities with rural communities, how educated youth understood their social role, and how colonial supervision shaped even voluntary and ostensibly apolitical activity.

Mission and private education had long been major providers. Protestant missions ran schools from primary to college level, and Catholic orders ran schools and orphanages, many of them in Pyongyang, Seoul, and other cities. In the 1910s the Government-General's private school regulations restricted religious instruction and curricula; the 1920s brought some relaxation, but private schools still faced inspection, and the shrine controversies of the late 1930s, treated in Chapter 60, lay ahead. Night schools (yahak) and short courses taught laborers, servants, and apprentices, and lectures and reading rooms operated in towns and villages.

The newspaper campaigns of the early 1930s were the largest volunteer literacy efforts. The Chosun Ilbo launched a "spread the letters" (munja bogeup) campaign in 1929, and the Dong-A Ilbo began its "to the people" (v narod) campaign in 1931, borrowing the Russian populist slogan. Each summer students traveled to villages to run short Hangeul courses, using printed primers, and to give talks on hygiene and agriculture; thousands of students took part over several years, and the campaigns produced fiction and reportage in the press, including the 1935 Dong-A Ilbo serial novel on the theme by Sim Hun. Police permission was required, and authorities restricted the content and curtailed the campaigns, reflecting concern about political mobilization. The campaigns show that the rural world was not outside the new communication networks, but they also show that mass literacy was a project of Korean civic organizations pursued under surveillance.

Section 57.16

Student Life, Reading Circles, Educational Aspirations, and Generational Change

Students were the cultural cutting edge of the 1920s. In schools and colleges they published magazines, formed debating societies and drama groups, organized sports clubs, and gathered in reading circles (dokseohoe) to discuss books and newspapers. Many read widely in translation, from Western literature and social theory to Japanese and Russian writing. Student associations provided networks, and school years created generational cohorts who recognized themselves as a distinct group with distinctive aspirations, distinct from parents who had grown up before annexation.

Education also shaped identity. Students experienced the contrast between the ideals expressed in lessons and the inequalities of daily life, and some channeled their reaction into cultural activity, social criticism, or political organization. Authorities watched reading circles and student associations closely, and arrests under the Peace Preservation Law targeted groups reading banned literature.

A major expression of student discontent came in the Gwangju student protests that began in November 1929, following a conflict between Korean and Japanese students and perceived unequal treatment by authorities, and that spread to schools across the peninsula in the following months. This section notes only the protests' social significance as evidence of educational grievance and student solidarity; the detailed history of student activism and anti-colonial organizing belongs to Chapter 59.

Student culture was dense and politically sensitive. Schools and colleges had student associations, drama clubs, athletic teams, alumni groups, and magazines, and the daily papers carried reports of student speech contests and debates. Reading circles met in boarding rooms and school clubs to read books in translation, and the authorities treated unregistered reading circles as potential vehicles for banned ideas, so that arrests of students for reading socialist literature became a recurring story in the 1920s and 1930s. Strikes by students in school (dongmaeng hyuhak) over teachers, curricula, and discriminatory treatment occurred repeatedly in the 1920s.

The Gwangju incident shows how everyday school life could become a political event. It began on October 30, 1929, when a conflict between Korean and Japanese students on a train at Naju station, involving harassment of Korean girls, escalated; on November 3 a larger clash occurred in Gwangju, and, after what many Koreans saw as one-sided treatment by police and school authorities, protests spread to schools across the peninsula in late 1929 and early 1930. Students linked the incident to a broader set of grievances about education and discrimination. The significance for this chapter is that it grew out of the institutions described here: trains that brought students from different communities into contact, schools whose students shared reading and organization, and a press that reported on events despite restrictions. Its political history belongs to Chapter 59.

Section 57.17

The Return of Korean-Owned Newspapers in 1920

After annexation, the Korean-owned press had been largely closed; the main Korean-language daily, the Maeil Sinbo, was the Government-General's own organ. In 1920, authorities licensed several Korean-language newspapers as part of cultural rule. The Chosun Ilbo began publication on March 5, 1920, and the Dong-A Ilbo on April 1, 1920, and a third, the Sisa Sinmun, was associated with pro-Japanese interests. The licensing is generally understood as including an element of balancing different tendencies in the Korean public.

The Dong-A Ilbo was founded by Kim Seong-su and a group of investors and writers, with a statement of purpose emphasizing representation of the Korean people, democracy, and cultural advancement. The Chosun Ilbo drew on a different group of backers and changed ownership and management several times, including reorganizations in the mid-1920s and early 1930s. Both papers were commercial enterprises that depended on subscriptions, advertising, and distribution networks, and both employed a generation of Korean journalists, editors, and writers who worked in a precarious relationship with the authorities.

The papers played several roles at once. They reported news, including international news, carried editorials that tested the limits of permitted criticism, serialized fiction, promoted educational and social campaigns, covered sports, and provided advertising space for schools, books, medicines, and consumer goods. Circulation reached tens of thousands, and readership was much larger because newspapers were passed among households and read in groups. Their reappearance was a result of cultural rule, not the establishment of unrestricted press freedom.

The first months of the papers reveal how limited the new space was. The Dong-A Ilbo opened with Park Yeong-hyo as president, Yu Geun and Yang Gi-tak as editorial supervisors, Jang Deok-su as weekly editor, and Yi Sang-hyeop as editor-in-chief and publisher of record; the Government-General had licensed a joint petition of 78 persons in February 1920. Its first article seized by censors, according to a National Institute of Korean History survey, appeared on April 14, 1920, in the thirteenth issue. Kim Seong-su, the principal organizer, became president in July 1920, and in September 1921 the firm was reorganized as a joint-stock company with Song Jin-u as president, supplying capital through subscribers across the country.

The papers differed in tone and in ownership. The Chosun Ilbo began on March 5, 1920, under a group of businessmen, and underwent management reorganizations through the 1920s and 1930s. The Dong-A Ilbo drew on landowner and educator networks associated with Kim Seong-su and, in the mid-1920s, recruited writers who had worked on the Shanghai Independence Newspaper, including Yi Gwang-su (1923), Jo Dong-ho (1924), and Ju Yo-han (1925). A third paper licensed in 1920, Sisa Sinmun, was associated with pro-Japanese interests. The Government-General's own Maeil Sinbo, the only Korean-language daily to survive from before 1919, remained the semi-official voice.

The papers sold by subscription, street sale, and advertising. Advertising revenue tied them to consumer markets and to firms that advertised in both Korean and Japanese papers, and the papers organized events, lectures, and sports tournaments to broaden their audience. This commercial base meant that survival depended on advertisers and distribution as well as on principle. Later controversy over the papers' wartime conduct, which belongs to Chapter 60 and remains a subject of public argument in Korea, should not obscure how they functioned in the 1920s.

Section 57.18

Colonial Censorship, Newspaper Suspensions, and the Boundaries of Public Debate

Licensing came with a regime of supervision. Newspapers had to submit copies to police censors, who could order deletions, confiscate issues, issue warnings, and suspend publication, and who also monitored journalists. Deleted passages were sometimes visible as blank spaces or marks in the printed paper, and readers learned to notice what had been removed. Frequent confiscations imposed financial costs on the papers, which lost sales and advertising, and suspensions could last weeks or months.

Journalists developed strategies. They wrote in indirect language, used cultural and historical subjects to discuss national themes, reported foreign events whose parallels were clear to readers, and used serialized fiction to explore social problems. Editorials criticized specific policies and defended Korean rights while avoiding categorical challenges to Japanese rule. Papers also organized campaigns around education, language, and economic self-reliance, framing national concern in forms the authorities tolerated or could not easily prohibit.

These practices show both the possibilities and the constraints of the colonial press. Debate was lively, and papers contributed to national consciousness and civic organization, but it operated within a narrow band set by censors, and editors and journalists faced real risk of arrest and ruin. The Dong-A Ilbo's suspension in 1936, examined below, is a well-documented example, and the forced closure of both major Korean dailies in 1940 belongs to Chapter 60.

The papers were seized and suspended often. Seizures, in which that day's issue was confiscated before or after printing, cost the paper revenue and could be triggered by an article, an editorial, or a photograph. Suspensions, imposed by administrative order, halted publication for periods ranging from days to months. The Dong-A Ilbo was suspended four times before its forced closure in 1940, according to a National Institute of Korean History summary, beginning in 1920, and the Chosun Ilbo was likewise suspended in its first year. Journalists could also be prosecuted in court, and editors were arrested.

Newspapers adapted to this environment. They used metaphor and historical analogy, relied on foreign news for implicit comparison, ran series on Korean history and customs, and relied on fiction to explore social conflict. Editorials in the mid-1920s on subjects such as education, economic self-help, and the "native products" movement fit within the culture of the cultural movement. Sports and cultural events organized by the papers provided public occasions for national feeling. The 1936 episode examined below is notable because it concerned a photograph rather than a written argument, showing that symbolism, as well as text, was policed.

Section 57.19

The Magazine Explosion: Gaebyeok, Women's Journals, and General-Interest Publishing

Alongside newspapers, magazines flourished. Gaebyeok (Kaebyŏk, "Creation of the World"), launched in June 1920, was published by the Cheondogyo religious movement, successor to Donghak (Chapter 46), and became the most influential general-interest monthly of its time. It carried essays on politics, society, and culture, fiction and poetry by emerging writers, reportage, and reader contributions, and it promoted popular education and social reform. It was repeatedly censored, with issues banned or cut, and authorities suppressed it in August 1926 after roughly seventy numbers, though shorter revivals followed.

Other magazines served specialized audiences. Literary journals published fiction and poetry; women's journals, including Sinyeoja and Sinyeoseong (Section 57.37), addressed women's issues; children's magazines, discussed in Section 57.38, created a new reading public; and popular magazines in the later 1920s and 1930s mixed entertainment, news, and advice. Youth publications and the journals of schools and religious groups multiplied.

The economics of magazine publishing involved subscriptions, advertising, bookshop distribution, and sometimes sponsorship by an organization, and editors, contributors, printers, and readers formed networks. Small circulations and constant risk of seizure meant that many magazines were short-lived. Yet they provided platforms for new writers and ideas and helped to form a reading public that crossed regions and, to a degree, classes, and their pages show how Koreans debated modernity, nationalism, socialism, religion, and gender in print.

Gaebyeok is a useful case study of a magazine operating on the edge. It was founded in June 1920 by the Cheondogyo organization, whose leadership included figures who had taken part in the 1919 declaration of independence, and it combined essays on politics, economics, and thought with fiction, poetry, readers' pages, and reports from the provinces. Its editors included Yi Don-hwa, and its pages carried the first work of several young writers. The magazine published materials on socialism and on Russia, on women, on labor, and on children, and its readers' network was linked to Cheondogyo's regional organization.

The Government-General subjected Gaebyeok to repeated bans on single issues and deleted passages, and in August 1926 forced it to close after roughly seventy issues. It briefly reappeared in the 1930s in a different form. Other magazines took up the space it left: Byeolgeongon (1926), a popular monthly of the same publishing house, Samcheolli (from 1929), Sin Donga (1931), Jogwang (1935), and women's and household magazines. The business of magazines depended on subscriptions and advertising, and many closed after a few issues. Taken together they show a periodical culture much wider than a few "great" journals, and one in which audiences included students, teachers, clerks, and merchants.

Section 57.20

Modern Korean Fiction, Poetry, and the Emergence of a Literary Public

Modern Korean literature, in vernacular Korean written in Hangul and mixed script, matured in these decades. Yi Gwang-su's Mujeong (1917), serialized in the Government-General's newspaper, is often cited as an early modern novel, and Yi also published a controversial essay on national reconstruction in Gaebyeok in 1922, though his later wartime career is a subject of Chapter 60. Kim Dong-in, a writer of short stories such as "Gamja" (1925), developed a stripped, aesthetic realism and a theory of the modern short story. Yeom Sang-seop, in works from the early 1920s onward, explored naturalism and the lives of colonial Koreans, and his later novel Samdae (1931) traced three generations of a family. Hyeon Jin-geon's short fiction depicted poor urban life. Poetry flourished in the work of Kim Sowol, Han Yong-un, Jeong Ji-yong, and others.

The 1930s brought experimentation and a concern with the modern city. Pak Tae-won's story A Day in the Life of Kubo the Novelist (1934) followed an idle writer walking through Gyeongseong, and Yi Sang's fiction and poetry, including "Nalgae" (Wings, 1936), explored fragmented consciousness and urban alienation. Serialized novels in newspapers reached broad audiences, and their economics encouraged melodrama and romance as well as social themes.

Literature is evidence, but not transparent evidence. Fiction reflects its authors' educated, mostly urban, mostly male perspectives and was shaped by censorship and commercial considerations. It shows how some Koreans imagined and criticized colonial society, not how all Koreans lived. Historians read texts alongside archival and statistical evidence rather than treating them as unmediated descriptions of ordinary life.

Literary history in these years is a history of forms as much as of authors. The serialized novel, published in daily installments, dominated popular reading; Yi Gwang-su's Mujeong (1917) set the pattern of the love-and-enlightenment novel, and his later serials for Dong-A Ilbo drew mass readerships. Kim Dong-in's story "Gamja" (Potatoes, 1925) exemplified the compressed naturalist short story. Yeom Sang-seop's early fiction, such as "The Frog in the Specimen Room" (1921), explored alienation under colonial conditions, and his novel Samdae (Three Generations, 1931) examined the generational conflict in a Seoul family. Hyeon Jin-geon's "A Lucky Day" (1924) followed a day in the life of a rickshaw puller. Poetry included Kim Sowol's Azaleas collection (1925) and Han Yong-un's Silence of the Beloved (1926).

The 1930s produced modernist experiment. Pak Tae-won's A Day in the Life of Kubo the Novelist (1934) follows an unemployed writer walking through Gyeongseong; Yi Sang's serial poem "Crow's-Eye View" (Ogamdo, 1934), halted by reader complaints, and his story "Wings" (Nalgae, 1936), which includes a department-store rooftop scene, treated the colonial city as a space of fragmentation. Women writers, including Kim Myeong-sun, Na Hye-seok, and later Kang Gyeong-ae and Baek Sin-ae, published fiction and essays, though they faced especially harsh press treatment.

Texts of this kind are evidence of how educated writers perceived colonial society. They are not representative samples of ordinary experience, and they are also shaped by commerce, because serial novelists wrote to keep subscribers buying the paper. Historians therefore read literature alongside police records, statistics, and journalism.

Section 57.21

Proletarian Literature, KAPF, and Debates over the Social Responsibility of Art

In August 1925, writers formed the Korea Artista Proleta Federatio (KAPF), a federation that promoted literature serving the working class and criticized writing it considered bourgeois or merely aesthetic. Its leading figures included Pak Yeong-hui and Kim Ki-jin, and it published manifestos, reviews, and fiction depicting tenants, workers, and the poor. KAPF debated, among other things, the proper relationship between literary form and political content, the role of realism, and the question of whether art should serve social change. Rival currents, including nationalist writers and advocates of literary autonomy, argued that Korean literature should foreground national heritage or aesthetic value.

KAPF operated in an environment of heavy surveillance. Authorities arrested members in waves, notably in 1931 and 1934, and the federation formally dissolved in 1935. The history of Korean communist and socialist political organizations, which overlapped with but exceeded KAPF, belongs to Chapter 59. Here the point is cultural: the debates sharpened Korean writers' understanding of art's social responsibility, influenced fiction and criticism, and left a legacy, contested in both Korean states later, in debates over literature and politics.

KAPF emerged from a broader turn toward socialist ideas in the early 1920s. Its founders in August 1925 included writers who rejected both the romantic nationalism of some contemporaries and what they considered art for its own sake. Its leading critics, among them Pak Yeong-hui and Kim Ki-jin, argued that literature should depict the class structure of colonial society, and the federation published criticism and fiction describing tenants, factory workers, and the urban poor. A well-known controversy of 1926–1927, often called the content–form debate, concerned whether proletarian writers should prioritize political message or literary craft. Others, including nationalist writers such as Yi Gwang-su and Yeom Sang-seop, and the writers associated with the "literature of the nation" and "pure literature" currents, disputed KAPF's premises.

KAPF's organizational life included internal disputes about political direction, shifting relations with socialist movements, and repeated police action. Members were arrested in 1931 and again in 1934, in a case centered on Jeonju, and the federation formally dissolved in 1935. Its influence outlasted the organization, however: it shaped the language of social realism, trained a generation of critics, and left a legacy that became politically contested after 1945, when many members went to the North and others remained in the South. The history of the broader communist and socialist political movements is treated in Chapter 59.

KAPF's sources are principally its own journals, criticism, and manifestos and the police records that followed it. Memoirs of members, written after 1945 when many were in a politically charged position in North or South Korea, require careful reading. Historians generally treat the federation as one current among several in a lively literary field rather than as the whole of Korean literature in the 1920s and 1930s, and note that many writers, including some prominent novelists, never joined.

Section 57.22

Korean Language, Reading Culture, Translation, and the Standardization of Hangul

A reading public requires a standard written language. The Korean language and its script, Hangeul, introduced in the fifteenth century (Chapter 27), had not been uniformly spelled, and schooling, newspapers, and printing exposed inconsistency. Scholars who revived the Joseon Language Research Society (Joseoneo Yeonguhoe) on December 3, 1921, and renamed it the Joseon Language Society (Joseoneo Hakhoe) on January 10, 1931, worked on grammar, orthography, and a dictionary; their organization drew on earlier language-reform associations associated with Ju Si-gyeong. The Government-General also issued its own orthographic rules at various times, including revisions in the 1920s and 1930s.

In October 1933 the Society published its Unified Proposal for Korean Orthography, a systematic set of spelling rules that was widely adopted by newspapers, publishers, and schools in the following years and that became a basis of later standard spelling in South Korea, with later revisions. Related efforts included a campaign to promote Hangeul, an annual commemorative day, and the compilation of a dictionary begun in 1929. Newspapers promoted standardized spelling and used literacy campaigns to spread Hangeul. Translation from Japanese, Western, and Russian literature enlarged vocabulary and genre.

Standardization mattered because a common orthography made printed Korean easier to learn and read, strengthened a sense of linguistic community, and linked language to identity. The Government-General permitted Korean-language education in a limited form and tolerated the Society's work in the 1930s, but suppression of Korean-language organizations came later, a subject of Chapter 60.

Standardization raised technical questions. Hangeul spelling had followed several principles, some writing words as pronounced and others preserving word roots; dialect variation, vowels, and spacing needed rules, and the Government-General's own orthography, which it revised during the period, differed from the Language Society's proposals. The 1933 Unified Proposal argued for rules based on morphological principles and standard Seoul speech of the educated classes, and set spacing rules between words. Supporters in newspapers and publishing adopted it quickly, while critics objected to some of its principles, and revisions followed after 1945.

The Society, which had been revived as the Joseon Language Research Society on December 3, 1921 and renamed the Joseon Language Society on January 10, 1931, also established a dictionary project in 1929 and published a journal, and its members included linguists such as Choe Hyeon-bae, Yi Geuk-ro, and Kim Yun-gyeong. Hangeul commemorations, first held in 1926 as "Gagyanal" and later named Hangeul Day, served to publicize the script. Language mattered also for translation: Korean translators brought European and Russian literature, often through Japanese, into print, contributing to the development of a modern literary vocabulary. The suppression of the Society and the arrest of its members in the early 1940s belong to Chapter 60.

Section 57.23

Theater, New Drama, Traditional Performance, and the Modern Stage

Urban theater drew on older and newer forms. Traditional performance, including pansori (Chapter 41), folk dance and song, and the gisaeng arts, continued, and from the early twentieth century changgeuk, a staged form of pansori with multiple actors, developed in urban theaters such as Wongaksa. Sinpa ("new school") theater, a melodramatic form influenced by Japanese shinpa and beginning with companies such as Hyeoksindan in the 1910s, toured widely and drew large popular audiences. Modern spoken drama, influenced by Western realism, developed through student groups, including Korean students' Towolhoe in Tokyo in 1923, and through later Korean theater research groups in Gyeongseong in the early 1930s, which staged plays such as Yu Chi-jin's Tomak.

Performers, audiences, and economics varied. Traveling companies performed in provincial towns; Gyeongseong theaters sold tickets to a mixed audience; women performed in a profession of uncertain social status, with gisaeng and actresses facing moral scrutiny. Censors read scripts and attended performances, banning or altering politically sensitive content. Theater served commercial entertainment, artistic experimentation, and social criticism, sometimes within the same production, and its relationship to older performance traditions was one of both competition and borrowing.

Distinguishing the forms clarifies the stage. Pansori, the solo sung narrative with a drummer, flourished in the nineteenth century (Chapter 41) and continued in the colonial period in private gatherings, gisaeng houses, and, increasingly, recordings and radio. Changgeuk divided a pansori story among costumed actors on a modern stage; its early home was Wongaksa, established in 1908, and it developed through touring and city theaters in the 1910s and 1930s. Sinpa (new school) drama, adapted from Japanese shinpa, staged melodramas of family tragedy, romance, and social conflict, and was the staple of commercial theater and touring companies. Singeuk (new drama), a term for modern spoken drama inspired by European realism, was pursued by student groups such as Towolhoe (founded in Tokyo in 1923) and by Korean theater research societies in Gyeongseong in the early 1930s, whose stagings included Yu Chi-jin's Tomak (Earth Huts), a play about slum dwellers.

Theater was a business. Companies depended on ticket sales, advertising in the papers, and the costs of renting halls, and many dissolved after a few seasons. Actresses became public figures with an uneasy social standing; gisaeng, who were trained performers regulated by licensing associations, performed in both traditional and new venues. Censors reviewed scripts in advance and could stop performances; plays touching on labor, class conflict, or national themes were cut or banned. Even so, audiences used the theater as a place to watch social criticism and, sometimes, to hear forbidden feeling expressed in code.

Documentary evidence includes theater advertisements and reviews in the daily press, published scripts, police and censorship records, and a small number of recordings and photographs. It is far easier to find evidence about Gyeongseong's theaters than about touring companies in the provinces, and about published plays than about improvised commercial performances, so histories of the stage lean toward the capital and toward the literary.

Section 57.24

Cinemas, Imported Films, and the Creation of a Moviegoing Public

Cinema became the most widely discussed new entertainment. Film exhibition had begun in the Korean Empire period, and by the 1920s Gyeongseong had many cinemas, with a rough division between Korean-oriented theaters in the northern districts, such as Danseongsa, and Japanese-oriented theaters in the southern ones. Provincial cities also had cinemas. Audiences saw Japanese period dramas and contemporary films, and imported American, and some European, films, with Hollywood productions especially popular among Korean urban audiences.

Silent film screenings were accompanied by live narrators, known as byeonsa (Japanese: benshi), who explained the plot, voiced characters, and added commentary, sometimes becoming celebrities whose style drew fans. Publicity, handbills, newspaper advertisements, and posters promoted films. Ticket prices varied by theater and seat, so that cheaper seats allowed wider access but remained significant expenses for low-income households. Spectators included students, clerks, shopkeepers, and others, and moviegoing became a marker of urban life, a place of sociability, and a subject of moral concern and cultural criticism.

The result was a moviegoing public that did not depend on literacy and that crossed some, though not all, social lines, and that connected Korean cities with global popular culture and with Japanese entertainment industries.

Cinemas formed a visible urban geography. In Gyeongseong, theaters in the northern district, notably Danseongsa, drew largely Korean audiences, while theaters in the southern district showed films to a largely Japanese public; admission prices and programming differed. Danseongsa, a Korean-owned theater, became the best-known site of Korean film premiere and was associated with the entrepreneur Park Seung-pil, who had also been involved in its early film exhibition. Provincial cities had their own cinemas, often built by local Korean or Japanese investors.

The programs mixed several kinds of film. Japanese period dramas and contemporary comedies were screened; American films, including serials and adventures, drew large Korean audiences; and European films appeared in limited numbers. Korean-made films were a small fraction of what audiences saw, so that the story of Korean filmmaking should be told alongside the dominance of imports. Distribution depended on rental networks that supplied prints to regional theaters, sometimes with years' delay.

The byeonsa were central to the experience. In silent film screenings in Korea the narrator stood beside the screen, explained the story, voiced characters, and added commentary in a style that could be elaborate and personal. Some byeonsa were celebrities who attracted their own audiences, and they were key to the success of Korean and foreign films alike, because they translated plots for audiences who could not read intertitles in Japanese or English. They were also a point of control, since police could require changes to the narration, and a point of controversy among critics who argued that the narrator's dominance hindered the development of film as an art. Moviegoing combined entertainment, courtship, school outings, and, for many, a first encounter with images of other societies.

Section 57.25

The Emergence of Korean Filmmaking and Na Un-gyu's Arirang

Korean filmmaking developed in stages that later national memory sometimes merges. In October 1919, a work called Fight for Justice (Uirijeok Guto) was shown at Danseongsa as a "chain drama," a stage play with filmed sequences, and it is often commemorated as an early step in Korean film history, though it was a mixed stage-and-screen production and not a feature film. Narrative films followed, including The Vow Made below the Moon (1923), made as part of a Government-General savings-encouragement campaign, and productions by Japanese and Korean companies in the early 1920s.

Na Un-gyu's Arirang, produced by Joseon Kinema and released in October 1926 at Danseongsa, became the best-remembered silent Korean film. Na directed it and played the lead. The film is lost, and descriptions of its story rely on contemporary reviews, publicity, and later recollections by participants and critics. As usually summarized, it concerns a young man who has suffered mental breakdown, possibly connected with imprisonment after the 1919 protests, and who kills a collaborator figure who threatens his sister; he is led away by police while the song "Arirang" is heard. Details differ among accounts, and no surviving print allows verification.

The film drew large audiences and became associated with the folk song, whose film version spread widely. Censors approved the film, which historians interpret in several ways: as evidence that the story's political implications were indirect enough to pass, or that censors misread it. Later interpretations read the film as a national allegory. These readings are historically meaningful, but they should not be assumed to represent Na's intention or the experience of every 1926 viewer.

The early history shows how milestones differ in kind. Fight for Justice (Uirijeok Guto), shown at Danseongsa on October 27, 1919, directed by Kim Do-san, was a "chain drama" (yeonswaegeuk) in which stage action alternated with filmed outdoor scenes shot in familiar Seoul locations, and it is commemorated annually as Korean Film Day. Its audience success encouraged further ventures. The Vow Made below the Moon (1923), made for a Government-General savings-encouragement campaign, was a narrative film of moral instruction. Companies formed by Japanese capital, Korean directors and actors, and technicians from both sides developed in Busan and Seoul, among them Joseon Kinema Productions, whose second production was Arirang.

Arirang premiered at Danseongsa on October 1, 1926. Na Un-gyu wrote, directed, and starred as the student Yeong-jin, with Sin Il-seon (credited under the stage name Sin Hong-ryeon) as his sister Yeong-hui, Nam Gung-un as Yun Hyeon-gu, and Ju In-gyu as the villain O Gi-ho. Contemporary accounts and later reconstructions describe a rural village setting, a mentally disturbed protagonist, and a climax in which Yeong-jin kills a man who threatens his sister and is taken away by police to the sound of the folk song "Arirang." The film was commercially successful, and the authorities passed it for exhibition but took action around its publicity: according to Korean press and scholarly accounts, publicity leaflets that included the song's lyrics were reportedly confiscated on September 30, 1926, before the film opened, and a verse deemed objectionable was deleted from published versions, so that the byeonsa reportedly could not perform the song as planned. These measures are among the best evidence that officials perceived the film's popular resonance.

Interpretive caution is essential. The film is lost, and its whereabouts have been the subject of search and rumor. Descriptions rest on reviews, advertisements, censorship notes, and memoirs written years later. Participants such as the filmmaker Yun Bong-chun recalled the film in strongly nationalist terms, as a "second March First," while scholars have noted that contemporary reviews in the Chosun Ilbo and Dong-A Ilbo differed, the former criticizing weaknesses of rural depiction and editing and the latter praising casting and direction. Some historians think the nationalist reading was elaborated later, and others argue that contemporary audiences understood the film's implications from the start. Both can be partly right, which is why the film is analyzed in the Debate box below.

Section 57.26

Film Production, Censorship, Studios, and the Coming of Sound

Filmmaking was expensive and precarious. Korean production companies were small and short-lived, financed by investors, entrepreneurs, and sometimes Japanese partners, and output in the later 1920s and early 1930s amounted to a handful of features a year in contrast with the large volume of imported films. Cameras, film stock, laboratory processing, and later sound equipment were imported or processed in Japan, and technical constraints shaped what could be made. Personnel included directors, actors, cinematographers, and narrators, many of whom trained in Japan or learned on the job.

Film censorship operated through several layers. It predated the 1926 regulations: local rules and discretionary intervention already restricted screenings. The Motion Picture Film Censorship Rules, brought into effect on August 1, 1926, formalized centralized approval and imposed film-examination fees, a substantial cost for exhibitors and distributors. Authorities cut scenes or banned films deemed threatening to public order or colonial policy, including imported films. Some exhibitors nevertheless saw centralized procedures as more predictable than the earlier patchwork of local controls. Surviving films from the era are rare; the 1934 film Crossroads of Youth, rediscovered in 2007, is among the oldest surviving Korean films.

The transition to sound was costly. Chunhyangjeon (1935), an adaptation of the classic story, is recognized as Korea's first sound feature film, and its success demonstrated audience appetite for Korean-language talkies, but the technology raised production costs and favored larger, better-financed operations. The coming of sound connected film with recorded music and radio and changed the position of the byeonsa, whose role as narrator became unnecessary.

Production was constrained by capital. Early companies often relied on investors who expected quick returns, and a feature could cost many times what a studio could safely lose. Equipment, film stock, and laboratory processing were imported from or processed in Japan. Films were shot with natural light and limited sets, and distribution depended on a few key theaters and on sales to provincial exhibitors. The 1926 national rules added a standard procedure and fees for every print submitted. Producers reacted by self-censoring scripts, shifting from contemporary social topics to romance, adaptations of classic tales, and adventure, and by building stories that could pass inspection.

The coming of sound transformed the industry. Hollywood and Japanese sound films reached Korean cinemas from the early 1930s, and Korean producers faced the high cost of sound recording, which in most cases required equipment and technicians from Japan. Chunhyangjeon (1935), directed by Lee Myeong-u and based on the classic story, premiered at Danseongsa in October 1935 and succeeded commercially, demonstrating that Korean-language sound films could attract audiences. Sound made the byeonsa obsolete, ended a distinctive period of live narration, and increased the pressure on small producers who could not afford the new technology. Surviving films from the 1930s are few; Crossroads of Youth (1934), rediscovered in 2007, offers rare direct evidence for the period.

Section 57.27

Gramophones, Popular Music, Traditional Song, and the New Sound Culture

Recorded sound created a new popular culture. Japanese and international record companies established branches or recording arrangements for Korean artists in the 1920s and 1930s, producing discs of pansori, folk songs, hymns and Western-influenced songs, comic monologues, and a developing genre of commercial popular song (yuhaengga). A 1926 recording of "Sa-ui chanmi" by soprano Yun Sim-deok, made in Osaka, became widely known partly through the singer's tragic death. Pansori and folk performers gained new audiences through records, and new "folk-style" songs were commissioned by record companies. The emotional ballad style that later became known as teuroteu emerged in the 1930s, drawing on Japanese and Western influences.

Gramophones were costly, so ownership was concentrated among wealthier households, while cafés, shops, and community gatherings allowed wider listening. Recorded music connected with film and radio, and new careers appeared for singers, composers, and lyricists, though performers faced economic pressure, contractual dependence on record companies, and social stigma, especially women. Censors examined lyrics, and records could be banned. The result was a new sound culture that blended Korean traditions with commercial imports and that created popular stars and shared repertoires.

The recording industry combined global firms and local talent. In the 1920s and 1930s Japanese and international labels such as Columbia, Victor, Okeh, Polydor, and others recorded Korean performers in Seoul, Osaka, and Tokyo, and sold discs through shops and mail order. Recording produced a repertoire of pansori excerpts, folk songs, Christian hymns, children's songs, comic dialogues, and a new popular song (yuhaengga). Yun Sim-deok's recording of "Sa-ui chanmi" (Hymn of Death, 1926) became a landmark seller, remembered partly because of her death shortly afterward. In the 1930s, popular singers including Yi Ae-ri-su ("Hwangseong yet-teo," Ruins of the Old Fortress), Go Bok-su ("Tahyang," Foreign Land), and Yi Nan-yeong ("Mokpo-ui nunmul," Tears of Mokpo) built careers on recorded songs in the style that came to be called teuroteu or in "new folk songs" (sin minyo).

Records reached audiences unevenly. Gramophones were expensive, so ownership concentrated in better-off households and in cafés and shops, which played records as entertainment and advertisement. Records were also broadcast, since radio stations used discs to fill air time. Singers entered a precarious profession in which contracts, stage name, and public scandal could make or break a career, and women performers faced moral criticism. Authorities examined lyrics and could ban records, as in the action against material associated with the film Arirang. The result was a mass musical culture in which Korean forms, Japanese influences, and Western styles mixed.

The principal sources are the discs themselves, many now preserved in archives and collections, together with record-company catalogues, advertisements, and press reports. Surviving records are biased toward commercially successful releases, and sales figures are scarce, so claims about how many people owned gramophones or bought particular records should be treated cautiously.

Section 57.28

Radio Broadcasting, JODK, and the Expansion of Electronic Mass Communication

Japan began radio broadcasting in 1925, and the Government-General licensed a station in Gyeongseong shortly after. Experimental transmission began in late 1926, and the Gyeongseong Broadcasting Station, with call sign JODK, began regular broadcasting on February 16, 1927. Initially, programs mixed Japanese and Korean in a single service, with Japanese predominating. In April 1933, a reorganized operation introduced two separate channels, one primarily in Japanese and one in Korean, a change that responded in part to Korean complaints.

Radio was expensive. Listeners had to buy a receiver, a substantial outlay relative to most wages, and pay a monthly license fee of two yen. As a result, early license holders were mostly Japanese settlers and wealthy Koreans, and Koreans remained a minority of licensees through the early 1930s. Coverage was concentrated near stations and in cities. Programs included news, weather, lectures, music, drama, agricultural and educational content, and sports reports, and Korean traditional music and pansori were broadcast alongside Western-style and Japanese programming.

Radio served both entertainment and instruction, and it was useful to authorities as a means of reaching listeners with official messages. It offered shared listening experiences, but the audience it created was smaller and more urban, wealthy, and Japanese than the audience of print or cinema.

Radio is best understood through institutions and numbers. Japan opened its first stations in 1925 (Tokyo, Nagoya, and Osaka), and the Government-General allowed only a licensed corporation to broadcast in Korea; a 1924 radio demonstration sponsored by the Chosun Ilbo on December 17 and the paper's later attempt to build a station were blocked under wireless telegraphy law. The Keijō Broadcasting Station (JODK), a licensed non-profit corporation supervised by the Government-General's communications bureau, began regular broadcasting on February 16, 1927, from Jeong-dong with a one-kilowatt transmitter at 690 kHz, after opening was delayed from late 1926 by the death of the Japanese emperor. Income came almost entirely from the monthly listener's fee of two yen, and at the opening only about 1,400 receivers were registered, mostly Japanese households and some wealthy Korean ones.

Language was the central issue. The 1927 service mixed Japanese and Korean, with Japanese predominating: accounts describe a ratio of roughly seven to three, and others a daily Korean-language allotment of an hour or two. In April 1933 the reorganized Chōsen Broadcasting Association introduced two channels: Keijō First Broadcast in Japanese on 900 kHz and Second Broadcast in Korean on 610 kHz, supported by a new transmitter at Yeonhui. The second channel carried Korean music, radio drama, language and general lectures, sports reports, and storytelling, with performers such as the Pyongyang singer Wang Su-bok and the storyteller Yun Baek-nam, and it was subject to prior inspection like all programming. Receiver numbers rose after 1933 and, by one account, exceeded 100,000 across the peninsula in 1937, still a small share of households.

Radio thus had a double character. It brought Korean-language entertainment and information to those who owned sets, and the Korean channel allowed performers a national audience. It also served authorities, who used it to broadcast official messages, and became a more important instrument of mobilization after 1937, a development treated in Chapters 60 and 62.

Section 57.29

Department Stores, Shopping Districts, and Commercial Modernity

Department stores symbolized commercial modernity. Japanese firms, including Mitsukoshi, whose large new Gyeongseong building opened in 1930, along with stores such as Chojiya, Minakai, and Hirata, concentrated in the southern commercial district around Honmachi and Namdaemun-ro. They sold imported and domestic goods in multi-story buildings with display windows, elevators, and sometimes restaurants and rooftop gardens, and they promoted a new style of fixed-price shopping.

Korean commercial enterprise was not new, however. Older markets such as Namdaemun and Dongdaemun, along with Jongno shops, remained central to Korean commerce, and Korean merchants established companies and shops. Hwashin traced its origins to Shin Tae-hwa's earlier retail business; Park Heung-sik acquired that enterprise in 1931 and reorganized it as a major Korean-owned department store on Jongno, creating the best-known Korean rival to Japanese stores. Its development exemplified Korean enterprise within the unequal commercial conditions of colonial rule.

Consumption was unequal in class, gender, and ethnicity. Shopping districts catered chiefly to the urban middle and upper classes and Japanese residents, women were prominent consumers and targets of advertising, and most Koreans continued to buy daily goods at markets and from peddlers. Department stores demonstrate a new retail form, not general prosperity.

Retail history ran on two tracks. The Japanese-owned stores grew from drapers and general merchants into multi-story department stores concentrated in the southern commercial district: Mitsukoshi, whose large new Keijō building opened on October 24, 1930, with a basement, four floors, and a rooftop garden and observation area; Chojiya; Minakai; and Hirata. By the 1930s observers counted five department stores in the capital, four of them Japanese-owned and one Korean-owned, Hwashin. Hwashin's antecedent was Shin Tae-hwa's Hwashin Sanghoe, a Jongno shop that had begun as a precious-metals dealer and added tailoring in 1922 and general goods in 1923; Park Heung-sik, who had made money in rice and paper trading, acquired it in 1931, rebuilt it, and in 1937 opened a new six-story building with elevators and escalators. A second Korean store, Dong-A Department Store, was absorbed by Hwashin.

These stores stood alongside older Korean commercial life: Namdaemun and Dongdaemun markets, peddlers, neighborhood shops, and the shops of Jongno. Pricing and display in department stores introduced fixed prices, window displays, delivery services, and sales promotions, while traditional markets continued to dominate everyday shopping. The stores also became places to look: Yi Sang's 1936 story "Wings" uses the Mitsukoshi rooftop as a setting for a protagonist who watches the city from above. Consumption through department stores was concentrated among the urban middle and upper classes and Japanese residents; women were key customers; and Korean entrepreneurs competed in a retail field shaped by superior Japanese capital, links to Japanese wholesalers, and colonial commercial law.

Evidence includes advertisements, photographs, store histories, and later museum research, along with contemporary journalism. Because stores promoted themselves, their claims about visitors and goods are marketing, and independent data on customers are limited. The surviving image of the department store as a symbol of modern Seoul owes something to literature and to later nostalgia, which should be kept in view when evaluating its social reach.

Section 57.30

Fashion, Modern Boys, Modern Girls, and Changing Public Appearance

Appearance became a public issue. Western-style clothing, including suits for men and Western dresses, shoes, and hats, spread among urban elites and students, while "improved" Korean clothing (gaeryang hanbok) shortened and modified traditional garments. Hairstyles changed, with short bobbed hair (danbal) for women becoming a flashpoint, and cosmetics, including Korean-made face powders and imported products, spread through advertising. School uniforms standardized the dress of students.

The terms modan boi and modan geol (modern boy and modern girl, from Japanese usage) entered Korean vocabulary and became labels for young urban people who followed fashion, frequented cafés and cinemas, and displayed new styles. These were contemporary cultural categories, often used by critics who associated them with frivolity, consumerism, and moral decline, and sometimes by cartoonists and satirists. They were not neutral descriptions of all young Koreans, most of whom could not afford such lifestyles.

Debates involved tensions between new personal freedom, commercial advertising, nationalist concern that consumption reflected dependence on foreign goods, and the continued strength of family expectations. Fashion thus shows how modernity reached daily appearance and how it was contested.

Critiques of modern appearance were a staple of the periodicals. Popular magazines such as Byeolgeongon published cartoons and essays ridiculing the modan boi and modan geol as superficial copyists of Tokyo and Hollywood, while others defended personal freedom. Bobbed hair for women, introduced by a few women in the 1920s, provoked public debate and, sometimes, school discipline; shortened skirts and Western shoes, school uniforms, and lighter, "improved" Korean dress appeared in photographs and advertisements. Men's suits and short hair spread among the urban professional class, while farmers and laborers continued to wear Korean clothes adapted to work.

Cosmetics and fashion were commercial products. Korean-made face powders and creams competed with Japanese and imported brands, and advertising in newspapers and magazines linked beauty with hygiene, modernity, and sometimes national self-reliance, as in campaigns urging purchase of Korean products. Nationalist critics argued that foreign fashion drained resources and eroded identity; socialist critics argued that consumption distracted young people from social conflict; conservatives feared the loosening of gender norms. The labels were therefore terms of debate, and many young women and men who wore new clothes were also students, workers, and activists. Historians are cautious about using the labels as descriptions of a social group, because the people most visibly criticized were a small minority.

The Modern Girl (modan geol) was a label for fashion-conscious urban young women. Popular sovereignty is the idea that political authority rests in the people. A consumer society is one in which buying goods shapes identity and daily life.

Section 57.31

Cafés, Restaurants, Hotels, Dance Halls, and Urban Sociability

New urban places allowed people to meet outside household and community structures. Cafés, introduced by Japanese entrepreneurs, spread in the 1920s and became fashionable among Korean writers, artists, and students, some opened by Koreans themselves, such as the Jebi tea room and the Nakran parlor associated with the writer Yi Sang in the early 1930s. Tea rooms, restaurants serving Korean, Japanese, Chinese, and Western food, hotels such as the Chosen Hotel (opened 1914), and, in limited circles, dance halls, provided venues for sociability.

Women worked in many of these places as waitresses, hostesses, and entertainers. Their employment drew public anxiety about morality, and newspapers published exposés and warnings, while their working conditions, dependence on tips and employers, and risk of exploitation went less discussed. The places also fostered professional, artistic, and student networks: cafés hosted literary gatherings, and public lectures and exhibitions linked cultural circles.

Urban sociability thus involved real freedom and real constraint. It created spaces for new forms of association, but it was concentrated in central districts, open mostly to those with money, and entangled with commercial entertainment industries.

Cafés had several overlapping meanings. Introduced by Japanese entrepreneurs in the 1910s and 1920s, they became gathering places for writers, artists, and students. The writer Yi Sang ran the tea room Jebi in 1933 and later the Nakran parlor, which became known as artistic meeting places, although they failed commercially. Women employed as waitresses and hostesses were subjects of both fascination and moral alarm, and magazines published exposés of "café girls." Their working conditions, dependence on tips and employer control, and vulnerability to harassment were less visible. Korean-owned and Japanese-owned establishments differed in clientele, and patrons crossed the lines more often in leisure than in housing.

Restaurants served Korean, Japanese, Chinese, and Western dishes, and the Chosen Hotel, opened by the railway administration in 1914, offered Western-style accommodation for officials and travelers. Dance halls and licensed ballroom dancing existed in limited circles in the 1930s, and film, cafés, and music formed a connected leisure district in central Gyeongseong. Women in commercial entertainment, including gisaeng, café workers, and singers, occupied an ambiguous position, publicly visible but socially stigmatized. These venues show a new urban sociability, but they remained commercial spaces open to those who could pay.

Evidence for these spaces is mostly journalistic and literary. Magazines such as Byeolgeongon and Samcheolli published reportage on cafés and "café girls," and fiction of the 1930s used them as settings, which makes them vivid and unreliable at once: reporters sought the sensational, and novelists the symbolic. Police and municipal records, which regulated licensing and hygiene, give a drier picture. A careful account therefore treats the café as a documented institution whose social meaning was contested, and avoids generalizing from famous literary cafés to the many small tea rooms and drinking places whose patrons left no records.

Section 57.32

Advertising, Consumer Credit, Modern Time, and the Commercialization of Everyday Life

Advertising pervaded newspapers, magazines, cinema programs, and streets. Patent medicines, cosmetics, food seasonings such as Ajinomoto, household goods, books, and school products were advertised in Korean papers, and advertising revenue supported the papers themselves. Advertisements promoted ideals of hygiene, family, and fashion and encouraged aspiration to a modern household with new equipment and branded products.

Credit shaped consumption, but not mainly through installment buying. Sewing machines and some other goods were sold on installments, and department stores and merchants offered limited credit, but most households relied on informal arrangements: rotating credit associations (gye), moneylenders, pawnshops, and borrowing from kin, often at heavy cost. Consumer goods thus coexisted with indebtedness and uncertainty, and an advertised product did not mean broad purchasing power.

Time itself changed. Korea's standard time was set to the Japanese standard in 1912, aligning clocks with Tokyo, and school bells, factory shifts, office hours, tram schedules, and radio programs spread clock time, especially in cities. Rural life continued to follow agricultural and lunar rhythms, and the two timekeeping systems overlapped.

Advertising offers a window into aspirations and markets. Newspapers carried advertisements for patent medicines, face creams, seasonings such as Ajinomoto, school and book notices, and household goods, and the advertising pages show which firms regarded Korean readers as customers. Cinemas published program advertisements, and department stores announced seasonal sales. The papers' dependence on advertising meant that editorial independence and commercial interest were entangled. Economic historians treat advertising volumes as indications of urban consumer markets, but not as direct measures of purchases.

Credit was largely informal. Rotating credit associations (gye) pooled savings for weddings, tuition, or business capital; pawnshops and moneylenders supplied short-term loans, often at high rates; and landlords and merchants extended credit to tenants and customers, a subject developed in Chapter 58. Installment purchase of items such as sewing machines existed, but it was not the main form of household credit. Debts rose with consumption aspirations, particularly in urban households.

Time was commercialized as well. Korean time was aligned with Japanese Standard Time in 1912; schools, factories, offices, tram timetables, and radio schedules imposed fixed hours; and clocks and watches became desirable goods. In cities, the working day became standardized, while village life followed agricultural and lunar calendars. The coexistence of calendars, including the solar calendar of government and business and the lunar calendar of festivals and farming, is a reminder that modern time was layered over older rhythms.

Section 57.33

Sports, Recreation, Spectatorship, and the Formation of Urban Communities

Sports spread through schools, missions, and clubs. Baseball, introduced in the early twentieth century, football (soccer), track and field, and other sports developed in schools and civic organizations, and the Korean-led Joseon Sports Association, formed in 1920, organized competitions, while Japanese settler organizations and commercial promoters ran their own. Gyeongseong Stadium, opened in 1925, hosted school and civic events. Football matches between Gyeongseong and Pyongyang teams, begun in 1929, drew large crowds and local pride.

Spectatorship created new communities. Fans followed teams in the newspapers, schools competed, and events brought together students, clerks, and families. Differences among school teams, Korean civic organizations, Japanese settler clubs, and commercial promoters shaped participation. Sports were not purely political, but they could carry national feeling, as the 1936 marathon victory shows (Section 57.18).

This section distinguishes cultural and social participation from the explicitly political mobilizations assigned to Chapter 59. Sports provide evidence of leisure, community formation, and the commercialization of entertainment, as well as the entanglement of apparently non-political activity with colonial relations.

Sports mixed leisure, education, and nationalism. Baseball and football were played at mission and government schools; the Korean-led Joseon Sports Association (Joseon Chiyukhoe), founded in 1920, organized national championships, and newspapers promoted events: the Dong-A Ilbo sponsored a women's tennis tournament from 1923, and later swimming and marathon contests. Gyeongseong Stadium (opened 1925) hosted school and civic events, and the annual Gyeongseong–Pyongyang football match, begun in 1929, built a regional rivalry. Japanese settler clubs and commercial promoters ran their own competitions, and cross-ethnic competition was possible but tension-prone.

Sports created spectators and heroes. Korean runners attracted attention in the early 1930s, and in August 1936 the marathon victory in Berlin of Sohn Kee-chung, who competed for Japan under a Japanese name, and the bronze medal of Nam Sung-yong, produced celebration among Koreans and provoked the press episode discussed in Section 57.18. The episode shows how athletic achievement could become a national symbol even when the athlete was formally a member of the Japanese team. Participation, however, remained concentrated among students and urban men; women's sports, such as tennis and basketball, developed in girls' schools but reached fewer people.

Sport also reveals the institutional structure of civic life. School teams belonged to school and mission networks; the Joseon Sports Association represented Korean organizations; Japanese settler clubs and the colonial authorities supported their own bodies; and newspapers acted as promoters, using events to raise circulation and prestige. A tournament was therefore simultaneously a leisure event, a commercial promotion, and an arena in which communities defined themselves against one another. Contemporary reports are the main evidence, and they emphasize victories, controversies, and crowds; they say much less about the large majority of Koreans who never attended a match and for whom play was informal.

Section 57.34

The New Woman and the Debate over Gender, Education, and Independence

The sinyeoseong, or New Woman, emerged in Korean discourse in the 1910s and became prominent in the 1920s. The label referred to women, usually educated and urban, who questioned conventional expectations about marriage, education, work, and artistic expression. Na Hye-seok (1896–1948), a painter and writer, studied in Japan, published fiction and essays, and held an exhibition of her oil paintings in 1921, and she is widely regarded as one of the first Korean women to do so. Kim Il-yeop (1896–1971) founded the women's journal Sinyeoja in 1920 and wrote on women's liberation and free love. Kim Myeong-sun (born 1896) published fiction and poetry in the late 1910s and became both celebrated and vilified in the press.

These women claimed education, independent identity, and freedom of expression and criticized marriage customs and the double standard in sexual morality. Their self-representation, in their own essays, fiction, and art, differed sharply from how newspapers and many male commentators portrayed them: press treatment often sensationalized their personal lives, mocked their ideas, and treated them as warnings about social disorder. Na Hye-seok's later public confession about divorce, published in 1934, drew intense condemnation, which shows the hostility that could follow a woman who publicly challenged norms.

The New Woman was a discourse and also a set of lives. How many women lived in these ways, and what the label meant to women outside the educated urban elite, is the subject of the debate that follows Section 57.35.

The self-representation of New Women and their public portrayal diverged. Na Hye-seok, born in 1896 and educated in Korea and Japan, published the story "Gyeong-hui" (1918) about a young woman who rejects an arranged marriage and asserts her standing as a person, wrote essays on women and marriage, and held an exhibition of her oil paintings in Gyeongseong in 1921. Kim Il-yeop, who founded Sinyeoja in 1920, wrote on women's liberation, sexual morality, and free love, and later became a Buddhist nun. Kim Myeong-sun, whose story "A Girl of Suspicion" won a magazine literary contest in 1917, became a target of press sensationalism for her private life and was eventually silenced by scandal.

Newspapers and mass-circulation magazines often presented the New Woman as a figure of fashion, vanity, or moral danger. Cartoons and gossip columns ridiculed short hair and Western dress; articles warned of "free love" and of educated women who refused housework. Male reformers sometimes supported women's education while insisting on traditional family roles, and the phrase "wise mother, good wife" (hyeonmo yangcheo), imported from Japanese educational discourse, framed the aims of much girls' schooling. Women's own writing therefore stands in tension with male commentary, and it must be read with attention to who published it and why. Na Hye-seok's fate illustrates the risks: after her 1934 published account of her divorce and its aftermath in Samcheolli, she was widely condemned, and she died in poverty in 1948.

Section 57.35

Women's Education, Professions, Employment, and the Limits of Opportunity

Girls' education expanded unevenly. Girls' higher common schools, mission schools such as Ewha, and teacher-training institutions educated a small but growing number of young women, and Ewha developed a college-level program in the 1920s. Educated women became teachers, nurses, midwives, physicians in small numbers, and, from the 1920s, journalists, editors, and clerical staff, and some pursued music and art. Telephone operators, shop assistants, and later bus conductors held new urban jobs.

The experiences of educated women differed profoundly from those of other women. Factory workers in textile and rubber industries, whose conditions and strikes are examined in Chapter 58, domestic servants, market traders, and farm women worked long hours for low pay with little schooling. Rural women made up the large majority. Many educated women found that credentials did not guarantee employment or equal pay, and marriage often ended careers. Opportunity grew, but it remained limited by gender, class, and colonial status.

Educational and occupational opportunity for women expanded in particular niches. Girls' higher common schools and mission schools trained girls in domestic subjects, teaching, and, in some cases, academic subjects; Ewha developed college-level programs in the 1920s, and nursing and midwifery schools offered professional training. The Severance hospital and medical school and other institutions trained nurses and a few women physicians. In the 1920s and 1930s, women worked as teachers in primary schools, as nurses, as telephone operators and clerks, and as reporters and editors for papers and magazines, a small number but a visible one.

Most women, however, worked in farms, households, markets, and factories. Rural women combined farm labor with household production; urban poor women worked as street sellers, laundresses, and servants; and young women entered textile and rubber factories, as treated in Chapter 58. Evidence on these groups is thinner and often mediated by others: newspaper exposés, social surveys, labor organizers' reports, and fiction. A history of "the modern woman" that relied only on magazines would privilege those with the education and leisure to write them.

Interpreting these opportunities requires attention to proportion. The women who appear in newspapers as teachers, nurses, and journalists were visible because they were exceptional. Even within girls' schooling, curricula emphasized domestic skills and "wise mother, good wife" ideals alongside academic subjects, so schooling could open doors and at the same time confirm expected roles. Marriage often ended careers, and employers paid women less than men. The comparison with women in factories, homes, and fields is not a ranking of whose lives mattered; it is a reminder that "women's modernity" was several different experiences, and that archives overrepresent the literate.

Section 57.36

Marriage, Family Reform, Sexuality, and Conflicting Moral Expectations

Marriage and family became topics of intense public debate. Writers and activists criticized arranged and early marriage, advocated romantic love (jayu yeonae) and free choice of spouse, and discussed divorce, concubinage, and women's control over their lives. Newspapers ran advice columns, serialized novels about love, and published debates, while conservatives defended family authority and Confucian morality and warned against moral decay.

Public cultural debate must be distinguished from legal change. In family matters the colonial government applied Korean customary law, as interpreted by the courts, for Koreans, so that patriarchal family structures remained legally strong, though registration rules introduced in the early 1920s formalized marriage and household records. Legal and economic power remained unequal, with wives dependent on husbands' families. New aspirations coexisted with patriarchal structures, and women who acted on them faced social penalties.

Changes were therefore uneven: urban, educated couples sometimes chose their own spouses, while most marriages in villages followed family arrangement. The debates show a society negotiating between competing ideals of the modern and the traditional.

Marriage debates took place in magazines, advice columns, and fiction. Reformers attacked early marriage, parental arrangement, and the practice of taking concubines, and defended the idea that couples should know and choose each other. Others, including many Confucian-minded commentators and some of the women's own families, replied that free love undermined family order. Student and urban couples increasingly pursued "love marriages," but the number is unknown and probably small outside cities.

The legal framework remained patriarchal. The colonial administration applied Joseon custom, as interpreted by courts and officials, to family matters affecting Koreans, so that rules on household headship, inheritance, and divorce continued to favor male heads of household. Household registration rules introduced in the early 1920s required the registration of marriages and births, formalizing family status in ways that affected inheritance and legitimacy. Women who left unhappy marriages faced loss of children, property, and social standing. Public debate and legal practice thus moved on different tracks: ideas shifted faster than law, and law shifted faster than most families' lives.

Evidence on private life is difficult. Newspapers printed letters, advice columns, and reports of scandals and suicides, which indicate public anxieties but not typical practice; surveys by Korean researchers and by the Government-General recorded marriage ages and household structure for some localities; and fiction dramatized conflicts. Historians use these sources to reconstruct the argument rather than the statistics, and they are careful to separate the loud public controversy over "free love" from the quieter ways in which families negotiated marriage choices, education, and dowry in the changing economy.

Section 57.37

Women's Publications, Organizations, and the Emergence of Collective Activism

Women created publications and organizations. Sinyeoja (New Woman), founded in 1920 by Kim Il-yeop, was short-lived but influential, and Sinyeoseong, relaunched in 1923 under the Gaebyeok publishing company, became a more durable platform, alongside other women's and household magazines in the 1920s and 1930s.

In May 1927, women formed Kunuhoe (Friends of the Rose of Sharon), an organization that brought together nationalist and socialist women, including Christian and secular activists, to pursue education, social improvement, and women's rights. Local branches formed in Korea and among Korean communities abroad. The organization faced persistent difficulties uniting groups with different political goals; by 1931 its national activity had largely ceased amid internal disagreement, insufficient funds, and colonial repression. It did not issue a clear, formal nationwide dissolution declaration, and some local branches continued activity afterward. This section keeps the focus on gender and cultural organization; the wider history of mass movements and political organizing is Chapter 59's subject.

Women's publications provided platforms. Sinyeoja (March 1920), founded by Kim Il-yeop and associates, lasted only a few issues but opened discussion of women's independence. Sinyeoseong, relaunched in September 1923 under the Gaebyeok company, published essays, fiction, and household advice, and later magazines, such as Sin Gajeong (1933) and Yeoseong (1936), addressed a growing female readership with a mixture of domestic advice and modern fashion. These magazines combined feminist argument with commercial household content, and their contributors included both women and men.

Kunuhoe, founded on May 27, 1927, was the best-known women's organization of the decade. It brought together Christian women, socialist women, and nationalists in a united front, with leaders including Kim Hwal-lan, Hwang Sin-deok, and Yu Gak-gyeong, and established branches in Korea and abroad. Its stated aims included women's education, the abolition of feudal practices, and improvement of women's position, and it sponsored lectures, night classes, and campaigns on marriage reform. Internal debates between nationalist and socialist women over priorities, financial weakness, and police pressure reduced its activity, and by 1931 its national organization had effectively ceased to function, though no clear formal nationwide dissolution was declared and some local branches continued. This section is limited to gender and cultural organization; the political history of the united-front movement is treated in Chapter 59.

The contribution of these publications and organizations lay in making women's concerns public. A women's magazine could connect isolated readers, publish letters from provincial women, and create a pool of writers and editors; an organization like Kunuhoe could hold lectures and form branches that trained women in public speaking and administration. Their limits were equally clear: small circulations, dependence on a few educated leaders, and disagreement over whether women's liberation should be pursued separately or as part of a wider national or class movement. Contemporary police records and the organization's own reports document its meetings and branches, but the experience of ordinary members is thinly recorded.

Section 57.38

Childhood, Youth Culture, Bang Jeong-hwan, and the Idea of the Modern Child

Children came to be understood as a distinct audience. Bang Jeong-hwan (1899–1931), writer and activist associated with Cheondogyo youth organizations, promoted respect for children and is credited with popularizing the word eorini (child) as a respectful term. In May 1922 he and colleagues began Children's Day activities, and in March 1923 he helped launch the children's magazine Eorini, which published stories, poems, and songs. Children's literature, youth organizations, school clubs, and children's pages in newspapers formed a new cultural sector, and writers argued for children's education and rights.

Experience varied. Middle-class urban children might attend school, read magazines, and play organized games. Rural children often worked in fields or households, and girls in poor families might care for siblings, work as servants, or enter factories. Class and gender shaped childhood, and the modern ideal of the child was most visible in the lives of a minority.

Child-centered culture was promoted by educators, religious organizations, and writers. Bang Jeong-hwan, associated with Cheondogyo's youth organization, helped establish Children's Day on May 1, 1922, and the children's magazine Eorini in March 1923, which carried stories, poems, and songs by Bang and others and encouraged children to write for it. The word eorini, which gave the magazine its name, was promoted as a respectful term for children, in line with Cheondogyo's teaching about the dignity of every person. Children's Day events included parades, leaflets, and gatherings that were sometimes watched by police. Songs written for children, school choirs, and children's pages in papers helped to create a distinct repertoire.

The idea of the modern child shaped schooling, publishing, and family life, but actual childhood varied. A middle-class urban child might have school uniforms, books, and toys; a village child might work in the fields or care for siblings; a poor girl might serve as a household servant or enter a factory. Bang Jeong-hwan died young in 1931, but the movement he helped found continued, and the idea of children's rights in Korea has roots in these years.

Children's publishing was also a business and a field of ideological competition. Cheondogyo organizations, Christian groups, socialist youth circles, and commercial publishers produced rival children's magazines in the 1920s and 1930s, each with its own idea of a good child, and school texts under the Government-General offered another. Evidence includes surviving issues of children's magazines, newspaper reports of Children's Day events, memoirs, and, rarely, children's own writings submitted to periodicals. Because magazines published selected contributions by literate children, they show how children were taught to write about themselves more than how most children lived.

Section 57.39

Who Experienced Modernity? Class, Ethnicity, Gender, and the Urban-Rural Divide

Comparing costs and access clarifies the limits of generalization. Japanese settlers, concentrated in cities, enjoyed better school facilities, higher wages in many occupations, wider access to radio, and the best-served districts. Educated urban Koreans, a small group, could afford schooling, newspapers, occasional films, and some consumer goods. Industrial workers earned low wages and could afford little beyond necessities, and their access to schools and leisure was limited by time and cost. Women had less access than men to schooling and independent income. Rural populations, the large majority, had limited access to schools, newspapers, cinemas, and radios, though they were affected by commercial change, taxes, and migration.

The costs of schooling, newspapers, cinema tickets, receivers, and transport were significant relative to income for most households. Using comparative evidence guards against the assumption that visible modernity was widely shared. At the same time, it would be an error to assume that rural people were isolated or unchanged: markets, schools, travelers, newspapers read aloud, and migrants linked villages to the wider world.

A comparative reading of costs is the most direct test of generalization, with caution about what has been verified. The monthly radio license fee in 1927 was two yen, and only about 1,400 receivers were registered at opening; common school pupils paid tuition and fees, and university and professional college expenses were far higher; newspaper subscriptions required regular cash payment; cinema admission varied by theater and seat. These items can be compared with household budget surveys and wage series compiled by the Government-General and by Korean and Japanese researchers, but such comparisons require careful attention to year, occupation, and region, and this chapter does not reproduce figures that could not be checked against the sources. The qualitative conclusion is robust: many goods that appeared in advertisements and literature required more cash than most rural and many urban working households could spare.

The groups differed in kind of access as well as cost. Japanese settlers formed a significant share of Gyeongseong, Busan, and other cities and used schools, hospitals, and shops designed for them; educated Korean urbanites used a mixture of Korean and Japanese institutions; industrial workers could afford little leisure; women faced additional constraints; rural villagers could reach print and film mainly through intermediaries. At the same time, a spirit of selective appropriation appears in every group: villagers pooled money to buy a newspaper subscription or a school building, and urban workers saved for occasional cinema tickets. Modernity was shared unevenly and appropriated actively.

Section 57.40

Cultural Creativity under Colonial Rule: Accommodation, Criticism, and Resistance

Korean newspapers, schools, literature, language scholarship, cinema, radio, and associations operated under colonial restrictions while fostering new forms of expression and identity. They cannot be neatly sorted into pro-colonial and anti-colonial categories. A newspaper might promote Korean language and national pride while depending on advertisers and licenses and avoiding forbidden topics. A school might provide skills for colonial employment and also nurture national consciousness. A film might be a commercial melodrama approved by censors and later remembered as national allegory. An organization might cooperate with authorities in some ways and resist in others.

This complexity requires careful language. Historians distinguish accommodation, criticism, cooperation, and resistance, and recognize that individuals and institutions could move among them, or occupy several at once. A publication could be commercially successful, politically constrained, and socially significant simultaneously. Later wartime choices of some individuals, a matter of Chapter 60, should not be read backward to flatten earlier complexity.

Scholars have debated how to characterize colonial-era culture. Some emphasize the "colonial modernity" approach, which stresses that modern institutions in Korea emerged within colonial relations and were not simply an authentic indigenous modernity suppressed by Japan. Others criticize this approach for underplaying coercion or for seeming to normalize colonial rule, arguing that earlier Korean reform and capitalist development, from the late Joseon commercial economy and the Korean Empire's reforms, laid foundations that colonial rule distorted. A third group of scholars emphasizes the entanglement of Korean elites with colonial institutions and the ambiguity of "collaboration," asking when participation in licensed institutions amounted to cooperation. These debates are not settled, and this chapter draws on all three while avoiding both the idea that colonialism produced Korean modernity and the idea that Koreans lived outside it.

Examples show the difficulty of classification. A newspaper might promote Hangeul education and national pride while running advertisements for Japanese products and accepting licensing; a filmmaker might produce a work that censors passed and audiences read as national feeling; a school might teach Japanese language and prepare students for colonial employment while training the generation of local leaders; a cultural figure might criticize colonial policy in the 1920s and cooperate with it in the 1940s. Historicizing collaboration means asking what a person did, when, and under what pressure, and not applying a single label to a career or an institution.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Korean Cultural Practices from Late Imperial Reform through the 1930s

What changed?

Print, schooling, performance, entertainment, consumption, and gender discourse expanded, and new institutions such as radio, cinema, and department stores appeared.

What remained continuous?

Village schools, markets, lunar festivals, extended-family obligation, shamanic and Buddhist practice, and agricultural rhythms continued, and reformist ideas from the Korean Empire period shaped debates.

When did change occur?

Reform-era foundations were laid before 1910; expansion accelerated after 1919; a second wave occurred around 1927–1935.

Was change gradual, uneven, abrupt, contested, or cyclical?

Change was uneven and contested, concentrated in cities, interrupted by repression, and shaped by commercial and political forces.

Who experienced change most directly, and who greater continuity?

Urban students, writers, entrepreneurs, and some women experienced change most directly; most rural people experienced greater continuity alongside economic pressure.

Why does the pattern matter?

It shows that cultural modernization was real and uneven and cannot be assumed to represent the whole society.

Section 57.41

The Cultural Landscape on the Eve of the Intensified Wartime Period

By the mid-1930s Korea had a dense network of schools, a Korean-language press, a literary field, film and radio, department stores, and a vibrant urban culture, alongside persistent inequality and surveillance. These were achievements of Korean initiative within colonial constraints, and also embodiments of those constraints.

Pressure increased. KAPF was dissolved in 1935, the Dong-A Ilbo was suspended in 1936, and in the same year a new Governor-General, Minami Jirō, took office and began promoting policies of assimilation under the slogan of Japan and Korea as one body. Japan's seizure of Manchuria in 1931 (Chapter 61) had already made Korea more important strategically, and in 1937 full-scale war with China began. The intensification of control, mobilization, and cultural policy, including language and name policies, is examined in Chapters 60 and 62.

This is a boundary section. The cultural developments of the 1920s and early 1930s were possibilities experienced by participants who did not know what would follow, and they should not be read merely as a prelude to wartime repression. They also left legacies in institutions, language, literature, and memory that outlasted the colonial period.

The mid-1930s combined cultural maturity with rising control. The Manchurian Incident of September 1931 and Japan's creation of Manchukuo (Chapter 61) made Korea a strategic rear base and pushed the colonial government toward stricter control of dissent; arrests of communists and writers increased; KAPF members were prosecuted in 1931 and 1934 and the federation dissolved in 1935; the Dong-A Ilbo was suspended in August 1936; and in the same year Minami Jirō became Governor-General and pursued policies of assimilation, treated in Chapter 60. The Rural Revitalization Campaign launched by Governor-General Ugaki Kazushige from 1932, described in Chapter 58, brought the state into villages with a new mobilizing program. The outbreak of full-scale war with China in July 1937 deepened these trends.

At the same time cultural institutions continued to grow. Sound film, a Korean-language radio channel, expanded newspapers, mass magazines, new literary generations, and department stores all developed in the early to mid-1930s. This overlap means that students should avoid reading the earlier period as a pure "golden age" or as a mere prelude to repression: both expansion and control were happening at once, and participants could not foresee how the war would reshape their institutions. Treatment of language policy, shrine participation, name changes, forced closures, and wartime mobilization begins in Chapter 60.

Section 57.42

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This final section tests whether students can explain how cultural and institutional modernization differed from political self-determination, and how Korean agency operated within an unequal colonial system. It contains the chapter summary and review apparatus.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 57 has examined how colonial Korea developed new urban spaces, educational opportunities, communication networks, cultural industries, and social identities during the 1920s and early 1930s, and why those transformations remained inseparable from Japanese colonial power and social inequality.

The policy of cultural rule after 1919 created a limited legal space for Korean-language publishing, association, and education while preserving Japanese sovereignty, expanding police presence, and keeping censorship and licensing in place. It is best understood as both a concession to the pressures of 1919 and a method of control, and historians weigh these elements differently.

The chapter distinguished modernization, colonial modernity, and agency. Modernization names changes in technology, institutions, and daily practice; colonial modernity names the unequal forms those changes took; agency names Korean capacity to create, adapt, and contest. Together these concepts avoid both a story of stagnation and a story of benefit.

The colonial city, above all Gyeongseong, was a spatial hierarchy of Korean northern and Japanese southern districts, monumental colonial architecture such as the Government-General headquarters completed in 1926, and unevenly distributed services. Other cities, including Busan, Pyongyang, Incheon, and Daegu, show a network of differing centers, while migrants and the poor reveal the human costs of urban growth.

Education expanded under the 1922 ordinance but retained unequal structures of language, admission, and resources. Enrollment rose from a very low base without universal access, and the private university campaign of 1923 failed while Gyeongseong Imperial University opened its preparatory course in 1924 and its faculties in 1926. Mission schools, night schools, and literacy campaigns extended learning beyond the state system, and students became a distinct generational force.

Korean-owned newspapers returned in 1920, magazines multiplied, and literature, proletarian writing, and language standardization created a Korean reading public. Censorship, suspensions, and surveillance bounded debate, as the 1936 Dong-A Ilbo suspension shows, yet journalists, writers, and scholars used indirect strategies to build public discussion.

Theater, cinema, recorded music, and radio created new audiences and careers. Korean filmmaking, including Arirang in 1926 and the sound film Chunhyangjeon in 1935, must be distinguished from later national mythology, especially because many films are lost. Radio, beginning regular broadcasts in 1927, was expensive and unevenly accessible and served authorities as well as audiences.

Consumer society, fashion, cafés, advertising, and sports showed modernity in daily life, but advertisements reveal marketing, not affordability, and credit and clock time reorganized daily life unevenly. Women, youth, and children became subjects of debate: the New Woman challenged norms, but most women lived in labor and household conditions very different from those of educated writers, and children’s culture, led by figures such as Bang Jeong-hwan, reached some more than others.

Comparative evidence shows that modern culture was concentrated by class, ethnicity, gender, and region. Cultural activity cannot be sorted neatly into pro- and anti-colonial categories, and institutions could be commercially successful, constrained, and socially significant at once.

The chapter closed at a boundary: by 1936–1937, pressure on cultural organizations was increasing as militarization and war with China approached. The developments of the 1920s and early 1930s should be understood as possibilities experienced by their participants, with legacies in language, institutions, and memory, rather than only as a prelude to later repression.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a society can acquire modern institutions without acquiring sovereignty, and how those institutions can carry both the marks of unequal power and the energy of the people who built and used them. Understanding colonial modernity avoids two errors: assuming that colonial rule produced Korean modernity, and assuming that Koreans lived outside it.

The processes examined here shaped what came later. The Korean-language press, standardized orthography, literary field, film and broadcasting industries, and educated generation became foundations for cultural life in both Korean states after 1945, though each state remembered and used them differently. The debates over gender, education, and consumption continued long after the colonial period.

The chapter also connects to its neighbors.
