The March First Movement and the Korean Provisional Government

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

At about two o'clock on the afternoon of March 1, 1919, the same declaration of independence was read aloud in two different places in Seoul: in a restaurant called Taehwagwan, before a small company of men who then waited to be arrested, and in Tapgol Park, before a crowd that had gathered in the expectation of hearing it. Within weeks, Koreans in hundreds of towns and villages had followed, and by the end of the year a republican government in exile existed in Shanghai. The question this chapter asks is how a protest became a political order.

The ceremony at Taehwagwan was deliberately small and, by design, ended in arrest. Twenty-nine of the thirty-three men who had put their names to the declaration were present; they read the text, notified the colonial police of what they had done, and waited. In Tapgol Park, a short distance away, students and townspeople who had received handbills or heard word of mouth listened to the declaration being read, shouted manse ("ten thousand years"), unfurled the Korean flag, and set off in processions through the streets. The two events were connected but distinct, and the distinction is one of the first things this chapter asks students to keep in view.

Eight years after annexation, the Government-General ruled Korea through a police and gendarmerie apparatus whose construction was examined in Chapter 55. It restricted public assembly, supervised the press and limited Korean access to higher education. Yet in the winter of 1918–1919 the networks that colonial rule had not destroyed, among them Christian congregations and mission schools, the Cheondogyo religious community, Buddhist circles, student associations in Tokyo, and migrant and exile communities abroad, were pulled together by two events: the end of the First World War, with its international discussion of national self-determination, and the death of the former emperor Gojong on January 21, 1919.

The movement that followed was not a single event. It began as a carefully planned declaration by a small coalition of religious leaders, but it became a mass movement whose later phases were initiated by communities that had received no instruction from Seoul. Its participants included farmers, market vendors, schoolgirls, teachers, workers, monks, pastors, and local notables. The Japanese authorities responded with police, gendarmes, and army units. Some demonstrations stayed peaceful and some did not, and some incidents of repression, such as the killings at Jeam-ri in April, became part of the historical memory of the period.

The chapter has a second subject. Declarations of independence do not themselves constitute governments, and the political history of 1919 includes the effort to turn mass assertion of sovereignty into institutions. In March and April, three separate provisional-government initiatives arose, in the Russian Far East, in Seoul, and in Shanghai. They had different leaders, different claims and different sources of legitimacy. The Shanghai body was proclaimed on April 11, and a constitutional settlement of September 11 unified the competing claims. Students must not collapse these into a single founding date.

Evidence for the events is rich but uneven. The text of the declarations survives; Japanese police, gendarmerie, and judicial records survive in large quantity; missionaries and journalists left testimony; and Korean nationalist historians compiled accounts almost immediately, most influentially Park Eun-sik's account written in exile. Each of these sources has limits. Japanese records reflect the viewpoint of the repressive apparatus, and nationalist compilations were produced for political purposes. The chapter therefore pays particular attention to how historians weigh figures and testimony.

This chapter lies between Chapter 55, which explained how the colonial state was built, and Chapter 57, which turns to colonial urban life, education and mass culture. Its principal narrative ends in 1919. Later developments, including the longer operation of Japan's revised colonial policies and the later independence movements, are treated in Chapters 57 to 60 and in Chapters 59 and 63, and are referred to here only to explain consequence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the March First Movement became possible in early 1919, identifying the domestic, diaspora, and international conditions that preceded it.
  2. Distinguish the February 8 Tokyo declaration, the March First declaration, and the provisional-government proclamations of 1919 as different kinds of documents with different purposes.
  3. Reconstruct the events of March 1 in Seoul, distinguishing the meeting of the national representatives at Taehwagwan from the public reading at Tapgol Park.
  4. Trace the geographic and social spread of the movement from March through April, identifying the mechanisms by which it expanded beyond its original organizers.
  5. Analyze Japanese repression, including the roles of police, gendarmes and the army, and the incidents at Aunae and Jeam-ri.
  6. Evaluate participation and casualty figures from nationalist and Japanese sources, and explain why they differ.
  7. Assess the role of diaspora communities and of international opinion, and why they did not produce recognition of Korean independence.
  8. Compare the Russian Far East, Hanseong, and Shanghai provisional-government initiatives and explain how they were unified in September 1919.
  9. Interpret the provisional charter of April 11 and the constitution of September 11, identifying their claims about sovereignty and their practical limits.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether the movement was a failure, a transformation, or both.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What conditions in early 1919 made coordinated organizing across religions, schools, and borders possible?
  3. Why did Korean activists believe that the postwar international order might favor independence, and why did it not?
  4. How did Japanese authorities respond, and what do their responses reveal about the structure of colonial rule?

  8. In what sense did the movement fail, and in what sense did it transform Korean politics?

Historical Context

Korea had been annexed by Japan in August 1910, ending the Korean Empire proclaimed by Gojong in 1897 (Chapter 53) and placing the peninsula under a Governor-General appointed by Tokyo. As Chapter 55 explains, the Government-General combined military-police authority with civilian administration, carried out a cadastral survey and imposed restrictions on Korean organizations and publications. Korean political life did not end. It moved into schools, churches, religious communities, and small associations, and abroad into Manchuria, the Russian Far East, China, Japan, Hawaii, and the American mainland.

The decade before 1919 had also left a heritage of ideas. Reform and enlightenment movements before annexation (Chapters 49 to 54) had produced newspapers, schools, and political societies; the Donghak tradition (Chapters 46 and 51) lived on in Cheondogyo; and mission Christianity, particularly Presbyterian and Methodist, had built schools and congregations that provided organizational capacity. The notion that sovereignty belonged to something broader than a monarch had begun to take root, although Korean political thought remained diverse, and some activists still looked to the restoration of the monarchy.

The international setting changed abruptly in 1918. The collapse of the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires, the Bolshevik revolution, Woodrow Wilson's public statements about the postwar order, and the convening of a peace conference in Paris all encouraged subject peoples to press their claims. These principles were not applied equally. Japan was one of the victorious powers, and the peacemakers showed little appetite for dismantling the colonial holdings of their own side. The tension between encouraged expectations and limited outcomes is a recurring theme of the chapter.

Section 56.1

Korea after Eight Years of Japanese Colonial Rule

This section recalls only what students need from Chapter 55. By early 1919 the Government-General had spent eight years building a system in which military-police authority reached into daily life. The gendarmerie, a military police force that also performed ordinary policing, was supplemented by civilian police, and ordinances restricted public assembly, the press, and private associations. Korean-language publication was heavily limited, and the main Korean-language daily was the Government-General's own organ. Political organizations had been broken up: the prosecution of Korean Christian and nationalist figures in 1911 and 1912, known as the 105-Person Incident, demonstrated what open organizing could cost.

Colonial rule also produced economic and social grievances. The land survey and related legal changes (Chapter 55) altered property documentation in ways that disadvantaged many cultivators, and taxation and administrative requirements fell on rural communities. The educational system was organized so that opportunities beyond primary school were limited for Koreans, while mission schools and private schools, which the authorities regulated, remained important alternatives. Korean elites varied in their relationship to the regime; some cooperated, some retreated into private life, and some kept up covert nationalist ties.

It would be a mistake to see colonial Korea as silenced. Churches continued to meet; Cheondogyo maintained a nationwide organization; students formed informal circles; and Koreans who left the peninsula built communities and organizations abroad. What these networks lacked was a common occasion and a shared language of action. The events of 1918 and early 1919 supplied both.

Evidence for conditions on the eve of the movement comes from several kinds of source, and each has its own bias. Government-General publications, annual reports, and ordinance texts describe what the regime intended, and they present colonial rule as orderly administration. Mission reports, which survive in church archives, describe the same years from the viewpoint of foreign residents who depended on the regime's permission but sympathized with many Korean grievances. Memoirs written after liberation record how participants remembered the period, and they were shaped by the later knowledge that 1919 was approaching. A historian reading across these sources learns to treat each description of the "calm" of the 1910s skeptically: a colonial administration that restricts public speech will often appear quiet in its own records.

A second caution concerns the word "colonial" itself. In 1919 the people who lived under this system did not all experience it in the same way. A rural cultivator, an urban shopkeeper, a clerk in a government office, a mission-school student, and a landowner with connections to the administration faced different pressures and different possibilities. The movement of 1919 drew on this variety, and its later breadth cannot be understood without recognizing that dissatisfaction with colonial rule took many forms before it found a common expression.

Section 56.2

Gojong's Death, National Mourning, and the March 1919 Funeral

Gojong died at his residence in Seoul on January 21, 1919, and the news was announced the following day. He had been forced to abdicate in 1907 and was thereafter styled by the Japanese court as a retired king of the Yi house. For many Koreans, however, he remained the last monarch of an independent state. His death therefore produced widespread mourning, and mourning altars were set up in Seoul and in provincial towns.

Rumors that he had been poisoned spread quickly, and they remain part of the popular memory of the period. Historians distinguish between the existence of the rumors, which is well documented, and the claim itself, which is not established by the available medical or documentary evidence. The official Japanese statement attributed the death to illness. No independent examination was conducted, and the surviving allegations rest on hearsay and later recollection. What can be said securely is that many Koreans believed the rumor, and that its circulation intensified anger and suspicion toward the authorities. Rumors about Prince Yi Eun's engagement to a member of the Japanese imperial family also circulated, and also reflect the climate of distrust.

The funeral was scheduled for March 3, and the authorities allowed mourners to travel to Seoul. Funeral gatherings are one of the few circumstances in which a colonial regime that restricts assembly can find it difficult to forbid large crowds. Organizers understood that thousands of visitors from the provinces would be in the capital, and that a declaration distributed before the funeral could reach them. The funeral did not cause the movement, which had been in preparation since at least the previous autumn, but it shaped its timing and its reach.

Funerals have a particular political character in societies where public assembly is restricted. A funeral is a sanctioned gathering, with its own rules of conduct, a defined schedule, and an expectation that large numbers of people will travel to attend. Colonial authorities in many settings have found them difficult to forbid, and difficult to police without appearing disrespectful. In Korea in 1919, the combination of Confucian norms of mourning, popular attachment to the dynasty, and political resentment gave the funeral a significance that went beyond the death of an individual. Mourning garments, altars, and processions were forms of expression that could carry more than one meaning.

It is also worth noting what the funeral did not do. It did not produce the independence movement, which had been planned before and which used its own timing. Historians sometimes summarize the relationship as one of opportunity: the funeral offered a time and a place, and the organizers decided to use it, while taking care not to profane the ceremony. The scale of the mourning also suggests how much feeling for the former emperor survived in a population that had lived under Japanese rule for a decade, and that feeling should be distinguished from a political program of restoring the monarchy, which fewer activists held by 1919.

Section 56.3

The First World War, Wilsonian Self-Determination, and the Paris Peace Conference

The armistice of November 11, 1918 ended the fighting in Europe, and the Paris Peace Conference opened in January 1919 to settle the postwar order. Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, announced in January 1918, had called for an open diplomatic order and a settlement of colonial claims that gave weight to the interests of the populations concerned. In other speeches in 1918 he spoke in broader terms about self-determination. The Bolshevik government, for its part, had declared the right of peoples to determine their own future. These ideas circulated through newspapers, missionary networks, and diaspora organizations, and Korean activists read them as an opening.

The reality was more limited. Wilson's statements were most directly applied to territories of the defeated empires, in Central and Eastern Europe and in the Ottoman and German colonial realms. Neither Wilson nor the other principal Allied governments intended to apply self-determination to colonies held by victorious powers, and Japan was one of the five principal powers at Paris. Korea, Egypt, India, Ireland, and other colonized societies all sent petitions or delegations, and in general none obtained a hearing. In late 1918 an American envoy, Charles Crane, visited Shanghai and met with Korean activists, whose accounts suggest that they came away with hopes of American attention. Such reports, and the inflated expectations they created, appear in the recollections of several participants.

Students should therefore keep two points in view. First, the language of self-determination was a real and consequential intellectual resource; it gave Korean activists a framework acceptable to international audiences. Second, the peace settlement did not guarantee, and in the event did not produce, recognition of Korean independence. The movement's organizers understood this to varying degrees. Some counted on international intervention, while others saw the declaration as an assertion of principle and a way to keep the question before the world.

The Paris Peace Conference was not a single negotiation but a sequence of committees and bargains among the principal powers, in which questions of the defeated powers' territories, reparations, and the League of Nations took precedence. Colonial petitions arrived from many parts of the world, and they were mostly received by secretariats and sympathetic individuals and not by the decision-makers. Japan's delegation pursued its own agenda, including a proposal on racial equality and claims regarding former German possessions in China and the Pacific. The Korean question was not on the agenda, and Japan had no incentive to put it there.

This context helps explain a theme that runs through the rest of the chapter. Korean activists were responding to a real change in the international discussion, one in which the legitimacy of empire was being questioned in principle. But the political arrangements of the peace settlement gave the victors broad discretion. The distance between principle and practice was a source of disappointment in many colonized societies in 1919, and the Korean case belongs to a wider pattern of encounters between anti-colonial movements and the postwar order, which produced protests in several regions that year.

Historical Vocabulary
Self-Determination, Republicanism, and Popular Sovereignty

Self-determination. The principle, widely discussed in 1918 and 1919, that peoples should decide their own political status.

Historical context. Associated with Wilson and with Bolshevik declarations, it became a standard idiom of anti-colonial petitions.

Why it matters. Korean activists used it to frame independence in terms that international audiences recognized.

Potential misunderstanding. It was not a legal rule that bound the victorious powers, and it was applied selectively.

Republicanism. A form of government in which authority derives from the citizens and is exercised through representative institutions rather than inherited monarchy.

Historical context. The provisional institutions of 1919 adopted republican forms, in contrast to the Korean Empire.

Why it matters. It marks a change in the vocabulary of Korean legitimacy.

Potential misunderstanding. It does not mean that every Korean activist in 1919 rejected monarchy.

Popular sovereignty. The doctrine that ultimate political authority rests with the people.

Historical context. The September 1919 constitution stated that sovereignty resided in the people.

Why it matters. It was a claim about legitimacy made before any government controlled territory.

Potential misunderstanding. A constitutional statement is not the same as the effective exercise of power.

Section 56.4

Korean Independence Networks before March 1919

Several networks converged in 1918 and 1919. Inside Korea, the most important were religious. The Presbyterian and Methodist churches, with their schools, hospitals, and congregations, provided meeting places and a means of communicating across provinces. Cheondogyo, the successor of the Donghak movement, had a nationwide membership, a hierarchy, and financial resources, and its leader Son Byeong-hui had been working to reconstitute its organization. Buddhist figures such as Han Yong-un and Baek Yong-seong had their own contacts. Student circles in Seoul and in other cities, many of them connected to mission schools, formed another network.

Outside Korea, the networks were more diverse. In Manchuria and the adjacent Russian Maritime Province, Korean migrant communities supported schools, associations, and independence societies, and several exiled activists traveled between them. In Shanghai, young activists organized the New Korean Youth Party in late 1918 and planned to send a representative to Paris. In Japan, Korean students formed associations that discussed independence and, in February 1919, issued a declaration. In the United States and Hawaii, the Korean National Association and other organizations raised funds and published newspapers, and Syngman Rhee and An Chang-ho were prominent figures.

One document circulating in this period is known as the Muo Declaration, or Daejong Declaration, associated with Korean exiles in Manchuria and signed by about three dozen figures. It is traditionally called the “Muo Declaration” and dated to 1918 or February 1919 in older references, but that chronology is disputed. The National Institute of Korean History cites the diary of Jeong Won-taek and documentary research indicating that Jo So-ang drafted it after March 1 and that copies were printed around March 11, 1919. Other archival catalogues date a surviving declaration to February 1919. Consequently it should not be used as secure proof of a declaration published before the March First demonstrations; it instead illustrates the wider mobilization and divergent documentary chronologies of the 1919 independence movement. The chapter's next division shows how these separate networks were drawn together.

Reconstructing these networks requires combining sources that were never meant to be read together. Church records describe congregations and schools; Cheondogyo materials describe its internal organization; student publications and police files describe associations in Tokyo and Seoul; and exile newspapers and later memoirs describe the communities abroad. The links among them were often personal: a teacher who had studied in the same school as a Cheondogyo official, a pastor who corresponded with a missionary, a student who traveled between Tokyo and Seoul during a school break. Such connections do not appear in formal organization charts, and historians infer them from testimony and from the pattern of events.

The Muo Declaration illustrates the difficulty. Surviving copies exist, and the list of signers is known in outline, but the circumstances of its drafting and the extent of its circulation are less clear. The familiar “1918” label is not a reliable publication date; Jo So-ang is identified as principal author, and recent source-based analysis argues for composition and circulation in 1919 after March First, although older published chronologies differ. This is a useful reminder that even a well-known document can raise questions of dating and transmission, and that historians have to treat conventional labels as starting points for inquiry.

Section 56.5

The February 8 Declaration by Korean Students in Tokyo

Korean students in Tokyo formed one of the most politically active communities of Koreans abroad. Their Korean Students' Association, centered on the Korean YMCA building in the capital, held discussions during the winter of 1918 and 1919, and a committee of student activists prepared a declaration of independence. The text was drafted by Yi Gwang-su, then a student and already a prominent writer; other organizers included Choe Pal-yong, Song Gye-baek, and Kim Do-yeon, among others. It was composed in Korean, Japanese, and English, and copies were sent to the Japanese Diet, to foreign embassies, and to newspapers.

On February 8, several hundred Korean students assembled at the YMCA, where the declaration was read. Police broke up the meeting and arrested a number of participants, including the organizers; some were later fined or imprisoned. Yi Gwang-su had by this time left for Shanghai, where he continued to work with activists. The declaration was notable for its direct language, which placed the blame for annexation on Japanese policy and warned of continuing conflict if independence were not recognized, a more confrontational tone than the later March First declaration.

The Tokyo declaration mattered in two ways. It showed that organized independence activity was possible even inside the empire's capital, and it reached Korea through returning students and through written copies, encouraging organizers there who were already preparing their own declaration. Historians generally treat it as an important precedent and influence, not as the direct cause of March First, which grew from separate domestic planning.

Student communities have often played a distinctive role in anti-colonial politics. They were literate, they moved between cities and countries, they had access to foreign publications, and they were comparatively free of the family and occupational obligations that constrained older leaders. The Korean students in Tokyo lived in the capital of the empire that ruled their homeland, and many studied politics, law, or economics. They were exposed to Japanese liberal and socialist debates and to the news of Europe. At the same time they were subject to police surveillance and to the pressures that came with being colonial subjects in the metropole.

The decision to write in three languages indicates the audiences the authors imagined: Koreans, Japanese officials and public, and foreign governments and press. The attempt to deliver copies to the Diet and to embassies was a form of political communication that depended on the open publicity of the imperial capital. The consequences for the students were serious, and some were imprisoned, but the episode demonstrated that independence could be demanded openly in Tokyo before it was demanded in Seoul, which gave organizers at home a model to consider.

Context: The declaration was prepared by a committee of Korean students in Tokyo in early 1919 and distributed to Japanese and foreign audiences around the meeting at the Korean YMCA on February 8.

Excerpt or paraphrase: A paraphrase of the declaration's themes follows. It is not a quotation.

The declaration states that the Korean people have a long independent history and a distinct civilization, that annexation was imposed against the will of the Korean people, that Japanese rule has harmed Korean interests, and that the signatories, invoking the principle of national self-determination, demand independence and appeal to the powers meeting after the war. Section 56.6

Cheondogyo, Christianity, Buddhism, and the Construction of an Independence Coalition

In Korea, the organizing proceeded through religious communities because they were the only large associations the colonial regime had not suppressed. Cheondogyo leaders, among them Son Byeong-hui, Choe Rin, and Kwon Dong-jin, were among the first to consider a nationwide declaration. They contributed organizational capacity, funds, and, crucially, ownership of a printing house, Boseongsa. Christian leaders, including Yi Seung-hun, a merchant and founder of the Osan School, and Pak Hui-do, approached the question from within their own networks and contacted students in Seoul. Buddhists were brought in through contacts with Han Yong-un.

The negotiation between these groups was not simple. Cheondogyo and Christian leaders initially worked separately and combined their efforts in the final weeks of February. The groups differed in theology, in the social composition of their memberships, and in political method. Some preferred petitions to the Government-General or to the peace conference, while others favored a public declaration. The coalition settled on a declaration followed by a nonviolent public demonstration. Confucian scholars were also approached, but did not join the coalition, and they pursued a separate appeal, sent in the following weeks, to the Paris conference.

The role of religious networks should not be overstated or understated. They provided money, printing, meeting places, and communication channels, and these were indispensable. But the participation that followed was wider than the coalition's membership, a point explored in the Historical Debate in Division IV.

Religious organizations were more than doctrines. They were institutions with buildings, funds, schools, printing presses, regular meetings, and leaders who commanded loyalty. In a setting where secular political associations were restricted, these institutions had a degree of protection that other bodies lacked. Mission churches had the shelter of foreign citizenship among their missionaries, which gave them some insulation, although it did not prevent police suspicion. Cheondogyo had suffered persecution in the late Joseon period, and its leaders had learned to maintain organization carefully.

The three religious communities also differed in their approach to politics. Some Christian leaders worried that political activity might endanger the churches and schools they had built, and the missions themselves generally urged their members to avoid politics. Some Cheondogyo leaders had previous experience of mass mobilization in the Donghak tradition (Chapters 46 and 51) and had to weigh this inheritance against the risks. The decision to cooperate across these boundaries was therefore not automatic, and it needed negotiation. The result was a coalition limited in its representativeness, but effective in its reach.

Section 56.7

The Thirty-Three National Representatives and Their Political Strategy

The declaration was issued in the names of thirty-three men conventionally called the national representatives. Fifteen were associated with Cheondogyo, sixteen with Christian churches, and two with Buddhism. They included Son Byeong-hui, the leader of Cheondogyo; Yi Seung-hun; Han Yong-un and Baek Yong-seong, the two Buddhists; and Gil Seon-ju, a Presbyterian pastor from the north. They were not elected, and they did not claim to be a formal delegation. They were chosen because they were recognizable figures with organizations behind them and because they were willing to accept the consequences.

Their strategy was to publish a declaration, make sure the authorities knew who had issued it, accept arrest, and rely on public sympathy and international attention. They explicitly rejected violence. The strategy had advantages, since leaders who surrendered voluntarily could not easily be portrayed as insurgents, and it gave the movement a morally clear identity. It also had limits. The representatives were all men, almost all middle-aged, and they did not include workers, women, or representatives of rural communities. Because they intended to be arrested, they did not plan the later course of the movement, and they could not direct it from prison.

Later nationalist and popular memory has sometimes treated the thirty-three as the movement's entire leadership. Historians instead treat them as the authors of its opening act, whose strategy was soon overtaken by the actions of others.

The strategy of the representatives has attracted both admiration and criticism. Admirers emphasize the moral clarity of the act: by announcing the declaration and accepting arrest, the signatories made it clear that they were not conspirators, and they gave the movement a principled identity that attracted international sympathy. Critics, including some contemporaries, noted that the strategy left the movement without leaders once the representatives were in prison, and that it depended on a belief that the powers meeting in Paris might respond to a peaceful appeal.

The composition of the group also deserves attention. The absence of women, workers, and rural leaders reflects the constraints of the organizing process: the coalition drew on existing religious hierarchies, which were male, and on the people who could be approached in a short time without alerting the police. It would be anachronistic to expect a modern representative body in these circumstances, but it is accurate to note that the representatives spoke for a particular segment of Korean society, and that others, as the following divisions show, acted independently of them.

Section 56.8

Choe Nam-seon and the March First Declaration of Independence

The text issued by the representatives is known as the Gimi Independence Declaration, after the sexagenary-cycle name of the year 1919. It was drafted by Choe Nam-seon, a historian and man of letters, who did not sign it himself, at the request of the organizers. Han Yong-un added a short set of three pledges at the end. The text was dated using a calendar counting from the traditional founding of Gojoseon, the 4252nd year, an echo of the tradition discussed in Chapter 1 and Chapter 6.

The declaration proclaims that Korea is an independent state and its people a self-governing people, and it announces this to all nations as an affirmation of human equality. It describes annexation as the product of coercion and of an old way of thinking based on force. It states that Korea's independence will contribute to the peace of East Asia and of the world, and it calls on Japan to abandon its policy of domination. It avoids hostility toward the Japanese people as such. The closing pledges call on Koreans to act in a spirit of principle and not of anger, to continue to the end, and to behave in an orderly and honorable manner.

Students should work from translations carefully. Many popular versions paraphrase or condense the text, and later patriotic retellings have added phrases that are not in the original. The declaration is a statement of principle addressed to Koreans, Japanese, and the world. It was not a constitution and did not establish a government.

The declaration's intellectual language rewards close reading. It draws on several sources at once: a vocabulary of universal human rights and equality that echoes Wilsonian and Western liberal language; a historical argument about the long existence of the Korean state and people; and an East Asian rhetoric of peace and of the shared interests of neighboring countries. Its tone toward Japan is critical but not hostile, in keeping with its pledge of orderly conduct. Readers have interpreted this mixture in different ways: as tactical moderation, as sincere conviction, or as a combination.

The authorship also shows how a collective text was produced. Choe Nam-seon drafted it, but the signatories approved it, and Han Yong-un contributed the closing pledges. Choe's decision not to sign has been explained in various ways, including a wish to continue writing and publishing, and students should note that explanations of motive remain interpretive. What can be said securely is that the text circulated in printed form, that it was read aloud in many places, and that its moral and historical claims were repeated and adapted in later declarations.

Section 56.9

Secret Printing, Distribution, and the Decision to Demonstrate on March First

The declaration was printed at Boseongsa, a press owned by Cheondogyo and managed by Yi Jong-il, on the night of February 27. Approximately 21,000 copies were produced, and they were distributed over the following day through networks of students, church members and Cheondogyo officials, who carried copies by train and on foot to Seoul's districts and to provincial centers, including Pyongyang, Euiju, Seoncheon, Wonsan, and Haeju. Distribution could not be perfectly synchronized, and local implementation depended on whether individual organizers accepted the plan, so students should treat the coordination as impressive but incomplete.

The organizers had at first considered acting on March 3, the day of the funeral. They dropped the idea because a demonstration on the day of the funeral risked disrespect to the dead and the possibility of violent confrontation among the mourning crowds. March 2 was a Sunday, and Christian leaders objected to a demonstration on the Sabbath. March 1, a Saturday, was therefore chosen. For the same reason of caution, the organizers also moved the representatives' own ceremony from Tapgol Park to a restaurant, Taehwagwan, fearing that a large crowd would produce disorder.

The decision to hold a public reading in Tapgol Park was made separately by student organizers, who had been told of the plan and intended to bring crowds to hear the declaration read. The coalition's commitment to public mobilization, in other words, had two components, one planned by the representatives and one by students, a division that determined what happened on March 1 itself.

The printing of the declaration was an act of logistics as well as politics. A press had to be available, paper and ink had to be obtained, and the operation had to be kept secret long enough to allow distribution. The use of a press owned by a religious organization made this possible, since it could produce the pamphlets in the guise of ordinary work. Distribution relied on trusted individuals traveling by railway to carry bundles to local contacts, who in turn passed them on. A single missed connection or an informant could have led to arrest, and the fact that copies reached many towns on the eve of March 1 indicates careful planning and a degree of luck.

The choice of date shows how religious and practical concerns shaped political decisions. The reluctance to hold a demonstration on the Sabbath, and the concern over the funeral, were considerations that a purely strategic account would overlook. The coordination was also never complete: some localities received the declaration late, some organizers decided to wait, and some places acted on their own. Historians therefore describe March 1 as a coordinated beginning with variable local implementation, which is a more accurate description than a simple picture of a single simultaneous event.

Section 56.10

March 1, 1919: The Declaration at Taehwagwan

On the afternoon of March 1, twenty-nine of the thirty-three signatories assembled at Taehwagwan, a restaurant in central Seoul. The four absent signatories were Gil Seon-ju, Kim Byeong-jo, Yu Yeo-dae, and Jeong Chun-su, who were away from the restaurant, including representatives active in northern communities. Their absence does not mean they withdrew their signatures; Yu Yeo-dae and Kim Byeong-jo, for example, participated in the demonstrations at Uiju. Sources differ on some individual movements and should be read alongside trial records. The signatories had decided not to go to Tapgol Park, where a crowd was gathering, because they feared that a mass meeting might provoke violence. Instead they met in the restaurant's rooms, where they read the declaration, and Han Yong-un is generally reported to have given a short address before the group cheered for Korean independence.

The representatives then arranged for the police to be told what had happened. They sent word to the colonial police authorities, and in the course of the afternoon police arrived and took them into custody. By the evening the leaders of the coalition were in detention. In the following days the other representatives, and Choe Nam-seon, who had written the text, were also arrested or surrendered. Their conduct was consistent with the strategy they had chosen: they had issued the declaration, announced it, and accepted the consequences.

The ceremony at Taehwagwan should be distinguished from the demonstration at Tapgol Park. The signatories did not lead the crowd, and the crowd did not wait for them. A narrative that places all thirty-three men in the park, which later popular accounts sometimes give, is inaccurate. The movement began with two connected but separate acts, and its subsequent course was determined largely by the second.

The Taehwagwan meeting is a useful case for thinking about how historical events are remembered. In popular retellings, the scene is often compressed into a single image: the signatories read the declaration and surrender. The documents suggest a more complex sequence, with the decision to move from the park to the restaurant, the choice to notify the police, and the arrest all unfolding in the course of an afternoon. Contemporary reports, trial records, and later recollections by participants provide the evidence, and they differ on small details, such as the exact time and the words spoken.

The ceremony also had a symbolic logic. By announcing their act to the authorities, the signatories reinforced the claim that they were not hiding, and that the declaration was a public statement and not a plot. This had legal consequences, since they could be charged with offences under colonial law, and political consequences, since it marked the movement as an assertion of principle. It is a reminder that some forms of resistance depend on the willingness of participants to accept punishment.

The absentees also complicate the familiar image of passive leaders waiting to be arrested. In Uiju, Yu Yeo-dae and Kim Byeong-jo were associated with local protest activity; the latter eventually escaped Japanese custody and became active abroad. For students comparing political strategies, this means the group of thirty-three cannot be reduced to the conduct of the twenty-nine assembled at Taehwagwan. The organizational coalition contained members with distinct regional responsibilities and different subsequent experiences.

Section 56.11

Tapgol Park, Students, and the Public Declaration of Independence

Tapgol Park, in the heart of Seoul's old commercial quarter, was the site that student organizers had chosen for the public reading. Students from Seoul's schools, including those linked to mission and private institutions, had been informed by their own contacts, and many ordinary residents had heard of the gathering through handbills and word of mouth. By early afternoon several thousand people are generally estimated to have assembled, though no precise count exists.

When it became clear that the representatives would not attend, a student, Jeong Jae-yong, is reported to have mounted the pavilion and read the declaration. The crowd responded with cries of manse, and flags of Korea, the Taegeukgi, were displayed in defiance of the colonial prohibition on Korean national symbols. The crowd then formed processions. Students and townspeople moved out into the streets, marching toward Deoksugung, the former imperial palace where Gojong's mourning altar stood, and then along main streets, carrying the flag and shouting.

For many in the crowd the day was a mixture of political demonstration and funeral mourning. The police, taken by surprise at the scale of the gathering, did not at first use force against the processions. The separate but connected roles of the representatives and the street demonstrators, a legacy of the planning described in Division II, are essential to understanding what happened next: the independence movement now had a public presence which was no longer under the control of its organizers.

Tapgol Park was a public space in the commercial center of Seoul, an area through which many ordinary people passed. A crowd gathering there on the afternoon of a Saturday could draw in passers-by who had not been told of the plan, and the mix of organized students and curious townspeople made the event more fluid than a ceremony. Observers of the time recorded the surprise of the police, the excitement of the participants, and the display of the national flag, which had been banned.

The reading of the declaration by a student in the representatives' absence is often cited as a sign of the movement's improvisational character. The students had planned to hear the declaration read by older leaders, and when this did not happen, they acted. The episode is a good example of how a plan can be reshaped in the moment, and how those who carried it forward were not simply following instructions. It also illustrates why historians prefer the careful distinction between Taehwagwan and Tapgol Park: the two events involved different people with different intentions, and the movement's later course owed more to the second.

Section 56.12

The Seoul Processions, Police Response, and the Mobilization of the Capital

The processions followed several routes through central Seoul, passing commercial districts, the area around Deoksugung, and the neighborhoods where foreign consulates and missions were located. Some groups went to the residences of consuls to present copies of the declaration, an effort to place the independence claim before foreign governments. The participants included students, merchants, workers, and ordinary residents. Contemporary accounts note that the mood on March 1 was peaceful and that the crowd's conduct was disciplined.

The police response developed over the following days. Seoul's police and the gendarmerie, together with army units stationed in the capital, were deployed in the streets, and arrests began on March 1 and increased rapidly. The authorities attempted to prevent further gatherings, shut down printing, and trace the origins of the handbills. Demonstrations in the capital continued over several days. On March 2 and 3 there were further crowds, and mourners for Gojong's funeral added to the numbers. In the following weeks shopkeepers in Seoul closed their businesses in protest, and workers held demonstrations of their own, notably a labor demonstration on March 22.

Underground newspapers and handbills were printed and circulated. The authorities had a large surveillance apparatus, but it was designed for a population under tight control, and it was not organized to cope with a movement that arose from many directions. For several weeks, demonstrations in Seoul recurred, and the capital's authorities found it difficult to restore routine order.

Policing a large demonstration in an unfamiliar situation tests an institution's training and assumptions. The police in Seoul were accustomed to surveillance, arrests of individuals, and control of small assemblies. Dispersing processions of thousands moving through different parts of the city required coordination between police, gendarmes, and army units, and the evidence suggests that this took some days to organize. In the meantime the processions succeeded in reaching their destinations, delivering declarations to foreign consulates and gathering at the palace.

The participation of merchants and workers is a significant indication of the movement's reach into urban society. The closure of shops, a form of protest that cost the shopkeepers income, was a visible sign of solidarity, and it made the movement's presence felt in the daily life of the city. Labor demonstrations by workers show that participation was not limited to students and elites. The underground press that grew in the following weeks was another way in which the capital's population participated, by writing, copying, and distributing news and handbills.

Section 56.13

Gojong's Funeral and the Student-Led Demonstrations of March 5

The funeral of Gojong took place on March 3, following a procession through Seoul that drew very large crowds of mourners. Some mourners carried or shouted independence slogans, but the authorities, deploying troops and police in large numbers, prevented a major demonstration during the funeral.

The second major mobilization in Seoul came on March 5, when students planned a large demonstration at Namdaemun Station, the railway terminus at the southern gate. Student leaders, including Kim Won-byeok and Gang Gi-deok, organized the gathering, which brought together students from many Seoul schools together with townspeople. Several thousand people are reported to have gathered, and the crowd marched toward the station and into the city before police dispersed it and arrested many participants. In the following weeks authorities arrested a number of student leaders.

The March 5 demonstration matters because of what it showed about the movement's character. By March 5 the religious representatives were in prison, yet a second large demonstration had been organized by a different group, the students, acting on their own initiative. The movement was therefore not dependent on the original signatories. It also shows that the March 1 event was not a single uprising which later subsided but the first of a series of mobilizations, a pattern repeated in the provinces.

Funeral processions in Seoul on March 3 were large and subject to strict control. The authorities understood that a demonstration during the funeral could not be easily stopped without confrontation with mourners, and they deployed forces accordingly. That no major demonstration occurred during the funeral does not mean that political feeling was absent, and accounts mention the presence of slogans and handbills. It does mean that organizers, who had decided not to use the funeral day, kept to that decision.

The March 5 demonstration is a useful example of the emergence of new leaders. Students such as Kim Won-byeok and Gang Gi-deok are less well known than the thirty-three, but their action illustrates a generation of activists who became important in later years. The use of a railway station as a gathering point also reflects the importance of transportation as a space where crowds, travelers, and police met. After March 5, the authorities moved to close schools and arrest student leaders, but the students' network, which extended to many schools and to their home regions, carried the movement outward when students returned home.

Section 56.14

Pyongyang, Wonsan, and the Simultaneous Beginning of a Nationwide Movement

March 1 was not only an event in Seoul. The organizers had arranged for demonstrations in several northern cities, where Presbyterian, Methodist, and Cheondogyo networks were strong. In Pyongyang, a large gathering took place on the afternoon of March 1, which began with a memorial for Gojong and the reading of the declaration at a school and a church complex, followed by processions. Participants included students, teachers, church members, and Cheondogyo adherents, and the crowd is generally reported to have numbered in the thousands.

Similar events took place on the same day in other northern towns, including Seoncheon and Euiju in the northwest, Anju, and Wonsan in Hamgyeong province. In several cases local religious leaders had received copies of the declaration from couriers sent from Seoul in the preceding days and organized readings in churches, schools, or marketplaces. Local police and gendarmes responded with arrests, and in some places with firearms.

The northern demonstrations demonstrate the importance of the religious networks, which could coordinate action in several provinces on a single day, and also the importance of local organizers who decided how to carry out the plan. The nationwide movement that followed was neither wholly centralized nor wholly spontaneous. It grew from a plan that had a communications structure, and then it spread through channels the plan had not anticipated, as the next division explains.

The northern demonstrations reveal how a religious network operates as a communication system. Pastors, elders, and teachers were connected by visits, meetings, and correspondence, and they could circulate information quickly. Schools provided buildings and an audience, and church services provided regular gatherings. In Pyongyang, where Presbyterian and Methodist institutions were prominent, an event could be planned by people who knew one another well and who held positions of trust in their communities.

At the same time, the northern movement was not simply a church phenomenon. Cheondogyo members, students, merchants, and ordinary townspeople joined, and the demonstrations had a character of their own. The responses of local authorities were also varied. In some places, police dispersed crowds quickly; in others they waited and then arrested the leaders. Historians who study the north rely on a mixture of Japanese police reports, church records, and local histories compiled after liberation, and the northern record is therefore particularly rich in sources that need to be compared with one another.

Section 56.15

How the Independence Movement Spread: Markets, Schools, Religious Networks, and Local Initiative

Within days of March 1, news of the declaration traveled across the peninsula. It moved through railways and roads, through students returning home from Seoul, through travelers and merchants, through letters and printed handbills, and through word of mouth. The Gyeongui and Gyeongbu railway lines, which connected Seoul to the north and the southeast, carried people and printed material quickly, and the colonial authorities noticed. Handbills were copied by hand and printed in secret.

The first phase, in early March, consisted mainly of demonstrations in cities where the original organizers had contacts. The second phase, from mid-March through April, was largely driven by local communities. Villagers and townspeople learned of the declaration and organized their own gatherings, often on market days, when large numbers of people were already assembled. Local notables, teachers, church elders, and Cheondogyo members planned readings, set dates, and distributed flags, often with little or no contact with Seoul. In many places signal fires on hills announced a demonstration the following day.

This distinction between a coordinated beginning and locally initiated expansion is essential. It means that historians cannot explain the movement from the decisions of the thirty-three alone. It also explains why the Government-General found it hard to suppress: there was no central organization to dismantle, and each locality had to be dealt with separately.

Historians reconstruct the spread of the movement by compiling dates and places from police reports, court records, newspapers, nationalist compilations, and local histories. The result is a map of events that grows denser from early March to April and thins out in May and June. Such a map should be read with caution, since the records differ in what they count as a demonstration and in how carefully they were kept. A gathering of fifty people at a village market might be recorded in one source and not in another.

The importance of markets deserves emphasis. In rural Korea, periodic markets, held every five days in many places, were the main occasions on which people from different villages met. A demonstration on market day could draw on a large and varied crowd, and could reach people who had not been told in advance. The same feature made markets vulnerable: the authorities knew when they would be held, and could increase police presence. The use of hill-top fires and the passing of messages between villages were other ways in which the movement used the existing rhythms of rural life.

Section 56.16

The Northwestern Provinces: Pyeongan, Hwanghae, and Hamgyeong

The northwestern provinces were among the earliest and most active centers. Pyeongan and Hwanghae contained dense Protestant communities, with their schools, congregations, and local leaders, and Cheondogyo had a significant following. The coordinated beginning on March 1 in Pyongyang, Seoncheon, Euiju, and other towns was facilitated by these networks, and in the following weeks demonstrations occurred in many county seats and rural markets. In Hwanghae, towns such as Haeju saw demonstrations in which students and teachers participated, and in Hamgyeong, Wonsan and other places followed.

The authorities responded with arrests, and in many places police and gendarmes fired on crowds. Mission and church institutions were prominent among the places suspected by the police, and missionary accounts report that church members and buildings were disproportionately targeted. The reports come from sympathetic observers, so students should treat them as evidence of how the events were perceived as well as of the events themselves.

The northwest demonstrates a mechanism of organization based on religion and education. Its prominence also shows that a history of 1919 which centers only on Seoul would miss a large part of the movement, and that Korea north of the later 38th parallel was central to the independence movement, as it would be central to later political history (Chapters 64 to 66).

The northwestern provinces were also the area where Protestantism was most deeply rooted in Korea, and where the missions had their largest institutional networks. This had both political and social effects. The churches had given many people experience of organization, of meetings run by procedure, and of schools with Korean teachers. The pattern of mobilization reflects that experience. It also meant that the colonial authorities regarded Christians with special suspicion, and that many of the arrests and punishments fell on church members.

The regional record is a reminder that the movement had a distinct geography. The northwest was among the earliest and most active regions, but it was not the whole movement, and the mechanisms observed there did not apply everywhere. A responsible account therefore treats the northwest as one case among several, and compares it with regions where other forms of organization predominated.

To establish regional differences rigorously, historians need county-level demonstration records and the dates on which colonial officials compiled them. A police report listing an incident in a northern town may not capture a later gathering at its nearby markets, and church membership is not equivalent to the number of demonstrators. Specific comparisons between Pyongyang, Uiju, Seoncheon, and Wonsan should therefore ask whether printed declarations, congregational organization, school closures, or market traffic served as the immediate mobilizing channel in each locality.

Section 56.17

Gyeonggi and Gangwon: The Capital's Hinterland and Rural Demonstrations

In the communities around Seoul, in Gyeonggi and Gangwon provinces, the movement spread largely through rural markets and village networks. In Gyeonggi, demonstrations occurred in Suwon, Anseong, Ganghwa, and many smaller places; in Gangwon, in Chuncheon, Gangneung, and other towns. Because the region lay close to the capital, news and printed materials arrived quickly, and because police presence in rural areas was thinner than in cities, local gatherings could grow large before authorities responded.

The character of demonstrations changed as the authorities intervened. In many places an early peaceful assembly was followed by arrests, and later gatherings were larger and angrier. Some crowds attacked gendarmerie posts or township offices. In Gyeonggi, the repression was intense, and the events of April, including those at Jeam-ri (Section 56.23), took place in this region.

Village leadership mattered. In many places the organizers were teachers, church elders, Cheondogyo members, or local notables who could gather a crowd and who bore the consequences. The Gyeonggi and Gangwon experience illustrates how a nationwide movement could take root in agricultural communities with little formal connection to the original organizers.

In the region around Seoul, the proximity of the capital meant that the movement's events and the authorities' responses were closely linked. News from Seoul arrived quickly, but so did police reinforcements. Rural communities in Gyeonggi and Gangwon often had strong lineage and village structures, and local leaders, who held authority through landholding, learning, or age, could organize gatherings. Their involvement shows that the movement reached beyond the religious and student groups into the established rural leadership.

The changing character of protests as authorities intervened is an important theme. The first demonstrations in a locality were typically peaceful readings and processions. After arrests or shootings, subsequent gatherings could become larger, more determined, and more willing to confront police. This pattern, which historians have described in a number of regions, shows that the movement's course was shaped by interaction with the authorities and was not determined by the plans of its organizers.

In the Suwon and Hwaseong area, confrontations unfolded over several market days rather than during a single continuous march. Demonstrations at Song-san and other townships could attract participants from adjacent villages; attacks on police posts then contributed to the authorities' decisions to carry out punitive operations. Separating the sequence of local protest, Japanese police deaths, and subsequent military reprisals avoids treating the massacre at Jeam-ri as either an isolated incident or an inevitable consequence of every demonstration. Gangwon, by contrast, requires its own case-level evidence because terrain, transportation, and official deployment differed markedly from Gyeonggi.

Section 56.18

Chungcheong and the Aunae Market Demonstration

On April 1, 1919, a large crowd gathered at the Aunae market, near Cheonan in South Chungcheong, in one of the most widely remembered demonstrations of the movement. Organizers included Jo In-won, a local Methodist leader and teacher, and members of the Yu family, among them Yu Jung-gwon and Yu Jung-mu. Yu Gwan-sun, a student at the Ewha school in Seoul who had returned home after schools were closed, took part in the organizing. The night before, signal fires burned on the surrounding hills. On market day, a crowd of residents from the district, in the thousands according to most accounts, assembled and began to shout manse.

Gendarmes and soldiers fired on the crowd. The Academy of Korean Studies and the Independence Hall of Korea report nineteen demonstrators killed and approximately thirty severely wounded, including Yu Gwan-sun’s parents, Yu Jung-gwon and Yi So-je. The figures describe a particular documented tradition and should be distinguished from later embellishments and other police tallies. Many participants were arrested. Yu Gwan-sun was arrested and imprisoned; she died in Seodaemun Prison in September 1920, an event that belongs to the later consequences discussed in Section 56.24.

Aunae became a major symbol of the movement, and Yu Gwan-sun became one of its best-known figures. Historians caution against telling her story as a solitary act of heroism. She acted within a family, a church, and a community, and the demonstration was organized by many people, of whom she was one. The date matters as well: Aunae was an April event, not part of March 1, and it illustrates how rural mobilization developed some weeks after the opening.

The Aunae demonstration is unusually well documented because of the later fame of Yu Gwan-sun, and for this reason it has been the subject of many retellings, some of which add details not found in the earliest sources. Historians therefore distinguish between what contemporary records say, what participants later recalled, and what became part of popular memory. The core facts are that a demonstration took place on April 1, that organizers included members of several local families and a Methodist leader, that security forces fired on the crowd, and that people died and were arrested.

The case also shows how rural mobilization worked. The use of signal fires, the preparation by organizers over several days, and the participation of residents from surrounding villages indicate a locally organized event with its own leadership, with students returning from Seoul as one element. The losses suffered by families, including the deaths of parents, remind readers that the consequences of the movement for rural households were severe and lasting.

Section 56.19

Jeolla and Gyeongsang: Southern Networks and Regional Variations

The southern provinces, Jeolla and Gyeongsang, joined the movement somewhat later than the northwest, and with regional differences. In Daegu, demonstrations on March 8 were organized by students and church members, and the city's schools were active. In Gwangju, Busan, Masan, and Jinju, demonstrations occurred in the middle of March, often led by students from schools, including girls' schools, and by teachers and religious figures. In smaller towns and villages in the south, mobilization occurred through markets, local associations, and personal relationships.

Compared with the northwest, the south had fewer dense Protestant communities and a more varied pattern of organization. Where mission schools and churches existed, they served as centers. Elsewhere, local notables, schoolteachers, and market networks played the leading roles, and Confucian and Buddhist communities in some places joined. Repression was also varied: some demonstrations were dispersed with arrests, while others, particularly in rural districts, led to firing on crowds.

The southern demonstrations show that the movement drew on more than one organizational base. They also complicate any account that treats religion as the only channel of mobilization, a question taken up in the Historical Debate that follows.

The south of the peninsula had a different religious and social map from the north. Protestant communities existed in several southern cities, and in some places, such as Daegu, they were prominent, but in many rural areas Confucian local elites, Buddhist temples, and market networks were more important. The student demonstrations in cities like Daegu, Busan, and Gwangju reflected the educational institutions of those cities, while rural events relied on village leadership.

This variety supports a more general point: the movement was a convergence of several local movements, each with its own organizational base, and not the extension of one structure across the country. The record of the southern demonstrations is also an important corrective to histories that concentrate on Seoul and the north, and it shows why a regional approach is necessary to understand the movement's breadth.

Everyday Life and Social History

Organizing a Rural Market-Day Demonstration

In a rural district in 1919, a market day brought together farmers, artisans, peddlers, and travelers from surrounding villages, and the authorities regarded such gatherings as normal. Those who wanted to hold a demonstration needed a date, a place, a way to inform others, and a flag, and they needed to do this without drawing police attention. Handbills were copied by hand at night; flags were sewn at home, often by women; and word was passed within families, congregations, and groups of friends.

On the day, a few organizers might begin the reading, and the crowd would join. The consequences fell on households. A person who was arrested left a family without a worker at a time when agricultural labor mattered; those killed or injured left relatives to care for them; and fines and property losses could be heavy. The records of the period, mostly produced by police, say less about these household costs than about the events themselves, and historians reconstruct them from memoirs, local histories, and testimony collected later.

The southern demonstrations should be reconstructed from identifiable local episodes rather than inferred from a list of cities. Daegu's school and church networks, for example, require different kinds of evidence from assemblies tied to market-day traffic in agricultural communities. Relevant records include court judgments specifying individual defendants and venues, missionary correspondence, and police incident logs. A geographically rigorous account must also explain uneven participation: districts differed in the density of Christian and Cheondogyo associations, the timing of news from Seoul, and the risks of assembly under local gendarmes.

Section 56.20

Women, Students, Farmers, Workers, and the Social Composition of the Movement

Participation extended well beyond the original coalition. Students were prominent from the beginning, and girls' schools, including those at Ewha, Ilsin, and mission institutions, sent demonstrators into the streets. Women took part through churches, schools, and religious networks, and women's patriotic associations were formed in 1919 to support prisoners and to raise funds. In some towns, women entertainers, gisaeng, also joined demonstrations, an indication of how widely the movement reached across social positions.

Farmers made up the majority of participants in many rural demonstrations, which is unsurprising since most Koreans lived by agriculture. Market vendors, artisans, wage workers, and local intellectuals also took part. Workers in Seoul and in other cities held strikes and demonstrations, and merchants in several cities closed their shops. Teachers and religious figures were often the organizers, but ordinary people formed the crowds. The movement also included Buddhists and some Confucian scholars, and it crossed regional lines.

The movement's social breadth was one of its distinguishing features. It also meant that participants held different aims. Some understood independence in terms of national restoration, others in terms of release from specific grievances, and others in terms of the universal principles of the declaration. Those differences would shape the political debates described in later divisions.

Women's participation deserves close attention because the sources tend to emphasize male leaders. Female students from mission and private schools took part in demonstrations, and some were arrested and imprisoned. Women in churches and in Cheondogyo provided support, prepared flags, carried messages, and tended prisoners. Women's patriotic associations formed in 1919 became the basis for later women's organizations. These activities took place in a society where Confucian norms had limited women's public roles, and participation in a political demonstration was a significant act.

The participation of farmers is more difficult to document because they left fewer written records, but court and police files provide names, ages, and occupations of those arrested, and these show that cultivators were a large share of arrested participants in many rural counties. Workers and merchants appear in the records of urban actions. Taken together, the evidence shows a movement that crossed lines of gender, class, occupation, and region, though not equally and not without tension.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was the Movement Principally Religiously Organized or Popularly Driven?

The question: How much of the movement's expansion should be attributed to religious organization, and how much to the initiative of communities and individuals outside those networks?

Interpretation A — organized by religious networks. Some historians emphasize that the declaration, printing, and early demonstrations were planned and carried out by Cheondogyo, Christian, and Buddhist leaders, and that the pattern of early demonstrations follows the geography of these communities, especially in the northwest.
Interpretation B — driven by popular mobilization. Others argue that after the first days the movement was driven by communities, markets, students, and farmers acting on their own initiative, and that the religious networks provided the opening but were overtaken.
Interpretation C — interaction of both. Many historians combine the views, treating the religious networks as the framework that made early coordination possible and popular initiative as the force that produced mass participation.

Evidence: Evidence includes the distribution of the printed declaration, the geography of early demonstrations, police records of local organizers, and testimony about village-level decisions.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely about weight and interpretation, not about whether both factors operated.

Why it matters: The answer changes how the movement is remembered, as an elite initiative or as a popular uprising, and how it is connected to later politics.

Section 56.21

The Government-General's Response: Police, Gendarmes, and Military Forces

The Government-General was headed in 1919 by Governor-General Hasegawa Yoshimichi, an army general who had held the post since 1916. His administration was structured around a combined police and gendarmerie system and had the army's Korea garrison available to support it. The initial response to the demonstrations was handled by this apparatus: police and gendarmes made arrests, broke up gatherings, and in many places used firearms. As the demonstrations spread beyond what the local police could manage, army units were deployed, and reinforcements were sent from Japan in April.

The authorities also adopted legal measures. An ordinance issued in April 1919 provided for severe punishment of political offences, giving the courts a basis for harsh sentences. Censorship was tightened and gatherings were banned. The administration was in contact with the cabinet in Tokyo, where the government of Prime Minister Hara Takashi was concerned about both the crisis and the international reaction, and officials debated the balance between suppression and reform.

The response shows how an administration designed for close surveillance of a population dealt with a movement that overflowed its capacity. It could suppress individual demonstrations, but it could not prevent new ones from arising in other places, and its violence generated further anger. This outcome contributed to the policy reassessment discussed in Division IX.

The relationship between Tokyo and Seoul in this crisis was complicated. Officials in the Government-General had responsibility for restoring order, while the cabinet in Tokyo was concerned about how events would be perceived internationally and about the political consequences at home. Army and civilian officials did not always agree, and the authorities had to balance the use of force with the desire to avoid further damage to Japan's image at a time when it was seeking recognition as a major power at Paris.

The legal measures adopted in 1919 are significant because they show the authorities creating new tools to suppress the movement. The April ordinance, by defining political offences and prescribing heavy penalties, extended the reach of colonial criminal law. The use of such ordinances is a common feature of colonial states responding to challenges, and it illustrates how an administration based on emergency powers could transform temporary measures into lasting instruments.

The scale and timing of reinforcements are essential for assessing state response. A police patrol equipped for surveillance and arrests was not equivalent to a military detachment deployed for crowd suppression; the latter could escalate the likely consequences of confrontation. Official descriptions of ‘disturbance’ must also be tested against independent testimony about whether warnings were issued and whether demonstrators were armed. These distinctions are particularly important in the Gyeonggi reprisals and in the regional casualty data examined later.

Section 56.22

Peaceful Demonstrations, Local Confrontations, and Escalating Violence

The organizers of March First committed themselves to nonviolence, and the early demonstrations in Seoul and the north were largely peaceful. The movement, however, was not uniformly nonviolent. As demonstrations spread and as authorities fired on crowds, some crowds attacked gendarmerie posts, township offices, and police buildings, in some places setting fires or destroying property. In some districts there were deaths among Japanese police and officials, though these were far fewer than Korean deaths. Japanese civilian residents in some places organized vigilante groups, which took part in reprisals.

Historians therefore avoid two simplifications. The movement was not a conventional armed rebellion, since most participants were unarmed and most demonstrations were peaceful. Nor was it uniformly nonviolent, since local confrontations occurred in several places, often after the use of force by authorities. The balance of violence was sharply asymmetric: the colonial state had weapons, troops, and the legal authority to use them.

How violence is described is also part of the political record. Japanese reports tended to emphasize disorder to justify force, while nationalist accounts tended to emphasize nonviolence to underline the legitimacy of the movement. The chapter's Historical Debate examines these differences.

The question of violence in 1919 cannot be separated from the question of sources. The records of the Government-General were compiled by an administration that needed to justify its response and therefore had reason to stress disorder. Nationalist records were compiled by people who wished to honor the movement and who stressed its disciplined character. Missionary accounts, written by foreign observers with sympathy for Koreans, tended to emphasize the severity of repression. Each of these shaped how events were described.

A careful historian looks for events that are reported in more than one kind of source and examines how they are described. Attacks on police posts, for example, appear in both Japanese and Korean accounts, though their causes and scale are described differently. The pattern is one of an overwhelmingly unequal contest, in which a minority of confrontations among thousands of peaceful gatherings were used by the authorities to justify a response much more violent than the offenses it claimed to address.

The historian should distinguish intention from outcome when using the term nonviolence. The declaration's national representatives urged disciplined action, but they did not direct every protest staged after March 1. In some localities, participants attacked administrative offices, threw stones, or confronted police who were arresting marchers. Such incidents do not convert the movement as a whole into a planned military insurrection. Equally, identifying local violence does not relieve the colonial authorities of responsibility for disproportionate force or collective punishment.

Section 56.23

The Jeam-ri and Goju-ri Massacres of April 15, 1919

Jeam-ri is a village in the district of Suwon, in Gyeonggi province. After demonstrations in the area in late March and early April, which included an attack on a police post in which Japanese were killed, a Japanese army detachment under Lieutenant Arita Toshio was sent to the district. On April 15, soldiers assembled a group of male villagers, many associated with the local Christian and Cheondogyo communities, in the church. According to witnesses and later investigations, the soldiers fired on the people inside and set fire to the building. The reported number of dead is about two dozen. Houses in the village were burned.

The same day, soldiers moved on the neighboring village of Goju-ri, where six villagers were killed. Jeam-ri and Goju-ri should be kept distinct in the record. They are neighboring locations, with different victim lists and different accounts. Sources differ on the totals, and students should treat any single number with care.

The killings became known abroad because of foreign witnesses. Frank Schofield, a Canadian missionary and professor, visited the village and reported what he found, and the American resident Albert Taylor documented events and shared information with foreign audiences. Their accounts, together with testimony from survivors and residents, were published and used in protests. The Japanese authorities acknowledged that killings had occurred, but described them as responses to disorder. According to most accounts, the officer responsible was not seriously punished. Jeam-ri remains a major site of memory for the movement.

Atrocities require especially careful handling in a textbook. The record on Jeam-ri combines several kinds of evidence: the testimony of survivors and relatives, the observations of foreign visitors who saw the site soon afterward, photographs, Japanese military and police reports, and the later investigations and commemorations. Their agreement on the central facts, that soldiers killed villagers in and around the church and burned buildings, is strong. Their disagreement about numbers and about the immediate circumstances reflects different vantage points and different interests.

Responsibility should be stated precisely. The killings were carried out by Japanese soldiers under the command of an army officer, in the context of an army deployment ordered to restore order. The record does not support blaming the entire Japanese population, and in fact some Japanese individuals and Japanese newspapers criticized the repression. At the same time, the chain of command and the response of the authorities after the event, which minimized accountability, are part of the historical record. Where details remain disputed, the textbook identifies the uncertainty and does not resolve it by assertion.

Section 56.24

Arrests, Trials, Prisons, and the Experience of Political Detainees

Most of the thirty-three representatives were arrested on March 1 or in the following days, but they did not all stand trial: Kim Byeong-jo escaped to Shanghai, and Yang Han-muk died in detention. The surviving court files include thirty-one of the thirty-three among the prosecuted signatories, alongside other organizers. The principal case progressed through investigation and appeals to a final judgment on October 30, 1920; sentences varied and should be attached to named defendants and particular judgments rather than treated as a uniform punishment. Son Byeong-hui, the Cheondogyo leader, became ill in prison and died in 1922 after his release on medical grounds. Thousands of other participants were arrested across the country, tried in local and regional courts, and imprisoned or, in lighter cases, fined or flogged.

Conditions in prisons were reported to be severe: overcrowding, poor food and sanitation, and abuse during interrogation were described in prisoner testimony and in the nationalist literature. Seodaemun Prison in Seoul held many political prisoners. Women were also detained, including students and church members. Yu Gwan-sun, arrested after the Aunae demonstration, was imprisoned in Seodaemun and, according to widely repeated accounts, took part in prison demonstrations on the anniversary of March First in 1920. She died there on September 28, 1920, which is a year and a half after the events of 1919, and her death belongs to the later history of colonial repression.

The use of flogging as a punishment for Koreans, which the colonial law of the time allowed, was one of the practices most resented, and it was abolished in 1920 as part of the reforms discussed in Section 56.41.

The colonial judicial system had its own procedures and categories. Participants in the demonstrations were charged under security laws and ordinances, and many were convicted. Appeals courts reviewed some cases, reducing or confirming sentences, and the trial of the thirty-three attracted attention because of the prominence of the defendants. The proceedings were conducted in Japanese, with interpretation, and defendants and lawyers could make statements that were recorded and, in some cases, published, giving historians a source for their views.

The experience of prisoners also shaped later politics. Many who were jailed in 1919 became leaders in later organizations, and the prison itself became a place where political networks formed and where prisoners from different backgrounds met. Women prisoners faced distinct burdens, including reported sexual humiliation and harsh treatment, as well as the same overcrowding and hardship as men. Because prison conditions were reported through prisoners' memoirs and through foreign missionaries' accounts, the evidence is strong on general patterns but uneven on particular cases.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Family Experiencing Arrest, Imprisonment, and Political Repression

For a family in a village or a small town, the arrest of a household member in 1919 meant immediate disruption. A farmer who was detained could not tend his fields at planting time; a teacher lost his post; a student was expelled from school. Families sought information about where a relative was held, and visits to prison could be difficult or refused. Fines and court costs added to the burden, and relatives who were thought to have been involved could be placed under police surveillance.

Families also provided support. Women in churches and in newly formed associations raised money, delivered food and clothing, and tried to arrange legal help. After release, former prisoners often faced difficulty finding work, and many carried the effects of mistreatment. Sources for these experiences include prisoner memoirs, court records, church records, and later oral testimony, each with its own limitations.

Section 56.25

How Many Demonstrated, Died, Were Injured, or Were Arrested?

Counting the movement is difficult. The most frequently cited figures come from Park Eun-sik's The Bloody History of the Korean Independence Movement, compiled in exile in 1920. Park reported about 1,542 demonstrations with approximately 2.02 million participation instances, 7,509 killed, 15,961 wounded, and 46,948 arrested. These figures should be attributed to Park and treated as a historical estimate. They are not an independently verified census. Park compiled them from reports reaching Shanghai, in the months after the events, from correspondents and from nationalist networks.

Japanese official figures are substantially lower. The Government-General reported deaths in the hundreds, wounded in the low thousands, and arrests of about twelve thousand or more, according to the date and the compilation. They also counted demonstrations and participants differently. Both sets of figures have limits. Japanese figures reflected the administration's interest in minimizing the scale of the repression, and counted only those cases that police recorded. Park's compilation reflected a nationalist author's access to information and his purpose of demonstrating the scale of the movement, and its participation figure counts occasions of participation, not individuals.

The distinctions matter: the number of demonstrations is not the number of participants, which is not the number of individual people, and deaths, injuries and arrests are measured under different conditions. Responsible use of the numbers requires stating whose figures they are, when they were compiled, and what they measure.

The practical problem of counting a dispersed, illegal movement is considerable. No central body recorded events as they happened. Participants did not register; crowds were estimated by observers with different vantage points and interests; and many local events were reported only in a single source. Park Eun-sik compiled his data from reports sent to Shanghai, which meant that it depended on the reach of nationalist networks. Government-General figures depended on the reporting systems of police and gendarmerie, which varied in thoroughness and in willingness to report deaths caused by their own forces.

A further problem is categorical. A figure of two million "participation instances" does not mean two million people; many individuals took part more than once. A figure of arrests may include people detained briefly and released, while a figure of those imprisoned would be smaller. Deaths may be counted at the scene or include those who died later of wounds. Careful historians therefore do not choose a single number but describe a range, name the source, and state the definition. A student who writes that "7,509 Koreans were killed" has made an error of attribution; the accurate statement is that Park Eun-sik reported that number.

Division 6 · Sections 56.26–56.30
The Korean Diaspora and International Diplomacy

Examines how Koreans abroad responded and how international diplomacy and opinion engaged with Korea, without achieving recognition.

Section 56.26

Korean Communities in Manchuria and the Overseas Expansion of March First

Northeastern China, particularly the Jiandao (Gando) region across the Duman River, had a large Korean migrant population, which by most estimates numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Its communities had schools, churches, religious organizations, and independence societies, and activists traveled between them and the Russian Far East. News of the March First declaration reached them within days.

On March 13, a large demonstration was held in Longjing (Yongjeong), the principal Korean center in the region, with thousands attending. Authorities of the Japanese consulate and Chinese local troops were present. Chinese troops, deployed under the local authorities, fired on Korean demonstrators; Japanese consular influence was part of the surrounding political conflict but should not be confused with the identity of the troops that fired. The Academy of Korean Studies reports seventeen dead in total, including thirteen killed at the scene, and more than thirty wounded; other accounts give nineteen fatalities. Similar gatherings occurred in other Korean communities in Manchuria. The exile communities raised funds, circulated the declaration, organized schools and associations, and began to form groups that would later undertake armed activity, a development treated in Chapters 59 and 63.

The Manchurian Koreans show that the movement did not stop at the peninsula's edge. Korean history in this period includes communities in China whose members were subject to Chinese authority and Japanese consular pressure at once, which complicated their situation.

Korean society in Manchuria had developed over the preceding decades through migration driven by poverty, famine, and, after 1910, by political exile. The region was under Chinese sovereignty but with a weak local administration, and Japan exercised influence through its consulates and the protection it claimed to extend to Korean subjects. This created a situation in which Korean communities could hold meetings and demonstrations with fewer restrictions than at home, but where they were also exposed to Japanese pressure and Chinese local politics.

The Manchurian Koreans were not only supporters of events in Korea. They had their own concerns, including land rights, schooling, and the status of Koreans under Chinese law, and these concerns shaped their political organizations. The demonstration at Longjing and the associations that followed were part of a long-running effort to build a community able to sustain itself and to support independence. The later development of armed groups in this region, discussed in Chapters 59 and 63, grew from these communities.

Longjing illustrates why a cross-border demonstration cannot simply be categorized as Japanese military repression on Korean soil. The event occurred in Chinese territory, where Chinese military authorities exercised the immediate coercive power even as Japanese consular influence and the politics of local administration shaped relations with Korean migrants. The distinction bears on both the identity of perpetrators and the diplomatic recourse available to the bereaved. Korean community leaders protested to Chinese officials and organized public funeral rites, and the aftermath helped consolidate local nationalist organization.

Section 56.27

The Russian Maritime Province, Siberia, and Korean Independence Networks

Korean communities in the Russian Maritime Province, with centers around Vladivostok and Nikolsk-Ussuriysk, were among the largest outside Korea. Their situation in 1919 was shaped by the Russian revolution and civil war, and by the presence of Japanese and other foreign forces in Siberia. Korean organizations there included nationalist associations, which had been active before 1919, and, from 1918, socialist groups such as a Korean socialist party formed around Yi Dong-hwi.

After March First, Korean communities held demonstrations and assemblies, and nationalist leaders formed representative bodies, including a council that claimed national authority and is discussed in Section 56.32. The distinctions among these activities matter: a public demonstration, a political assembly, and an attempt to establish a representative institution are different kinds of act.

The Maritime Province offered a base close to Korea and a large community, but also political instability and exposure to the shifting situation of the Russian civil war. The region's role in the independence movement became one source of later disputes among Korean activists about strategy and ideology (Chapters 59 and 63).

The Russian Far East in 1919 was a region in turmoil. The collapse of the Russian empire, the civil war between Bolshevik and anti-Bolshevik forces, and the intervention of foreign troops, including Japanese, created shifting political conditions. Korean communities there, whose members included both people who had settled over several decades and more recent exiles, had to navigate these conditions, and different groups aligned in different ways.

This situation helps explain why the Korean organizations in the region combined nationalist and socialist elements, and why relations between them were sometimes cooperative and sometimes strained. For the Provisional Government's early history, it meant that a significant part of the exile community had interests and sponsors that differed from those of the Shanghai group. These differences would be an important element in the negotiations of the summer of 1919 and in later disputes, which later chapters examine.

The political setting of the Russian Far East differed fundamentally from that of Shanghai. Korean nationalists worked among competing Russian authorities, Allied intervention forces, and Japanese troops, so allegiance to a local administration could alter their freedom of movement and their ability to hold meetings. The experience also brought independence activists into contact with socialist and revolutionary arguments about sovereignty, land, and national liberation. These did not automatically translate into a common program: Korean organizations varied in their willingness to cooperate with Bolshevik networks, local Russian anti-Bolshevik forces, or foreign missions. The historian should therefore avoid treating the Korean migrants of the Maritime Province as a single bloc, even when they participated in a common anti-Japanese declaration.

Section 56.28

Korean Americans, Hawaiʻi, and the Philadelphia Korean Congress

Koreans in the United States and Hawaiʻi were few, perhaps several thousand, but they were organized. The Korean National Association, with branches in Hawaiʻi and on the American mainland, raised funds, published newspapers, and maintained contact with American supporters. Syngman Rhee, An Chang-ho, and Seo Jae-pil, who had become a naturalized American citizen, were among prominent figures.

After March First, American Koreans held meetings and issued appeals. The best known was the First Korean Congress, held in Philadelphia from April 14 to 16, 1919, organized with the leadership of Seo Jae-pil. Delegates and sympathetic Americans attended, and the congress adopted statements calling for American support of Korean independence, and a publicity effort followed through a press bureau. Korean Americans also raised money that supported activities elsewhere, including the Provisional Government.

Their influence should be measured carefully. They obtained attention and some sympathy, especially among American churches and journalists, but the American government did not recognize Korea. Their financial contributions were nonetheless important to the Shanghai institutions.

Korean migration to Hawaiʻi began in 1903, largely to work on sugar plantations, and a smaller number of Koreans, including students, political exiles, and ginseng merchants, lived on the American mainland. Their communities built churches, schools, and associations, and a network of newspapers kept them in touch. Many of these institutions were organized around the political aim of Korean independence, and they raised money regularly for that purpose.

The Philadelphia congress, held in a city with a historical association with American independence, was designed to attract American public attention. Its organizers staged meetings and issued statements in English, aimed at American audiences, and used the press to publicize the Korean cause. The results illustrate the opportunities and limits of diaspora politics: a small community with organizational skill, access to foreign publics, and financial resources could achieve visibility, but it could not by itself change the policy of its host government toward a distant colony of an ally.

The Philadelphia congress was an exercise in diaspora representative politics as well as a publicity campaign. Its delegates had to claim that a geographically dispersed migrant population could speak for Koreans living under Japanese colonial authority, while American audiences had little familiarity with Korean conditions. Processions, resolutions, English-language publicity, and appeals to democratic principles therefore operated together. Evaluating the congress requires asking what practical channels it created for fundraising and information transfer, not assuming that public sympathy produced formal diplomatic recognition.

The congress's public demonstrations and formal proceedings should be distinguished. Speeches and processions attracted outside attention, but resolutions, petitions, and organizational committees sought an enduring channel for international advocacy. Publicity in the United States depended on language and access: newspaper editors could report the event without thereby endorsing recognition of Korean independence, while sympathetic churches and civic groups were more willing to host meetings than to influence government policy. The Korean American participants also had to coordinate communities spread from Hawaiʻi to California and the eastern states. Their achievement lay partly in making geographical separation an instrument of organized advocacy and fundraising.

Section 56.29

Kim Kyu-sik, the Paris Peace Conference, and the Failure of Diplomatic Recognition

In Shanghai, young Korean activists formed the New Korean Youth Party in late 1918 and decided to send a representative to the Paris Peace Conference. They chose Kim Kyu-sik, a scholar with experience of the United States, who traveled to Paris in the early months of 1919, carrying a petition asking the conference to recognize Korean independence. After the provisional government was established in April, he was named its foreign representative.

In Paris, Kim and his colleagues sought meetings with delegations, distributed written appeals, and spoke with journalists and sympathizers. They were not admitted to the conference, and no delegation brought the Korean question before it. Japan was one of the principal powers, and the conference had no mechanism or inclination to hear complaints about colonial possessions of its members. Confucian scholars inside Korea also sent an appeal, which reached the Korean mission in Paris.

The distinction between publicity and recognition is crucial. The Korean missions succeeded in making the Korean question known to some foreign audiences, but no government recognized Korean independence or the Provisional Government in 1919. Students should treat the diplomacy as real and consequential in building networks, while recognizing that it did not achieve its principal goal.

The mission to Paris was an effort to take advantage of a rare international assembly. Kim Kyu-sik's preparation involved obtaining documents, funds, and travel arrangements through a network of Shanghai activists and sympathizers. Once in Paris, the Korean representatives worked in the margins of the conference, meeting delegates informally, publishing materials, and seeking the support of journalists. The conference's formal procedures, which excluded delegations not recognized as representing states, meant that they had no standing to be heard.

Historians evaluate the mission in two ways. As an attempt to secure recognition it failed. As an act of political organization it succeeded in establishing a Korean representation abroad and in creating contacts that were used afterward. It also gave the new Provisional Government an early diplomatic function, and thereby supported the argument that a government was needed. The distinction between these two kinds of result helps to avoid both exaggerated claims of diplomatic achievement and dismissals of the effort as pointless.

Section 56.30

Missionaries, Journalists, International Public Opinion, and the Limits of Foreign Support

Foreign missionaries, journalists, and residents were important witnesses. Missionaries, many of them Americans and Canadians, had communities and schools that were among the targets of repression, and they reported what they saw to mission boards and to newspapers. Frank Schofield and Albert Taylor are two figures who became well known for documenting events. Their reports, together with photographs and diplomatic correspondence, circulated in the foreign press and in church publications.

Public reaction abroad was sympathetic in many quarters, and the repression drew criticism in Britain, the United States, and elsewhere. In China, commentators noted and in many cases admired the Korean demonstrations, and Chinese student protest that arose in May 1919 has often been discussed in relation to the Korean example, although the Chinese movement had its own causes. Yet no government changed its policy. The governments of the major powers regarded Korea as part of Japan's sovereign territory and declined to intervene. Missionary bodies were also cautious, because their work in Korea depended on the permission of the colonial authorities.

The pattern was one of international sympathy and limited effect. Foreign opinion contributed to pressure on Japan to reconsider colonial policy, as the Government-General's later reforms indicate, but it did not translate into recognition.

Foreign witnesses played a double role. They provided documentary evidence, which was cited in protests and in the press, and they were themselves actors with their own institutional constraints. Missionaries who wrote reports often addressed mission boards that wanted to protect their work in Korea, and their language was frequently cautious. Journalists who published stories faced restrictions and sometimes had to rely on secondhand accounts. Their testimony is valuable but has to be read with attention to who wrote it and for whom.

The reception of Korean events in other parts of Asia is a further topic. Commentators in China and elsewhere discussed the Korean demonstrations as an example of popular political action. Whether and how the Korean movement influenced others is a question for comparative history, and the answer is not simple, since the Chinese student movement had its own causes in the peace settlement over Shandong. The Korean example is better seen as one among several expressions of a wider moment of political mobilization in 1919.

Section 56.31

From Independence Declaration to Republican Government

A declaration of independence proclaims a claim; a government attempts to act on it. By March and April 1919, activists in several places concluded that an institution was needed. A government could speak for Korea to foreign governments, coordinate fundraising and communication, give structure to a movement that had no center, and counter the claim that Korea was merely a collection of unorganized protesters. It could also provide legitimacy to representatives such as Kim Kyu-sik, who would otherwise speak only for a party.

The choice of form was significant. The Korean Empire had been a monarchy, and some activists in 1919 still thought of restoring the dynasty. The initiatives of 1919, however, all adopted republican forms, with representative assemblies and executive officers answerable to them. The change reflected the influence of republican and Wilsonian ideas, the experience of exile politics, the fact that Gojong was dead and the heir was in Japan under Japanese supervision, and a conviction that the Korean people, not the dynasty, was the source of authority.

Korean political thought remained diverse. Some activists expected to restore the monarchy, some favored republics of different kinds, and some looked toward socialist alternatives that became more prominent later. The provisional governments represent one stream of this thought, which became the most institutionally visible.

The decision to establish a government was also shaped by practical considerations. Diplomats and fundraisers needed credentials. Koreans abroad wanted an institution to which they could pay contributions with some assurance that the money would be used for independence. Activists wanted a body that could coordinate the dispersed communities and give direction to the movement at home. These practical needs interacted with ideological commitments and with personal ambitions, as in every political enterprise.

The shift to republican forms is a significant development in the intellectual history of Korea. Reformers of the 1890s and 1900s had discussed constitutional monarchy and popular rights, but a republic was a more radical step. The decision of the exile politicians in 1919 to adopt it indicates how far political thought had moved in a decade, and how much the experience of annexation had discredited the idea of a monarchy as the only vehicle of national identity. It is important, however, not to exaggerate the unanimity of this view.

A government-in-exile faced an additional dilemma that a declaration committee did not: it needed to specify who could authorize decisions, distribute offices, make law, and resolve disputes between distant representatives. Monarchists, republicans, religious activists, former officials, and exiled students did not necessarily agree on the political order that should succeed Japanese rule. The decision to create a provisional republic therefore reflected coalition-building as much as a simple conversion from dynastic to popular allegiance. This context explains why multiple provisional initiatives emerged and why their later unification required constitutional and personal negotiation.

Section 56.32

The Korean National Council in the Russian Far East, March 1919

The first of the three initiatives arose in the Russian Far East. In March 1919, Korean activists in the Maritime Province, meeting around Vladivostok and Nikolsk-Ussuriysk, established the Korean National Council, Daehan Gungmin Uiheoe, conventionally dated to March 17. The date is associated with a demonstration of Koreans in the region and with the council's proclamation. The council emerged from the expansion of the All-Russian Korean Central Association and was led by Mun Chang-beom (often Romanized Moon Chang-bum), who chaired it; Kim Cheol-hun served as deputy chair and O Chang-hwan as secretary according to the Korean Ministry of Patriots and Veterans Affairs. Yi Dong-hwi and Korean socialist circles belonged to the region’s larger political landscape but should not be automatically identified as holders of these particular council offices. It announced itself as a representative body for the Korean people and published lists of officers, some of whom were not present in the region.

Its claims to authority rested on the size of the Korean population in the region and on the activists' standing, not on any election by Koreans in Korea. It had organizational capacity among Korean communities in Russia, but operated in an unstable political environment. The council later took part in the negotiations that unified the governments.

The council is significant as a reminder that the 1919 provisional government was not initially a single Shanghai event. Three groups proclaimed institutions, each with different resources and different claims.

The council in the Maritime Province drew on a tradition of Korean organization in Russia that went back to the late nineteenth century. Local leaders had experience in running schools, newspapers, and associations, and the council could draw on these assets. Its proclamation on March 17 was timed to follow the news of the demonstrations in Korea and was accompanied by public gatherings.

The council's weaknesses were also apparent. It operated in a region without stable government; its communications with Korea and with other exile centers were slow; and its leadership was not united on every question. Nevertheless, its early existence meant that, when negotiations began over a unified government, the Russian Far East had to be taken into account. In that sense, the council served as a political actor and not only as a symbol.

The council's origins in an existing Korean association explain both its initial organizational strength and its representational limitations. Local Korean groups in Russia had practice electing or appointing delegates, but they operated amid the Russian Civil War and Japanese intervention in Siberia. Its March proclamation asserted a government-like mandate before Shanghai assembled its cabinet. Subsequent merger negotiations required determining whether this council's deliberative and executive functions could coexist with the Shanghai assembly and cabinet.

Section 56.33

The Hanseong Provisional Government, April 1919

The second initiative arose in Seoul. Activists with connections to Christian and Cheondogyo networks, including figures such as Han Nam-su and Hong Jin, organized a National Congress, which gathered representatives in early April. A meeting of delegates was held at Incheon in early April, and a proclamation was issued on April 23, 1919, announcing a provisional government associated with the name Hanseong, the old name of Seoul. The announcement listed officeholders, including Syngman Rhee as the head of the executive and Yi Dong-hwi as premier, and was distributed by handbill.

The Hanseong government's significance was symbolic as well as political. It claimed to originate from within Korea, from delegates said to represent the thirteen provinces, and in this respect had a stronger claim to legitimacy than bodies formed abroad. Yet it operated in a territory controlled by the Government-General, and its practical capacity was minimal. The authorities arrested several organizers. Its documents survived and were used in later negotiations as a source of legitimacy.

The Hanseong government illustrates the particular problem of claiming authority from inside a territory ruled by an occupying power. Its legitimacy was potentially the strongest, its operational capacity the weakest.

The National Congress movement was an attempt to bring together representatives of the provinces and to give the proclamation a basis in domestic opinion. Organizers had to work in secret, and the proclamation was distributed by handbill because open publication was impossible. The documents they produced are valuable to historians because they show how activists inside Korea thought about a provisional government: as a body representing the people, with named officers, and with a claim to precede the government in exile.

Because the Hanseong government never functioned as an administration, its main importance lay in the legitimacy it conferred. When negotiations began, supporters of the Shanghai government and the Council recognized that a government that ignored the domestic movement would be vulnerable to criticism. Incorporation of the Hanseong lineage into the unified government was a way of acknowledging this. The episode shows that legitimacy can be a political resource even for an institution without administrative capacity.

The Hanseong initiative had a different problem: a claim of domestic authority under active colonial surveillance. Delegates and organizers could prepare lists, circulate proclamations, and assert representation from the thirteen provinces, but they could not convene a durable public legislature in Seoul. The distinction between holding preparatory consultations and exercising government is especially important here. Its powerful claim was jurisdictional and symbolic—originating inside Korea—rather than evidence of actual command over police, treasury, or territory.

Section 56.34

Shanghai's Independence Office and the Provisional Assembly of April 10

Shanghai became a principal center for several reasons. Its foreign concessions, especially the French Concession, offered legal protection from Japanese police, and the city was an international port with good communications and a small but active Korean community. Activists from Manchuria, Korea, Japan, and elsewhere arrived in March and April, among them Yi Dong-nyeong, Yi Gwang-su, Yeo Un-hyeong, Hyeon Sun, and others. They established an independence office, which began to coordinate activity.

After preparatory meetings, delegates convened the Provisional Assembly, Imsi Uijeongwon, on the night of April 10. The assembly, with about thirty members drawn from various provinces and from the exile community, elected Yi Dong-nyeong as its chair. It discussed the name of the state, the form of government, and the officers to be chosen, and it carried on into the early hours of the following day.

Students should distinguish the preparatory activity of the independence office and the April 10 assembly from the government's formal founding. The assembly created the institutional basis; the proclamation of the government and its charter, discussed next, followed.

The choice of the French Concession as a base reflects the legal pluralism of treaty-port China. The concession had its own police and administration under French authority, and it did not hand over political refugees to Japan as a matter of course. French authorities were not always consistent, and at times they restricted Korean activities under pressure from Japan, but in 1919 the setting offered substantial freedom. The Korean community in Shanghai was small but included students, merchants, and activists, and the city's press and international connections allowed news to travel.

The assembly's deliberations on April 10 and 11 were reported in later memoirs, and participants differ about details such as the name of the state and the structure of government. These disagreements show that the founding was a negotiated process with competing proposals. The adoption of the name Daehan Minguk linked the new state to the empire and to the nationalist tradition of the Daehan name, while the republican form distinguished it from the monarchy.

The meeting of April 10–11 illustrates how constitutional procedure could confer legitimacy in the absence of territorial control. Delegates selected a presiding officer, settled rules for discussion, determined the name of the republic, and voted on officials and a charter. Their claim to represent Korean provinces was not equivalent to a national election, since colonial Korea offered no mechanism for such voting. The meeting's importance lay instead in converting exile cooperation into a repeatable institutional form, one capable of issuing resolutions, appointing agents, and negotiating with other Korean bodies claiming authority.

Section 56.35

April 11, 1919: The Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea

The Provisional Assembly adopted a ten-article provisional charter and established the name Daehan Minguk, the Republic of Korea, which draws on the "Daehan" name of the empire (Chapter 1 and Chapter 53). It also selected a cabinet whose original designations must be distinguished from later reorganizations: Syngman Rhee, then in the United States, was named premier; An Chang-ho, interior; Kim Kyu-sik, foreign affairs; Yi Si-yeong, justice; Choe Jae-hyeong, finance; Yi Dong-hwi, military affairs; and Mun Chang-beom, communications. Yi Dong-nyeong chaired the assembly. Several appointees were absent and the actual assumption of office varied. Many were not in Shanghai when selected, and some did not accept or arrive for weeks or months.

April 11 is now accepted as the date of the government's establishment. April 13, the date of a public announcement, was observed in earlier decades as the commemorative date, and the shift to April 11 reflects later scholarly and official reconsideration. Students will encounter both dates in older and newer sources.

The provisional government of April 11 existed beside the other two initiatives, with no territorial control and limited resources. What it had was a charter, a set of officers, a base in a protected foreign concession, and a connection to diaspora networks. Its significance lies in having created a republican framework that was later unified with the others.

The question of the founding date illustrates the way commemorations are constructed. A government that operated in difficult circumstances left an imperfect documentary record, and later accounts differed on exactly when it had been established. The date of April 13, associated with a public announcement, became a customary commemoration, and April 11, supported by later study of the assembly's proceedings and documents, has become the accepted date. Students should be aware that dates in older books may reflect the earlier convention.

The first cabinet reflected the effort to include figures from different centers: Syngman Rhee and An Chang-ho from the American community, Kim Kyu-sik as a diplomat, and others from the Russian Far East and from Korea. Many were absent, and some appointments were honorific. This was intentional, since a government that claimed to represent a dispersed nation needed to have officers drawn from its various parts, even if they could not all act at once.

Cause and Effect: How the Demonstrations Encouraged Republican State-Building

Long-term causes

Decades of reform and enlightenment movements, the experience of exile politics, mission and school education, and the weakening of the monarchy before annexation.

Short-term causes

The death of Gojong, the postwar discourse of self-determination, the success of the declaration in bringing people into the street, and the need for a body to speak for the movement abroad.

Immediate trigger or context

The demonstrations of March and April, and the arrival of activists in Shanghai and other centers.

Event or transformation

The establishment of provisional governments in the Russian Far East, Seoul, and Shanghai, and their unification in September.

Immediate effects

A republican framework for the independence movement, a diplomatic voice, fundraising structures, and rival claims to legitimacy that had to be resolved.

Long-term consequences

An institutional center for independence politics, a lasting vocabulary of popular sovereignty, and a heritage later invoked by Korean governments, though with changing organization and personnel.

Historical complexity: The government was a product of the movement and of the preexisting exile politics, and it was shaped by competing plans and personal rivalries. The movement did not mechanically produce it.

The April cabinet list also illustrates the provisional government's dependence on international correspondence. It named ministers in Shanghai, the United States, and the Russian Far East, reflecting a deliberate attempt to unite activists across political and geographic divides. But distant appointment had costs: those selected could not always immediately attend meetings, exercise their departments' authority, or agree on titles. The April government's legal formation should thus be distinguished from the slower process of making its offices function, a problem compounded when rival provisional governments claimed authority.

Section 56.36

The Provisional Assembly, Executive Departments, and the Problem of Representation

The Shanghai government comprised a legislature, the Provisional Assembly, and an executive, with a premier and ministers heading departments such as interior, foreign affairs, justice, finance, war, and communications. The assembly's members came from the exile community and represented provinces and regions on a nominal basis. Because the population of Korea could not vote, representation was necessarily indirect and symbolic.

Key figures played different roles at different stages. Yi Dong-nyeong chaired the assembly in April. An Chang-ho arrived from the United States in May and became the leading figure in organization, acting as the effective head of the executive. Syngman Rhee, in the United States, held the premiership by selection and used the English title of president; his relationship with colleagues in Shanghai became a source of tension. Yi Dong-hwi, a veteran of the Russian Far East, arrived later in 1919 and took office as premier after the September reorganization.

The problem of representation was permanent: the government claimed to speak for twenty million people it could not consult. This problem shaped its debates and the criticisms made of it.

Representation in an exile government raises a basic question: whom did it represent? The assembly's members were chosen by the exile community and by delegates who claimed to speak for provinces, and they could not be elected by those whom they claimed to represent. They defended their legitimacy by pointing to the March First movement as a popular mandate, interpreting the mass participation as support for a government. Critics, then and later, said that exile politicians were distant from the realities of life in Korea.

The personalities involved also shaped the government's early history. An Chang-ho's organizational abilities and moral authority gave him a central role. Syngman Rhee's education, American connections, and ambition made him a prominent but divisive figure; his long absence from Shanghai and his use of the title of president became a source of friction. Yi Dong-hwi, with experience in the Russian Far East, brought the support of a different group. The interactions among these men are a major theme of the government's history, and later chapters take up their subsequent course.

The distance between a ministerial title and a functioning department should shape the institutional narrative. The foreign affairs office could draft appeals, but its diplomatic work depended on the availability of accredited intermediaries and sympathetic contacts. A finance officer might have responsibility for receipts and disbursements while lacking reliable revenue or control over collectors. Likewise, a military affairs department could encourage armed organization without commanding regular national forces. These differences are not evidence that the Provisional Government was fictitious; they establish the specific constraints within which an unrecognized government attempted to operate.

Section 56.37

The Provisional Charter, Citizenship, Equality, and the New Republic

The ten-article charter of April 1919 is often summarized as follows, and students should work from a reliable translation. It declared the state a democratic republic; vested government in a provisional body; and established the equality of all Korean people regardless of sex, class, or wealth. It guaranteed freedoms such as religion, speech, publication, association, and residence; gave citizens the right to vote and hold office; and stated duties of education, military service, and taxation. It expressed commitment to contributing to world peace and culture; promised generous treatment of the former imperial household; and called for abolition of the death penalty, corporal punishment, and licensed prostitution. It also anticipated the convening of a national assembly after independence.

The equality provisions, particularly that of men and women, were notable for their time. They reflect the influence of republican and reform ideas and the participation of women in the demonstrations. The charter also reveals compromise, as in the provision for the former imperial family.

Students should distinguish promises from practice. The charter set principles for a government that controlled no territory, and its provisions on rights could not be enforced in Korea. It is best read as a statement of intended legitimacy and of the political ideals of its authors.

The equality provisions of the charter deserve careful reading in context. Declaring all people equal regardless of sex, class, or wealth challenged the hierarchy of the Joseon status order, which had been legally abolished by the Gabo reforms in 1894 (Chapter 52) but continued to shape social life. The inclusion of women indicates the influence of the women who participated in the demonstrations and of the reformist circles in the exile community. It stands among the early statements in East Asia of constitutional equality of the sexes, though caution about "first" claims is appropriate here, since such claims depend on definitions.

The charter's promise of generous treatment of the former imperial household reflects political compromise and respect for the dynasty among many Koreans. The charter's commitments on punishment and prostitution show the influence of Christian and reformist social ideals. Students may compare these provisions with the later practice of the government and with the provisions of the September constitution to see which elements persisted.

Section 56.38

Financing the Provisional Government: Donations, Communications, and Secret Administrative Networks

The Provisional Government had no tax base. It depended on donations from Koreans in the United States, Hawaiʻi, Manchuria, and elsewhere, on bond and fundraising campaigns, and on contributions from supporters inside Korea who were willing to risk punishment. Funds from Korean Americans were especially important in these early months.

To connect with Korea, the government created the liaison system, Yeontongje, in July 1919, with regulations establishing a network of offices at provincial and county levels, staffed by secret agents. It also set up a communications bureau, used couriers and codes, and published a newspaper, the Independence (Dongnip Sinmun), launched in August 1919, for propaganda and for reaching Koreans abroad. The network gathered intelligence and attempted to deliver orders and collect funds.

These arrangements were creative responses to a government in exile's problem. Their operation, repeated disruption by Japanese police, and evolving history belong to later chapters, and this chapter notes only their origins in 1919.

Financing an exile government is a fundamental problem, and the Provisional Government's history of financial difficulty began in 1919. Voluntary donations are unreliable, and collections inside Korea were dangerous. Korean American communities, many of whose members were workers with modest incomes, made regular contributions, and their loyalty supported the government through its first years.

The liaison system was an ambitious effort to create a clandestine administration. It envisaged chains of local agents who would carry out the government's orders, collect funds, and transmit information. The Japanese police recognized the threat and moved against it, and the network was repeatedly broken. This tension between the government's aspiration to administer and its limited ability to do so became a recurring feature of its history, and illustrates the distance between legitimacy and power.

The government's first challenges were administrative and financial rather than purely ideological. It adopted provisional liaison regulations in July 1919 and separate communications-bureau regulations in August; these created different functions, one aimed at covert administration and the other at transmitting information and moving personnel and funds. The Shanghai independence newspaper began appearing on August 21, 1919, providing another means of institutional continuity. Its circulation and finances, like the liaison networks, had to be built in hostile conditions; the existence of a printed regulation is not proof that every proposed office operated.

Section 56.39

Rival Claims to Legitimacy and Negotiations among the Provisional Governments

By mid-1919 there were three bodies claiming authority. Disputes concerned where the government should be located, whether its executive should be a president or a premier, and which body had precedence. The Hanseong government claimed legitimacy from the National Congress inside Korea; the Council in the Maritime Province claimed the support of a large Korean population; and the Shanghai government had a charter, officers, and a protected base. Syngman Rhee's use of the title president in the United States added to the confusion, since the Shanghai documents named a premier.

Negotiations were led by activists, notably An Chang-ho, who worked to reconcile the groups. The Council's leaders were wary of subordinating themselves to Shanghai, and personal rivalries and ideological differences complicated discussions. There was no assumption that a single government would exist; it was produced by negotiation, and not by the movement's spontaneous unity.

The negotiations should be understood as political bargaining among exile groups with differing resources and strategies. They show the fragility of a government created without territory, population, or revenue, and the importance of legitimacy as a resource.

The disagreements among the exile groups were not only personal. They involved different assessments of how independence could be achieved. Some activists favored diplomatic appeals to the powers, which made the United States and Paris central. Others looked to the Russian Far East and Manchuria as bases for military preparation. Some thought of the government as a symbolic representation of the nation, while others expected it to organize armed action. These differences of strategy were tied to the geographic bases of each group.

The negotiations also show a political process in which no group could impose its will. The Shanghai government had officers and a charter but needed the support of the Russian Far East communities and of domestic organizers. The Council had numbers but lacked the access to the diplomatic and financial networks of the Americans. The eventual agreement was a compromise, and the fact that it was reached reflects both the strength of the shared goal of independence and the practical need for a single voice.

Unification was therefore both a constitutional and organizational bargain. The provisional bodies had to decide whose claim of legitimacy would be treated as continuous, where the headquarters should remain, and whether legislative and executive authority could be reconciled across distant communities. The compromise made it possible for activists to use a common governmental name in external advocacy. It did not eliminate individual rivalries or ideological disagreements, nor did it give the new administration effective power within Korea. A rigorous account of September 1919 must register these simultaneous gains and limitations.

Section 56.40

September 11, 1919: Constitutional Unification and the Reorganized Provisional Republic

In early September, the Provisional Assembly in Shanghai approved a revised constitution, which was promulgated on September 11, 1919. The settlement merged the claims of the three initiatives: the government was to be recognized as continuing the lineage of the Hanseong government, the Council in the Russian Far East agreed to merge, and the government remained based in Shanghai. The executive was reorganized with Syngman Rhee designated provisional president and Yi Dong-hwi designated premier; Yi Dong-hwi did not take up the premiership in Shanghai until November 1919. The constitution set out separate legislative, executive, and judicial functions. Its actual staffing and operations must be distinguished from the offices named in the constitutional settlement.

The constitution stated that sovereignty resided in the Korean people. This is a notable statement, since it replaced the principle of royal sovereignty that had governed the Korean Empire. The wording also described the territory as that of the former Korean Empire.

April 11 and September 11 must be distinguished. The first marks the establishment of the Shanghai government; the second marks a constitutional revision and unification. After September 1919 the government continued to function with a gap between its legal claims and its practical capacity, as would be true in the years that followed. Later Korean constitutions referred to the Provisional Government as a source of legitimacy, but this retrospective use should not be read back into 1919.

The September constitution can be read as a record of compromise. Its institutional structure drew on separation of powers as understood by the activists, and its provisions on the executive reflect the debate over the roles of president and premier. By recognizing the Hanseong lineage and by incorporating the Council, it met the concerns of the groups that had been outside Shanghai. The decision to place the seat in Shanghai but to describe it as provisional pending the return to the homeland indicates how its authors saw their position.

The gap between legal form and political capacity is the key analytic point. The constitution described a government with legislative, executive, and judicial branches, but it could not hold elections, collect taxes, or enforce judgments in Korea. Its effects were felt in other ways: in the maintenance of a republican tradition, in a framework for the Korean community abroad, and in a symbolic assertion of sovereignty. Whether these are described as achievements or limits depends on what is being measured.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From the Korean Empire to the Provisional Republic

What changed

  • Sovereignty shifted in principle from the monarch to the Korean people
  • Institutions shifted from court and imperial ministries to an assembly, a cabinet, and courts
  • The seat of authority moved from Seoul to a foreign concession in Shanghai
  • Legitimacy came to depend on mass participation and diaspora support rather than on dynastic inheritance

What remained continuous

  • The claim to the territory and the name of the former Korean Empire, including the use of Daehan
  • Many personnel with experience in reform movements and the empire
  • A commitment to restoring Korean independence

When did change occur, and what was its pace? The shift occurred between 1910 and 1919 but became institutionalized in a matter of months in 1919; it was abrupt in form and gradual in ideas.

Who experienced change and continuity most directly? Exile activists experienced it most directly; Koreans in the homeland experienced the continuity of colonial rule, with the Provisional Government largely a symbol.

Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that new political principles can emerge before the means to enforce them exist.

The institutional change also affected political terminology. In April, Syngman Rhee had been chosen as premier, whereas the September constitution organized a presidency. Yi Dong-hwi's selection as premier was politically important because it brought another current of the Russian Far East independence movement into the government, but his actual assumption of office came later in the year. Read alongside the April charter, the September constitution demonstrates how the independence movement was negotiating executive authority while lacking normal mechanisms of national elections and parliamentary accountability.

Section 56.41

Japan's Reassessment of Colonial Rule and the Turn toward Cultural Rule

The movement forced a reassessment in Tokyo and Seoul. The government of Prime Minister Hara Takashi was concerned at the scale of the unrest, at the criticism abroad, and at the apparent failure of a system based heavily on military-police control. In August 1919, Hasegawa Yoshimichi was replaced as governor-general by Admiral Saitō Makoto, who arrived in Seoul in early September, when the Korean independence activist Kang U-gyu threw a bomb near his party at Namdaemun Station on September 2; Saitō was unhurt.

Saitō announced a policy usually termed cultural rule, bunka seiji. Concrete changes during 1919 and 1920 included the end of the gendarmerie-based police system, with its replacement by a civilian police organization, the abolition of corporal punishment for Koreans, relaxation of some restrictions on the Korean-language press, with newspapers licensed in 1920, and promises of more educational opportunity and wider Korean employment in administration. The reorganization of police, however, was accompanied by an expansion in the number of police and a continued emphasis on surveillance.

Students should distinguish administrative changes from rhetoric. The reforms were real in some respects, and they opened space for Korean cultural and press activity that later chapters examine. They were also designed to preserve Japanese rule, not to concede independence. Their longer operation belongs to Chapters 57 to 60.

The shift to cultural rule needs to be understood as a response to specific pressures. Within Japan, political change was under way, with party politics gaining ground and critics of the military-dominated colonial regime finding a hearing. Abroad, criticism of the repression gave the government reason to show that it could reform. The choice of an admiral as governor-general, in place of an army general, was itself a signal of a change of approach.

The colonial authorities' aims, however, were consistent with the maintenance of control. The expansion of police numbers and the extension of surveillance into new forms accompanied the opening of limited space for publishing and associations. Historians therefore describe cultural rule as a strategy of more flexible domination. How it operated, how Koreans used the new spaces, and how it ultimately evolved are the subjects of Chapters 57 to 60.

Section 56.42

Did the March First Movement Fail or Transform Korean Independence Politics?

In its immediate objective, the restoration of independence, the movement did not succeed. No foreign government recognized Korea; Japanese control was not shaken; thousands were killed, wounded, or imprisoned; and the provisional governments had no territory. By the end of 1919 Korea remained a Japanese colony.

Yet the movement had lasting effects. It mobilized participants across regions, genders, and classes, creating a shared experience that no earlier movement had matched. It produced new networks and organizations, among them women's associations and student groups. It made the Korean question visible internationally. It established a republican institutional framework and a vocabulary of popular sovereignty. And it contributed to Japanese policy change, even if on Japanese terms.

Historians weigh these outcomes differently. Some emphasize failure and the movement's inability to alter the balance of power, while others emphasize transformation of politics and identity. The disagreement concerns how outcomes should be measured, in the short or the long term, by material or symbolic criteria.

The question of evaluation depends on what historians choose to compare. Compared with the movement's goals, the result was failure. Compared with the preceding decade, in which public political action had been nearly impossible, it was an enormous change. Compared with later independence movements, it appears as a beginning that set patterns of organization, diplomacy, and memory.

Some scholars point to the limits of the movement's transformation as well. The divisions that appeared among exile groups, the failure to maintain unity, and the difficulty of sustaining mass mobilization under repression show that the movement did not resolve the problems it exposed. The memory of March First, in both South and North Korea, has also been shaped by later politics, and historians therefore distinguish between what happened in 1919 and how it was subsequently narrated.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was 1919 a Political Failure, a National Transformation, or Both?

The question: How should historians assess the movement's outcomes in light of its failure to win independence?

Interpretation A — a political failure. Emphasizes that the movement did not restore independence, secure foreign recognition, or end Japanese rule, and that repression was severe. Some activists, in later years, drew the conclusion that nonviolent petitioning was inadequate.
Interpretation B — a national transformation. Emphasizes the mass participation, the emergence of new political institutions, the vocabulary of popular sovereignty, and the lasting memory of March First in Korean political culture.
Interpretation C — both, on different timescales. Holds that the movement failed in its immediate aim but transformed the conditions of later politics, and that the two assessments are compatible.

Evidence: Evidence includes the immediate political results, the course of later independence movements, policy changes by the Government-General, and the later commemoration of March First.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and criteria for success. It is partly about whether a movement should be judged by its goals or its legacy.

Why it matters: The assessment shapes how the independence struggle is understood, and how historians weigh nationalist memory against political outcomes.

Student task: Select two criteria for evaluating a movement's success and apply them to March First. Do they lead to the same conclusion?

A more precise assessment must separate at least three kinds of outcome. Japanese sovereignty over Korea remained effective in 1919, so the uprising did not achieve immediate territorial liberation. At the level of social mobilization, however, participation made local political networks and national solidarity visible across regions and social groups that had often acted separately. At the level of institutions, the republic-in-exile gave independence activists legal forms, offices, and a claim of political continuity. The three outcomes do not share a single measure of success; they must be evaluated through different kinds of evidence.

Section 56.43

From the 1919 Uprising to the Continuing Independence Struggle

The movement shaped the course of the independence struggle in several ways. New institutions, chiefly the Provisional Government, continued to function, and exile diplomacy continued. Some activists concluded that organized armed struggle was necessary, a turn that built on communities in Manchuria and the Russian Far East and is treated in Chapters 59 and 63. Nationalist and socialist currents grew, and the disagreements among them became sharper in the following years. Participation by women, students, religious groups, and rural communities continued to be significant.

At home, the cultural and press activity permitted under the revised colonial policy opened new avenues of expression, which later chapters examine. The experience of 1919 also provided a reference point for later generations, for movements in the colony and for postwar politics after 1945.

This chapter ends its narrative in 1919 deliberately. The independence movement from 1920 to 1945, with its many organizations and strategies, is the subject of later chapters. What the chapter has established is how a declaration became a mass movement and how a movement tried to become a government.

The long-term effects of 1919 also extended to the ideological landscape. The failure of appeals to the powers contributed to interest in alternative approaches, including socialism, which was gaining attention among Korean activists in the Russian Far East, Manchuria, China, and Japan, and armed struggle, which some groups began to organize. Others turned toward gradual strategies of education and economic development within the colonial framework. These divergences would define independence politics in the 1920s and beyond.

The memory of March First has remained politically significant. It was commemorated as a founding event in both Korean states after 1945, with differing emphases, and it continues to be a subject of historical interpretation. By ending the chapter in 1919, the textbook leaves these developments to later chapters, but it stresses that the movement's significance lay in both what it achieved in 1919 and what it made possible afterward.

The later memory of March First was itself politically consequential. Different Korean movements emphasized its popular crowds, religious leaders, gender participation, constitutional outcome, or human costs, depending on the political questions of their own time. Historical commemoration should not be allowed to erase this diversity, or to make every participant an advocate of one later political program. Chapter 56 ends with an unresolved tension: mass claims to sovereignty had been publicly demonstrated, and a republic had been proclaimed, while the colonial state retained the armed capacity to govern Korea. That tension connects the uprising to the distinct social, political, and military developments assigned to the subsequent chapters.

Section 56.44

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This section asks students to pause and connect the chapter's two histories, the mass movement and the effort at state-building, before turning to the formal review apparatus.

Consider four distinctions that run through the chapter. Popular support is the participation of crowds and communities. Institutional legitimacy is the acceptance of a body's right to speak and act. A constitutional claim is a written assertion of authority and principle. Diplomatic recognition is the acknowledgment of a government by other states. Practical exercise of government is the actual ability to collect revenue, enforce laws, and control territory. In 1919, the Korean independence movement possessed the first in abundance, the second in contested measure, the third in written form, and the last two hardly at all. Students should be able to explain this pattern with specific evidence from the chapter.

Students should also be able to distinguish three events that are often confused: the March 1 declaration in Seoul, the April 11 establishment of the Shanghai government, and the September 11 constitutional unification. Each belongs to a different phase of the story, and each had a different meaning.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 56 has examined two connected histories of 1919: the nationwide movement for independence that began on March 1 and the effort to create a republican government in exile. The governing question was how the movement transformed opposition to colonial rule into a national independence struggle and how activists attempted to turn popular assertion of sovereignty into organized government.

The movement became possible because networks survived colonial rule: religious communities, schools, student associations, and exile groups. The end of the First World War, the discourse of self-determination, and the death of Gojong in January 1919 gave these networks an occasion and a language. The Tokyo students' declaration of February 8 and the Muo Declaration of exiles in Manchuria provided precedents.

The coalition that planned March First brought together Cheondogyo, Christian, and Buddhist leaders. Thirty-three representatives signed a declaration drafted by Choe Nam-seon, which was printed in about 21,000 copies and distributed on the eve of the demonstration. The date was chosen for practical and religious reasons. On March 1, twenty-nine representatives read the declaration at Taehwagwan and were arrested, while a separate crowd gathered at Tapgol Park, heard the declaration read, and marched.

The movement spread beyond its organizers. Demonstrations in the northwest began on March 1, and in the following weeks communities in Gyeonggi, Gangwon, Chungcheong, Jeolla, and Gyeongsang organized their own gatherings, often on market days. Students, women, farmers, workers, and local notables took part. The student demonstration of March 5 and the rural demonstration at Aunae on April 1 showed that the movement did not depend on the original signatories.

The Government-General responded with police, gendarmes, and the army. Most demonstrations were peaceful, but some included attacks on police posts and offices, and the response was far more violent than the actions of crowds. The killings at Jeam-ri and Goju-ri on April 15 became a symbol of repression, and foreign witnesses helped spread knowledge of them. Thousands were arrested and tried. Casualty and participation figures are contested: the often-cited figures from Park Eun-sik are historical estimates, and Japanese official figures are substantially lower.

Koreans abroad responded in Manchuria, the Russian Far East, the United States, and elsewhere. Diplomatic efforts, including Kim Kyu-sik's mission to Paris and the Philadelphia Korean Congress, brought publicity but not recognition. Missionaries and journalists provided sympathetic reports, but the major powers regarded Korea as Japanese territory.

Provisional governments arose in three places: the Korean National Council in the Russian Far East in March, the Provisional Government in Shanghai on April 11, and the Hanseong government proclaimed on April 23. After negotiations, a constitution promulgated on September 11 unified the claims. The charter and the constitution stated republican principles, equality, and popular sovereignty, though the government controlled no territory.

Japan responded to the crisis by replacing Hasegawa with Saitō Makoto and adopting a policy known as cultural rule, which changed some administrative practices while preserving colonial authority. The movement did not restore independence, but it altered the organization, legitimacy, and ambitions of the independence struggle.

The chapter's evidence is varied and uneven. Declarations, police and court records, missionary reports, nationalist compilations, and constitutional documents each reveal part of the story and each has limits. Historians continue to debate the relative roles of religious organization and popular initiative, the character of violence, and whether the movement should be judged a failure or a transformation.

Chapter Reflection

The March First Movement matters because it shows how a colonized society can assert a claim to sovereignty without possessing the means to enforce it, and how such an assertion can alter political life even when it fails in its immediate aim. In 1919 Koreans across the peninsula and abroad acted publicly in ways that colonial rule had been designed to prevent.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs in later Korean history: movements that begin with organized leaders and expand through popular initiative, repression that deepens opposition, and the attempt to translate mass mobilization into institutions. The Provisional Government illustrates the difficulty of this translation, as its legitimacy rested on principle and participation while its capacity remained limited.

Later chapters will follow the consequences. Chapter 57 examines the colonial society and culture that developed under the revised policy; Chapters 59 and 63 follow the independence movements that grew from the experience of 1919; and the memory of March First continued to shape Korean politics in both postwar states, a subject for Volumes III and IV. The movement did not restore independence in 1919, but it changed the terms on which the struggle for independence was conducted.
