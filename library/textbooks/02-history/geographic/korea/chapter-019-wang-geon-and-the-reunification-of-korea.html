Wang Geon and the Reunification of Korea

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 918, senior commander and former chief minister Wang Geon, who had risen to the highest-ranking administrative post of sijung in 913, was elevated to the throne by leading officers after they overthrew Gung Ye, ruler of the state then known as Taebong. Wang Geon restored the older dynastic name Goryeo. Nothing about that moment guaranteed that his new dynasty would outlast its founder, let alone that it would eventually give the Korean peninsula its lasting English name. In 918 Silla still ruled from Gyeongju, however diminished. Later Baekje still commanded the southwest under an ambitious and battle-tested ruler named Gyeon Hwon. And across the peninsula, dozens of regional strongmen, the hojok, controlled fortified towns, private armies, and local loyalties that no single court could take for granted.

The story this chapter tells is not a simple march toward an inevitable unification. Wang Geon's Goryeo nearly collapsed within a decade of its founding. In 927, Gyeon Hwon's forces sacked the Silla capital itself, installed a new king of his own choosing, and then inflicted a humiliating defeat on Wang Geon's own army near modern Daegu, a battle in which, according to the tradition preserved in later Goryeo accounts, one of his most trusted commanders died protecting him from capture. Had that battle gone differently, or had Later Baekje's internal politics not later fractured so dramatically, the peninsula's political map by the middle of the tenth century might have looked very different, perhaps organized around a Later Baekje dynasty, perhaps around some other configuration of competing successor states entirely.

What eventually allowed Wang Geon's state to prevail was not simply superior generalship, although military capability mattered. It was an approach to political power that combined coercion with negotiation, marriage, patronage, and the deliberate absorption of rival elites rather than their destruction. When Silla's last king, Gyeongsun, formally submitted to Goryeo in 935, he did not disappear into obscurity; he was welcomed into the new court, granted high rank, and given a royal bride from the Wang family itself, while much of the old Silla aristocracy found continuing places within Goryeo's expanding administration. When Gyeon Hwon's own son overthrew him in a bitter succession struggle within Later Baekje, the aging founder of Goryeo's fiercest rival fled not into exile abroad but into the protection of the man he had spent two decades fighting, arriving at Wang Geon's court to be received, remarkably, as an honored guest, and would spend his final year under Goryeo's protection before joining Wang Geon's own campaign against Later Baekje in 936.

This chapter follows Wang Geon's reunification of the peninsula's principal successor states between 918 and 936, and it follows the political architecture he built in the years immediately afterward to make that reunification durable. Chapter 18 explained how Silla's centralized order collapsed into the competing polities of the Later Three Kingdoms period. This chapter explains how one of those polities absorbed the others and constructed, out of genuinely disparate and often hostile political traditions, a new ruling coalition capable of governing the peninsula for centuries to come.

Readers should resist a natural temptation to treat Goryeo's eventual triumph as foreordained. The historical record instead shows a contingent, frequently reversed struggle among three major powers and dozens of smaller ones, decided as much by defections, marriages, succession crises, and the selective incorporation of defeated rivals as by battlefield victories alone. Wang Geon's most consequential achievement may not have been any single military campaign but his capacity to make submission to Goryeo attractive rather than merely survivable, to offer former enemies continued status, landed wealth, and marriage ties rather than confiscation and humiliation.

This chapter also introduces a theme that will recur throughout Goryeo's history, examined further in Chapter 20: a dynasty assembled through negotiated incorporation inherits the political burdens of that incorporation along with its benefits. The powerful aristocratic families, marriage networks, and regional loyalties that made Wang Geon's reunification possible would also shape, constrain, and periodically threaten royal authority for generations afterward. Understanding how Goryeo was built helps explain both its remarkable stability across more than four and a half centuries and the recurring tensions between the throne and the great families that punctuate its history.

Finally, this chapter follows a claim Wang Geon made that reached beyond either Silla or Later Baekje: an inheritance from the fallen northern kingdom of Balhae, itself heir to Goguryeo, which collapsed under Khitan pressure in 926 and sent refugees, including members of its royal house, into Goryeo territory. By welcoming these refugees, Wang Geon positioned his new dynasty as heir not merely to Silla's mantle in the south but to a broader northern political tradition, a claim with consequences for Goryeo's later frontier policy examined in Chapter 22.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the political and military struggle among Goryeo, Silla, and Later Baekje between 918 and 936.
  2. Explain why Wang Geon relied on coalition-building, marriage alliance, and elite incorporation alongside military force.
  3. Analyze the significance of the hojok as a source of both instability and, once incorporated, political strength for Goryeo.
  4. Describe the crisis of 927, including the sack of Gyeongju and the Battle of Gongsan, and explain why it demonstrates the contingency of Goryeo's eventual victory.
  5. Assess the significance of Silla's voluntary submission in 935 as distinct from a straightforward military conquest.
  6. Explain how internal succession conflict within Later Baekje contributed to its defeat in 936.
  7. Evaluate what "reunification" meant in 936 and what it did not mean for regional identity, elite autonomy, and administrative uniformity.
  8. Connect the collapse of Balhae in 926 to Goryeo's early political self-understanding and later territorial ambitions.
  9. Distinguish conquest, submission, defection, and incorporation as different mechanisms of state formation.
  10. Interpret the Ten Injunctions as a later transmitted text reflecting early Goryeo governing concerns rather than a verbatim record.
  11. Compare the experiences of different social and political groups living through the reunification wars.
  12. Contextualize Wang Geon's settlement as the foundation for the institutional structures examined in Chapter 20.

Historical Context

Chapter 18 traced the disintegration of Unified Silla's centralized order during the ninth century: aristocratic factionalism at Gyeongju, an overburdened peasantry, and the rise of regional strongmen, the hojok, who built local power on fortified towns, private military forces, and control of maritime and agricultural resources. Out of this fragmentation emerged two rival successor states in addition to a diminished Silla. In the southwest, Gyeon Hwon, a former Silla military officer, founded Later Baekje in 900, drawing on the historical memory of the old Baekje kingdom and building a formidable base of territory, manpower, and access to maritime trade. In the central and northern peninsula, Gung Ye founded a state invoking Goguryeo's memory, initially named Goryeo in 901 and subsequently renamed Majin in 904 and then Taebong in 911 (the label "Later Goguryeo" is a retrospective historians' convenience rather than a name used at the time, as Chapter 18 explains); the state expanded rapidly through conquest and the absorption of hojok territory before Gung Ye descended into an increasingly erratic and violent personal rule that alienated even his closest supporters.

Wang Geon, a commander from a prominent merchant and hojok family based near Songak (modern Kaesong) with strong connections to maritime trade along the western coast, had served Gung Ye loyally and effectively for years, leading successful campaigns, earning wide respect among the officer corps, and rising by 913 to sijung, the highest-ranking administrative office in the state. In 918, when Gung Ye's rule had become intolerable to his own generals, a group of officers deposed him and elevated Wang Geon in his place. Wang Geon quickly renamed the state Goryeo, consciously invoking Goguryeo's memory just as Gung Ye had, and moved the capital to his own power base at Songak the following year.

At this moment, however, Goryeo was one of three principal political centers on the peninsula, not yet a clear favorite to dominate it, although the military strength of the three was increasingly unequal. Silla, though territorially reduced and politically weakened to the point that it could no longer seriously check either of its rivals militarily, retained genuine prestige, an established bureaucratic tradition, and control of the southeast around Gyeongju. Later Baekje under Gyeon Hwon controlled substantial territory in the southwest, commanded effective military forces, and maintained diplomatic and commercial contacts abroad, including with successor states in China during this politically fragmented period on the continent. Across the peninsula, numerous hojok, some nominally attached to one of the three major states and others functioning with near-total independence, controlled resources and manpower that any of the three contenders needed to secure in order to prevail.

This chapter examines how Wang Geon converted this precarious three-way, and in practice many-way, competition into a durable unified dynasty by 936, and how the settlement he constructed in the years immediately following set the terms for the institutional history examined in Chapter 20.

Section 19.1

Goryeo in 918: A New Dynasty, Not Yet a Unified Kingdom

When Wang Geon accepted the throne in 918, he inherited Gung Ye's territory, army, and administrative apparatus, but he inherited none of the peninsula's other two major powers, and no guarantee that his own supporters would remain loyal. His first recorded acts as ruler reflect the fragility of his position rather than confident authority. He restored the dynastic name Goryeo, deliberately distancing his new government from the increasingly erratic final years of Gung Ye's rule while still invoking the same historical memory of Goguryeo that Gung Ye himself had used to legitimate his own state. In 919, Wang Geon moved the capital from Gung Ye's base at Cheorwon to Songak, his own home region near modern Kaesong, a city with strong connections to maritime trade along Korea's western coast and to Wang Geon's own extended hojok network.

This relocation was itself a political statement. Songak was not a neutral administrative choice; it was the seat of Wang Geon's own family power, a place where his personal networks of trade, kinship, and military service could most directly support his rule. Establishing the capital there signaled that the new dynasty's authority rested substantially on regional alliance and personal loyalty rather than solely on the inherited machinery of Gung Ye's state.

At this stage, Goryeo remained one of three principal political centers rather than a clear favorite, though the real military contest was increasingly one between Goryeo and Later Baekje. Silla, though territorially confined to the southeast and politically diminished by decades of aristocratic factionalism, retained genuine prestige as the peninsula's oldest continuously governing royal house, along with an established bureaucratic tradition and networks of aristocratic families with centuries of accumulated status. Later Baekje, under the formidable Gyeon Hwon, controlled substantial territory in the fertile southwest, commanded effective and experienced military forces, and maintained diplomatic contacts with successor states on the fragmented Chinese mainland during this period, along with commercial links across the Yellow Sea.

Beyond these three courts, dozens of hojok across the peninsula controlled fortified towns, private military forces, and local economic resources with only loose or contested attachment to any of the three major states. Their cooperation, or at minimum their neutrality, would prove essential to whichever contender ultimately prevailed, and this chapter's central argument depends on recognizing that Wang Geon understood this dependence earlier and more consistently than his rivals did.

Section 19.2

Wang Geon's Strategy of Coalition Building

Historians examining Goryeo's founding consistently identify Wang Geon's approach to political consolidation as distinctive, and this distinctiveness deserves close attention because it explains much of what follows in this chapter. Rather than relying primarily on military conquest to eliminate rivals, Wang Geon pursued a strategy that combined selective military action with sustained efforts to make alignment with Goryeo attractive to regional elites, defeated rivals, and uncommitted hojok alike.

This strategy took several concrete forms. Wang Geon distributed titles, ranks, and honors to hojok leaders who aligned with Goryeo, formalizing their local authority within his emerging state rather than attempting to strip it away. He granted land and economic privileges to families whose cooperation he sought. Most distinctively, he pursued an extensive program of political marriage, reportedly taking wives from a large number of prominent regional families across the peninsula over the course of his reign, a practice examined further in Section 19.11. Each marriage created a kinship tie binding a powerful family's interests to the continued success of the Wang royal house, transforming potential rivals or neutral parties into stakeholders in Goryeo's survival.

This approach reflected both principle and necessity. Wang Geon lacked the resources to conquer and directly administer the entire peninsula through military force alone, particularly in the fragmented political landscape inherited from late Silla, where local power had become deeply entrenched. But the approach also reflected a recognizable political philosophy: a durable state, in Wang Geon's evident understanding, required broad elite buy-in rather than mere battlefield dominance. Rivals humiliated and dispossessed had every incentive to rebel at the first opportunity; rivals granted continued status, land, and marriage ties had a competing incentive to support the new order that had absorbed them.

This is not to suggest that coercion played no role. Wang Geon fought numerous campaigns, suffered serious defeats as well as achieving victories, and did not hesitate to use military force against determined resistance. The point is rather that military force functioned within a broader strategy in which incorporation, not elimination, was the preferred long-term outcome for defeated or submitting rivals. The remainder of this chapter traces how this strategy played out against Later Baekje's military challenge, Silla's gradual decline, and the disparate ambitions of the hojok.

Section 19.3

Regional Elites and the Problem of Hojok Power

Chapter 18 explained how the hojok emerged from the breakdown of Silla's central authority during the ninth century, building local power through fortified strongholds, private military retainers, control of agricultural surplus, and, in coastal regions, access to maritime trade. By the early tenth century, the peninsula's political landscape was less a contest among three unified states than a shifting mosaic in which Goryeo, Silla, and Later Baekje each represented coalitions of hojok loyalty layered atop a royal or dynastic center, with many hojok families retaining substantial independent leverage regardless of which state they nominally served.

This structural reality created both danger and opportunity for Wang Geon. The danger was that hojok loyalty could shift, and had shifted before: many of the same regional strongmen who had once served Silla, or who had backed Gung Ye's rapid early expansion, felt no automatic or permanent obligation to any particular court. A hojok family disappointed by Goryeo's treatment could defect to Later Baekje, or declare effective independence, with real consequences for the balance of power. The opportunity was that hojok loyalty could be won, and won repeatedly, through the same tools of title, land, and marriage that Wang Geon employed throughout his reign.

Wang Geon's approach to the hojok problem was neither to attempt their wholesale destruction, which would have required resources and provoked resistance he could not afford, nor to leave their autonomy entirely unchecked, which would have left his own authority perpetually contingent on local goodwill. Instead, he pursued a gradual transformation of autonomous hojok power into dynastic support: confirming existing local authority in exchange for formal allegiance, granting honorary titles that flattered hojok status while symbolically subordinating it to the Goryeo court, and binding leading families to the royal house through marriage.

This transformation was neither instantaneous nor complete by 936. Regional autonomy remained a persistent feature of Goryeo's political landscape for generations, and the tension between central royal authority and powerful aristocratic families descended in part from these hojok lineages would recur throughout Goryeo's history, examined further in Chapter 20 and Chapter 22. But the initial achievement of converting a critical mass of hojok families from potentially hostile independent actors into invested participants in Goryeo's success was essential to Wang Geon's ability to prevail over his rivals.

Section 19.4

Later Baekje Takes the Offensive

Any accurate account of the reunification wars must give Gyeon Hwon and Later Baekje genuine historical weight, since a narrative that treats Wang Geon's eventual victory as inevitable obscures how serious a threat Later Baekje actually posed for more than a decade. Gyeon Hwon had built his state on real foundations: a fertile agricultural base in the southwest, access to maritime trade routes across the Yellow Sea, an effective and experienced military establishment drawing on his own background as a Silla officer, and diplomatic outreach to political authorities on the Chinese mainland during a period when the collapse of the Tang dynasty had produced a fragmented and competitive continental landscape.

Through the 910s and 920s, Later Baekje pursued an active and often successful military strategy aimed at expanding its territory and weakening its rivals. Gyeon Hwon's forces campaigned against Silla's remaining territory, pressured Goryeo's southern frontier, and sought to extend Later Baekje's influence into contested border regions where hojok loyalty remained unsettled. Later Baekje's military capability during this period was, by most assessments available to historians, at least comparable to Goryeo's, and Gyeon Hwon showed himself willing to strike decisively when opportunity presented itself, as the events of 927 would demonstrate.

Gyeon Hwon's strategic position also benefited from Silla's continuing weakness. As the nominal center of the old order, Silla remained a symbolically important target and a source of legitimacy for whichever power could claim to have decisively acted upon it, whether through conquest, protection, or the installation of a compliant ruler. Later Baekje's willingness to strike directly at Gyeongju, examined in the following section, reflected a calculated bid to seize this symbolic and strategic prize before Goryeo could do so.

Section 19.5

The Sack of Gyeongju and the Crisis of 927

The events of 927 mark the chapter's central turning point and deserve sustained attention, because they demonstrate decisively that Goryeo's eventual triumph in 936 was not a foregone conclusion nearly a decade earlier. In that year, Gyeon Hwon led Later Baekje forces in a direct assault on Gyeongju, Silla's capital. The city, though no longer commanding the political and military resources of Silla's height, remained largely undefended against a determined military campaign, and Gyeon Hwon's forces entered it with limited resistance.

The consequences were severe. King Gyeongae, the reigning Silla monarch, died during or immediately following the attack, in circumstances that later Korean historical tradition describes in dramatic terms emphasizing the completeness of Silla's humiliation. Gyeon Hwon did not attempt to annex Silla territory outright at this stage; instead, he installed a new king, Gyeongsun, who would later prove central to the peninsula's political future in a manner Gyeon Hwon could scarcely have anticipated. The installation of a client ruler at Gyeongju represented a significant assertion of Later Baekje's dominance over the old Silla state and a direct challenge to any claim Goryeo might make to protect or inherit Silla's legitimacy.

Wang Geon responded by moving to relieve Gyeongju and confront Gyeon Hwon's forces, but the resulting engagement, traditionally identified as the Battle of Gongsan near modern Daegu, ended in a serious defeat for Goryeo. Wang Geon's forces were routed, and the king himself narrowly escaped capture or death, a danger from which he was saved, according to the tradition preserved in later Goryeo historical accounts, by the sacrifice of his commander Shin Sung-gyeom, who is said to have exchanged garments or otherwise drawn Later Baekje's forces toward himself to allow Wang Geon to escape, dying in the process.

The significance of 927 for this chapter's argument cannot be overstated. In the space of a single year, Later Baekje demonstrated the capacity to strike at the peninsula's most symbolically important city, depose and effectively cause the death of a reigning Silla king, install a client ruler of its own choosing, and inflict a near-catastrophic defeat on Goryeo's own founder. Had Wang Geon been killed or captured at Gongsan, Goryeo's future would have been thrown into immediate uncertainty, quite possibly ending the dynasty before it had survived a single decade. Students should understand 927, therefore, not as a minor setback on an otherwise steady path to reunification but as a moment when the entire trajectory of the Later Three Kingdoms period might plausibly have turned in Later Baekje's favor.

Section 19.6

Recovery and the Shift in Momentum

Wang Geon's response to the defeat at Gongsan illustrates the coalition-building strategy examined in Section 19.2 operating under genuine pressure. Rather than retreating into a purely defensive posture, Wang Geon worked over the following years to rebuild his military position while continuing to cultivate alliances with hojok and regional elites who might otherwise have concluded, after 927, that Later Baekje represented the stronger long-term bet.

The clearest evidence of a shift in momentum comes from 930, when Goryeo forces achieved a significant victory over Later Baekje at the Battle of Gochang, near modern Andong in the eastern peninsula. This victory mattered for reasons beyond its immediate military outcome. The Andong region sat within a zone of contested hojok loyalty, and Goryeo's success at Gochang appears to have encouraged a broader realignment of eastern provincial elites toward Wang Geon's cause, as local families calculated that Goryeo's fortunes were improving relative to Later Baekje's.

This pattern, in which military outcomes influenced elite political calculation as much as they determined territorial control outright, is characteristic of the entire reunification period and should inform how students interpret the individual battles this chapter describes. No single engagement, including Gochang, immediately resolved the contest between Goryeo and Later Baekje. Rather, a cumulative sequence of military successes, defections, and alliance-building gradually shifted the balance of advantage toward Wang Geon over the years following 930, setting the stage for the more dramatic developments of 935 and 936.

Section 19.7

Silla's Final Years and Voluntary Submission, 935

Silla's end as an independent state deserves careful historical framing, because the standard shorthand describing Goryeo as having "conquered" Silla misrepresents what actually occurred and obscures a key element of Wang Geon's broader strategy. By the mid-930s, Silla under King Gyeongsun, the ruler installed by Gyeon Hwon after the events of 927, controlled only a fraction of its former territory and possessed neither the military capacity nor, by most assessments, the political will to continue functioning as an independent power caught between two much stronger neighbors.

In 935, Gyeongsun made the decision to submit Silla to Goryeo voluntarily, formally ending the Silla royal house's rule, which by traditional chronology extended back nearly a millennium, though the precision of that early chronology is not equally secure across its full span, through negotiated transfer rather than military defeat. This distinction matters historically for several reasons. First, it reflects Gyeongsun's own political calculation that submission to Goryeo offered better terms, and a more secure future for himself and the Silla aristocracy, than continued independence or the alternative of eventual conquest by Later Baekje, whose forces had, after all, already sacked Gyeongju once and caused the death of a previous Silla king. Second, and more significant for this chapter's argument, Wang Geon's handling of the submission demonstrates the incorporation strategy examined throughout this chapter in its clearest form.

Rather than treating the defeated Silla royal house as a conquered and dispossessed remnant, Wang Geon welcomed Gyeongsun into the Goryeo court with substantial honor, granting him high aristocratic rank and, according to the tradition preserved in later Goryeo sources, marriage into the Wang royal family itself. Much of the existing Silla aristocracy found continuing places within Goryeo's expanding administrative and social hierarchy, bringing with them centuries of accumulated bureaucratic experience, cultural prestige, and Buddhist religious patronage that would substantially shape Goryeo's own governing institutions and court culture, examined further in Chapter 20 and Chapter 21.

This outcome should not be romanticized as painless or universally welcomed. Submission ended nearly a millennium of Silla royal continuity, and the incorporation of Silla elites into Goryeo's hierarchy inevitably created new competitions for status and position within the expanded court. But compared to the alternative outcomes visible elsewhere in this period, including Later Baekje's much more violent internal collapse examined in the following section, Silla's absorption into Goryeo through negotiated submission represents a striking demonstration of how incorporation could serve the interests of both the absorbing and the absorbed party.

Section 19.8

The Crisis Inside Later Baekje

While Silla's end came through negotiated submission, Later Baekje's collapse came through internal violence, and this contrast is itself historically instructive. Gyeon Hwon, by the mid-930s an aging ruler who had built and led Later Baekje for more than three decades, faced a succession crisis among his own sons. According to the tradition preserved in later historical accounts, Gyeon Hwon favored a younger son as his intended successor, a decision that provoked resentment among his elder sons, particularly Singeom.

In 935, Singeom moved against his own father, seizing power within Later Baekje and confining Gyeon Hwon, reportedly at a Buddhist monastery. This was not a foreign conquest or an external military defeat; it was a family and factional rupture at the heart of Later Baekje's ruling house, and it fractured the very military and political apparatus that had made Later Baekje such a formidable rival to Goryeo throughout the preceding decades.

What followed illustrates the central argument of this chapter with unusual clarity. Gyeon Hwon, rather than accepting confinement or seeking to rebuild his position within Later Baekje, escaped and made his way to Wang Geon's court, arriving as a refugee seeking protection from the very ruler he had fought for more than two decades, including inflicting upon him one of the most serious military defeats of his career at Gongsan in 927. Wang Geon received Gyeon Hwon not as a prisoner or a trophy but, according to the tradition, with considerable honor and material support, a further demonstration of the incorporation strategy that had already absorbed the Silla royal house.

This episode should not be read as evidence that Wang Geon's victory over Later Baekje was therefore inevitable or easily achieved. Later Baekje under Singeom remained a functioning military threat, and the final campaign examined in the following section still required actual fighting. But Gyeon Hwon's defection deprived Later Baekje of its founding leader's authority and, quite plausibly, of critical intelligence and support that assisted Goryeo's subsequent campaign. Historians reasonably regard the internal fragmentation of Later Baekje as at least as important to the final outcome in 936 as any single military engagement.

Section 19.9

The Defeat of Later Baekje and Reunification, 936

With Silla's submission secured in 935 and Later Baekje weakened by Gyeon Hwon's overthrow and defection, Wang Geon launched the campaign that would complete the reunification of the peninsula's principal successor states. In 936, Goryeo forces engaged and decisively defeated Singeom's Later Baekje army, in an engagement conventionally associated with the Battle of Illicheon. Later Baekje's resistance collapsed following this defeat, and Singeom submitted to Goryeo, ending Later Baekje's independent existence roughly thirty-six years after Gyeon Hwon had founded it.

Students should understand what "reunification" accurately describes at this moment and what it does not. Goryeo had brought Silla's former territory, Later Baekje's former territory, and its own original base under a single ruling dynasty, ending the three-way political competition that had characterized the Later Three Kingdoms period since the early tenth century. This was a genuine and historically significant achievement, restoring single dynastic rule over the peninsula's core territory for the first time since the fragmentation of Silla's centralized order examined in Chapter 18.

Reunification did not, however, mean the immediate erasure of regional identity, the instant dismantling of hojok autonomy, or the creation of a uniformly administered centralized state. Former Silla and former Later Baekje elites entered Goryeo's expanding hierarchy carrying distinct regional loyalties, cultural traditions, and, in some cases, lingering resentments that would continue to shape Goryeo's internal politics for generations. The administrative and institutional structures needed to govern this newly unified but still internally diverse territory were themselves a work in progress in 936, developed further over the following decades and examined in full in Chapter 20.

Nor should 936 be read as restoring some timeless, previously existing political unity. The peninsula had experienced substantial periods of multi-state competition throughout its earlier history, examined in Chapters 7 through 18, and Goryeo's unification represented a new political achievement rather than the recovery of an old and interrupted normal condition. Wang Geon had built something new out of genuinely disparate political traditions, not restored something that had always existed and merely been temporarily disrupted.

Section 19.10

Balhae Refugees and the Northern Inheritance

Alongside the southern struggle against Silla and Later Baekje, Goryeo's early history intersected with a second major political collapse: the fall of Balhae, the northern kingdom examined in Chapter 16, which had governed extensive territory in the northern peninsula and Manchuria as Silla's northern counterpart since the late seventh century. In 926, Balhae fell under sustained military pressure from the Khitan, a steppe power then building the state that would become the Liao dynasty, examined further in Chapter 22.

Balhae's collapse sent refugees southward into Goryeo territory, including, according to Goryeo historical tradition, members of the Balhae royal house itself. Wang Geon received these refugees, incorporating at least some prominent figures into his own court in a manner consistent with the broader incorporation strategy examined throughout this chapter. This reception carried significance beyond its immediate humanitarian or political dimensions. Because Balhae had itself been founded substantially by former Goguryeo elites, as Chapter 16 explains, Goryeo's welcome of Balhae refugees allowed the new dynasty to position itself as heir not only to Silla's southern legitimacy, absorbed through Gyeongsun's submission, but also to a northern political tradition reaching back through Balhae to Goguryeo.

This claim should not be overstated. Goryeo did not recover Balhae's former territory, most of which came under Khitan control or remained contested among various successor populations, and the integration of Balhae refugees into Goryeo society, while historically significant, involved only a portion of Balhae's former population rather than a wholesale absorption of the fallen kingdom. Nevertheless, the symbolic value of this northern connection mattered considerably for how Goryeo understood its own place in Northeast Asian history, reinforcing the dynasty's invocation of Goguryeo's memory, already visible in the names chosen by both Gung Ye and Wang Geon, and contributing to the northward-looking territorial ambitions and periodic conflicts with the Khitan-ruled Liao state examined in Chapter 22.

Section 19.11

Marriage, Incorporation, and the Construction of a New Dynastic Order

By the time reunification was completed in 936, Wang Geon presided over a coalition assembled from genuinely disparate and, in some cases, formerly hostile political traditions: his own original hojok and military base from the Songak region; the incorporated Silla royal house and aristocracy, carrying centuries of bureaucratic and cultural prestige; defeated and defected Later Baekje elites, including Gyeon Hwon himself; Balhae refugees claiming a northern political inheritance; and a wide array of regional hojok families across the peninsula whose loyalty had been secured through title, land, and marriage over the preceding two decades.

Marriage functioned as the connective tissue binding this coalition together, and its scale deserves emphasis. Later Goryeo historical tradition records that Wang Geon took a remarkably large number of wives over the course of his life, drawn from prominent families across the peninsula's major regions, including the incorporated Silla royal house. Each marriage created concrete kinship obligations linking a powerful family's fortunes to the continued success of the Wang royal line, transforming what might otherwise have remained separate and potentially rival power bases into an interconnected network with a shared stake in Goryeo's stability.

This strategy explains much of why Wang Geon's settlement proved durable where Gung Ye's had not. Gung Ye had relied increasingly on personal authority, suspicion, and coercion, alienating even his own senior commanders, one of whom, Wang Geon, ultimately displaced him. Wang Geon instead built a coalition large enough, and with enough internal stake in the new order's success, that important former rivals, from the Silla aristocracy to Gyeon Hwon himself, had genuine reasons to support rather than undermine Goryeo's continuation.

This achievement carried longer-term consequences examined in Chapter 20. The same marriage alliances and incorporated aristocratic families that made Wang Geon's reunification possible also created a powerful, marriage-connected aristocracy with substantial independent standing at court, a structural feature that would shape, and periodically constrain, royal authority throughout Goryeo's subsequent history. The very success of the coalition-building strategy examined in this chapter thus planted the institutional seeds of dynamics that later Goryeo rulers would need to manage.

Section 19.12

Wang Geon's Legacy and the Political Principles of Early Goryeo

Wang Geon, known posthumously by his temple name Taejo, meaning "Great Progenitor," died in 943, according to Goryeo's traditional dynastic chronology, having reigned as founding ruler of a unified Goryeo for roughly seven years following the completion of reunification in 936. His reputation within later Goryeo historical memory rested less on any single military triumph than on his demonstrated capacity to convert a fragmented and violently competitive political landscape into a functioning, broadly legitimated dynastic order.

Later Goryeo tradition preserves a set of instructions attributed to Wang Geon, traditionally composed in 942 and entrusted to the official Park Sul-hui shortly before his death the following year, known as the Ten Injunctions, addressed to his successors and offering guidance on matters including royal succession, relations with Buddhism and native ritual practice, caution toward Khitan customs, the northern secondary capital, and the general conduct expected of Goryeo's rulers. This chapter's Primary Source Window examines this text in detail and urges appropriate caution about treating it as a verbatim record of Wang Geon's own words rather than a later transmitted statement of early Goryeo governing concerns, quite possibly shaped or elaborated by later compilers responding to their own political circumstances.

Read critically, the Injunctions nonetheless illuminate real and pressing concerns for a dynasty built through the coalition strategy examined throughout this chapter: how to manage a newly unified but regionally diverse kingdom without provoking renewed fragmentation, how to balance the influence of a politically powerful Buddhist religious establishment against royal authority, and how to secure orderly succession within a royal house whose own founding marriage alliances had produced an unusually large number of potential heirs. These concerns would occupy Goryeo's rulers and court institutions for generations, and this chapter's account of the coalition Wang Geon built provides the necessary background for understanding why they arose. Chapter 20 takes up the fuller institutional history of how Goryeo's monarchy, bureaucracy, and aristocracy actually functioned in the decades following Wang Geon's death.

Historical Vocabulary
Hojok and Marriage Politics

Hojok (regional strongmen) is the term historians use for the local power holders who emerged from the breakdown of Silla's centralized authority during the ninth century, examined fully in Chapter 18. By Wang Geon's era, hojok controlled fortified towns, private military retainers, agricultural surplus, and, along the coasts, maritime trade, and their cooperation was essential to any state seeking to govern the peninsula.

Political marriage, in this context, refers to the deliberate use of royal or dynastic marriage to create binding kinship obligations between a ruling house and powerful families, transforming potential rivals into invested stakeholders. Wang Geon's extensive marriage network, drawing wives from prominent families across the peninsula including the incorporated Silla royal house, exemplifies this practice and helps explain Goryeo's political durability.

Students should avoid treating these marriages as merely personal or romantic matters; in this political context, marriage functioned as a formal instrument of state-building, comparable in function to treaties, titles, or land grants, though it also created lasting social and successional consequences examined further in Chapter 20.

Historical Vocabulary
The Ten Injunctions (Hunyo Sipjo)

The Ten Injunctions are a set of ten instructions traditionally composed by Wang Geon in 942 and entrusted to the official Park Sul-hui shortly before his death in 943, intended to guide his royal successors. They are preserved only through later historical compilation rather than any surviving contemporary manuscript, and their full content is examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window below.

The provisions touch on Buddhist patronage and restraint in temple construction, native ritual observances such as the Palgwanhoe, orderly succession, caution toward Khitan customs following Balhae's collapse, the strategic importance of the northern secondary capital at Seogyeong, and standards of royal governance. One disputed provision concerning appointments from certain southern regions has drawn particular scholarly suspicion as a possible later addition.

Students should treat the Ten Injunctions as valuable evidence of early Goryeo's governing concerns while remaining aware that the text's transmission history makes it impossible to verify as a word-for-word record of Wang Geon's own instructions.

Primary Source Window

The Ten Injunctions Attributed to Wang Geon

Creator: traditionally attributed to Wang Geon (Taejo) · Date: traditionally composed in 942 and entrusted to the official Park Sul-hui shortly before Wang Geon's death in 943; preserved only through later historical compilation · Type: Testamentary instructions to royal successors

Context: Later Goryeo historical tradition preserves ten instructions said to have been composed by Wang Geon for his successors near the end of his reign. As with many testamentary political texts from this period, the instructions survive only through later transmission and compilation, which means their precise original wording cannot be verified with certainty, and later historians have particularly debated the authenticity of the eighth provision.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the ten provisions historians generally associate with this tradition.

The provisions are said to have addressed, among other matters: the dynasty's debt to Buddhist protection and a caution against indiscriminately founding new temples beyond what earlier geomantic and religious authorities had sanctioned; the importance of orderly royal succession; a warning against adopting the customs of the Khitan, described as a people without established civilization; the strategic and symbolic importance of the northern secondary capital at Seogyeong (Pyongyang); the continued observance of the Buddhist Yeondeunghoe festival and the native Palgwanhoe festival honoring heaven, spirits, and mountain and river deities; the necessity of winning popular support, governing moderately, and accepting the remonstrance of officials; fair treatment of official stipends and continued attention to military preparedness; and the ruler's obligation to study history and the Confucian classics. One provision, whose authenticity later historians have questioned, is described as urging caution toward the appointment of people from certain southern regions associated with Later Baekje's former territory.

Analysis: Whether or not every provision reflects Wang Geon's own exact words, the concerns the text addresses correspond closely to the historical situation this chapter has described: a kingdom assembled from formerly hostile regions and elites, continued vigilance toward the Khitan following Balhae's collapse, a religiously plural court balancing Buddhist and native ritual traditions, and a royal house whose marriage-based coalition strategy had produced numerous potential heirs. The text is therefore valuable evidence for the governing anxieties of early Goryeo, even where its precise authorship, wording, and completeness remain uncertain.

Limitations: Historians cannot confirm that the text preserves Wang Geon's actual words rather than a later reconstruction or elaboration shaped by the concerns of the compilers who eventually recorded it. The provision regarding southern appointments in particular has drawn sustained scholarly suspicion as a possible later interpolation reflecting regional prejudices of a later period rather than Wang Geon's own policy. Students should treat the whole text as evidence of early Goryeo political culture and later historical memory rather than as a verified transcript of the founder's final instructions.

Historical Debate

Conquest or Coalition? Explaining Goryeo's Success

The question: Should Goryeo's reunification of the peninsula by 936 be explained primarily as a military achievement, resulting from superior generalship and battlefield success, or primarily as a political achievement, resulting from Wang Geon's skill at incorporation, marriage alliance, and coalition management? Historians examining this period weigh these two kinds of explanation differently.

Interpretation A — Military capability was decisive: Some historians emphasize that Goryeo still needed to win real battles, including the costly recovery after the defeat at Gongsan and the final campaign against Later Baekje in 936, and that without adequate military strength no amount of alliance-building could have produced reunification.
Interpretation B — Political incorporation was decisive: Other historians emphasize that Goryeo's rivals, particularly Later Baekje, possessed comparable or superior military capability for much of the period, and that what ultimately distinguished Wang Geon's success was his capacity to convert hojok loyalty, secure Silla's voluntary submission, and absorb Gyeon Hwon's own defection, none of which resulted directly from battlefield victory alone.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: the recorded battles of 927 and 930, the circumstances of Silla's submission in 935, the internal collapse of Later Baekje in the same year, and the final campaign of 936. They differ in how much explanatory weight to assign to military versus political factors.

Why it matters: The interpretation chosen affects how students understand not only Goryeo's founding but state formation in general: whether military power alone typically determines the outcome of contests among rival states, or whether the capacity to build durable political coalitions often proves equally or more important.

Student task: Using the specific events examined in this chapter, construct an argument for which interpretation better explains Goryeo's success, or explain why both factors should be considered jointly necessary rather than competing explanations.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through Reunification

From the perspective of a Goryeo commander who survived Gongsan: The defeat of 927 demonstrated how quickly fortune could turn against even a well-established leader, and it reinforced the necessity of building alliances broad enough to survive individual military setbacks.

From the perspective of a hojok family in the contested eastern provinces: Repeated shifts in the military balance between Goryeo and Later Baekje through the 920s made calculating which side to support a genuinely difficult and consequential decision, one that could determine whether the family retained its local standing or lost everything.
From the perspective of a soldier in Gyeon Hwon's Later Baekje army after 935: The sudden overthrow of the founding ruler by his own son threw the army's loyalties into confusion at the worst possible moment, undermining morale and cohesion just as Goryeo prepared its final campaign.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Silla's Order to Goryeo's Order

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from three competing courts, plus numerous semi-independent hojok, to a single ruling dynasty by 936
  • The Silla royal house moved from independent rulership to incorporated aristocratic status within Goryeo
  • Later Baekje ceased to exist as an independent polity, its founder spending his final year under Goryeo's protection and joining Wang Geon's own campaign against his former state
  • The peninsula's capital shifted from Gyeongju, Silla's ancient seat, to Songak, reflecting a new center of political gravity

What Stayed the Same

  • Regional identity and hojok influence persisted well beyond 936, continuing to shape Goryeo politics for generations
  • Much of Silla's aristocratic and Buddhist cultural tradition carried forward into Goryeo court life rather than being erased
  • The practice of securing political loyalty through marriage, already visible under earlier Korean states, continued as a central tool of Goryeo statecraft
  • Manchurian and northern political concerns, inherited in part from Balhae, remained a continuing feature of the peninsula's international position

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine transformation of 936 and the substantial continuities that survived it helps students avoid treating reunification as either a complete break with the past or a simple return to an unchanged status quo.

Cause and Effect

Conquest versus Incorporation in Goryeo's Founding

Military factors

Wang Geon's recovery from the 927 defeat at Gongsan, the 930 victory at Gochang, and the final 936 campaign against Later Baekje all required genuine battlefield success and cannot be discounted.

Political factors

Marriage alliances, the incorporation of hojok families, Silla's voluntary submission, and the absorption of Balhae refugees built a coalition broader than any single military campaign could have secured.

Rival weakness

Later Baekje's internal succession crisis in 935 fractured Goryeo's strongest rival from within, a development Goryeo's own strategy did not directly cause but was well positioned to exploit.

Combined effect

Reunification by 936 resulted from the interaction of military capability, coalition-building strategy, and a rival's internal collapse, not from any single one of these factors operating alone.

Historical complexity: Treating reunification as the product of any single cause, whether military genius, political cunning, or simple luck, oversimplifies a process that unfolded unevenly across nearly two decades and depended on decisions made by several different historical actors, not only Wang Geon himself.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reunification Beyond the Courts

The dynastic and military narrative that dominates surviving sources for this period should not obscure the experience of ordinary people across the peninsula during nearly two decades of intermittent warfare among Goryeo, Silla, and Later Baekje. Farming communities in contested border regions faced repeated disruption from campaigning armies, requisitioned supplies, and shifting political control, sometimes changing hands between rival states more than once within a few years. Artisans, merchants, and coastal populations connected to maritime trade networks, particularly around Songak and the western coast, experienced the reunification wars partly through the economic opportunities and risks created by shifting patronage, as Wang Geon's own family background in maritime commerce suggests these networks remained economically significant throughout the period.

For the hojok themselves, and for the soldiers, retainers, and dependent populations attached to their fortified towns, the calculation of which side to support carried immediate and personal stakes, since a family's decision to align with Goryeo, Later Baekje, or continued independence could determine whether local landholding, status, and even physical safety survived the conflict intact. Buddhist monastic communities, already influential under Silla and increasingly patronized by all three competing states, found themselves courted for the legitimacy their support could confer, a dynamic that helps explain the prominence of Buddhist patronage in the Ten Injunctions examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window and treated more fully in Chapter 21.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Wang Geon's newly founded state, established in 918 after the deposition of Gung Ye, from one of three principal rival political centers into the dynasty that brought the peninsula's principal successor states under one rule by 936. The chapter has emphasized throughout that this outcome was not inevitable. Silla retained genuine prestige and administrative tradition despite its territorial and political decline, even though it could no longer seriously contest its rivals militarily, and Later Baekje under Gyeon Hwon possessed military capability and resources fully comparable to Goryeo's own for much of the period examined here.

The events of 927 demonstrated this contingency most sharply. Gyeon Hwon's sack of Gyeongju, the death of King Gyeongae, the installation of a client ruler, and Wang Geon's own near-catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Gongsan together show a moment when Later Baekje's dominance appeared, if anything, more secure than Goryeo's. The chapter has argued that Goryeo's eventual recovery and victory depended less on any single decisive battle than on a sustained strategy of coalition building: the granting of titles and land to hojok families, the extensive use of political marriage to bind powerful lineages to the Wang royal house, and, above all, a consistent preference for incorporating rivals rather than destroying them.

This strategy is visible with particular clarity in the events of 935, when Silla's King Gyeongsun chose voluntary submission over continued vulnerable independence and received, in return, high rank, land, and marriage into the royal family rather than dispossession. It is visible again in Later Baekje's internal collapse in the same year, when Gyeon Hwon's own son Singeom seized power and the aging founder fled to the protection of his former enemy, receiving honored treatment rather than punishment. The final military campaign of 936, traditionally associated with the Battle of Illicheon, then completed the reunification that these prior political developments had already made far more likely.

The chapter has also traced a second, northward-facing dimension of Goryeo's early identity: the collapse of Balhae under Khitan pressure in 926 and the reception of Balhae refugees, including members of its royal house, within Goryeo. This connection allowed the new dynasty to claim an inheritance reaching through Balhae to Goguryeo, reinforcing the historical memory already invoked in the names chosen by both Gung Ye and Wang Geon and shaping Goryeo's later northern frontier concerns, examined further in Chapter 22.

By the time of Wang Geon's death in 943, he had constructed a genuinely new political order out of disparate and formerly hostile elements: his own hojok and military base, the incorporated Silla aristocracy, defected Later Baekje elites including Gyeon Hwon himself, absorbed Balhae refugees, and a broad network of regional families bound to the throne through marriage. The Ten Injunctions attributed to him, examined critically in this chapter's Primary Source Window, reflect the governing concerns natural to a dynasty built this way: managing regional diversity, balancing Buddhist religious influence, and securing orderly succession among an unusually large royal family.

This chapter's account of coalition building carries forward directly into Chapter 20's examination of Goryeo's mature governing institutions. The same marriage-connected aristocratic families and incorporated hojok lineages that made reunification possible would also constitute the social foundation, and periodically the political challenge, of Goryeo's monarchy, bureaucracy, and examination system in the decades that followed. Reunification, in other words, did not end the political work Wang Geon had begun; it established the terms on which that work would continue.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has told the story of how one regional successor state, founded amid genuine uncertainty in 918, became the dynasty that would govern the Korean peninsula for more than four and a half centuries. That story is not one of steady, predictable advance toward an assured outcome. It is a story of near-catastrophic defeat at Gongsan in 927, of patient alliance-building among hojok families whose loyalty could never be entirely taken for granted, of a defeated Silla king who found greater security in submission than in continued independence, and of a rival dynasty's founder who, received with honor by his fiercest enemy, spent his final year under Goryeo's protection and ultimately marched with Wang Geon's own army against the state he had founded.

The habits of historical thinking this chapter has emphasized, distinguishing conquest from incorporation, recognizing contingency rather than assuming inevitable outcomes, and giving genuine historical weight to defeated as well as victorious parties, will matter throughout the remainder of this textbook's treatment of Goryeo and beyond. Wang Geon's reunification succeeded not because his rivals were weak or his path was clear, but because he built a coalition resilient enough to survive real setbacks and flexible enough to absorb former enemies as readily as it rewarded loyal supporters.

This achievement set the terms for everything that followed. The marriage-connected aristocracy, incorporated regional elites, and absorbed Balhae inheritance examined in this chapter would shape Goryeo's monarchy, its relationship with Buddhism, its social hierarchy, and its northern frontier policy for generations, subjects taken up directly in Chapters 20 through 23. Understanding how Goryeo was built, through negotiation and incorporation as much as through victory in battle, is essential preparation for understanding how it was governed, and eventually how and why it would face the crises examined later in Volume I.
