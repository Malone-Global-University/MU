Japan Returns: Culture, Memory, and a Complicated Neighbor

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In October 1998 the president of South Korea, once kidnapped from a Tokyo hotel room by agents of his own country's intelligence service, stood in Japan as an honored guest and signed a declaration with Japan's prime minister. The document joined an apology for colonial rule to a promise of cultural exchange. The question it raised has not been settled in the decades since: can two societies grow closer in everything they watch, eat, wear, and buy while remaining divided over what their states owe each other for the past?

Kim Dae-jung's state visit to Japan in early October 1998 was freighted with personal and national history. As an opposition politician in 1973, Kim had been abducted from a hotel in Tokyo by operatives of South Korea's Korean Central Intelligence Agency and taken back to the peninsula, an episode that had long strained relations between Seoul and Tokyo. Twenty-five years later, he addressed the Japanese Diet as president of a democratic Republic of Korea, met Emperor Akihito, and on October 8 concluded the Joint Declaration with Prime Minister Keizō Obuchi. In it, Obuchi expressed deep remorse and a heartfelt apology for the damage and suffering that Japanese colonial rule had caused the Korean people, and Kim accepted that expression and spoke of building a future-oriented relationship. The declaration also noted South Korea's plan to open its market to Japanese popular culture in stages.

This pairing, apology and cultural opening in one diplomatic text, is the entry point to the chapter. For more than half a century after liberation in 1945, South Korea had restricted the legal import and distribution of Japanese films, recorded music, broadcasts, and much else, even though Japanese cultural influence never disappeared. The "return" of the chapter title therefore refers to the reopening of legal cultural markets beginning in 1998, not to the arrival of something wholly absent. Over the following years, Japanese manga, anime, games, films, and music became legally available; Korean television dramas, singers, cuisine, and cosmetics found audiences in Japan; the two countries co-hosted the 2002 FIFA World Cup; and students, tourists, and small businesses crossed the Korea Strait in growing numbers.

Yet the political relationship did not follow a single upward line. Disputes over the wartime system of sexual slavery, wartime forced labor, history textbooks, visits to Yasukuni Shrine, and the sovereignty of the Dokdo/Takeshima islets produced repeated crises, culminating in the 2018 South Korean Supreme Court judgments on forced labor, the 2019 trade dispute, and a diplomatic freeze. A March 2023 South Korean compensation proposal and the restoration of summit diplomacy then reopened cooperation, which continued through the sixtieth anniversary of normalization in 2025 and into 2026. The pattern is one of simultaneous cooperation and contest, and this chapter argues that cultural exchange did not erase historical memory, while historical disagreement did not prevent meaningful cooperation.

The chapter draws on many kinds of evidence, and its sections ask students to treat them differently: official declarations and treaties; court judgments; diplomatic statements that the two governments often described in conflicting ways; survivor testimony preserved in advocacy and archival records; regulatory decisions on cultural imports; broadcasting and publishing data; polling and journalism; and the memory politics of museums, textbooks, and shrines. A documented abuse, a legal ruling about who must remedy it, and a government's diplomatic position on that ruling are three different kinds of statement, and the chapter keeps them apart.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. One treats Korea and Japan as permanent antagonists whose cultural exchange is superficial. The other treats cultural affinity as the natural path to reconciliation, interrupted only by nationalist politicians. Both ignore the fact that people who enjoyed Japanese or Korean entertainment often retained firm views about their neighbor's government, and that governments cooperated on security and finance even as their publics quarreled about history. Participants on both sides include victims and their families, Zainichi Korean communities, fans, students, business owners, judges, bureaucrats, and elected leaders with different incentives.

The chapter's responsibility within the textbook is specific. Earlier chapters examined Japanese colonial rule (Chapters 55–62) and the developmental-state relationship with Japan after 1965 (Chapter 75). Chapter 84 described the financial crisis in which Kim Dae-jung took office, Chapter 85 his policy toward North Korea, and Chapter 87 the technology industries touched by the 2019 dispute. Chapters 89 and 90 will trace the Korean entertainment industries and their worldwide expansion, and Chapter 94 the wider strategic setting. Chapter 88 follows the bilateral relationship itself, from 1998 to November 2026.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why South Korea restricted Japanese popular culture after 1945, and why that restriction coexisted with informal Japanese cultural influence.
  2. Analyze the October 1998 Kim–Obuchi Joint Declaration as a document linking acknowledgment of the past to future-oriented cooperation.
  3. Trace the staged opening of South Korea to Japanese cultural products between 1998 and 2004 and distinguish import legalization from broadcasting access.
  4. Evaluate Korean reception of Japanese culture as selective adaptation rather than passive imitation.
  5. Describe Japan as a receiving society for Korean popular culture, including the case of Winter Sonata, and the 2002 World Cup as cooperative event.
  6. Explain the diverse experiences of Zainichi Koreans and why the relationship was never only between governments.
  7. Distinguish diplomatic normalization, intergovernmental settlement, individual legal claims, and demands for historical acknowledgment in the 1965 settlement and its aftermath.
  8. Compare the 1993, 1995, and 1998 Japanese statements and the 2015 and 2023 agreements, and assess why some victims rejected government settlements.
  9. Distinguish documented historical abuse from the legal and diplomatic controversies over remedy, using the comfort-women and forced-labor cases.
  10. Represent the Dokdo/Takeshima positions of both governments accurately as positions, separate from South Korea's present administration of the islets.
  11. Assess how economic interdependence and security concerns produced vulnerability in 2019 and cooperation afterward.
  12. Evaluate whether the relationship in 2026 is best described as durable cooperation, persistent contest, or both.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could South Korea and Japan grow more connected through culture, commerce, travel, and security while remaining divided over their shared history?
  2. Why did a state that restricted Japanese culture for decades choose to open it in stages, and why in 1998?
  3. Does consuming another country's culture imply approval of its government's historical conduct?
  4. How do an apology, a legal settlement, and individual compensation differ, and why did these differences matter so much?
  5. Whose consent is required for a historical settlement: governments, courts, victims, or all three?
  6. Why have small islets and a few historical sites carried such large symbolic weight?
  7. How did economic interdependence create both stability and political vulnerability in the 2019 dispute?
  8. How did democratization, political change, and shifting regional security shape each phase of the relationship?
  9. What does the Zainichi experience reveal that a state-to-state narrative cannot?
  10. What evidence would show that the relationship in 2026 is durably improved rather than temporarily calm?

Historical Context

Japan annexed Korea in 1910 and ruled it until Japan's surrender in 1945 (Chapters 54–62). Colonial rule brought a governing apparatus, railways, industrial and agricultural transformation, an expanding urban and print culture, and, especially after 1937, intensified mobilization of labor, soldiers, and women, together with assimilation policies directed against Korean language, names, and religion. Koreans experienced those decades unevenly: as resisters, accommodators, collaborators, migrants, workers, students, and many people who were none of these in any simple way. Liberation in 1945 did not end Japanese presence in Korean daily life, for the colonial period left behind buildings, institutions, vocabulary, educational habits, and a generation of Koreans fluent in Japanese.

After 1948, both Korean states constructed national narratives in which anti-colonial resistance was central. South Korea and Japan had no formal diplomatic relations until the Treaty on Basic Relations of June 1965, concluded under Park Chung-hee and Japanese Prime Minister Satō Eisaku after long negotiations and amid large South Korean protests (Chapter 75). The accompanying agreements provided Japanese grants and loans and declared property and claims issues settled, a bargain that served South Korea's developmental needs and the American-backed alliance structure of the Cold War but left many questions of colonial responsibility open. Economic ties and personal contacts deepened thereafter, yet Seoul continued to bar most Japanese films, songs, and broadcasts.

Democratization after 1987 (Chapter 82) changed the terms of the debate. Civil society organizations could press historical issues, survivors of colonial and wartime abuse could speak publicly, and governments faced electorates that were no longer silent about the past. In 1991 Kim Hak-sun became the first Korean woman to give public testimony as a former "comfort woman," a moment that opened a long campaign. Japan, for its part, issued the 1993 Kōno Statement and the 1995 Murayama Statement. The 1997–98 financial crisis (Chapter 84) and Kim Dae-jung's election supplied the immediate setting for the 1998 turn: a president with democratic credentials, a government seeking economic partners, and a Japanese leadership prepared to offer a written apology.

Two further conditions shaped the period. First, economic interdependence ran deep: South Korea's industrial rise had relied on Japanese machinery, components, and materials, and Japanese firms had in turn relied on Korean markets and manufacturing. Second, both countries sat within an American alliance system facing North Korea and, increasingly, China. These shared interests created incentives for cooperation, but they did not decide how either public would remember the colonial period.

Section 88.1

From Colonial Rule to Cultural Restriction: The Historical Background

South Korea's restrictions on Japanese popular culture rested on a mixture of legal measures, administrative rules, and broadcasting standards that, over decades, barred or limited the legal import of Japanese films, recorded music, television programs, and similar products. The rationale combined several concerns. After liberation, Korean nationalism defined itself against colonial rule, and a state led by veterans of that struggle regarded Japanese-language culture as a reminder of the assimilation policies that had pressed Koreans to adopt Japanese language and names (Chapter 60). Policymakers also feared that a larger and more commercially developed Japanese entertainment industry could overwhelm a smaller Korean one, and that wounds from the colonial period would be reopened by unrestricted circulation of Japanese cultural products. Distrust of Japanese political intentions persisted, reinforced by controversies over Japanese textbooks and statements by Japanese politicians.

Restriction was never complete. Historians who study this period emphasize the leakage. Japanese television signals could be received along parts of the southern coast near Tsushima. Japanese manga circulated in unauthorized translations, and Korean publishers adapted Japanese stories under altered names or without attribution. Japanese animation reached Korean children through broadcasts that changed character names and removed signs of origin, so that many young viewers did not know the programs were Japanese. Korean studios performed subcontracted animation work for Japanese producers. Japanese video games and consoles entered through gray markets, and Japanese music traveled by cassette and later by imported disc. Korean musicians and writers absorbed Japanese models, sometimes openly and sometimes quietly. Business travelers, students, and Korean families with Japanese connections kept personal channels open.

This history matters for the chapter's argument. The post-1998 opening did not introduce Japanese culture into a society without it; it brought into the open, and under legal and commercial regulation, a presence that had been informal, unacknowledged, or illegal. The change was in visibility, legality, and market structure, and the shift made it possible for audiences, critics, and businesses to discuss Japanese influence in public. The evidence for pre-1998 circulation is uneven: it relies on memoirs, press reports, industry recollection, and later scholarship rather than on systematic records, and its scale is easy to exaggerate.

Cultural prohibitions also have to be understood as administrative history, not as a single law applied uniformly to every medium. Film distribution, broadcasting schedules, customs licensing, and record sales followed distinct official channels; publishers, local broadcasters, and importers could therefore experience the restrictions differently. Liberalization altered those channels in stages, producing new incentives for copyright licensing and legal distribution. A historian seeking to measure its impact would compare contemporaneous ministry decisions with import records, theater bookings, publishing sales, audience surveys, and broadcast schedules. Those records are more persuasive than retrospective claims that an entire nation either embraced or rejected Japanese culture. The legal and commercial transition changed how Japanese-origin works could be labeled, advertised, criticized and purchased openly.

Section 88.2

Kim Dae-jung, Keizō Obuchi, and the 1998 New Partnership

When Kim Dae-jung took office in February 1998, South Korea was in the early stages of the financial crisis and IMF program described in Chapter 84. Kim, who had spent years as an opposition leader, imprisoned and under threat, entered office with a program of democratic consolidation and engagement with North Korea (Chapter 85). Toward Japan, he proposed a different kind of relationship from the confrontational pattern of earlier decades. Japan, under Prime Minister Obuchi, was itself dealing with a prolonged economic downturn and sought stable regional partners. Both leaders had practical reasons to seek a durable agreement, and both faced domestic critics who suspected concessions.

The Joint Declaration of October 8, 1998, titled in English a declaration on a new Japan–Republic of Korea partnership toward the twenty-first century, is a short text with an accompanying action plan listing numerous items of cooperation. In its central passage, as paraphrased here, the Japanese prime minister stated in a spirit of humility that Japan's colonial rule had inflicted tremendous damage and suffering on the Korean people and expressed deep remorse and a heartfelt apology. The Korean president accepted this acknowledgment with appreciation and expressed the hope that both countries would overcome their unfortunate history and build a future-oriented relationship. The declaration committed both sides to expanded exchange in education, youth contacts, and culture, to cooperation in the economy and security, and to joint success in the 2002 World Cup. It also addressed South Korea's plan to open its market to Japanese popular culture.

The declaration's drafters connected acknowledgment and future cooperation deliberately: Kim argued that Japan's postwar record as a peaceful democracy deserved recognition and that Korea should not hold future generations hostage to the past, while the apology was meant to give that argument moral credibility with Korean audiences. The text did not address individual compensation, did not name specific wartime systems, and did not claim to resolve the legal questions left from 1965. Critics in Korea thought the apology insufficiently concrete; critics in Japan thought it conceded too much. Later events, including statements by Japanese officials that seemed to qualify earlier acknowledgments, tested how far such declarations could bind successor governments. See the Primary Source Window on the declaration later in this chapter.

The declaration deserves attention as a crafted diplomatic bargain rather than simply a symbolic apology. Paragraph 2 linked direct recognition of the historical injury with a call for education about the past, especially among young people; other provisions addressed economic cooperation, regional stability, political exchange, and cultural contacts. These subjects were placed in the same instrument because mutual confidence was expected to support cooperation across policy fields. Still, an intergovernmental declaration could not determine what surviving victims considered adequate, and its promises depended on later ministries, broadcasters, cultural regulators, and elected leaders. The right historical comparison is therefore among the document's language, the changes that followed in cultural and economic practice, and the issues deliberately left outside its legal scope.

Section 88.3

The Four Stages of South Korea's Opening to Japanese Popular Culture, 1998–2004

The South Korean government implemented cultural opening in four announced stages rather than in a single decision. The first stage, announced on October 28, 1998, allowed Japanese films that had won awards at major international festivals, or were co-produced with Korean partners, and permitted the import of Japanese comics and some publications in book form. The second stage, announced on September 10, 1999, expanded film categories and permitted smaller indoor concerts by Japanese popular singers; permission for a concert was not the same as general authorization to sell Japanese-language recordings. The third stage, announced on June 27, 2000, widened film and animated-film categories, permitted larger live performances, computer games and selected cable or satellite programming, and allowed instrumental versions of Japanese popular-music CDs under specified rules. The fourth stage, effective January 1, 2004, allowed much broader film, game and music distribution, including CDs featuring Japanese-language vocals. Even then, Japanese content on terrestrial television remained restricted, although cable and satellite channels carried more. These distinctions follow the staged cultural-opening schedule summarized in historical scholarship citing South Korean cultural-policy records; the precise scope of a particular broadcast license must still be checked against the relevant regulation.

Why did the government proceed in stages? Officials in the Kim Dae-jung administration told audiences that Korean cultural industries needed time to prepare and that public opinion required reassurance. Gradualism allowed the government to answer critics who accused it of giving up national cultural defenses, and it allowed Korean producers to expand their capacity, a goal consistent with the administration's broader policy of promoting cultural industries as an economic sector after the financial crisis. It also allowed adjustment if Japanese products provoked public backlash. Movement was sequential partly because the Korean public's willingness to accept Japanese culture varied by genre: films and comics, associated with an educated audience, came before popular music and broadcasting, which were seen as more intimate and pervasive.

Students should keep three distinctions in view. Legalization of import is not the same as unrestricted broadcasting; staged liberalization is not full liberalization; and legal opening did not determine consumer response. Some Japanese films performed well in Korea after 1998, while others did not, and some predicted flood-tide imports of Japanese pop music failed to materialize in the form critics had feared. The best evidence includes official regulatory records, industry sales and box-office data, press coverage, and scholarship, and the chapter relies on those general patterns rather than precise audience counts.

The stages also followed the interruption and resumption of political trust. Controversies about Japanese history textbooks in 2001 complicated the intended timetable, while the successful joint hosting of the 2002 World Cup helped create conditions for later liberalization. This makes the opening a test of the chapter's larger argument: consumer-market policy could be affected by memory disputes even when officials sought to separate them. Students should build a comparative chart identifying for each stage the date, media category, permitted distribution channel and remaining restriction. Such a chart would show that the same year could bring freer access to one form of culture while other forms remained limited by terrestrial-broadcast policy.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultural liberalization

Definition: The relaxation of legal limits on the import, distribution, performance, and broadcast of foreign cultural products.

Historical context: In South Korea, liberalization of Japanese culture proceeded by categories and stages from 1998 to 2004 and left some broadcast restrictions in place.

Why it matters: It separates changes in law from changes in consumption, and shows that governments often manage cultural change gradually.

Potential misunderstanding: Liberalization did not mean that Japanese culture was previously unknown, that all restrictions ended at once, or that Koreans uniformly welcomed it.

Section 88.4

Manga, Anime, Japanese Games, Cinema, and Popular Music

Each cultural form followed its own path. Manga and comics, already circulating in unauthorized translation, moved into licensed publication with subtitles, translation standards, and rights payments. Anime shifted from altered television broadcasts to openly identified Japanese series, and an audience of fans emerged who valued Japanese origin rather than hiding it, including those who learned Japanese through viewing. Japanese console games, long available through gray markets, became legal commercial imports. Japanese films appeared in theaters and at film festivals, with art-house and mainstream audiences responding differently. In music, Japanese recordings and concerts entered more slowly and found a niche audience, while rock and independent scenes engaged with Japanese counterparts.

Korean consumers and producers did not simply absorb these products. Some Korean readers and viewers preferred domestic work and rejected Japanese titles; others became enthusiastic specialists. Korean publishers adapted formats and distribution models. Korean game developers, working in a market shaped by online games and internet cafés, followed a different path from the Japanese console tradition. Korean filmmakers had long watched Japanese cinema and now did so openly, sometimes producing remakes or adaptations, and sometimes creating work that engaged with Japanese models in ways that audiences read as competition. Korean musicians who had absorbed Japanese pop structures in the 1990s now worked within an environment in which that influence could be acknowledged and negotiated. Students should distinguish three categories: official imports, unofficial circulation, and Korean productions influenced by Japanese formats, and should avoid describing Korean cultural development as imitation.

The longer outcome undercut fears of cultural domination. South Korean entertainment industries grew in the decade after opening and began their own export expansion, a development examined in Chapters 89 and 90. That outcome was not determined in advance and had many causes, including digital infrastructure (Chapter 87), industry organization, state support, and market competition. The point for this chapter is narrower: the experience of opening suggested that exposure to Japanese culture did not produce one-directional dependence, and that a country can be both an importer and an exporter of cultural forms at once.

Copyright and translation are central to this transformation. When works moved from unauthorized editions and uncredited adaptations into licensed releases, rights owners, translators, distributors and regulators acquired new responsibilities and revenue opportunities. The shift changed how Korean fans encountered authorship: a reader could seek an original Japanese creator or compare a Korean localization with the source. Producers meanwhile faced competitive pressure in some genres but found opportunities for collaboration, joint promotion and co-production. Rather than infer a single national reaction from box-office successes, a stronger history would use specific releases, distribution arrangements and audience responses. Popularity in one genre could coexist with indifference in another, and both could change with economic conditions or political controversy.

Section 88.5

Cultural Liberalization, National Identity, and the Debate over Foreign Influence

The decision to open generated a public argument. Supporters, including some cultural-industry leaders, intellectuals, and younger consumers, contended that the restrictions were outdated, impossible to enforce given the leakage described in Section 88.1, and inconsistent with a democratic country's commitment to free expression. They argued that Korean producers could compete internationally, that openness would improve understanding of Japanese society, and that fear of Japanese culture implied insecurity unworthy of a confident country. Some added that Koreans could separate enjoying a manga from approving a political position.

Opponents raised other concerns. Survivors of colonial rule and their families, some veterans' and nationalist groups, and some cultural producers worried that an apology on paper was no guarantee of changed Japanese attitudes, particularly while Japanese politicians periodically made remarks that Koreans found dismissive of colonial wrongs. Producers feared competition from a larger industry with greater capital. Others worried about the effect on children and on Korean language and identity. These objections were not simply xenophobic; they reflected judgments about historical justice, economic fairness, and the unequal position from which Korea had entered the relationship.

The argument introduces a distinction central to the chapter. Cultural participation, meaning watching, reading, listening, buying, and traveling, is different from political approval of another state's historical conduct. A person can read Japanese novels and still hold strong views about wartime labor; a Japanese viewer can love Korean dramas and know little or nothing about the colonial period. Surveys and scholarship suggest that the two attitudes often coexisted, though the evidence for individual belief is limited and varies by generation and period. The opening therefore transformed what ordinary people encountered without resolving what governments, courts, and historians disputed.

The cultural debate also exposed a generational and institutional divide. A ministry concerned with the competitiveness of Korean publishers had different incentives from a family remembering wartime displacement, a cinema owner seeking new films, or a youth consumer who had already encountered Japanese animation. Public disagreement need not be reduced to a contest between an enlightened younger generation and an irrational older one: questions about market power, historical accountability and the practical enforceability of restrictions could all be reasonable subjects for debate. Evaluating the consequences therefore requires separate measures—legal availability, sales, employment in domestic cultural industries, surveys of attitudes and diplomatic trust—rather than an assumption that liberalization automatically produces reconciliation.

Section 88.6

The 2002 Korea–Japan FIFA World Cup and the Possibilities of Cooperation

FIFA's 1996 decision to award the 2002 World Cup jointly to South Korea and Japan followed a contest between rival bids and was, in effect, a compromise that required two countries with a difficult history to organize a single tournament. The 1998 Joint Declaration committed both governments to supporting the event's success. Preparations involved new stadiums in each country, coordination of security, visa and entry arrangements, transport, and media operations, and separate national organizing committees—the Korean Organizing Committee (KOWOC) and the Japan Organizing Committee (JAWOC)—which worked with FIFA and negotiated practical and symbolic matters, including the order in which the host names were listed. The tournament ran from May 31 to June 30, 2002, with South Korea's team reaching the semifinals and finishing fourth.

The event produced large public celebrations in South Korea, especially street gatherings in Seoul and other cities, and brought many visitors and international media attention to both countries. It gave journalists, volunteers, officials, and fans occasions for contact. Evidence for its effects comes from official evaluations, press coverage, and later surveys, and scholars differ on how lasting the effect on public attitudes was. Opinion polls in the period suggested warmer views of the neighbor among some groups, but these were hard to separate from the broader effect of cultural opening and the generational shift under way.

The World Cup shows what practical cooperation can and cannot do. Two governments could organize a complex event together, and spectators could cheer without hostility; the tournament did not, however, alter the positions of either government on historical or territorial questions, and these disputes returned within a few years. The event is best read as an example of the capacity of the two societies and governments to cooperate on specific, bounded tasks.

The organizational work behind joint hosting deserves as much attention as the spectacle. Sporting bodies, municipal governments, transport authorities, hotels, broadcasters and volunteer organizations had to cooperate while running venues in separate countries and operating under different administrative systems. This coordination formed a temporary but unusually visible infrastructure of bilateral contact. Yet the powerful public celebrations were also national events: Korean supporters celebrated their team's achievements, and Japanese spectators experienced a different host narrative. The fact that one tournament could strengthen pride in each country and provide opportunities for cross-border cooperation at the same time is historically important. Students should compare contemporaneous media coverage with post-tournament surveys rather than assuming that momentary enthusiasm represented a permanent shift in opinion.

The 2002 tournament can also serve as a source-comparison exercise. FIFA's organizational histories emphasize the achievement of co-hosting; national newspapers emphasize supporters, sporting performance and the identity of each host; municipal reports emphasize expenses and infrastructure. These perspectives are complementary but not identical. A balanced conclusion would distinguish the economic and logistical success of hosting from the much harder-to-measure development of enduring mutual trust. The competition offered shared experience, not a new legal or political settlement.

Section 88.7

Korean Popular Culture in Japan: Winter Sonata and Changing Public Perceptions

Reciprocity gave the relationship a second dimension. Korean films had appeared in Japan before the 2000s, but the wider reception of Korean popular culture there gathered pace in the early part of the decade. The Korean television drama Winter Sonata, first broadcast in South Korea in 2002, aired on Japan's public broadcaster NHK on its satellite service in 2003 and on its terrestrial service in 2004, and became a major success. Its lead actor, Bae Yong-joon, gained a devoted Japanese following; fans, a large number of them middle-aged women, formed fan groups, traveled to filming locations in Korea, studied Korean, bought associated merchandise, and joined online communities.

This section treats Japan as a receiving society. Japanese viewers brought their own interests, including attraction to the drama's emotional style, its portrayal of family and romance, and the sense of discovering a neighboring country that earlier media had represented mainly through news about North Korea, politics, and disputes. Japanese media, publishers, tour companies, and retailers responded by creating markets that did not exist before. Korean language classes grew, and the expansion of tourism and consumer markets connected individual enthusiasm to larger commercial networks. Some Japanese commentators welcomed the shift as evidence of improved understanding, while others criticized the fandom or minimized it, and a minority of Japanese media voices expressed hostility toward Korean cultural presence in later years.

The broader history of Hallyu, the industry structures behind it, and the global spread of Korean music, film, and television belong to Chapters 89 and 90. What matters here is the symmetry: after 1998 South Korea became a legal market for Japanese culture, and in the same period Japan became a major receiving society for Korean culture. The two processes shaped each other's public image, although neither dissolved the political disputes taken up below.

Audience history adds another layer beyond television ratings. Fans organized informal translation, travel plans, commemorative gatherings and purchases through personal networks, while broadcasters and tour operators then commercialized this demand. The Japanese reception of the drama also challenged a tendency to portray Korean life solely through political or economic news, permitting a different emotional vocabulary of familiarity and curiosity. At the same time, this did not necessarily translate into knowledge of colonial history or support for any particular diplomatic policy. The reactions of journalists, cultural critics and anti-Korean commentators should be examined alongside those of enthusiasts; a cultural phenomenon can broaden visibility and produce backlash. Its significance rests in how Japanese audiences actively made meaning, not simply in how Korean producers marketed a program.

Section 88.8

Tourism, Student Exchanges, Language Learning, and Youth Encounters

Government-sponsored reconciliation and everyday contact are different phenomena. The 1998 action plan encouraged youth exchange, and later years saw growth in travel in both directions, in study abroad, in university partnerships, in school-to-school visits, in working-holiday arrangements established in the years after 1998, and in language education. Proximity helped: Busan and Fukuoka are connected by ferry and short flights, and low-cost airlines and eased visa rules made short trips affordable for large numbers of people. Tourism flows were sensitive to political crisis and exchange rates; they declined during periods of diplomatic tension and boycotts and recovered afterward, and the global pandemic of 2020–2022 sharply interrupted travel.

Not everyone participated equally. Cost, language ability, visa status, family circumstances, and social background influenced who could study or work abroad. Students from wealthier urban families had more access to exchange programs; working-holiday participants often worked in service jobs; and the experiences of Korean workers in Japan and Japanese workers in Korea differed by sector and legal status. Some travelers returned with warmer views; others found their prejudices or historical grievances confirmed. Evidence for these encounters is drawn from survey research, program records, memoirs, and ethnographic work, and generalizations should be cautious.

Everyday contact also altered how each society talked about the other. Younger South Koreans who had grown up with Japanese games and anime often had a more differentiated view of Japanese society than their grandparents, who remembered colonial rule directly. This generational distinction should not be overstated: younger people also responded strongly to the comfort-women and forced-labor controversies, and many were among the participants in the 2019 consumer boycott described in Section 88.17.

The institutions that sustained exchanges were often modest but durable: university agreements, language programs, municipal sister-city arrangements, ferry services, professional associations and school visits. Unlike a summit declaration, each depended on annual budgets, staff, host families, visa conditions and individual willingness to participate. The pandemic revealed the fragility of physical mobility, while remote interaction continued through classes, digital media and personal networks. Travel also exposed asymmetries: Japanese and Korean visitors did not necessarily have equal income, mobility or language support, and residents of smaller towns had fewer opportunities than those in metropolitan areas. A balanced account compares participation records and documented experiences rather than assuming that all members of either society met their neighbors.

Working-holiday and study programs additionally raise the issue of whose stories survive in the record. Official exchanges often describe successful visits and partnership totals, while private journals and surveys reveal interrupted courses, homesickness, unequal wages or discrimination alongside friendships and professional growth. Historians should examine both the institutional aim and the actual participant experience. A carefully documented exchange can illuminate a larger trend without pretending that a single student represents a whole generation.

Section 88.9

Zainichi Koreans, Migration, and Communities across National Borders

The bilateral relationship was never only between two governments. Ethnic Koreans in Japan, known as Zainichi Koreans, include descendants of people who migrated to Japan or were brought there during the colonial period, many for work, as discussed in Chapters 58 and 62. After 1945, many remained. In 1952, with the San Francisco peace treaty coming into effect, Japan removed Japanese nationality from former colonial subjects living in Japan, leaving many Koreans with a precarious legal status and, for decades, restricted access to certain social benefits and employment. Special permanent residency, created in stages from the 1960s and given its present form in 1991, gave long-resident Koreans and their descendants a more stable status, though without Japanese citizenship unless they naturalized.

Zainichi communities were never uniform. Organizations aligned with the Republic of Korea, such as Mindan, and with the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, such as Chongryon, ran separate institutions, including schools, and their political loyalties reflected the division of the peninsula (Chapter 66). Between 1959 and the early 1980s, a repatriation program sent 93,340 people between 1959 and 1984, including approximately 6,700 Japanese nationals (among them spouses and children), from Japan to North Korea, many of whom later faced severe hardship. Others chose naturalization, adopted Japanese names, or lived with an identity defined by neither state. Experiences of discrimination in schooling, employment, and marriage were real and well documented, and became the subject of campaigns, litigation, and eventually Japanese legislation against hate speech in 2016. Younger generations expressed a range of identities, including "Zainichi" itself, Japanese citizen of Korean descent, Korean national, and others.

Students should distinguish long-established Zainichi communities from more recent South Korean migrants and temporary residents who arrived after the 1980s as students, professionals, and workers, sometimes called "newcomers." These groups differ in history, legal status, and relationship to Korean and Japanese society. The Zainichi case shows that the historical consequences of colonial migration continued inside Japanese society, that neighboring states' disputes affected individuals directly, and that people living across the border sometimes served as cultural intermediaries and sometimes as targets of hostility when diplomatic relations deteriorated.

Repatriation is especially revealing because the program's language promised a return to a homeland that many participants had never personally lived in: the overwhelming majority of adult migrants came originally from the southern part of Korea. The program was shaped by Japanese institutions, Red Cross organizations, North Korean promotion, Chongryon networks and Cold War geopolitics. Memories of those who traveled, including people who later left North Korea, differ from the official rhetoric of humanitarian resettlement. The total of 93,340 counts everyone moved from Japan to North Korea, including Japanese spouses and family members, and must not be recast as the number of ethnic Korean returnees alone. This case demonstrates how categories of citizenship, ethnicity, homeland and voluntary choice can become unstable when states frame migration in political terms.

Section 88.10

Food, Fashion, Media, and the Everyday Culture of a Connected Region

Reciprocal borrowing extended into daily consumption. Japanese restaurants, from sushi counters to noodle shops and izakaya-style bars, became familiar in South Korean cities, and Japanese convenience-store and retail formats, design products, and fashion influenced Korean consumers, as did Korean adaptations of such formats. In Japan, Korean restaurants, barbecue, and Korean-style cuisine reached wide audiences, and Korean cosmetics, fashion, and entertainment-related goods built markets, with neighborhoods in Tokyo and Osaka becoming known for Korean shops. Food culture itself had mixed origins and sometimes contested labels, and disputes over the origins of particular dishes occasionally became public arguments.

A range of intermediaries made these exchanges possible: retailers, importers, broadcasters, translators and subtitlers, internet fan communities, tourists, and small business owners, many of them Zainichi or recent migrants. Online communities allowed fans to share information, translations, and criticism across national lines, though they could also amplify hostile content. Consumer markets are evidence of attraction, but they should not be taken as evidence of agreement on history.

The limits of consumer culture as a measure of reconciliation became clear in 2019, when many South Korean consumers boycotted Japanese goods even though Japanese products had been widely popular. A person could enjoy another country's entertainment while holding strong objections to its government's positions, and could just as easily boycott a brand in one season and return to it in another. Consumption indicates connection; it does not by itself indicate trust, nor agreement about the past.

The geography of exchange further shaped everyday culture. Port cities and air corridors enabled short visits, while Tokyo's Shin-Okubo and Osaka's Korean commercial districts offered accessible venues for purchasing Korean food and entertainment. Seoul and Busan likewise supported restaurants and retailers through which Japanese cultural forms were localized. These places were not simply passive outlets for national brands: migrant entrepreneurs, translators, wholesalers, chefs and neighborhood residents decided what to offer and how to adapt it for local tastes. A rigorous historical comparison would ask who operated these businesses, how the customer base changed, and how boycotts or shifts in tourism affected revenue. Local market evidence offers a way to observe social relations without mistaking purchasing decisions for permanent political attitudes.

Digital networks intensified these interactions. Fan translation, restaurant reviews, social-media promotion and online ticket sales could make a cultural trend visible across borders before an official institution recognized it. They also allowed political controversies to circulate quickly, including calls for consumer boycotts. This combination of commercial linkage and rapid political mobilization helps explain why the two societies could be deeply familiar with one another's consumer products and still experience sudden downturns in public sentiment.

Section 88.11

The Colonial Past and the Unfinished Settlement of 1965

The 1965 normalization package consisted of the Treaty on Basic Relations and several agreements, including one on the settlement of problems concerning property and claims and on economic cooperation. Under it, Japan provided South Korea with grants and loans, commonly cited as three hundred million dollars in grants and two hundred million dollars in loans over ten years, with additional private commercial credits, and the two governments agreed that claims between them, and between their nationals, had been settled. The treaty stated that earlier treaties between Korea and the Japanese empire were "already null and void", a phrase that the two governments interpreted differently: Korea held that those agreements were invalid from the start because they were imposed under duress, while Japan held that they had been valid when concluded and became void only with Korea's independence in 1948.

The settlement served immediate goals. Park Chung-hee's government gained capital for the development program discussed in Chapter 75, and both states strengthened an American-supported anti-communist alignment. It also provoked strong opposition in South Korea at the time, where critics saw it as trading dignity for money, and its terms left unaddressed several questions: whether the settlement covered claims of individuals, whether it addressed unlawful colonial rule or only property and finance, and how it applied to categories of harm not considered in 1965, such as sexual slavery. Government use of the funds, which were largely directed toward economic development rather than individual victims, became an issue in later decades. Diplomatic documents on the negotiations were released in South Korea in 2005, and an official review in that year reportedly concluded that the government bore responsibility for compensating certain wartime victims, which fed later legal arguments.

Students should keep four concepts apart: diplomatic normalization, the intergovernmental financial settlement, legal claims by individuals, and broader demands for historical acknowledgment. They are related but not identical, and later disputes often arose when speakers moved among them without noting the change. The 1965 settlement became a recurring source of disagreement because each government and each court could defensibly emphasize a different one.

Legal language matters because the settlement contained both a political agreement between states and wording about claims connected with their nationals. Japan's government has argued that these commitments settled relevant compensation matters completely and finally; South Korean courts later drew distinctions concerning claims tied to unlawful colonial rule. The presence of a legal disagreement does not mean that the historical conduct itself is uncertain. Nor does the transfer of economic assistance establish that each individual claimant had consented to a waiver or received direct payment. For students, the methodological task is to place the treaty text, records of negotiations, domestic legislation, judicial opinions and claimants' testimony in separate evidentiary categories before drawing conclusions about what the 1965 arrangement accomplished.

Section 88.12

Apologies, Historical Responsibility, and the Politics of Acknowledgment

Between 1993 and 1998 the Japanese government issued three statements that Korean and international observers often cite together. The Kōno Statement of August 1993, issued by Chief Cabinet Secretary Yōhei Kōno after a government study, acknowledged that the Japanese military had been involved in establishing and managing "comfort stations" and in transferring women, and that in many cases women were recruited against their will, and expressed apology and remorse. The Murayama Statement of August 1995, issued by Prime Minister Tomiichi Murayama on the fiftieth anniversary of the war's end, expressed deep remorse and heartfelt apology for the damage and suffering caused by Japan's colonial rule and aggression. The 1998 Joint Declaration applied that language specifically to Korea.

Scholars of reconciliation distinguish among expressions of remorse, formal apology, acknowledgment of legal responsibility, compensation, and political reconciliation. A statement can offer one without the others, and recipients can accept some while rejecting others. Many Koreans welcomed the statements as significant, particularly because they came from official sources; others considered them inadequate because they did not specify legal responsibility or provide state compensation, because they were issued by cabinets that could be succeeded by others, or because their language was thought to allow wide interpretation. Japan established the Asian Women's Fund in 1995, financed largely by private donations with government support, to offer atonement payments and a letter to former comfort women; some recipients accepted it, while many Korean survivors and advocates rejected it as a substitute for state compensation.

Trust was repeatedly undermined by statements from Japanese political leaders and officials that appeared to qualify earlier acknowledgments, by debates about whether the Kōno Statement's finding of coercion was supported by evidence, and by changes of government. In Korea, successive governments also shifted emphasis. These cycles show that acknowledgment is a process, not a single act, and that statements are read against the conduct that follows them.

Public apologies also operate at several scales. A prime minister's statement may express a cabinet's position, be reaffirmed in subsequent diplomacy, and become a reference in courts or textbooks, yet it cannot compel every politician or citizen to share the same interpretation. Equally, criticism of an apology may mean different things: a victim might seek direct legal redress, a historian might question omissions, and a government might object to later contradictory conduct. Chronology prevents these responses from collapsing into one national judgment. Students should compare the exact wording of the three statements and the institutions that issued them, then examine subsequent actions, including educational and compensatory measures, to assess how acknowledgment was implemented or contested.

Section 88.13

The Comfort Women: Survivors, Testimony, Activism, and the 2015 Agreement

The historical abuse is well documented. During the Asia-Pacific War, the Japanese military and associated authorities established and supervised a system of "comfort stations" in which women and girls from Korea, other parts of Asia, and elsewhere were subjected to sexual slavery (Chapter 62). Korean women, then colonial subjects, were among the largest groups affected, though the total number of victims and their national breakdown remain debated, with scholarly estimates varying widely. Evidence comes from Japanese military and government documents, testimony of survivors and former soldiers, and wartime and postwar investigations. In August 1991 Kim Hak-sun became the first Korean survivor to testify publicly, and others followed. In January 1992 supporters began weekly demonstrations, known as the Wednesday demonstrations, in front of the Japanese embassy in Seoul, and these continued for decades. A memorial statue installed near the embassy in 2011 became a focus of diplomatic argument.

The issue became a central subject of diplomacy. Korean advocacy groups and survivors demanded acknowledgment of state responsibility, an official apology, legal compensation, truthful education, and commemoration. In 2011 South Korea's Constitutional Court held that the government's failure to seek resolution through diplomatic channels violated survivors' rights, increasing pressure on Seoul. The Japanese government maintained that claims had been settled in 1965 while pointing to its statements and the Asian Women's Fund.

On December 28, 2015, the foreign ministers of the two countries, Yun Byung-se and Fumio Kishida, announced an agreement. The Japanese side stated that the issue was one of the honor and dignity of many women and acknowledged the involvement of the Japanese military, that the government was painfully aware of its responsibilities, and that the prime minister expressed apologies and remorse. Japan would fund, from its government budget, a foundation set up by South Korea to support survivors, in an amount of about one billion yen. The two governments confirmed that, with implementation, the issue would be resolved finally and irreversibly, and agreed to refrain from criticizing each other over it internationally. South Korea stated that it would work to address Japanese concerns about the statue. Many survivors and advocacy organizations objected that they had not been adequately consulted, that the Japanese statement did not clearly acknowledge legal responsibility or use direct language of state accountability, and that the "final and irreversible" language foreclosed further claims; some survivors accepted funds from the foundation, and others did not. After taking office, the Moon Jae-in government concluded in 2018 that the process had been flawed, and moved in 2018–2019 to dissolve the foundation without formally abrogating the agreement, while Japan insisted the agreement bind South Korea. Later South Korean court rulings in damages suits against Japan reached differing results, reflecting disputes over sovereign immunity. By the mid-2020s only a handful of survivors were still living.

This section must distinguish the historical abuse from the political disagreement about remedy. The existence of the system is not seriously disputed by historians; disputes concern the degree and forms of coercion in particular cases, responsibility, numbers, and what redress is adequate. See the Primary Source Window on the 2015 agreement.

The category of survivor testimony demands particular care. Testimony gives historians access to experiences often absent from official paperwork, yet it was produced under conditions of stigma, age, trauma and changing public recognition. Cross-checking surviving documents, oral histories and contemporary administrative records strengthens interpretation without turning memories into a courtroom test of whether systemic abuse occurred. The diplomatic record similarly has distinct layers: what the foreign ministers stated publicly in December 2015; how the governments implemented the foundation; how individual survivors and organizations responded; and how later South Korean administrations treated the arrangement. None of these layers substitutes for the others. The primary-source study below should be used to reconstruct the exact governmental commitments before comparing them with independent records of survivor responses.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical memory

Definition: The ways a society publicly remembers, commemorates, teaches, and argues about its past, as distinct from the historical record established through evidence.

Historical context: Korea and Japan maintain memory in textbooks, museums, monuments, ceremonies, and legal and political statements, each shaped by present interests.

Why it matters: Memory disputes can persist even when the underlying facts are widely accepted, because they concern meaning, responsibility, and dignity.

Potential misunderstanding: "Memory" does not mean "false" history; scholars study memory as an object in its own right alongside the evidence for events.

Section 88.14

Wartime Forced Labor, Compensation Lawsuits, and the 2018 Court Judgments

From the late 1930s, and on a large scale after the National Mobilization Law and subsequent labor mobilization measures, the Japanese empire drew Korean men and women into factories, mines, construction sites, and military-related work in Korea, Japan, and other parts of the empire (Chapter 62). Mobilization combined recruitment, official allocation, and compulsion in varying proportions and changed over time, and the experience of individual workers ranged from conditions that were harsh but contractual in form to coerced and dangerous labor with confinement, unpaid wages, and deaths. The existence of large-scale coercive mobilization is well documented in Japanese and Korean records and in testimony, though the number of workers and the proportions in each category are debated.

Former workers and families sought redress in Japanese courts from the 1990s, where their suits were generally dismissed, with courts citing the 1965 agreement or time limits, and later in South Korean courts. In 2012 the South Korean Supreme Court remanded two cases, reasoning that the 1965 agreement had not extinguished individual claims arising from unlawful colonial rule, and after further proceedings the Court issued its judgments on October 30, 2018, in the case of workers against Nippon Steel, and on November 29, 2018, in cases against Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. The Court affirmed awards of compensation for emotional suffering, set at ₩100 million for each of the four plaintiffs in the October 30 Nippon Steel judgment, while awards in the November Mitsubishi cases varied by judgment and claimant group in the first case. Most of the plaintiffs by then were elderly, and several had died during the decades of litigation.

The competing positions were precise. The Japanese government held that the 1965 agreement had settled claims "completely and finally," including claims of individuals, that the judgments violated international law, and that companies should not pay. The South Korean Court held, in the reasoning of the majority, that the claims were for compensation for suffering arising from unlawful colonial rule directly connected to wartime aggression, that the 1965 agreement was a political and financial arrangement that did not address such claims, and that the right of individuals had not been extinguished by the government's waiver of diplomatic protection. South Korean governments before and after the judgments differed in how they treated the rulings. Students must separate three questions: whether the labor abuses occurred (documented), who is responsible and under what law (contested between the governments and, within the Korean judiciary, a matter of judicial reasoning), and what remedy is available and who should pay (the subject of the later diplomatic crisis and the 2023 plan). Courts ordered asset seizures, which the companies resisted, and the dispute escalated into the trade conflict examined in Section 88.17. See the Primary Source Window on the judgments.

The precise remedy in the October 30, 2018 ruling was compensation for non-pecuniary harm, not a general ruling that every category of wartime compensation had the same status. The court examined how the claimed injury related to Japan's unlawful colonial rule and interpreted the 1965 agreement accordingly. Japanese official statements rejected the resulting legal position, maintaining that claims issues had been settled between the two states. The subsequent dispute over whether and how to execute the judgments therefore crossed national legal systems and diplomatic commitments. Students should read the court's case summary, the agreement's claims language and the Japanese government's response side by side. This is a problem of legal interpretation and remedy, not a reason to obscure the historically documented coercive labor system.

Historical Vocabulary
Individual compensation claim

Definition: A legal demand by a person, rather than a government, for redress for harm suffered.

Historical context: In 1965 the two governments agreed on financial settlements and declared claims resolved; the question whether this extinguished individual claims became central after the 2018 judgments.

Why it matters: It explains why governments could reach agreements that individuals did not consider binding.

Potential misunderstanding: A government's waiver of the right to espouse a claim diplomatically is not automatically the same as extinguishing a person's right to sue, and the two governments and courts reached different readings.

Section 88.15

History Textbooks, Yasukuni Shrine, Monuments, and the Politics of Remembrance

Disputes over memory concern how the past is documented and taught. Japanese textbooks are written by private publishers and approved by the education ministry, and revisions in the early 1980s, in 2001, and at other points drew protest in Korea and China over the treatment of colonial rule, the wartime mobilization, and the comfort-women system. Korea has its own textbook disputes, including a government effort in 2015–2016 to return to state-issued history textbooks that provoked domestic controversy over the representation of the colonial and Cold War periods. Historians from both countries have also taken part in joint research, with some success in shared documentation and limited success in a shared narrative.

Yasukuni Shrine in Tokyo honors Japan's war dead, including, since 1978, fourteen figures indicted for Class-A war crimes, of whom twelve had been convicted and two died before a verdict, and also includes names of Koreans who died as colonial subjects in Japanese military service, in some cases enshrined without family consent, a point that bereaved Korean families have contested in court. Visits by prime ministers and cabinet ministers, and offerings sent by leaders who did not visit, repeatedly drew protest from Seoul, which viewed them as endorsing a narrative of the war that minimized aggression. Supporters in Japan describe the visits as a personal act of mourning and not a political statement, a distinction Korean critics reject.

The Sado Island Gold Mines case shows the issue in a current form. Japan nominated the Sado mines in Niigata Prefecture for UNESCO World Heritage status, with a focus on the Edo period, and the committee inscribed the site in July 2024. South Korea had argued that the nomination should address the full history of the mine, including forced labor by Koreans during the war, and Japan agreed to include exhibits and commitments about the wider history. Disagreement continued over whether the exhibition and the later commemoration adequately represented that history, and On November 23, 2024, Seoul announced that it would not attend the Japanese-organized memorial scheduled for November 24, citing unresolved differences over the ceremony; the selection of Japan's representative also contributed to the controversy. Korean representatives and families held a separate commemorative event. This distinction matters: the disagreement concerned the ceremony's treatment of victims, not a refusal to commemorate them. The case demonstrates that memory disputes concern not just the events but also the documents, exhibitions, and institutions that present them.

The form of a memorial matters as well as its wording. Museums choose dates and labels; shrines establish criteria of commemoration; public ceremonies decide who is invited to speak and which histories are named. The Sado controversy made those curatorial choices visible to international audiences. Seoul's decision not to attend the Japanese ceremony on November 24, 2024, did not mean the Korean victims went uncommemorated: representatives of victims and Korean officials held their own remembrance. The disagreement therefore concerned the adequacy and political meaning of the proposed joint commemoration. Historians should distinguish the UNESCO inscription of the historic mining site, the separate account of wartime Korean labor, and the later diplomatic dispute about memorial arrangements.

Section 88.16

Dokdo/Takeshima: Sovereignty, Historical Geography, and National Identity

The islets known in Korea as Dokdo and in Japan as Takeshima lie in the East Sea (Sea of Japan), roughly midway between the Korean mainland and Japan's Oki Islands and somewhat closer to Korea's Ulleungdo. They consist of two main rocky islets and numerous smaller rocks, with a total land area of well under one square kilometer, and have limited fresh water and vegetation. South Korea has administered them for decades, with a small police detachment stationed there, and a small number of civilians have lived or been registered as residents there. The surrounding waters are valuable for fishing, and the islets' position affects maritime boundary claims.

The two governments' positions are stated precisely and should be reported as positions. South Korea maintains that Dokdo is an integral part of its territory historically, geographically, and under international law, and that no sovereignty dispute exists to be settled, citing historical records referring to islands near Ulleungdo and a 1900 imperial ordinance, among other evidence. Japan maintains that Takeshima is an integral part of Japan, citing historical use and a 1905 cabinet decision incorporating the islets into Shimane Prefecture, and has proposed referral to the International Court of Justice, which South Korea has declined. Korea argues that the 1905 decision occurred during the Russo-Japanese War as Japan was extending control over Korea (Chapter 54) and so cannot be treated as an ordinary act of territorial acquisition; Japan contests that reading. Interpretations of old maps, texts, and place-names, including whether certain historical names refer to the present islets, are disputed. The 1951 San Francisco peace treaty did not mention the islets by name, and each side draws different conclusions from that silence.

Episodes of escalation recur. In 2005 Shimane Prefecture enacted an ordinance designating a "Takeshima Day," prompting protests in Korea and a toughened stance from President Roh Moo-hyun. In August 2012 President Lee Myung-bak visited the islets, the first visit by a South Korean president, and Japan responded by recalling its ambassador temporarily. The significance of such small islets lies in what they symbolize. In South Korea, Dokdo is linked to the memory of the loss of sovereignty, since Koreans associate Japan's claim with a continuation of colonial expansion; in Japan, the claim is presented as a matter of territorial integrity and legal principle. Public events, school materials, and political campaigns in both countries keep the dispute active. Students should treat attribution carefully: South Korea's administration is a present-day fact; the sovereignty positions are claims.

Maps are necessary but insufficient evidence in territorial disputes. Historical charts may use changing names and administrative conventions; diplomatic documents may represent jurisdiction differently; current coast-guard and administrative practice shows who actually governs a place. The analytical task is to keep these different forms of evidence apart. In the present, South Korea administers Dokdo, while Japan maintains a competing sovereignty claim; a student should describe both official positions without treating an official assertion as settled proof. The dispute acquired particular force because colonial memory turns arguments about small territories into questions about recognition and state legitimacy. Its importance cannot be measured by the islets' size or population alone.

Section 88.17

The 2019 Trade Dispute: Semiconductors, Export Controls, and Diplomatic Retaliation

Relations deteriorated through 2018 and 2019. The 2018 judgments, the dissolution of the comfort-women foundation, and an incident in December 2018 in which the two sides gave conflicting accounts of an encounter between a South Korean naval vessel and a Japanese patrol aircraft all contributed to a climate of mistrust. On July 1, 2019, Japan announced tighter export-licensing procedures for three materials used in semiconductor and display manufacturing, namely fluorinated polyimide, photoresists, and hydrogen fluoride, with the measures taking effect in early July, and in August it removed South Korea from the list of countries receiving preferential treatment in export procedures. South Korea responded by removing Japan from its own list and filing a complaint with the World Trade Organization.

The governments' explanations differed. Japan described the measures as a review of export control management, citing concerns about the trust relationship and the adequacy of controls, and did not formally describe them as retaliation for the court judgments. South Korea and many observers viewed the timing as linked to the judgments and described the measures as retaliatory and damaging to supply chains. The chapter does not adjudicate motives; it records that the governments disputed motivation and justification.

Consequences were felt in business and society. Japanese suppliers faced uncertainty over exports; Korean manufacturers, particularly semiconductor makers (Chapter 87), sought alternative sources and invested in domestic production of materials, and the government promoted that effort. Many South Korean consumers boycotted Japanese goods and travel, and sales of some Japanese beer, clothing, and automobiles, as well as tourist travel to Japan, fell. The boycott was a consumer action that many participants framed as a response to government policy, and its effects faded unevenly afterward. The episode shows how interdependence could be both a source of mutual benefit and a means of pressure. See the vocabulary box below.

The business consequences should not be described as a complete collapse of bilateral manufacturing cooperation. Japan's changes targeted the licensing treatment of specified materials and then South Korea's preferential export-control status; Korean chip producers continued operating, sought alternative supply arrangements and adopted inventory and localization strategies. Japanese exporters also faced the cost of reduced certainty and a public boycott. The two governments differed over the rationale for the controls, so a textbook should separate official export-control explanations from the timing and political interpretations offered by Seoul and observers. The later restoration of procedures in 2023 demonstrated that regulatory choices could be reversed, but the episode exposed a vulnerability built into specialization across national supply chains.

Historical Vocabulary
Economic interdependence

Definition: A relationship in which economies rely on each other for goods, capital, technology, or markets.

Historical context: South Korean manufacturers depended on Japanese materials and equipment, and Japanese firms on Korean customers and production, a pattern that had developed since the 1960s.

Why it matters: Interdependence can restrain conflict, and it can also create points of leverage, as the 2019 dispute showed.

Potential misunderstanding: Interdependence does not guarantee political harmony, and it is rarely perfectly symmetrical.

Section 88.18

Security Cooperation, North Korea, and the Limits of Bilateral Trust

North Korean missile and nuclear development (Chapter 86), the American alliance system, and regional military concerns gave South Korea and Japan reasons to cooperate even when politics divided them. Both are allies of the United States, host American forces, and are within range of North Korean missiles. Their governments had held discussions of closer military information exchange for years, and public opinion in Korea, wary of anything resembling military ties with the former colonial power, had blocked an earlier attempt. The two governments signed the General Security of Military Information Agreement, known as GSOMIA, in November 2016, creating a framework for sharing classified military information, principally about North Korea.

The agreement became a bargaining chip in the 2019 dispute. In August 2019 South Korea announced that it would not renew it, linking the decision to the export-control measures; the United States urged retention. In November 2019, shortly before the termination would have taken effect, South Korea announced that it would suspend the notice of termination conditionally, and the agreement remained in force. In March 2023, the Yoon Suk Yeol government announced the restoration of normal operation.

The episode demonstrates that defense cooperation, historical grievance, public opinion, and economic disputes could intersect without being identical. Cooperation on North Korea persisted at the working level, while political trust fluctuated. The full regional strategic framework, including the American alliance, China's role, and Russia, belongs to Chapter 94.

Information-sharing agreements are narrower than alliances. GSOMIA supplied procedures to protect and exchange classified information; it did not create an independent mutual-defense treaty or dissolve the separate security relationships that Seoul and Tokyo maintained with Washington. Its vulnerability in 2019 arose partly because leaders could attach diplomatic significance to the renewal process, even while military and intelligence professionals valued continuity. The conditional suspension of South Korea's termination notice in November 2019 kept the instrument operating, and the March 2023 normalization removed that immediate source of uncertainty. A useful comparison is between the legal status of the agreement, the political declarations surrounding it, and the practical need to evaluate North Korean missile activity. These dimensions overlapped but were not interchangeable.

The agreement also illustrates why 'security cooperation' is not a single policy choice. Threat assessments, classified intelligence rules, joint exercises, trilateral consultation and the domestic legitimacy of military contact are separate issues. In South Korea, the colonial past made direct security ties with Japan unusually sensitive even when officials agreed on the risks posed by North Korean missiles. Japan likewise faced public debate over regional security responsibilities. The result was institutional cooperation that could function despite political strain, but whose public justification had to be renewed repeatedly.

Section 88.19

Diplomatic Repair, the 2023 Forced-Labor Plan, and the Return of Summit Diplomacy

After taking office in May 2022, President Yoon Suk Yeol made improved relations with Japan a priority, citing the need for cooperation on security and the economy. On March 6, 2023, South Korea's foreign minister, Park Jin, announced a plan under which eligible claimants in the cases decided by the Supreme Court would be compensated through a public foundation in South Korea, financed by contributions from Korean private-sector sources, instead of by direct payment from the Japanese companies named in the judgments. This approach is often called third-party reimbursement. The government stated that its aim was to resolve the matter for victims, advanced in age, without further delay, and to repair relations with Japan.

Reactions divided. Some claimants and supporters accepted the arrangement; others, together with advocacy organizations and opposition politicians, rejected it, arguing that the responsible companies had neither paid nor apologized, that Japan had not offered a new acknowledgment, and that the plan substituted domestic funds for the redress ordered by the Supreme Court. Some of the survivors refused payment under it, and legal proceedings by some claimants continued. The Japanese government welcomed the plan and stated that its position on earlier statements, including the 1998 Joint Declaration, was unchanged; Prime Minister Fumio Kishida, visiting Seoul in May 2023, said he felt sympathy for those who had suffered, in personal terms, and did not announce new compensation by Japanese firms.

The plan was followed by rapid steps. Yoon visited Tokyo in March 2023, the first such leader visit in many years; Kishida visited Seoul in May. Japan announced revised licensing treatment for three semiconductor and display materials on March 16, implementing that change on March 23; South Korea announced that it would withdraw its WTO complaint. South Korea restored Japan to its preferential export-control list on April 24; Japan approved South Korea's restoration to its Group A list in late June, effective July 21. These bilateral list changes should not be conflated with material-specific export licenses. The GSOMIA's normal operation was also restored. A trilateral summit with the United States at Camp David in August 2023 also embedded the improvement in a wider framework. In December 2023 the two governments' currency swap arrangement of $10 billion took effect. The chapter must not present this diplomatic improvement as proof that the compensation controversy was resolved to the satisfaction of all claimants: improved government relations and unresolved claims by individuals existed side by side. See the Primary Source Window on the 2023 documents. Yoon's December 2024 martial-law crisis and subsequent removal (Chapter 95) interrupted South Korean politics, though working relations with Japan continued.

The 2023 settlement attempt was deliberately bilateral in ambition but domestic in its payment mechanism. The South Korean foundation, not the Japanese firms named in the 2018 judgments, was to transfer the court-awarded amounts and related interest to eligible claimants. That distinction explains both the diplomatic opening and the objections of those who continued to demand direct corporate compliance. The economic restoration then unfolded on a separate regulatory track, with material-specific licensing changes in March and preferential-country status restored in April and July. The sequence is essential: a summit, a court judgment, a foundation payment and an export-control rule have different legal effects. They should not be presented as one comprehensive settlement of colonial-era responsibility.

Section 88.20

Japan and South Korea in 2026: Connected Societies, Contested History

As of November 2026 the relationship combines extensive cooperation with unresolved disputes. The sixtieth anniversary of normalization in June 2025 was marked by statements, receptions, and exchanges in both countries and became an occasion for reassessing the previous six decades. Lee Jae-myung, elected in June 2025 after Yoon's removal, had been a critic of earlier policy toward Japan but in office pursued a pragmatic line, holding his first meeting with Japanese Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba on June 17, 2025, on the sidelines of the G7 summit in Kananaskis, Canada, and continuing leader-level exchanges later that year. Both governments' meeting summaries emphasized communication, people-to-people exchanges and trilateral coordination with the United States. After Sanae Takaichi became prime minister in October 2025, the two leaders met at the APEC summit in Gyeongju. On January 13, 2026, Lee and Takaichi held a summit in Nara, Takaichi's hometown, where they agreed to strengthen cooperation, including on economic security and the search for remains of Korean forced laborers, which included DNA analysis connected with the 1942 flooding of the Chōsei coal mine, and they agreed on continued reciprocal visits. The leaders ended the occasion with an impromptu drum performance to K-pop songs, a moment widely reported as a sign of rapport.

Financial cooperation continued. On October 15, 2026, the two countries' finance ministers, meeting in Bangkok, agreed to extend their $10 billion currency swap by three years, through November 2029. The swap had begun in 2001 at $2 billion, grew to as much as $70 billion by 2011, lapsed in 2015 amid political tension, and was revived in 2023. The history of the arrangement is itself a record of the relationship's cycles.

The disputes remain. The sovereignty positions on Dokdo/Takeshima are unchanged, the forced-labor settlement of 2023 is accepted by some claimants and rejected by others, comfort-women survivors and advocates continue to seek acknowledgment, and memory disputes over sites such as Sado persist. Whether shared economic and security interests, particularly in view of tensions with China and North Korea's weapons programs and its closer relations with Russia (Chapters 92–94), have made the relationship more durable is a question that evidence available in late 2026 cannot settle. Cooperation has repeatedly depended on leaders willing to bear the political costs of engagement, and public legitimacy for that engagement has been limited by memory. The relationship cannot be reduced to enduring enmity or to inevitable reconciliation.

Recent diplomacy provides evidence of renewed working relationships but not of a final settlement of memory disputes. The June 2025 G7 meeting, the January 2026 Nara summit and the October 2026 finance ministers' announcement address different policy fields—political consultation, economic security and liquidity cooperation. The currency swap's extension is a contingent financial safety arrangement; its existence is not evidence that either central bank drew upon it. Likewise, an agreement to cooperate on identifying remains from the Chōsei mine must be evaluated separately from the historical claims and legal compensation issues that continue to divide the governments and affected families. The durability of cooperation will be better assessed through implementation, continuity across administrations and public acceptance than through ceremonial photographs alone.

Historical Debate

Does Cultural Exchange Reduce Historical Antagonism?

The question: Did the expansion of cultural exchange after 1998 improve political relations, or can cultural enthusiasm coexist with persistent political hostility?

Interpretation A — Exchange builds understanding: Some analysts argue that contact, shared consumption, and travel reduce stereotypes over time, particularly among younger people, and make cooperation politically easier.
Interpretation B — Exchange and antagonism coexist: Others note that historical disputes intensified in periods of heavy exchange, that consumer enthusiasm did not prevent the 2019 boycott, and that political attitudes depend more on leadership, courts, and memory politics than on consumption.

Evidence: Survey data, tourism flows, sales records, and political events, none of which cleanly separates cause from correlation.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation, and measurement: surveys differ in wording, and consumption may reflect choices unrelated to attitudes.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether policymakers should treat cultural exchange as a route to reconciliation or as a separate domain.

Student task: Identify evidence from this chapter supporting each reading, and describe evidence that could better distinguish them.

Historical Debate

Can Governments Settle Historical Claims Conclusively When Individual Victims Contest the Settlement?

The question: Is an agreement between governments final if the people harmed did not consent?

Interpretation A — Settlements bind: Proponents argue that states must be able to close disputes through agreements, that repeated reopening undermines diplomacy, and that the 1965 and 2015 agreements represent such closure.
Interpretation B — Individual rights endure: Critics argue that governments cannot extinguish the rights of persons who were not parties to the agreement, that serious human-rights violations require acknowledgment and remedy, and that victim consultation is essential to legitimacy.

Evidence: The texts of the agreements, court judgments, international law debates, and survivor statements.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns legal interpretation and normative judgment, and not the occurrence of the abuses.

Why it matters: It shapes how such settlements are designed and whether they last.

Student task: Evaluate the interpretations using the 2015 and 2023 cases, and propose what evidence about victims' views would be relevant.

Cause and Effect

Cooperation and Crisis in the South Korea–Japan Relationship

Long-term causes

Colonial memory, the unresolved scope of the 1965 settlement, economic interdependence, a shared alliance system, and democratization that enabled victims and civil society to press claims.

Short-term causes

The 1997–98 financial crisis; Kim Dae-jung's strategy; Japanese leaders' willingness to offer an apology; changes of government in both countries.

Immediate triggers

The 1998 visit and declaration; the 2018 Supreme Court judgments; Japan's 2019 export-control changes; Yoon's 2023 proposal.

Immediate effects

Cultural opening and exchange; diplomatic and commercial crisis in 2019; restored summitry in 2023.

Long-term consequences

Reciprocal cultural markets, lasting memory disputes, a cyclical pattern of repair and rupture, and a relationship tied to wider regional security.

Historical complexity

No single factor, whether culture, economics, security, or memory, determined outcomes; each interacted with leadership and public opinion.
Everyday Life and Social History

Students, Viewers, Consumers, and Families

For many South Korean and Japanese people, the relationship was experienced through ordinary choices: a student taking a language class, a viewer following a television drama, a reader buying a translated comic, a traveler taking a short flight, a shopper deciding whether to buy a Japanese product during the 2019 boycott. For families whose members had been mobilized for labor or who had suffered under the colonial system, understandings of Japan were shaped by inherited accounts and by the unresolved status of claims. A grandchild might enjoy Japanese animation while a grandparent remembered wartime hardship, and the two experiences could coexist in a single household.

Social position affected access. Exchange students came disproportionately from families able to afford overseas study; working-holiday participants often worked in service jobs; Zainichi families navigated Japanese schools, employment, and nationality rules; and small business owners in Korean shopping districts in Japanese cities felt political tension directly in their customer numbers. Historians reconstruct these experiences from surveys, oral histories, memoirs, and local records, and must remember that sources over-represent the vocal and the educated.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how South Korea and Japan became increasingly connected through culture, commerce, travel, and shared security concerns while remaining divided over the interpretation and consequences of their common history. Its governing argument is that cultural exchange did not erase historical memory, and that historical disagreement did not prevent meaningful cooperation.

South Korea's restrictions on Japanese popular culture after 1945 reflected colonial memory, nationalism, fear of competition, and distrust of Japanese political intentions, yet they never produced complete separation. Japanese manga, animation, games, broadcasts, and music circulated through informal channels. The "return" of the chapter title refers to the reopening of legal markets, not to the arrival of something entirely new.

The October 8, 1998 Joint Declaration by Kim Dae-jung and Keizō Obuchi paired an expression of remorse and apology for colonial rule with a commitment to future-oriented cooperation and referred to South Korea's cultural opening. It did not address individual compensation or resolve the legal questions left by 1965, and it did not bind later governments against statements that Koreans read as qualifying it.

The opening proceeded in four stages between 1998 and 2004, legalizing imports by category while leaving some broadcasting restrictions in place. Korean producers and consumers adapted, resisted, and reinterpreted Japanese forms, and the expected domination did not occur. The domestic debate introduced a distinction that recurs throughout: cultural participation is not political approval of another state's historical conduct.

Exchange was reciprocal. The 2002 World Cup demonstrated practical cooperation, Winter Sonata and its audience showed Japan as a receiving society for Korean culture, and tourism, student exchange, food, fashion, and online communities linked the two societies. Zainichi Koreans, with histories rooted in colonial migration and varied loyalties and statuses, showed that the relationship was never solely a matter between states.

The 1965 settlement provided capital for South Korea's development and normalized relations, but its scope became a recurring dispute because normalization, intergovernmental financial settlement, individual claims, and historical acknowledgment are different things. Japan's 1993, 1995, and 1998 statements were significant but were received differently by different Koreans, and their durability was tested by later statements and changes of government.

The comfort-women system and wartime forced labor are documented historical abuses; the disagreements concern responsibility, legal remedy, and who must pay. The 2015 agreement and the 2023 compensation plan were government arrangements that some victims accepted and others rejected, and the chapter has insisted on distinguishing government agreement from victims' acceptance. The 2018 Supreme Court judgments recognized individual claims, while the Japanese government maintained that the 1965 agreement had settled them.

Memory disputes extended to textbooks, Yasukuni Shrine, monuments, and the Sado mines, and territorial disagreement centered on Dokdo/Takeshima, where South Korea administers the islets and the two governments hold incompatible sovereignty positions. These disputes concern not only events but also how they are documented, taught, and commemorated.

The 2019 trade dispute demonstrated that economic interdependence can create vulnerability; the GSOMIA controversy showed security cooperation entangled with political quarrels without being identical to them. The 2023 plan and renewed summitry restored working relations without resolving the claimants' dispute.

By November 2026, leaders had met repeatedly, including the January 2026 Nara summit, and the $10 billion currency swap had been extended in October 2026 through November 2029. Historical and territorial disagreements persisted. The relationship is best understood as neither enduring enmity nor inevitable reconciliation but as a deeply interconnected and frequently contested one.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 88 matters because it shows that societies can grow close without agreeing on their past. The reopening of Korean markets to Japanese culture, and the reciprocal reception of Korean culture in Japan, altered daily life for millions of people in ways that the colonial-era generation could scarcely have imagined, yet the same decades produced court judgments, diplomatic crises, and arguments over islets and exhibitions. Both facts belong to the history.

The chapter also illustrates a broader feature of modern Korean history: the colonial period remained a living political resource long after 1945. It informed the 1965 settlement, the democratic movements that brought survivors into public view, the judicial reasoning of 2018, and the symbolic weight of Dokdo. At the same time, the chapter shows Korea as an active participant, with its own producers, courts, activists, and leaders making choices, not as a passive recipient of Japanese influence or a permanent victim.

The developments here shaped later chapters. The cultural openness that began in 1998 formed part of the environment in which Korean industries grew (Chapters 89 and 90); the bilateral security relationship sits within the wider system examined in Chapter 94; and the questions of memory and justice raised here recur in the democratic politics of Chapter 95. As of November 2026, the relationship rests on summits, trade, and finance, and on unresolved disagreements. Whether that balance holds will depend on decisions yet to be made.
