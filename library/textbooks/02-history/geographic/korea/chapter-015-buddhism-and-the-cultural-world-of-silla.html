Buddhism and the Cultural World of Silla

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Inside an artificial granite grotto high on Mount Toham, a monumental Buddha has faced east for more than twelve centuries. Lower on the mountain at Bulguksa, two stone pagodas of strikingly different design stand in the same courtyard: Seokgatap, associated with Sakyamuni Buddha, and Dabotap, associated with Prabhutaratna, the Buddha of Many Treasures. Their pairing materializes the Lotus Sutra episode in which the two Buddhas appear together. Neither the grotto nor the temple complex can be understood only as art. Both were built inside a religious, intellectual, and political world that Silla's scholars, monks, rulers, and artisans constructed together, and often argued over, in the decades and centuries after the wars examined in Chapter 13.

Chapter 14 traced how Silla's kings and aristocracy governed an enlarged and, for a time, unusually stable kingdom after 676: how they organized administration, taxation, landholding, and military service, and how far that political integration actually reached across newly incorporated territory. This chapter turns from institutions of government to institutions of belief, learning, and artistic production, asking what kind of religious and cultural world Silla's stability made possible, and what that world reveals about the society that built it.

Buddhism did not arrive in Silla only after 676. It had already taken root in the peninsula's Three Kingdoms period, examined in Chapter 12, where it entered Korea through diplomatic and religious contact with Chinese dynasties and quickly became entangled with royal legitimacy, aristocratic patronage, and rival religious and philosophical traditions. What changed after the seventh-century wars was scale and opportunity. A larger territory, a more concentrated flow of resources toward the capital at Gyeongju, renewed and easier contact with Tang China, and a court eager to commemorate its victories and its dead gave Buddhist institutions, artists, and scholars new means to build, to study, and to teach.

This chapter follows that development along several intertwined threads. It examines monasteries not simply as beautiful buildings but as working institutions with landholdings, communities, and social functions. It examines patronage, showing how kings and aristocratic families used religious commissions to seek merit, commemorate the dead, and assert status, without collapsing Buddhism into a mere tool of politics. It follows two of Silla's most significant Buddhist thinkers, Wonhyo and Uisang, whose writings and teaching placed Silla scholarship inside sophisticated East Asian Buddhist debates rather than at their margins. It considers devotional practice alongside doctrinal scholarship, since most Silla Buddhists were not authors of philosophical treatises. And it turns at length to the material world Buddhism produced: the temple complex of Bulguksa, the carved grotto of Seokguram, stone pagodas and reliquaries, images of the Buddha and bodhisattvas, and a great bronze bell whose surviving inscription remains one of the richest pieces of contemporary evidence for eighth-century Silla society.

Students should resist two temptations as they read this chapter. The first is to treat Silla Buddhism as a single, uniform system handed down from the throne and accepted without variation. The evidence does not support this: doctrinal schools disagreed with one another, devotional practice differed from monastic scholarship, elite patronage differed from village religious life, and the religious culture of the capital differed from that of the provinces. The second temptation is to treat Buddhist monuments as simply decorative achievements disconnected from belief, politics, and social structure. Bulguksa and Seokguram were not built merely to be admired; they were built to embody specific religious ideas, to generate merit for specific patrons, and to occupy a landscape already understood in sacred terms.

A further caution concerns evidence itself. Much of what historians know about Silla Buddhism comes from later compilations, above all the twelfth-century Samguk Sagi and the thirteenth-century Samguk Yusa, both of which recorded earlier traditions long after the events they describe, alongside surviving texts by Buddhist scholars themselves, physical remains of temples and images, and a small number of contemporary inscriptions, of which the 771 bell inscription discussed later in this chapter is among the most valuable. Distinguishing what these different kinds of evidence can and cannot tell historians is essential to studying this chapter's subject responsibly.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Silla Buddhism not as a finished monument or a settled doctrine, but as a living, contested, and creative religious culture, one that connected Gyeongju to Tang China and the wider Buddhist world while producing distinctly Silla forms of thought, worship, and art. That religious culture forms an essential part of the "North and South States" era introduced in Chapter 13 and continued in Chapter 16, which turns to the very different, though related, cultural world built by Balhae to Silla's north.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how postwar political consolidation and resources affected the scale, but not the origins, of Buddhist institutional life in Silla.
  2. Describe monasteries as multifunctional religious, educational, artistic, and economic institutions rather than uniform arms of the state.
  3. Analyze royal and aristocratic patronage of Buddhism as a reciprocal relationship involving merit, legitimacy, and commemoration rather than simple propaganda.
  4. Summarize Wonhyo's scholarship and his approach to reconciling apparently conflicting Buddhist teachings, associated with the concept of hwajaeng.
  5. Trace Uisang's study in Tang China, his teacher Zhiyan, and his subsequent establishment of Hwaeom Buddhism and its monastic institutions in Silla.
  6. Distinguish elite doctrinal scholarship from devotional practices such as Pure Land worship, and explain why evidence for popular religious life is more limited than evidence for elite scholarship.
  7. Connect Buddhist learning to literacy, manuscript production, and Silla's wider written culture.
  8. Evaluate the construction history of Bulguksa and Seokguram, distinguishing later foundation narratives from archaeological and textual evidence.
  9. Explain the religious significance of pagodas, relics, and Buddhist images rather than treating them only as artistic objects.
  10. Interpret the 771 bell inscription as contemporary evidence, while separating it from the later Emille Bell legend.
  11. Assess continuity and change in burial practice, including cremation's emergence as a major, and in many contexts standard, funerary practice alongside continuing older traditions.
  12. Contextualize Silla Buddhism within a wider East Asian religious world connected by scholarship, texts, and travel, while recognizing its distinctly local developments.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did postwar stability change the scale of Buddhist institutional and artistic life without changing its underlying religious foundations?
  2. Why should historians treat royal patronage of Buddhism as reciprocal rather than as evidence that religion served only political purposes?
  3. What do Wonhyo's and Uisang's careers reveal about the relationship between Silla Buddhism and the wider East Asian Buddhist world?
  4. Why is evidence about elite monastic scholarship more abundant than evidence about ordinary devotional life, and what does this imbalance mean for historical interpretation?
  5. What can Bulguksa and Seokguram tell historians about Silla religious belief, and what do later foundation legends about them obscure or complicate?
  6. Why do historians treat a contemporary inscription, such as the 771 bell inscription, as different in kind from a later legend about the same object?
  7. How did burial practices in Silla both change and persist under Buddhist influence?
  8. In what ways did Silla's Buddhist culture depend on, and contribute to, a wider Buddhist world connecting Korea, China, and the Japanese archipelago?
  9. Why might historians describe Silla Buddhism as internally diverse rather than as a single unified religious system?

Historical Context

Chapter 12 examined how Buddhism, along with royal ideology, tomb culture, and diplomatic exchange, developed across Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla during the Three Kingdoms period. Buddhism reached the peninsula from continental East Asia and quickly became entwined with royal authority: Silla kings, like their counterparts in Baekje and Goguryeo, used Buddhist temples, images, and ceremonies to express and reinforce their legitimacy, while Buddhist monks served as advisors, envoys, and sources of continental learning. By the time of the seventh-century wars examined in Chapter 13, Buddhism was already a well-established religious and institutional presence in Silla, with monasteries, royal temples, and a tradition of monks traveling to China for study.

Chapter 14 explained how the Silla–Tang alliance's destruction of Goguryeo in 668 and Silla's subsequent conflict with Tang produced a kingdom that governed much of, though not all of, the former territory associated with Goguryeo, alongside continuing aristocratic government, bone-rank social hierarchy, and an expanding administrative and military apparatus. That chapter also noted the limits of Silla's political integration: newly incorporated populations, including former Goguryeo and Baekje subjects, were absorbed unevenly, and the kingdom's stability, while real, was neither complete nor permanent.

This chapter begins from that partially integrated, resource-rich kingdom. Postwar Silla controlled substantial territory, extracted revenue and labor through the systems described in Chapter 14, and maintained close, if periodically strained, diplomatic and cultural relations with Tang China. These circumstances gave the royal court, aristocratic families, and monastic institutions new capacity to commission temples, images, and texts on a newly concentrated and sustained scale. This did not mean prewar Silla lacked monumental Buddhist architecture; the kingdom had already completed the enormous nine-story wooden pagoda at Hwangnyongsa in 645. Postwar patronage instead produced a distinctive concentration and new forms of monumental building rather than monumentality itself for the first time. At the same time, this chapter's subject is not confined to the capital or to royal patronage alone. Buddhism's religious and cultural reach extended, unevenly and with uneven documentation, into provincial society, village life, and burial practice throughout the kingdom.

Understanding this chapter also requires some awareness of the wider Buddhist world Silla belonged to. Chinese Buddhism during the Tang dynasty was itself intellectually dynamic, home to competing doctrinal schools, active translation projects, and monastic centers that drew students from across East Asia, including Korea and Japan. Silla monks who traveled to Tang China, most famously the pilgrim-scholar Uisang, participated directly in these debates rather than merely receiving finished doctrines. This chapter's account of Wonhyo, Uisang, Bulguksa, Seokguram, and the Seongdeok bell should be understood against that connected, intellectually active backdrop, one further developed in Chapter 17's discussion of trade, travel, and the maritime world.

Section 15.1

Buddhism after the Wars

It would be a mistake to describe Silla Buddhism after 676 as though it began anew. As Chapter 12 explained, Buddhism had already been established in Silla for well over a century by the time of the wars examined in Chapter 13, having entered the kingdom through diplomatic and religious contact with Chinese dynasties and Silla's neighboring Korean states. Sixth-century Silla kings had already used Buddhist temples and ceremonies to express royal authority, and Silla aristocrats had already begun patronizing monasteries before the century of warfare that produced Silla's expanded postwar kingdom.

What changed after 676 was less the presence of Buddhism than its scale, resources, and setting. Silla's postwar territory, while smaller than the full extent Goguryeo had once controlled, was considerably larger than pre-war Silla, and the revenues, labor, and political stability described in Chapter 14 gave the royal court, aristocratic families, and monastic institutions new capacity for building, patronage, and scholarship. Silla's Buddhist connections with Tang did not begin only after the wars. Uisang's study in Tang from 661 until his return in 670, examined in Section 15.5, demonstrates that scholarly exchange continued even amid the political conflicts of the seventh century. Postwar Silla inherited these transregional networks and provided new institutional settings in which teachings, texts, and monastic structures associated with figures such as Uisang could be developed further.

This chapter therefore treats the period after 676 not as the beginning of Silla Buddhism but as a period of expansion, consolidation, and intellectual flowering built upon foundations already in place. The distinction matters because a common but misleading shorthand, "Unified Silla Buddhism," can imply that Buddhism suddenly appeared alongside a supposedly complete national unification. Chapter 13 already cautioned against overstating the meaning of Goguryeo's fall in 668 and the limits of the territorial consolidation Silla achieved through the wars ending in 676, and this chapter applies a parallel caution to the religious history that followed: continuity, not sudden invention, best describes the relationship between Three Kingdoms-era and postwar Silla Buddhism.

Section 15.2

Monasteries as Religious and Social Institutions

A Silla monastery was far more than a place where monks meditated and worshippers prayed. Major monasteries functioned as religious centers, educational institutions, manuscript workshops, artistic production centers, and holders of land and other economic resources. Understanding this multifunctional character is essential to understanding why monasteries could accumulate the wealth and skilled labor necessary to sponsor major building projects, commission images, and support long-term scholarly study.

As religious centers, monasteries hosted regular ritual observances, festivals tied to the Buddhist calendar, memorial services for deceased patrons and family members, and daily monastic practice including meditation, chanting, and study. As educational institutions, they trained monks in Buddhist doctrine, Classical Chinese literacy, and, for advanced students, philosophical debate, while also sometimes providing broader literate and cultural training that extended beyond specifically religious instruction. As centers of manuscript and artistic production, monasteries housed workshops where sutras were copied, commentaries composed, images carved or cast, and architectural projects planned and executed, drawing on skilled artisans who were not necessarily themselves ordained monks.

Economically, major monasteries held agricultural land, sometimes donated by royal or aristocratic patrons and sometimes acquired through other means, along with the labor needed to work it. This landholding gave monasteries a degree of institutional independence and continuity that did not depend entirely on any single patron's favor, though it also tied monastic fortunes to broader patterns of aristocratic wealth and political stability. Students should avoid imagining a single, uniform "Silla monastery" model; individual institutions varied considerably in size, wealth, doctrinal affiliation, and relationship to the royal court, ranging from small provincial temples with modest resources to major capital-area institutions capable of sponsoring monumental building campaigns.

Monasteries were not simply arms of royal government, even when they enjoyed royal patronage. Monks often pursued scholarly and spiritual projects that served religious rather than narrowly political purposes, and monastic communities sometimes maintained distinct doctrinal identities and institutional interests that did not always align neatly with court priorities. The following sections examine how patronage, scholarship, devotion, and artistic production interacted within and around these institutions.

Section 15.3

Royal and Aristocratic Patronage

Silla kings and aristocratic families commissioned temples, images, pagodas, and bells for a range of overlapping purposes: to generate religious merit for themselves and their deceased relatives, to commemorate specific individuals or events, to seek protection for the kingdom or family, and to display status and piety within a competitive aristocratic society. These motives should be understood as intertwined rather than as alternatives that cancel one another out; a patron seeking genuine religious merit could simultaneously be asserting political and social standing, and neither motive need be considered insincere.

This chapter treats patronage as fundamentally reciprocal. Patrons provided resources, land, and protection to monasteries and monastic communities; in turn, monasteries provided ritual services, education, religious legitimacy, and cultural prestige to their patrons. Royal patronage in particular could reinforce a monarch's claim to rule wisely and righteously, a claim with roots in Buddhist ideas about righteous kingship that had already shaped Three Kingdoms-era Buddhist patronage, examined in Chapter 12. But this reciprocal relationship should not be flattened into a claim that Buddhism functioned merely as state propaganda. Buddhist monks, doctrines, and institutions maintained their own religious logic and, at times, their own independent interests, and individual patrons pursued personal as well as political motivations.

Aristocratic patronage extended this pattern beyond the royal family itself. Prominent aristocratic households commissioned temples and images associated with their own lineages, sometimes in direct competition with, and sometimes in cooperation with, royal projects. The building campaign later associated with Kim Daeseong at Bulguksa, examined in Section 15.8, illustrates this aristocratic dimension of patronage, situated within, but distinct from, purely royal commissions.

Section 15.4

Wonhyo and the Problem of Doctrinal Difference

Wonhyo (617–686) stands among the most significant scholars in the history of East Asian Buddhism, not only Korean Buddhism. His career spanned the period immediately before and after the wars examined in Chapter 13, and his extensive body of commentaries and treatises engaged directly with major currents of Mahayana Buddhist thought, including Yogācāra philosophy, Madhyamaka reasoning, the influential text known as the Awakening of Faith, and Pure Land devotional thought. Rather than adopting a single school uncritically, Wonhyo worked across these traditions, seeking to understand how their apparently competing claims might be reconciled.

This effort at reconciliation is often associated with the concept usually rendered in English as hwajaeng, meaning something like "harmonization of disputes." Wonhyo's approach did not deny genuine differences among Buddhist teachings; instead, it sought to show how different teachings might address different audiences, different levels of spiritual development, or different aspects of a shared underlying truth, rather than treating doctrinal disagreement as evidence that one tradition must simply be wrong and another simply right. This approach reflects sophisticated philosophical engagement with real intellectual problems debated across the Buddhist world, not a simplified or provincial version of continental Buddhism.

Students should resist the temptation to describe Wonhyo as the inventor of a uniquely isolated "Korean Buddhism" standing apart from continental traditions. His scholarship was deeply engaged with the same textual and philosophical questions occupying Buddhist thinkers throughout East Asia, and his influence, while significant within Korea, should be understood as a distinctive contribution to a shared, transregional intellectual tradition rather than as evidence of separate national development. At the same time, his specific interpretive choices and the particular texts he chose to emphasize reflect his own scholarly judgment and the concerns of his own time and place.

Wonhyo's life also illustrates the diversity of religious roles available within Silla Buddhism. Later tradition presents him, alongside his rigorous scholarly output, as someone who also engaged with popular religious life outside strictly monastic settings, though historians should treat such biographical details with appropriate caution given that much of what is known about his life comes through later compiled accounts rather than strictly contemporary documentation.

Section 15.5

Uisang and Hwaeom Buddhism

Uisang (625–702) represents a complementary but distinct model of Silla Buddhist scholarship: direct study abroad followed by institutional development at home. In 661, Uisang traveled to Tang China, where he studied under the master Zhiyan, a leading figure in the tradition known in Chinese as Huayan and in Korean as Hwaeom, centered on the vast Avatamsaka (Flower Garland) Sutra and its vision of the interpenetration of all phenomena, in which each part of reality is understood to contain and reflect the whole. Zhiyan died in 668, and Uisang returned urgently to Silla in 670 after learning of Tang military plans against his homeland.

After returning to Silla, Uisang did not simply repeat what he had learned from Zhiyan. He established Hwaeom teaching within Silla through his own writings, his teaching of disciples, and his founding or support of monastic institutions associated with this tradition. This pattern, transmission followed by local development, deserves emphasis because it corrects a common oversimplification: Uisang should not be described merely as someone who imported Chinese Hwaeom wholesale into Korea. He engaged with his teacher's ideas, then shaped a Silla institutional and scholarly tradition that trained subsequent generations of monks and influenced Silla Buddhist thought and practice well beyond his own lifetime.

The doctrine of interpenetration associated with Hwaeom teaching, in which every phenomenon is understood to be interconnected with every other phenomenon, carried implications not only for abstract philosophy but for how patrons and worshippers might understand devotional acts, architectural symbolism, and the relationship between the individual and the cosmos. Some scholars have connected Hwaeom cosmological ideas to the architectural and sculptural program of Bulguksa and Seokguram, examined later in this chapter, though students should treat such connections as informed interpretive arguments rather than as certainties directly stated in surviving contemporary sources.

Uisang's career, alongside Wonhyo's, demonstrates that Silla Buddhism in this period was not a passive recipient of Chinese religious culture. Silla monks traveled to Tang China as active participants in shared scholarly communities, studied directly with leading continental teachers, and returned to build institutions, train students, and produce scholarship that shaped Buddhism's subsequent development on the peninsula.

Section 15.6

Pure Land, Devotion, and Broader Religious Participation

Wonhyo's and Uisang's sophisticated doctrinal scholarship represents only one dimension of Silla Buddhism. Most Silla Buddhists, whatever their social position, were not authors of philosophical treatises, and historians must be careful not to let the disproportionately rich evidence for elite monastic scholarship stand in for the religious lives of the wider population.

Devotional practices centered on Amitabha, the Buddha associated with a Pure Land or paradise into which devotees hoped to be reborn, offered a religious path that did not require extensive philosophical training. Recitation of Amitabha's name, prayer, and other devotional acts were understood to generate merit and to aid rebirth in this favorable realm, making such practices accessible to worshippers across a wider social range than specialized doctrinal study. Merit-making more broadly, through donations, the sponsorship of rituals, the commissioning of images or texts on however modest a scale, and participation in temple festivals, provided additional avenues through which people without monastic training or aristocratic wealth could engage with Buddhist religious life.

Historians should handle claims about the "popularization" of Buddhism during this period with care. Surviving sources are disproportionately produced by, and about, monastic elites and aristocratic patrons, meaning that direct evidence for how ordinary farmers, artisans, or villagers understood and practiced Buddhism is comparatively thin. What evidence does survive, including some inscriptions, donation records, and later reflections on religious practice, suggests broadening religious participation across Silla society, but historians cannot reconstruct the full texture of non-elite religious life with the same confidence they can bring to monastic scholarship or royal patronage.

Section 15.7

Buddhist Learning, Literacy, and the Written Word

Buddhist scholarship in Silla depended on literacy in Classical Chinese, the shared written language of learning across much of East Asia during this period, as introduced in Chapter 1's discussion of language and writing and developed further in Chapter 12. Monks studied sutras and commentaries composed originally in Sanskrit and translated into Chinese, along with a growing body of commentary produced by Chinese, and increasingly Silla, scholars themselves. Advanced students engaged in textual analysis, philosophical debate, and the composition of their own commentaries, a scholarly practice exemplified by Wonhyo's extensive body of writing.

Monasteries served as centers of manuscript production, where sutras were copied by hand, a labor-intensive process that required trained scribes and substantial resources for materials. This copying served devotional purposes, since the act of copying a sutra was itself often understood to generate religious merit, as well as practical purposes, since manuscript copies were necessary for study, teaching, and ritual use. Calligraphy connected to this manuscript culture attained recognized artistic status in its own right, related to broader East Asian traditions of calligraphic art.

Buddhist literacy also intersected with Silla's wider literate culture beyond strictly religious contexts. Monks sometimes served in advisory, diplomatic, or scholarly roles connected to the royal court, drawing on their training in Classical Chinese and their familiarity with continental intellectual and administrative models. This intersection between religious and administrative literacy should not be overstated into a claim that monks simply ran the Silla government, but it does illustrate how thoroughly Buddhist institutions were woven into the broader intellectual life of the kingdom.

Inscriptions carved onto stone monuments, bronze bells, and other durable materials provide historians with valuable evidence that survives independently of later manuscript transmission. The 771 bell inscription examined in Section 15.12 is among the most significant surviving examples of this kind of contemporary Buddhist-related epigraphy from Silla.

Section 15.8

Bulguksa: Building a Buddhist World

Bulguksa, situated on Mount Toham near Gyeongju, ranks among the most significant surviving expressions of Silla Buddhist architecture. The temple complex was designed to express multiple Buddhist cosmological visions and devotional traditions simultaneously, incorporating separate courtyards and structures associated with different Buddhas and bodhisattvas rather than presenting a single simplified religious message.

Later tradition, preserved above all in the thirteenth-century Samguk Yusa, associates a major eighth-century building campaign at Bulguksa with the courtier Kim Daeseong, traditionally dated to 751, with the project said to have been completed after his death in 774. This narrative, composed many centuries after the events it describes, should be treated as a later written tradition rather than as a contemporary eyewitness account of construction, following the same evidentiary caution introduced in Chapter 1's discussion of foundation mythology and later written tradition. The actual building history of Bulguksa is more complicated than the Kim Daeseong narrative alone suggests: documents recovered from within Seokgatap indicate construction activity on Bulguksa's eastern and western pagodas as early as 742, before Kim Daeseong is recorded as having held the court position traditionally associated with initiating the project, making the site's eighth-century building history considerably more layered than a single mid-century campaign credited to one patron would imply. The surviving complex today also reflects extensive later repair and reconstruction, including substantial work carried out long after the Silla period itself.

Students should therefore distinguish three related but separate things when studying Bulguksa: the surviving physical complex as it exists today, shaped by centuries of repair and reconstruction; the historically documented eighth-century building campaign or campaigns, evidenced through architectural and textual analysis; and the later foundation legend crediting Kim Daeseong with singular responsibility for the temple's construction. Collapsing these three into a single, simple story, "Kim Daeseong built Bulguksa in the 750s," misrepresents both the complexity of the evidence and the layered history of the site itself.

Religiously, Bulguksa's design sought to render Buddhist cosmology architecturally legible: worshippers moving through its courtyards and up its stone stairways experienced a structured progression evoking movement from the ordinary world toward realms of enlightenment, expressed through gates, staircases, courtyards, and the placement of specific halls dedicated to specific Buddhas and bodhisattvas. This architectural program should be understood as a deliberate expression of Buddhist teaching in stone and wood, created for a community of patrons, monks, and worshippers who understood these cosmological references, rather than as a general-purpose religious building later given Buddhist meaning.

Section 15.9

Seokguram and the Architecture of Enlightenment

A short distance from Bulguksa, also on Mount Toham, the Seokguram grotto represents a distinct but closely related achievement in eighth-century Silla Buddhist architecture. Unlike the natural cave shrines found in some other Buddhist traditions, Seokguram is an artificial structure, built using cut granite blocks assembled into a domed chamber and then covered with earth to simulate a natural grotto, a considerable feat of stone engineering and geometric planning for its time.

At the center of the grotto sits a large seated Buddha image, surrounded by a carefully organized program of relief sculptures depicting bodhisattvas, disciples, and guardian figures arranged according to a coherent iconographic scheme. The precision of the chamber's geometry and the arrangement of its sculptural program indicate sophisticated planning, likely involving specialists in stone construction working in close coordination with religious advisors familiar with the required iconographic conventions.

UNESCO treats Bulguksa and Seokguram together as a religious architectural complex, recognizing both the architectural and artistic achievement of the two sites; scholars have further interpreted their relationship in terms of coordinated devotional and cosmological programs centered on Mount Toham. As with Bulguksa, the traditional narrative crediting Kim Daeseong with the grotto's construction belongs to later written tradition rather than to contemporary construction records, and students should apply the same source-critical caution here as in Section 15.8.

Seokguram's placement, facing toward the sea to the east, has invited interpretation connecting the grotto to protective and cosmological functions associated with Silla's relationship to its maritime frontier, though such interpretations should be presented as scholarly argument rather than as directly documented intent. What can be stated with more confidence is that Seokguram, like Bulguksa, was conceived as sacred space deliberately organized to embody Buddhist teaching through architecture, sculpture, and orientation within a landscape already understood as significant.

Section 15.10

Pagodas, Relics, and Sacred Space

Stone pagodas represent one of Silla Buddhism's most distinctive architectural contributions, evolving from earlier Indian and Chinese stupa traditions associated with the enshrinement of relics believed to be connected to the historical Buddha or other significant religious figures. The pagoda's tiered form, rising in diminishing stages toward a finial at its summit, carried cosmological significance, often understood as representing a symbolic axis connecting earthly and heavenly or transcendent realms.

Earlier Silla pagoda traditions, including examples associated with the temple site of Gameunsa, established stone-building techniques and design conventions that later monuments, including the paired pagodas at Bulguksa, developed further. The two Bulguksa pagodas, Seokgatap and Dabotap, stand in deliberate visual contrast within the same temple courtyard: Seokgatap, plain and geometrically severe, is associated with Sakyamuni Buddha, while Dabotap, elaborately tiered and ornamented, is associated with Prabhutaratna, the Buddha of Many Treasures. Their pairing is conventionally connected to the episode in the Lotus Sutra in which Prabhutaratna appears beside Sakyamuni to affirm the truth of Sakyamuni's teaching, illustrating how architectural form itself could communicate specific scriptural and doctrinal meaning to worshippers who understood the relevant conventions. This interpretation should be kept distinct from claims about the exact intentions of individual builders, which surviving contemporary documentation does not establish in detail.

Pagodas frequently housed reliquaries, containers holding relics or other sacred objects, placed within specially constructed chambers inside the pagoda's structure. The deposit of such relics transformed a pagoda from a purely commemorative or decorative structure into an object of active religious veneration, a site where devotees could direct prayer and merit-making activity toward a physically present connection to sacred power. Archaeological investigation of pagoda relic chambers, where such investigation has been conducted with appropriate care, has provided historians with valuable evidence about devotional practice, artistic production, and the materials patrons considered appropriate for sacred deposit.

Understanding pagodas as religiously active sites, rather than simply as architectural or artistic monuments, is essential to grasping why Silla patrons invested such resources in their construction and why particular pagoda designs carried the doctrinal and devotional significance they did.

Section 15.11

Images of the Buddha and Bodhisattvas

Silla artisans produced Buddhist images in stone, gilt-bronze, and later iron, ranging from small devotional objects intended for personal or household use to the monumental granite Buddha at the center of Seokguram. These images followed iconographic conventions, including specific hand gestures, postures, and attributes, that communicated particular religious meanings to worshippers familiar with the relevant visual vocabulary, a vocabulary shared, with regional variation, across much of the Buddhist world.

Craftsmanship in gilt-bronze casting, in particular, required considerable technical skill, involving processes for shaping, casting, and applying gold surface treatment that demanded specialized workshops and trained artisans. The stylistic qualities of surviving Silla Buddhist images show clear engagement with continental artistic models transmitted through trade, diplomacy, and religious exchange, alongside distinctive Silla adaptations in proportion, facial modeling, and compositional arrangement.

Historians should exercise caution before treating every stylistic resemblance between a Silla image and a continental example as direct evidence of transmission from a specific foreign workshop or artist. Artistic influence in this period typically moved through multiple intermediate stages, including traveling images, imported texts with visual descriptions, monks and pilgrims who had seen continental models, and locally circulating artistic conventions already partly assimilated from earlier contact. Standard scholarly treatment of Silla Buddhist sculpture emphasizes both the presence of significant foreign influence and the emergence of new, locally developed combinations of form and style, rather than treating Silla artisans as simply copying finished continental designs.

The religious purpose of these images should remain central to their interpretation. A Buddha or bodhisattva image was not created primarily as an aesthetic object for later appreciation; it was created to serve as an object of veneration, a focus for ritual practice, and, in many cases, a vehicle for the specific merit-making intentions of the patron who commissioned it.

Section 15.12

Metalwork, Bells, and the Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok

Bronze casting technology in Silla reached a high level of technical sophistication, evident not only in Buddhist images but in large ceremonial bells used to mark ritual time, accompany temple ceremonies, and, in the case of major royal commissions, commemorate specific rulers. Casting a large bronze bell required careful control of alloy composition, mold construction, and casting technique to achieve both structural integrity and a specific desired tone, representing a considerable technical achievement for the artisans and workshops involved.

The most celebrated surviving example is the great bronze bell associated with King Seongdeok, completed in 771. This bell carries a lengthy contemporary inscription praising King Seongdeok, invoking Buddhist religious language and imagery, and recording information about the individuals and institutions involved in producing the bell, including its composer, Kim Pil-o, who composed the inscription by royal command. As a physically preserved, contemporary text, this inscription provides historians with a rare and valuable window into eighth-century royal commemoration, Buddhist rhetorical conventions, court personnel, and craftsmanship, distinct in kind from the later compiled narrative sources on which historians must rely for many other aspects of Silla history.

The bell has also attracted a well-known later legend, sometimes called the "Emille Bell" story, which claims that a child was sacrificed and cast into the bronze during the bell's casting in order to achieve its distinctive tone. This legend belongs to a later oral and literary tradition surrounding the bell and is not evidence for how the bell was actually produced. Historians studying Silla bronze-casting technology and religious practice must rely on the physical bell itself, its contemporary inscription, and comparative metallurgical and archaeological evidence, not later legendary elaboration, however culturally significant that legend later became in its own right as a piece of Korean folklore.

The distinction between the contemporary inscription and the later legend offers students an unusually clear example of the broader source-critical principle introduced in Chapter 1: later narrative tradition, however vivid or memorable, does not substitute for contemporary documentary evidence, and historians must keep the two carefully separated even when they concern the very same physical object.

Section 15.13

Burial Culture, Cremation, and Changing Ideas about Death

Buddhism's growing influence on Silla society is visible not only in temples and images but in changing approaches to death and burial, though this change was gradual, uneven, and incomplete rather than an abrupt replacement of earlier practice. Stone-chamber tombs, a burial form already established in earlier Silla and more broadly in the Three Kingdoms period examined in Chapter 12, continued to be used across the period examined in this chapter, particularly among aristocratic families maintaining established funerary traditions.

At the same time, cremation, a practice closely associated with Buddhist teaching regarding the impermanence of the physical body, became a major, and in many Unified Silla contexts standard, funerary practice, extending well beyond individuals and families closely connected to Buddhist institutions and teachings, even though inhumation and stone-chamber tombs did not disappear uniformly. Ordinary lay cremation burials commonly placed ashes in ceramic urns housed within protective stone caskets, a practice distinct from the enshrinement of relics associated with monks and Buddhist holy figures, which were more often deposited within or near pagodas and temple grounds as objects of religious veneration rather than as ordinary interment.

Royal tomb monuments during this period also developed distinctive forms, including structures incorporating guardian figures and other sculptural elements that drew on a combination of continental models and Silla artistic traditions, sometimes displaying Buddhist as well as other religious or symbolic influences. Students should resist the assumption that Buddhism simply and immediately displaced earlier funerary customs; the surviving archaeological and textual evidence instead points to a period of gradual change in which multiple burial practices coexisted, varying by social status, family tradition, religious affiliation, and region, a pattern consistent with this chapter's broader emphasis on Silla Buddhism's internal diversity.

Section 15.14

Buddhism beyond Court and Monastery Elites

Much of this chapter has necessarily focused on royal patrons, aristocratic families, prominent monks, and major monastic institutions, because these are the subjects for whom the richest evidence survives. A responsible account of Silla Buddhism must nonetheless attempt to describe, as far as the evidence allows, the religious lives of people who left far fewer traces in the historical record: villagers, farmers, artisans, women, and others who participated in Buddhist religious life without commissioning temples or composing philosophical treatises.

Evidence for this broader participation includes scattered inscriptions recording modest donations, references in later compiled sources to lay devotional practice and popular veneration of particular monks or holy sites, and textual evidence for accessible devotional practices such as Amitabha name-recitation, discussed in Section 15.6, that did not require specialized training. Recent scholarship has cautioned that the surviving material record contains fewer securely identifiable Amitabha images than earlier studies assumed, even as textual evidence continues to support widespread Pure Land practice among commoners through recitation rather than through image commissioning; historians should therefore weigh textual and epigraphic evidence for popular devotion more heavily than generalized claims about abundant surviving devotional objects. Temple economies, discussed in Section 15.2, also depended on local labor, craft production, and provisioning that connected surrounding communities to monastic institutions in ongoing, practical ways, beyond purely devotional participation.

Historians must be honest about the limits of this evidence. Because surviving written sources were overwhelmingly produced by, and about, a literate aristocratic or monastic elite, direct testimony from ordinary Silla worshippers is scarce, and much of what can be said about their religious lives depends on inference from indirect evidence rather than on their own recorded words. This chapter's account of Buddhism beyond the elite should therefore be understood as a plausible and evidence-informed reconstruction rather than a fully documented history, a caution consistent with the broader treatment of everyday life and social history introduced in Chapter 1.

Section 15.15

Silla Buddhism in an East Asian Religious World

Silla's Buddhist culture developed within, and contributed to, a wider East Asian religious world connected through scholarship, travel, texts, and artistic exchange. Uisang's study under Zhiyan in Tang China, examined in Section 15.5, exemplifies direct scholarly participation in continental Buddhist debate, while Wonhyo's engagement with a wide range of Mahayana traditions, examined in Section 15.4, situates Silla scholarship within shared East Asian intellectual currents even though Wonhyo himself is not known to have traveled to China.

Sutras, commentaries, and other Buddhist texts moved along the same diplomatic and commercial channels connecting Silla to Tang China described in Chapter 12 and developed further in Chapter 17's account of trade, travel, and the maritime world. Monks traveling in both directions, alongside diplomatic missions and merchants, carried texts, artistic models, and news of doctrinal developments across the Yellow Sea and the wider East Asian religious landscape. Silla Buddhism also maintained significant connections with Buddhist communities in the Japanese archipelago, continuing patterns of religious and cultural exchange already established during the Three Kingdoms period through Baekje's close relationship with early Japanese Buddhism, examined in Chapter 12.

This chapter deliberately limits its treatment of these connections to their religious and intellectual dimensions: the movement of monks, texts, doctrines, and artistic influence. The fuller history of merchants such as Jang Bogo, the maritime base at Cheonghaejin, overseas Silla communities, and the broader commercial and diplomatic networks connecting Silla to Tang China and the Japanese archipelago belongs to Chapter 17, which should be read as a direct continuation of the connections introduced here.

Understanding Silla Buddhism as embedded within this transregional religious world corrects any temptation to treat Silla's religious culture as an isolated national development. Silla monks were active participants in shared East Asian scholarly communities, and Silla Buddhist art and architecture drew on, while also creatively reworking, artistic and architectural models circulating throughout the wider Buddhist world.

Section 15.16

One Buddhist Culture or Many?

This chapter's central argument has been that Silla Buddhism, for all its shared religious foundations, was never a single uniform system imposed uniformly from the royal court. Hwaeom scholarship associated with Uisang and his successors developed alongside Wonhyo's distinctive approach to reconciling doctrinal difference; sophisticated philosophical inquiry coexisted with accessible devotional practices centered on Amitabha and merit-making; royal and aristocratic patronage produced monumental architecture and artistic commissions in the capital region, while provincial and village religious life, though less richly documented, followed its own patterns of practice and belief; and artistic and architectural production combined continental influence with locally developed forms and conventions.

Later developments not examined in detail in this chapter, including the growth of Seon (meditation-centered) Buddhist traditions that would become increasingly significant in subsequent centuries, further extended this diversity, a reminder that Silla Buddhism should be understood as a continuously developing religious culture rather than a fixed system reaching some final settled form during the period examined here.

Recognizing this diversity does not diminish Buddhism's central importance to Silla cultural life during this period; if anything, it reveals more accurately how thoroughly and variously Buddhist ideas, institutions, and practices penetrated Silla society, from royal court to village temple, from philosophical treatise to bronze bell inscription, from monumental grotto to modest household donation.

Silla's Buddhist cultural world demonstrates how religious ideas, texts, institutions, objects, and artistic forms could circulate across political borders while being reshaped locally into something recognizably Silla in character. But Silla was only one half of the new post-seventh-century order described in Chapter 13. North of its frontier, another kingdom was constructing its own political and cultural system from Goguryeo, Mohe, Tang, and regional traditions. Chapter 16 turns to Balhae.

Historical Vocabulary
Hwajaeng and Hwaeom

Hwajaeng, often translated "harmonization of disputes," describes Wonhyo's characteristic scholarly approach of seeking to reconcile apparently conflicting Buddhist teachings by showing how different doctrines might address different audiences, purposes, or levels of understanding rather than simply contradicting one another. It reflects a broader concern in East Asian Buddhist scholarship with reconciling the vast and sometimes seemingly inconsistent body of Mahayana teaching into a coherent whole.

Hwaeom (Chinese: Huayan) names both a body of Buddhist philosophy centered on the Avatamsaka (Flower Garland) Sutra and its associated doctrine of the interpenetration of all phenomena, and the specific school or lineage of teaching and monastic institutions that Uisang established in Silla after studying under the Chinese master Zhiyan. Students should not confuse Hwajaeng, an interpretive method associated primarily with Wonhyo, with Hwaeom, a specific doctrinal tradition associated primarily with Uisang, even though both figures were active in the same period and engaged with overlapping Buddhist intellectual concerns.

Why it matters: Distinguishing these terms helps students avoid collapsing Silla Buddhism's genuine intellectual diversity into a single undifferentiated "Korean Buddhist philosophy," and instead recognize two related but distinct scholarly contributions within a broader, shared East Asian Buddhist tradition.

Historical Vocabulary
Stupa, Pagoda, and Relic

A stupa is the South Asian Buddhist architectural form, originally a domed earthen or stone mound, developed to enshrine relics associated with the historical Buddha or other significant religious figures. As Buddhism spread into East Asia, this form was adapted into the pagoda, a taller, tiered structure built in wood, brick, or stone, of which Silla's stone pagodas, examined in Section 15.10, represent a distinctive regional development.

A relic is a sacred physical remain, such as a fragment of bone or ash, or a closely associated object, enshrined for veneration, typically within a specially constructed chamber inside a stupa or pagoda. Not every element of a pagoda's religious meaning can be reduced to relic storage alone; pagodas also carried independent cosmological symbolism, as discussed in Section 15.10. Relic enshrinement remains, however, fundamental to understanding why the form developed and why worshippers directed devotional practice toward it.

Primary Source Window

The Inscription on the Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok, 771 CE

Composer: Kim Pil-o (金弼奧), composing by royal command · Patronage context: begun under King Gyeongdeok in commemoration of his father King Seongdeok and completed under King Hyegong · Date: 771 CE · Object/location: the Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok, originally associated with Bongdeoksa and now held by the Gyeongju National Museum · Type: contemporary inscription on a bronze temple bell

Context: The bell was produced during a period of expanded royal Buddhist patronage in eighth-century Silla, examined throughout this chapter. Unlike the later compiled narrative sources historians must rely on for much of Silla's religious history, the bell's inscription was composed and cast at the time of the bell's completion and survives as a direct physical artifact from that moment, making it unusually valuable contemporary evidence.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the inscription's content.

The inscription praises King Seongdeok in elevated language drawing on Buddhist religious imagery, frames the bell's creation as an act of religious merit and royal commemoration begun by King Gyeongdeok and completed under King Hyegong, and records information concerning officials, craftsmen, and other individuals connected with the bell's production.

Analysis: This inscription allows historians to examine, in the patrons' and inscribers' own contemporary language, how Buddhist rhetoric was used to commemorate a specific monarch, how royal commemoration and religious merit-making were understood as connected activities, and how officials and craftsmen involved in a major royal commission, including the inscription's own composer, Kim Pil-o, might be recorded and honored within the finished object itself.

Limitations: The inscription reflects the perspective and purposes of the court and religious officials who composed it; it cannot tell historians how ordinary Silla worshippers experienced the bell's sound or ritual use, nor can it establish technical details of the casting process beyond what the text itself records. It should not be conflated with the much later "Emille Bell" legend concerning the bell's casting, which belongs to a separate and much later oral and literary tradition.

Perspective: The inscription represents an official, court-connected perspective centered on royal commemoration and elite religious patronage; it does not directly represent the perspectives of artisans, laborers, or ordinary worshippers, even though it may record some of their names or roles.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the inscription tell historians directly about royal commemoration and Buddhist rhetoric in eighth-century Silla?
  2. Why does the inscription's status as a contemporary, physically preserved text make it different in kind from the Samguk Yusa's later narratives about Bulguksa and Seokguram?
  3. What can the inscription not establish about the bell's casting or about the religious experience of Silla worshippers?
  4. Why must historians keep the inscription and the later Emille Bell legend clearly separated, even though both concern the same physical bell?
Historical Debate

How Broadly Did Buddhism Shape Silla Society?

The question: This is best understood as an evidentiary problem rather than a settled dispute between two named scholarly camps: how confidently can historians extend conclusions drawn from a documentary record dominated by royal, aristocratic, and monastic sources to the religious lives of the wider Silla population?

Interpretation 1 — the surviving evidence permits a relatively broad reconstruction: Accessible devotional practices such as Amitabha name-recitation, textual references to lay devotion and popular veneration of holy sites, scattered donation inscriptions from a range of social backgrounds, and the distribution of provincial temples together suggest that Buddhism's meaningful reach extended well beyond the capital's royal and aristocratic circles, even if the documentary trail supporting this reach is thin.
Interpretation 2 — elite-source bias requires greater caution before generalizing: The overwhelming majority of surviving evidence, including monumental architecture, sophisticated doctrinal scholarship, royal inscriptions, and elite-commissioned art, concerns royal and aristocratic patrons and monastic scholars, and this imbalance may lead historians to overstate how thoroughly and evenly Buddhism shaped religious life among the broader population.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping evidence, including inscriptions, archaeological remains of temples and burials, later compiled narrative sources, and comparative study of religious practice elsewhere in the Buddhist world, but differ in how confidently they extend conclusions drawn from elite-focused evidence to the wider population.

Why it matters: This textbook does not resolve the question by declaring one interpretation simply correct. It presents Buddhism's cultural influence during this period as extraordinarily significant while explicitly acknowledging that the unevenness of surviving evidence limits how confidently historians can describe the religious lives of non-elite Silla society, a position developed further in Section 15.14.

Student task: Using the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, then explain what kind of additional evidence, if discovered, would most help historians resolve this question more confidently.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Surviving Evidence for Silla Buddhism

Note: rather than reconstructing hypothetical individual voices, this feature compares what different categories of surviving evidence can and cannot tell historians about Silla Buddhism, following the evidence-based method used throughout this textbook.

Contemporary inscriptions, such as the 771 bell inscription: Provide direct, physically preserved evidence about royal commemoration, court personnel, and Buddhist rhetorical language at a specific moment, but represent an official, elite-connected perspective and survive only in small numbers.
Surviving writings of Wonhyo and the Hwaeom tradition associated with Uisang: Offer rich evidence of sophisticated doctrinal scholarship and its place within a wider East Asian intellectual world, but represent the perspective of highly trained monastic elites rather than the broader monastic or lay population.
Later compiled narrative sources, such as the Samguk Sagi and Samguk Yusa: Preserve valuable traditions, including the Kim Daeseong Bulguksa narrative and the Dangun tradition examined in Chapter 6, but were compiled centuries after the events they describe and reflect the concerns and purposes of their own later compilers as much as, or more than, the periods they describe.
Archaeological and architectural evidence, including temple sites, pagodas, images, and burials: Provides evidence independent of later narrative tradition and can reveal patterns of practice, technology, and artistic development, but requires careful interpretation and rarely confirms specific narrative details such as named individual patrons.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Silla Buddhism Before and After 676

What Stayed the Same

  • Buddhism's presence in Silla and its association with royal legitimacy and aristocratic patronage, both already well established during the Three Kingdoms period examined in Chapter 12
  • Monastic institutions functioning as religious, educational, and economic centers
  • Ongoing diplomatic and religious contact between Silla and Chinese Buddhist centers, including Uisang's 661–668 study under Zhiyan, which continued even amid the Silla-Tang conflict examined in Chapter 13
  • The coexistence of elite doctrinal scholarship with more accessible devotional practices

What Changed

  • The scale of resources available to the royal court, aristocratic patrons, and major monasteries, following the territorial and economic consolidation examined in Chapter 14
  • The emergence of a newly concentrated and sustained program of monumental building, exemplified by Bulguksa and Seokguram
  • After Uisang's 670 return, Hwaeom teaching and associated monastic and discipleship networks became increasingly institutionalized within Silla
  • Cremation's emergence as a major, and in many contexts standard, funerary practice, alongside continuing but no longer universal stone-chamber burial traditions

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents the misleading assumption that "Unified Silla Buddhism" was a wholly new phenomenon beginning in 676, while still allowing historians to explain why this particular period produced such an extraordinary concentration of monumental religious architecture and scholarly achievement.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Monumental Buddhist Patronage Flourish in Eighth-Century Silla?

Long-term causes

Buddhism's deep prior establishment in Silla society, the doctrinal foundations laid by seventh-century scholars such as Wonhyo and Uisang, ongoing scholarly and artistic connections with Tang China, and a long tradition linking royal legitimacy to Buddhist patronage stretching back to the Three Kingdoms period, including prewar monumental precedents such as Hwangnyongsa's nine-story pagoda.

Short-term causes

The postwar territorial consolidation and administrative and economic organization examined in Chapter 14, which concentrated resources, labor, and stability sufficient to support monumental building campaigns.

Immediate context

Specific royal and aristocratic patrons, such as those associated with the Bulguksa building campaign and King Seongdeok's commemoration, chose to direct available resources toward major religious commissions for reasons of merit, legitimacy, and commemoration.

Effect

The production of major surviving eighth-century monuments and artistic commissions, including Bulguksa, Seokguram, associated sculpture and pagodas, and the Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok.

Historical complexity: This chapter deliberately avoids the causal shortcut "political unification created Bulguksa." Political consolidation provided necessary resources and stability, but it did not by itself determine which specific religious projects were undertaken, how they were designed, or what doctrinal and artistic choices patrons and monks made; those choices reflected specific religious, aesthetic, and personal considerations that resource availability alone cannot explain.

Everyday Life and Social History

Buddhism in Daily Life

Religious culture shaped daily rhythms and practical concerns across Silla society, even for those who left no written record of their beliefs. Temple bells, including but not limited to grand royal commissions such as the Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok, marked ritual time and structured the daily and seasonal schedules of monastic and, to some extent, surrounding community life. Merit-making through donation, however modest, offered a means by which people of limited means could participate meaningfully in religious life alongside wealthy royal and aristocratic patrons.

Funerary practice brought Buddhist concerns directly into family life, as households navigated decisions about cremation, burial, and the disposition of remains within changing but not uniform religious expectations, as discussed in Section 15.13. Artisans, including stone carvers, bronze casters, and manuscript copyists, formed a skilled labor force whose work sustained temple economies and monumental building campaigns, connecting craft production directly to religious devotion. Travel to significant temples and sacred sites, participation in temple festivals tied to the Buddhist calendar, and the hearing, if not always the reading, of sutras and religious teaching offered additional points of contact between ordinary Silla households and the wider Buddhist cultural world examined in this chapter.

As Section 15.14 emphasized, historians must distinguish plausible reconstruction of these everyday connections from documented certainty, since the individuals most directly involved in this daily religious life are precisely those about whom the surviving written sources say the least.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the religious and cultural world Buddhism helped construct in Silla during the period following the wars traced in Chapter 13, building on foundations already well established during the Three Kingdoms period examined in Chapter 12. Buddhism did not begin in postwar Silla; rather, the territorial consolidation, economic resources, and political stability described in Chapter 14 gave existing Buddhist institutions, patrons, and scholars new capacity for building, patronage, and intellectual work.

The chapter treated monasteries as multifunctional institutions combining religious, educational, artistic, and economic roles, resisting any assumption that all monasteries functioned identically or served simply as instruments of royal government. It examined royal and aristocratic patronage as a reciprocal relationship involving merit, legitimacy, and commemoration, rather than reducing Buddhist patronage to political propaganda.

Two major scholarly figures received sustained attention. Wonhyo's extensive commentaries and his characteristic approach to reconciling apparently conflicting Buddhist teachings, associated with the concept of hwajaeng, demonstrated sophisticated engagement with major currents of East Asian Buddhist thought. Uisang's study under the Tang master Zhiyan and his subsequent establishment of Hwaeom teaching and institutions in Silla illustrated a pattern of transmission followed by local development, rather than passive importation of Chinese doctrine.

The chapter also examined devotional practices, particularly Amitabha-centered Pure Land worship and merit-making, as accessible religious avenues that reached beyond specialized doctrinal scholarship, while cautioning that surviving evidence for such broader participation remains far thinner than evidence for elite monastic and royal religious life. Buddhist learning's dependence on Classical Chinese literacy, manuscript production, and calligraphy connected religious scholarship to Silla's wider literate culture.

Substantial attention was given to Silla's major surviving Buddhist monuments. Bulguksa's architectural program and building history were examined with care to distinguish the later Kim Daeseong foundation narrative, preserved in the thirteenth-century Samguk Yusa, from the more complex archaeological and textual evidence for its actual construction. Seokguram's artificial granite grotto and sculptural program were presented as an integrated expression, alongside Bulguksa, of eighth-century Silla Buddhist cosmology and architectural achievement. Pagodas and reliquaries were examined as religiously active sites of relic veneration rather than merely decorative monuments, and Buddhist images in stone and gilt-bronze were discussed with attention to both continental influence and local artistic development.

The Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok, completed in 771, received particular attention as a rare example of contemporary inscriptional evidence, valuable precisely because it was physically produced at the time it describes, in contrast to the much later and legendary "Emille Bell" story that later became attached to the same object. This distinction between contemporary evidence and later legendary elaboration served as a recurring methodological lesson throughout the chapter.

Burial culture was shown to have changed substantially under Buddhist influence, with cremation becoming a major, and in many contexts standard, funerary practice, even as stone-chamber tomb traditions persisted unevenly rather than disappearing all at once, reflecting this chapter's broader emphasis on significant but internally diverse religious change rather than sudden uniform transformation.

Finally, the chapter situated Silla Buddhism within a wider East Asian religious world connected through scholarship, travel, and artistic exchange, while reserving the fuller account of trade networks, merchants, and maritime connections for Chapter 17. It concluded by emphasizing that Silla Buddhism should be understood not as a single unified system but as an internally diverse and continuously developing religious culture, encompassing doctrinal scholarship, popular devotion, royal patronage, provincial practice, and artistic innovation alike.

These themes prepare students to turn, in Chapter 16, to the very different political and cultural world Balhae constructed to Silla's north during the same broad period, a state whose Goguryeo, Mohe, and Tang-influenced culture offers an instructive contrast to the Silla religious world examined here.

A Buddhist doctrinal tradition centered on the Avatamsaka Sutra and the concept of the interpenetration of all phenomena, transmitted to Silla by Uisang after his study under the Tang master Zhiyan.
Yogācāra
A major school of Mahayana Buddhist philosophy concerned with the workings of consciousness, engaged with in Wonhyo's scholarship.
Madhyamaka
A major school of Mahayana Buddhist philosophy centered on the concept of emptiness, engaged with in Wonhyo's scholarship.
Pure Land Buddhism
A devotional tradition centered on rebirth in a Buddha's paradise, particularly associated with Amitabha, offering an accessible religious path beyond specialized doctrinal study.
Amitabha
A Buddha associated with a Pure Land or paradise, the focus of widespread devotional practice in Silla Buddhism.
Merit-making
Religious acts, including donation, ritual sponsorship, and image or text commissioning, understood to generate positive religious merit for the actor and, often, for deceased relatives.
Monastic landholding
Agricultural land held by monasteries, providing economic resources and institutional continuity independent of any single patron.
Stupa
An earlier Buddhist architectural form associated with relic enshrinement, ancestral to the East Asian pagoda tradition.
Pagoda
A tiered architectural structure, often housing relics, that developed from the stupa tradition and carried cosmological significance in Silla Buddhist architecture.
Reliquary
A container holding relics or other sacred objects, often deposited within pagodas as objects of religious veneration.
Gilt-bronze
Bronze covered with a gold surface treatment, a technically demanding medium used for Buddhist images in Silla.
Iconography
The established visual conventions, including gestures, postures, and attributes, used to convey religious meaning in Buddhist images.
Cremation
A funerary practice associated with Buddhist teaching on impermanence, which became increasingly, though not universally, common in Silla during this period.
Stone-chamber tomb
An earlier established Silla burial form that persisted unevenly alongside cremation's emergence as a major, and in many contexts standard, funerary practice.
Samguk Sagi
A twelfth-century compiled history of the Three Kingdoms and Silla, an important but later written source for this chapter's subject.
Samguk Yusa
A thirteenth-century compilation preserving numerous traditions, including the Kim Daeseong Bulguksa narrative, requiring careful distinction from contemporary evidence.
Contemporary inscription
Text physically produced at or near the time of the events it describes, such as the 771 bell inscription, distinct from later compiled narrative sources.
Foundation narrative
A later tradition explaining the origin or construction of a site or institution, such as the Kim Daeseong Bulguksa story, requiring the same source-critical caution applied to the Dangun tradition in Chapter 6.
Sutra
A Buddhist scriptural text, studied, copied, and commented upon within Silla monastic institutions.
Commentary
A scholarly text explaining or interpreting a sutra or other Buddhist text, exemplified by Wonhyo's extensive body of writing.
Hanja literacy
Literacy in Chinese characters, foundational to Buddhist scholarship, manuscript production, and Silla's wider literate culture.
Bone-rank society
Silla's hereditary social hierarchy, examined in Chapter 14, which shaped patterns of aristocratic Buddhist patronage discussed in this chapter.
Tang Buddhism
The Buddhist scholarly and institutional world of Tang dynasty China, directly engaged with by Silla monks such as Uisang.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Silla's cultural achievement after the wars examined in Chapter 13 cannot be separated from its religious life. Monasteries, monks, patrons, and artisans together built a Buddhist world that combined sophisticated doctrinal scholarship, accessible devotional practice, monumental architecture, and everyday religious participation into a single, though internally varied, cultural whole. Understanding Bulguksa, Seokguram, and the Sacred Bell of King Seongdeok as religious achievements, rather than merely artistic ones, is essential to understanding why Silla society devoted such extraordinary resources to their creation.

The chapter has also modeled a set of historical habits of mind that extend well beyond the study of Buddhism alone: distinguishing contemporary evidence from later written tradition, recognizing the limits of evidence that survives disproportionately from elite sources, and resisting simplified narratives, whether about a single inventive genius, a uniform state ideology, or an isolated national religious tradition. The contrast between the 771 bell inscription and the later Emille Bell legend offers a particularly vivid example of why these distinctions matter, even when both concern the very same object.

Silla's Buddhist cultural world also reinforces a theme introduced in Chapter 1 and developed throughout this textbook: that Korean history unfolded through continuous interaction with continental East Asia rather than in isolation. Uisang's study under Zhiyan and Wonhyo's engagement with shared Mahayana traditions show Silla monks as active participants in a connected religious and intellectual world, contributing original scholarship rather than merely receiving finished ideas from abroad.

Students who have understood this chapter should be equipped to recognize religious and cultural developments elsewhere in this textbook, including Goryeo's Buddhist culture in Part IV and the changing religious landscape of later Joseon in Part VII, with the same careful attention to institutions, evidence, patronage, and internal diversity applied here to Silla. The next chapter turns from this southern religious world to the different political and cultural order Balhae constructed in the north, inviting students to compare how two Korean states, coexisting during the same broad period, built distinct institutions and cultures from shared and overlapping historical materials.
