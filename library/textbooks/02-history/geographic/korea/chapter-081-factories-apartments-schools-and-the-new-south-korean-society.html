Factories, Apartments, Schools, and the New South Korean Society

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 1960, most South Koreans still lived in farming households and small provincial towns. By 1989, a majority lived in cities, and the capital alone held roughly ten million people. This chapter asks what that change felt like, who paid for it, who profited from it, and how millions of ordinary people helped make it happen.

Consider three representative experiences, each a composite built from patterns documented in censuses, surveys, labor records, and memoirs rather than the biography of a single named person. The first is a young man from a farming village in the southwest in the late 1960s. His family holds a small plot, roughly the size most rural households worked after the land reform of the early 1950s, and it cannot support all its sons. He leaves for Seoul or Busan, lodges in a rented room with relatives or acquaintances from home, and looks for work in construction, a workshop, or a factory. He sends money back and returns for the harvest or the lunar new year, one of the millions whose movement redistributed the country's population within two decades.

The second is a young woman from the same region, perhaps sixteen or seventeen. She enters a garment, textile, or electronics factory, often lives in a company dormitory or shared room, and works long hours. Part of her wage goes to her parents and sometimes to a brother's schooling. Some women in her position later organized unions and challenged their employers; many married and left wage work; many attended night classes in the hope of a different future. Her labor was central to South Korea's export growth, yet public discourse often treated it as temporary and secondary.

The third is a household in an expanding city in the 1980s. It may rent an apartment on a large refundable deposit called jeonse, and it spends a large share of its income on its children's schooling, including private lessons. Its members watch color television, shop at a department store, and hope to buy an apartment before prices rise further. Next door, in an older district or on a hillside, other families live in cramped rented rooms or informal housing that municipal redevelopment may soon demolish. These households lived in the same city but inside very different versions of South Korean modernity.

Chapters 75 and 76 explained how the developmental state and the large corporate groups organized economic growth. Chapters 79 and 82 turn to authoritarian politics and democratization. Chapter 81 occupies the space between them and asks what happened to the people living through the transformation. Its subject is social history: migration, factory work, women's labor, housing, apartments, schooling, family life, consumption, and the formation of a new middle class. Industrialization is the setting, but ordinary people's experiences are the principal subject.

The chapter's central argument is that between about 1960 and 1989 South Korea changed from a predominantly rural society into an urban, industrial, increasingly educated, and consumer-oriented one. It also argues that this change was neither an uninterrupted success story nor a story defined only by suffering. Wages, schooling, and housing improved for many; overcrowding, long hours, unsafe workplaces, educational pressure, gender inequality, displacement, and widening class distinctions accompanied those gains. Ordinary South Koreans were not passive beneficiaries or victims of development. Through migration, labor, household strategy, organization, and protest, they shaped the society that emerged.

Historians reconstruct this history from varied evidence: population and housing censuses, labor-ministry and union records, school-policy documents, municipal planning files, newspapers and magazines operating under censorship, photographs, factory memoirs, worker diaries and petitions, oral histories, and the studies of sociologists, economists, and historians. Each has limits. National statistics show scale but conceal households; official documents show policy goals but not compliance; memoirs and oral histories show experience but are shaped by later memory. The chapter therefore attends to what each type of evidence can and cannot establish.

Geography and terminology need care. Seoul municipality, the Seoul metropolitan region, and the national urban population are different measures, and urbanization percentages vary with how statisticians classify "urban" places. The chapter concludes in 1989, before the 1997 financial crisis examined in Chapter 84, and it reserves the June 1987 democratic struggle and the Great Workers' Struggle for Chapter 82, mentioning them only where they illuminate social change.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why millions of South Koreans migrated from rural areas to cities between 1960 and 1989, distinguishing permanent, temporary, and circular migration.
  2. Trace the growth of Seoul and of industrial cities such as Busan, Ulsan, Pohang, Gumi, and Changwon, and distinguish Seoul municipality from the wider metropolitan region.
  3. Assess Saemaul Undong as a rural development campaign that combined state direction, village participation, material improvement, and political mobilization.
  4. Describe working conditions in South Korean factories and compare large enterprises with small subcontracting workshops.
  5. Analyze the role of women workers in export industrialization, including remittances, gender discrimination, and labor organizing.
  6. Interpret the significance of Jeon Tae-il and the Pyeonghwa Market for the formation of worker consciousness without reducing labor history to a single event.
  7. Evaluate the limits of labor law and union organization, distinguishing legislation from enforcement.
  8. Explain informal settlements, jeonse, redevelopment, and apartment construction as parts of one housing history.
  9. Compare the 1969–1971 middle-school reforms, the 1974 high-school equalization policy, and the 1980 tutoring ban, and explain why none ended educational competition.
  10. Analyze how industrialization expanded women's economic participation while preserving gender inequality in family law and household life.
  11. Connect family planning, fertility decline, health provision, and household change, recognizing multiple causes.
  12. Evaluate how the new middle class, consumer culture, and the 1988 Olympics expressed both shared advancement and persistent inequality.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did ordinary South Koreans, rather than planners alone, drive migration and urban growth?
  2. Why did rural modernization occur at the same time that millions of people were leaving the countryside?
  3. How did workers' own assessments of acceptable conditions differ from the goals of employers and the state?
  4. Why were young women so important to export industrialization, and why was their labor so often described as temporary?
  5. Why is urbanization not the same thing as prosperity or modernization?
  6. How did housing become a source of both middle-class security and widespread insecurity?
  7. How did education serve both national development and household strategies of mobility, and why did expansion not produce equal access?
  8. In what ways did economic change alter family structure while preserving patriarchal institutions?
  9. How did consumer culture develop alongside censorship and political restriction?
  10. Whose experiences are best documented in the surviving evidence, and whose are hardest to recover?

Historical Context

In 1960, South Korea was among the poorer countries of East Asia. The Korean War, examined in Part XII and the reconstruction era of Chapter 73, had destroyed infrastructure and displaced millions, and the First Republic of Chapter 74 had depended heavily on foreign aid. Most people lived in farming or fishing households. Many cities were provincial market towns, and Seoul, though already the political center, was far smaller and less central than it would become. Literacy had risen sharply since liberation, and primary education was nearly universal in the early 1960s, but secondary and university education reached only a minority.

The land reform carried out around 1950 had reduced tenancy and left most rural households with small holdings, typically on the order of a hectare. This outcome gave many villagers a stake in the countryside, but it also meant that farms were too small to employ or support every child. Population was growing quickly because of high fertility and falling mortality, producing large cohorts of young people who would reach working age in the 1960s and 1970s. Those cohorts supplied labor to the factories and demand for the schools that the developmental state and the firms of Chapters 75 and 76 would require.

Park Chung-hee's government, which took power in 1961, adopted export-oriented industrialization. Early exports relied on labor-intensive goods such as textiles, garments, wigs, plywood, and electronics assembly, and the state later shifted toward steel, shipbuilding, machinery, petrochemicals, and automobiles. Government policy shaped investment, credit, and industrial location, but it did not by itself move people. Households decided whether a son or daughter would leave, whether to invest in schooling, where to rent, and when to organize. That interplay between structure and choice is this chapter's subject.

Several features of South Korean society shaped how these changes were experienced. Confucian-influenced kinship and gender norms assigned authority to male household heads and expected daughters to defer to fathers and brothers, as examined in earlier chapters on Joseon society. The family-registration system (hojeok) organized legal identity through male-headed households. A long tradition of examination-based status gave credentials great prestige. Authoritarian government limited press freedom, organized labor, and political association. Finally, war and division had made anti-communism a pervasive political language, which employers and officials often used against labor activists. Together these conditions framed the social history of industrialization.

Section 81.1 · Division I — Migration, Urbanization, and Communities

From Countryside to City: Migration, Industrial Employment, and Social Mobility

Around 1960 the typical South Korean household farmed. Villages were tied together by kinship, shared labor at planting and harvest, and ritual obligations, and provincial towns served as market and administrative centers. Agricultural income was low and unevenly distributed, and the small plots created by land reform could not absorb the growing number of young people. Rural population growth, combined with limited alternatives at home, created pressure to leave long before factory expansion created pull.

Over the next three decades, large numbers moved to Seoul, Busan, Incheon, Daegu, and new industrial centers. Seoul's population rose from about 2.4 million in 1960 to roughly 8.4 million in 1980, and over ten million by 1990, though part of that increase reflects boundary expansion as well as migration. The national share of people in places classified as urban rose from roughly a third around 1960 to well over half by 1980 and about three quarters by 1990, with the exact figures depending on the statistical definition used.

Migrants gave several reasons for leaving. Farm income was inadequate, and wages in factories and services, though low by later standards, were often higher and more regular than farm earnings. Cities offered schools, especially for younger siblings. Family and village networks lowered the risk of moving by supplying lodging, job leads, and guarantors. Military service took young men from home and exposed them to other regions and jobs. Others sought escape from a household in which they held a subordinate position, a motive often noted for young women. Overseas employment, including miners and nurses sent to West Germany in the 1960s and construction workers in the Middle East in the 1970s and 1980s, formed a smaller but significant parallel stream of labor migration.

Not all movement was permanent. Many young people moved for a period, worked as apprentices or factory hands, and returned home to marry, inherit, or help with the harvest. Seasonal and circular migration was common, and some households divided: a husband worked in the city while a wife and children remained on the farm, or siblings alternated. Others settled permanently, bringing parents and siblings after them in a pattern called chain migration. Because migration was often staged, official counts of residence do not capture the many in-between arrangements.

Migration changed families as much as it changed cities. Young adults left villages that aged and, in some regions, shrank; elders remained to farm; money, gifts, and news moved along kin networks. In cities, newcomers formed hometown associations, church groups, and neighborhood circles. Generational relations shifted because migrant children, educated in towns and cities and earning wages, no longer depended on parents for livelihood or marriage arrangements in the same way. Marriage choices, residence after marriage, and the care of aging parents were all affected, issues returned to in Sections 81.15 and 81.16.

The spatial sequence of migration was rarely a simple journey from a village to a permanent city job. A young person might first stay with an aunt in a provincial town, enter domestic service or a small workshop, acquire contacts and then seek a factory position in Seoul or an industrial complex. Returning home for holidays, illness, seasonal work, or marriage did not necessarily end the urban connection. A remittance could pay a family debt, buy fertilizer or support a sibling's school fees; a hometown contact could find a new arrival a room. Such networks reduced uncertainty without eliminating it. Contemporary censuses measured where people were enumerated, but not the unpaid assistance, emotional obligations and repeated journeys that held these households together. To reconstruct migration adequately, historians must read demographic totals alongside family correspondence, employment records and cautiously interpreted oral histories.

The balance of opportunities varied by age and gender. Young men sometimes reached industrial employment through military-service contacts, apprenticeship or construction work; young women could enter garment production, electronics assembly or the service economy while confronting distinct restrictions on residence and respectability. For parents, children's departures could mean welcome cash income and, simultaneously, reduced help at harvest time and growing worry over an unfamiliar city. Migration also changed rural authority: earning children could negotiate decisions previously made by senior relatives, although they remained tied to obligations of support. The resulting society was not divided neatly between a traditional countryside and a modern city. Homes, money, goods, marriage partners and expectations circulated across both, making industrial urbanization a transformation of rural life as much as a departure from it.

It would be wrong to portray migrants simply as people moved by government policy. State investment in factories and infrastructure created opportunities, and some policies, such as industrial complexes, concentrated them. But the decision to leave belonged to households and individuals, who weighed risk, kin connections, and expectations of advancement. Their choices, in aggregate, produced the urban labor force that made export industrialization possible. They did so with incomplete information and, often, at considerable personal cost: crowded lodging, unfamiliar urban routines, and the strain of separation from family.

Section 81.2 · Division I

The Expansion of Seoul: Population Concentration, Urban Planning, and Metropolitan Inequality

By the 1980s Seoul had become the dominant political, economic, educational, and cultural center of South Korea. Government ministries, major corporate headquarters, leading universities, banks, newspapers, and broadcasters concentrated there. This concentration reinforced itself: employment drew migrants, migrants created markets and labor supply, and prestige institutions drew ambitious students and families. The capital's growth was therefore not merely demographic but institutional.

The city grew in area as well as population. A major boundary expansion in 1963 incorporated surrounding districts, including areas south of the Han River. Bridges, notably the Third Han River Bridge (Hannam Bridge) completed in 1969, together with the Gyeongbu Expressway opened in 1970, made the southern bank accessible. The Han River, once largely a barrier at the edge of the old city, became the axis of a larger metropolis. In the early 1980s a comprehensive Han River development project added embankments and riverside roads, and later Olympic facilities clustered in the south of the city.

Municipal governments, national ministries, planners, and construction agencies directed the growth with varying aims. Planners sought to relieve crowding in the old northern core by promoting development south of the river, and authorities relocated some prestigious institutions, including some high schools in the late 1970s, to the new districts. Government policy also tried to limit sprawl: a greenbelt around Seoul was designated in the early 1970s. The new southern district, Gangnam, grew from largely agricultural land through land readjustment, road building, and large apartment complexes, a transformation treated in Section 81.10.

Growth outpaced services. Housing, buses, water supply, sewerage, schools, clinics, and waste collection lagged behind population, especially in the 1960s and early 1970s. Migrants lived on hillsides and along streams in self-built or rented housing without secure title, sanitation, or reliable water. The contrast between neighborhoods favored by public investment and those left behind was one of the most visible inequalities of the period. Planning decisions about which districts to improve, which to clear, and where to place schools and transit thus helped build lasting social geography.

Urban policy created different metropolitan futures for districts only a short distance apart. The 1963 enlargement of Seoul's boundaries brought extensive southern territory within municipal administration; later bridges, roads and utilities made large-scale development more feasible. By 1975 Gangnam had become a separate administrative district, but its growth depended on planning and public spending decisions taken years earlier. Land readjustment and the relocation of facilities to the south altered both real-estate values and the perceived geography of opportunity. Meanwhile, older northern neighborhoods continued to house much of the city's industry, small commerce and migrant population. A city map from 1960 cannot be compared mechanically with one from 1980: the boundaries, land use and transportation system had all changed. Institutional geography thus helps explain why the growth of a single municipality created sharply different household outcomes.

Planning also redistributed costs. Roads required land, utilities were extended unevenly, and demolition could separate residents from nearby work, customers and schools. A family relocated to the urban edge might obtain a better-built dwelling but lose the practical value of walking to a workshop or relying on familiar neighbors for childcare. Conversely, homeowners on newly serviced land could gain wealth without moving. Official photographs and plans favored wide boulevards and model estates because these were the visible accomplishments of government; they rarely recorded the rent increases or long commutes experienced by those outside the showcase districts. An adequate account of metropolitan expansion therefore needs three scales at once: municipal population totals, the locations of investment, and the everyday radius within which a household could afford to live and work.

Students should distinguish measures carefully. "Seoul" in census tables usually means the municipality, whose boundaries changed. The Seoul metropolitan region includes surrounding areas of Gyeonggi Province and Incheon, where much industrial and residential growth occurred; by the 1980s planned satellite cities and industrial districts there housed a growing share of commuters and workers. Statements about "Seoul's population" or "the capital region's share of the national population" therefore describe different things, and a careful reading of any source must identify which is meant.

Section 81.3 · Division I

Industrial Cities and Regional Development: Busan, Ulsan, Pohang, Gumi, and Changwon

Industrialization was regional as well as metropolitan. The southeast, along the corridor linking Seoul and Busan, became the heartland of state-promoted manufacturing, while other regions received less investment. Understanding the social consequences requires attention to how factories transformed particular local communities.

Busan, the country's leading port, combined shipping, commerce, textiles, footwear, and manufacturing, and it absorbed large numbers of migrants, including war-era refugees who had settled on its hillsides. Ulsan, designated an industrial center in 1962, grew into a city of petrochemicals, automobiles, and shipbuilding as large firms built plants there from the 1960s and 1970s. Pohang, where the state-backed steel company opened its first mill in 1973, became a steel city. Gumi, developed from the late 1960s, specialized in electronics and textiles. Changwon, planned in the 1970s, concentrated machinery and heavy industry. Daegu retained its textile base, Incheon combined port and manufacturing, and Masan hosted an export processing zone established in 1970. The corporate histories behind these developments belong to Chapter 76; here the focus is on what they did to communities.

New plants drew young workers, who needed housing, food, transport, schools, and services. Company towns and planned districts built dormitories, apartments for employees, and shops. Local governments extended roads, water, and electricity, often responding to corporate and national priorities rather than local demand. Commercial streets grew around factory gates and railway stations, and churches, clinics, and schools followed. For many young people, such cities offered a more accessible first step than Seoul, closer to home and with employer-provided housing.

Not every region shared in this growth. The southwest (Honam), with relatively few large investments, supplied many migrants to Seoul and other cities rather than retaining them. Mining areas in Gangwon Province, such as Taebaek and Jeongseon, thrived on coal in the 1960s and 1970s, creating dense mining communities with their own hazards and social life, before decline in the late 1980s. Islands such as Jeju developed through agriculture, notably tangerines, and later tourism, rather than heavy manufacturing. Regional resentment about uneven development became a political theme in the 1980s, and the point is a reminder that Seoul's experience cannot stand for the whole country.

Industrial location decisions created distinctive labor markets. The steelworks at Pohang needed trained operators, a dependable port and transport links, while Ulsan's automobile and shipbuilding complexes drew both skilled engineers and numerous manual workers. Gumi's electronics plants recruited workers whose age, education and gender profile differed from that of heavy-industry sites. Busan's commercial and manufacturing economy included small suppliers whose business fortunes depended on the orders of larger exporters. In each place, the factory gate became a social institution as well as an economic entrance: it influenced bus schedules, nearby lodging, restaurants, repair shops and the formation of worker neighborhoods. A wage comparison between two industrial towns tells only part of the story unless housing costs, company benefits, shift arrangements and opportunities for advancement are considered together.

The geography of growth also complicated claims of national integration. Investment concentrated along particular ports and transport corridors, and households in Honam, upland agricultural communities or declining mining districts often relied on migrants' income rather than local industrial wages. Yet calling a region simply 'left behind' would conceal differences within it: provincial capitals, universities, market towns and local enterprises offered their own forms of change. Coalfields in Gangwon sustained schools and shops during periods of high demand but exposed workers and families to underground accidents, dust and eventual insecurity as fuel policy shifted. Regional history should therefore combine production maps with the social histories of people who lived near industrial sites, supplied them with food or left their home districts to work in them.

The relationship among central government, corporations, and local authorities was uneven. The state selected industrial sites, provided infrastructure, and offered incentives; firms chose employment practices and built company housing; local governments handled services with limited resources and little autonomy. Workers moved between cities in response to wage differences, seasonal demand, and layoffs, producing a mobile labor force that crossed regional boundaries and later helped form shared identities.

Section 81.4 · Division I

Rural Transformation, Saemaul Undong, and the Changing Village

While millions left the countryside, the countryside itself was transformed. In 1970, President Park Chung-hee promoted the New Village Movement, Saemaul Undong, which became a nationwide campaign. Its slogan stressed diligence, self-help, and cooperation. It followed a period in which urban industrial growth had outpaced rural incomes, and it aimed to improve rural living conditions, raise agricultural productivity, and build political support in the countryside.

The government began by distributing materials, notably cement, to villages in the winter of 1970–1971 and asked communities to use them for projects of their choosing. Villages that responded with visible results received more support; others received less. Typical projects included widening paths, building bridges and village halls, improving irrigation and water supply, replacing thatched roofs with tile or slate, extending electricity, and improving housing and sanitation. Alongside infrastructure, agricultural policies promoted new high-yielding rice varieties, introduced in the early 1970s, and guaranteed purchase prices for grain, which raised farm incomes in many areas.

Villagers contributed labor, land, and money. Local leaders, often respected residents rather than formal officials, organized work, and women's groups ran savings clubs, kitchen improvements, and other projects. Agricultural cooperatives and local officials coordinated supplies and credit. Participants often remembered the movement as bringing practical improvements: roads that allowed produce to reach markets, electricity, and better housing. Government support supplied materials and credit, but the labor and many of the costs fell on villagers.

The movement was also controversial. It operated through official hierarchies and was linked to the authoritarian Yushin system examined in Chapter 75. Participation was sometimes pressured, with officials ranking villages and tying benefits to performance, and some residents resented compulsory labor and the diversion of effort. Villages with better leadership, more resources, or easier access benefited more, widening gaps among communities. After Park's death, the movement's organization was reshaped, and corruption in its national headquarters under Chun Doo-hwan's brother became a public scandal in the late 1980s. Students should therefore avoid portraying Saemaul as either entirely voluntary or uniformly coercive; it combined state direction, community participation, material gains, and political pressure.

Early Saemaul projects illustrate how public subsidies and village effort became entangled. Cement and other materials offered by government were not complete roads, roofs or drainage systems: residents had to choose projects, provide labor and resolve disputes over whose land or household would be affected. In some communities organized cooperation produced improvements that people remembered with pride. Elsewhere, required contributions imposed heavier burdens on poorer households or older residents with little labor to spare. Performance rankings encouraged energetic local leadership but could reward villages already endowed with better resources. The movement's outcomes therefore cannot be inferred from official awards or photographs alone. Oral testimony, budgets and the distribution of benefits within a village offer different measures of success and expose disagreements that celebratory publicity suppressed.

Rural change also affected agricultural work itself. Improved routes to urban markets made some crops more commercially attractive, while fertilizer, mechanization and new rice varieties increased the demand for credit, equipment and reliable distribution. Households that could adopt new methods did not all benefit equally; poorer farmers faced rising costs and uncertainty over harvest prices. Women often shouldered substantial farm and community work as younger men and women migrated or pursued nonagricultural wages. Better houses and electricity altered family expectations, especially regarding children's study and access to radio or television. These gains did not reverse the powerful pull of industrial salaries and urban schools. Rural modernization and rural depopulation were mutually connected developments, not contradictory evidence that the campaign either wholly succeeded or wholly failed.

The central question is why rural modernization coincided with rural depopulation. The answer lies in the difference between living conditions and income opportunities. Roads, electricity, and better houses improved village life, but farms remained small, and urban wages and schools pulled young people away regardless. Improved roads and electrification could even speed departure by connecting villages to cities. By the late 1980s the countryside was better equipped but older, with a smaller, aging farm population and growing dependence on remittances and off-farm income.

Historical Vocabulary
Urbanization, Migration, and Industrial Labor

Urbanization is the growth in the share of a population living in cities. Statisticians define "urban" differently across time, so percentages are not always comparable. Rural-to-urban migration is the movement of people from farming areas to cities; it may be permanent, temporary, or circular, meaning repeated movement between home and city. Wage labor is work paid in money by an employer, as opposed to family farm labor. Social mobility is movement of individuals or families among positions in the social hierarchy. The industrial working class is the population dependent on wages from manufacturing and related work, which formed shared identities in this period. Regional inequality refers to systematic differences in income, investment, and opportunity among geographic areas.

Why it matters: These terms describe processes of choice and structure; they do not describe automatic improvement.

Potential misunderstanding: Urbanization is not synonymous with modernization or prosperity. Migrants often exchanged one form of hardship for another.

Section 81.5 · Division II — The Industrial Working Class, Women Workers, and Labor Relations

Factory Life: Working Hours, Wages, Discipline, and Industrial Employment

Industrial employment grew across textiles, garments, footwear, electronics, steel, automobiles, shipbuilding, and machinery. In the 1960s and early 1970s, labor-intensive light manufacturing predominated; in the later 1970s and 1980s, heavy and chemical industries employed growing numbers of men. Working life varied sharply by industry, firm size, and gender, and it is a mistake to imagine a single "Korean factory worker."

Large enterprises and small workshops offered very different conditions. Large firms generally paid higher wages, provided benefits such as company housing, meals, or tuition support, and offered more stable employment. Small subcontractors, which supplied parts and services to larger firms or filled export orders, paid less, provided fewer facilities, and responded to demand pressures by lengthening hours or laying off workers. The division between large-firm and small-firm employment, along with the difference between permanent and temporary or daily workers, became a lasting feature of the labor market.

Hours were long. Manufacturing workers in the 1970s and 1980s worked among the longest weeks in the world, with overtime routine, night shifts common, and rest days limited, as recorded in labor-ministry statistics. Production quotas and export deadlines set the pace, and supervisors enforced discipline through fines, dismissal threats, and, in some factories, military-style routines and uniforms. The government encouraged low wages to preserve export competitiveness and regarded labor disputes as threats to growth and security.

Labor law existed but was weakly enforced. The Labor Standards Act of 1953 prescribed hours, wages, safety, and dismissal protections, but inspectors were few, employers could evade through subcontracting and informal arrangements, and workers often did not know or feared to claim their rights. Students must distinguish the existence of a law from compliance with it.

The workday was organized by more than the wage rate. In small garment or assembly workshops, production deadlines were transmitted through subcontractors and foremen, so the immediate employer could claim that longer hours were necessary to meet an order placed elsewhere. A garment worker might spend the day sewing identical seams under close supervision, then remain for urgent finishing work when an export shipment approached. Large plants had more elaborate machinery, training and company welfare facilities, but they also imposed shifts, seniority hierarchies and disciplinary rules. Industrial employment offered cash wages, co-worker networks and sometimes new technical skills; it could also expose employees to fatigue, accidents and dismissal when orders fell. Comparisons must identify the industry, enterprise size and the kind of employment, rather than relying on one representative 'factory worker.'

The experience of wages depended on household arithmetic. A migrant employee paying for a shared room, meals, transportation and remittances could retain little personal income even when a nominal wage was higher than farm earnings. Company dormitories reduced some expenses but often brought curfews, inspection and limited independence. Workers calculated whether overtime would pay school fees or clear debts, while parents weighed the loss of a child's labor at home against regular factory remittances. Inflation could erode increases in cash pay, and workplace compensation did not always include reliable sick leave or medical treatment. These everyday calculations help explain why workers could simultaneously value industrial employment and protest its conditions. The historical question is not whether development created jobs—it did—but what power workers possessed to negotiate the terms on which those jobs were performed.

For workers, factory wages mattered because they financed household goals. Earnings paid for siblings' schooling, parents' medicine, savings toward a deposit for housing, and eventually a family's step up. Workers also learned skills, formed friendships and mutual-aid circles, and built occupational pride. Many judged their conditions by what they could bring home, but they also evaluated them against fairness: whether pay matched effort, whether overtime was paid, whether they were treated with respect. Employers and officials emphasized efficiency and national development; workers' own assessments of acceptable conditions often differed, and that gap fed the organizing described in the following sections.

Section 81.6 · Division II

Women Workers and the Export Economy: Textiles, Garments, Wigs, and Electronics

Young women were central to the first phase of export industrialization. Textiles, garments, wigs, shoes, and electronics assembly relied on large numbers of low-paid, nimble-fingered workers, and employers drew them mainly from rural families. Wigs, made largely by women from imported hair, were among the leading exports of the late 1960s and early 1970s. The Guro Export Industrial Complex in Seoul, the Masan export zone, and textile mills in Daegu and elsewhere employed tens of thousands of young women.

Recruitment typically ran through kin networks, labor brokers, and advertisements. Many women entered factories at around fifteen to twenty, often after finishing primary or middle school. Employers housed some in dormitories, which saved them rent but subjected them to curfews and supervision; others rented shared rooms near the factory. Wages were low, and women were paid substantially less than men. A World Bank analysis drawing on Korean labor surveys reports female-to-male aggregate earnings ratios of approximately 42% in 1975 and 43% in 1980; these figures combine different occupations and worker characteristics and are not controlled equal-work comparisons. Occupational sorting, seniority, gender discrimination, and assumptions about supposedly temporary female employment all influenced earnings. Overtime and night shifts were routine.

Women's earnings supported households. Remittances to parents financed land, debts, and, notably, the education of brothers and younger siblings, a pattern in which daughters' wages enabled sons' mobility. Some women also saved for their own education, attending night schools or company-attached special classes that offered middle- or high-school credentials. Others saved for marriage. The decision to work was often shaped by family obligation, yet many women also reported gaining independence, income, and urban experience.

Public discourse praised young female workers as contributors to national development, sometimes calling them "industrial warriors," while social attitudes also stigmatized them with the demeaning term gongsuni. Employers often treated women's work as temporary, a stage before marriage, and used marriage or pregnancy as grounds for dismissal or discouraged promotion and training. Men filled supervisory positions and better-paid skilled jobs, and gender hierarchy reproduced itself inside the workplace.

Statistical evidence makes gender inequality visible, but it requires careful reading. A World Bank study reproducing Korean labor-survey data gives a female-to-male earnings ratio of roughly 0.42 in 1975 and 0.43 in 1980 for the surveyed employment measures. These are aggregate earnings comparisons, not proof that a woman and a man performing identical tasks in a particular factory received those exact ratios. Differences in occupation, seniority, hours, firm size and labor-force composition affected the averages; discriminatory pay and unequal promotion opportunities also mattered. The distinction is important because the category of 'unskilled female labor' was itself socially constructed and helped justify lower rewards for tasks essential to production. Factory payrolls and interviews with workers are needed to study discrimination at the level of particular workplaces.

Women's collective action developed from ordinary relationships before it became a public confrontation. Shared dormitory rooms and lunch breaks created occasions to exchange information about wages and management behavior; night schools and religious labor groups supplied books, meeting space and sometimes legal assistance. At Dongil Textile, women fought for representation in their union in the face of management-aligned opposition and humiliating attacks, illustrating that workplace conflict could involve gender hierarchy within labor organizations as well as employers' authority. The effects of such disputes were uneven: organizers might lose wages, face blacklisting or endure family pressure to return home, while those who remained could gain experience in speaking, negotiation and mutual aid. Their histories complicate an account of development centered on government planners or male industrial executives: women's paid and unpaid labor financed education, sustained households and helped build the organizations that demanded fairer treatment.

Women organized. Female workers formed many of the key labor actions of the 1970s, including the Dongil Textile union dispute in 1978, in which women who had elected a female union leader were attacked and humiliated by men acting against their union, and later disputes in the early 1980s at firms such as Wonpoong Textile and in the 1985 Guro solidarity strike. They built solidarity in dormitories and lunch groups, read, studied, and sought support from churches and student volunteers. Students should avoid portraying women workers simply as victims. They developed skills, supported households, made independent choices, organized unions, and contributed directly to industrial growth, even as they faced systematic discrimination. Historians such as Hwasook Nam, Seung-kyung Kim, and Soonok Chun have studied these workers; their work should be consulted for fuller treatment.

Section 81.7 · Division II

Jeon Tae-il, Labor Rights, and the Emergence of Worker Consciousness

Pyeonghwa Market in central Seoul was a district of small garment workshops, many in cramped multi-story buildings with low ceilings and poor ventilation. It employed a large workforce, many of them teenage girls working as assistants, alongside cutters and sewing machine operators. Hours were long, wages were low, and workers had little access to medical care, even though the Labor Standards Act prescribed limits on hours and conditions that most workshops did not observe.

Jeon Tae-il, born in 1948, worked in the market as a garment cutter and became aware of the conditions around him. He and a few fellow workers formed study and discussion groups, gathered information about workplaces, and appealed to government offices and newspapers for enforcement of the law. Their petitions and surveys documented excessive hours, inadequate pay, and health problems among young workers. Officials and employers responded slowly or dismissively, and Jeon lost his job.

On 13 November 1970, Jeon died after setting himself on fire during a demonstration at the market, calling for compliance with the Labor Standards Act. His death shocked many who had not previously considered industrial working conditions. It drew attention from journalists, students, churches, and intellectuals, and it helped legitimize labor rights as a public concern. In its wake, workers at Pyeonghwa Market formed a union, which became a symbol and training ground for later activism, and student and religious groups increasingly supported workers.

The death had lasting commemorative power, and Jeon became a central figure in later labor and democratic movements. Historians caution against treating every later worker organization as a direct consequence of one event. Labor activism had earlier roots in union organizing, strikes, and workers' own networks, and it developed through many local struggles in the 1970s and 1980s. Jeon's significance lies partly in the specific reforms and organizing he inspired and partly in the way his story gave language to widespread grievances.

Pyeonghwa Market gives this story a precise urban setting. The market combined commerce on the lower level with garment work in crowded upper-floor rooms and improvised lofts; production and sale existed in close physical proximity but wages and conditions were sharply unequal. The National Institute of Korean History's account of Jeon Tae-il's investigations describes a district with hundreds of workshops and a very large workforce, many of them young women. Jeon's use of the Labor Standards Act was politically significant because it exposed the gap between a law the state had enacted and the conditions it tolerated in workplaces supplying the national economy. He and fellow workers were not asking historians to imagine better regulations; they were documenting why existing ones failed to protect them. Their findings should be examined as situated activist evidence rather than as a random survey of all industrial employment.

The response to Jeon's death extended well beyond an individual tragedy. Organizing at the market connected employees with students, clergy and journalists, enabling demands that managers and officials might otherwise have ignored. Commemoration also changed over time: activists in later decades interpreted his death through broader arguments about democracy and class, giving the event meanings beyond those that observers in November 1970 immediately shared. Both dimensions matter. The contemporaneous history concerns cramped working spaces, hours, illness, petitions and attempts at union formation; the later memory concerns how a worker became a symbol of legal rights and collective dignity. Keeping the two separate allows the chapter to recognize Jeon's extraordinary influence without erasing other unions, workplace campaigns and women organizers whose work preceded and followed him.

Through the 1970s, workers in other workplaces and districts organized for better pay and conditions, supported by labor-oriented church groups such as the Urban Industrial Mission and by Catholic worker organizations. Their efforts met pressure from employers and police, including dismissals and arrests. This section's purpose is to explain how worker consciousness formed: through experience of exploitation, access to information about legal rights, solidarity networks, and moral claims. The broader history of opposition politics belongs to Chapter 79.

Section 81.8 · Division II

Trade Unions, Occupational Hazards, and the Changing Politics of Industrial Labor

Labor organization in this period operated within narrow legal limits. The Federation of Korean Trade Unions, reorganized in 1961 after the coup, served as the official national federation and was generally cooperative with government. Unions in most firms were organized at the enterprise level, and law restricted strikes, collective bargaining, and outside "third-party" involvement in disputes. Independent or "democratic" unions, such as those in Pyeonghwa Market and in several textile and electronics firms, struggled against employer resistance and official suppression.

Hazards were real. Workplace accidents, noise, dust, chemical exposure, long hours, and inadequate safety equipment produced injuries and occupational disease. Official statistics recorded large numbers of industrial accidents in the 1970s and 1980s, and activists drew attention to cases such as chemical poisoning among workers in small electronics and rubber plants. Safety inspections and compensation systems existed on paper but were weakly applied, particularly in small firms. Industrial disease also appeared in communities, as with pollution-related illness around industrial complexes, discussed in Section 81.11.

Employers resisted independent organization by dismissing activists, creating company-friendly unions, using hired men to disrupt meetings, and calling in police. Workers responded with new methods: study groups, support from churches and students, and, in some cases, the use of public occupations and hunger strikes to draw attention. Students and religious activists sometimes entered factories as workers, a practice that also carried political risks. These alliances spread knowledge and tactics but also exposed workers to accusations of subversion.

The Chun Doo-hwan government that followed the events of 1979–1980, examined in Chapter 79, tightened controls. A 1980 labor-law revision reinforced enterprise-level unions and restricted outside involvement, many labor activists were detained or blacklisted, and organizations were purged. Nevertheless, networks reorganized in the early 1980s through small-group activity, night schools, and local committees. In 1985, workers in several Guro-district firms struck in solidarity, one of the first multi-firm actions of the decade. A Minimum Wage Act passed in 1986 and took effect in 1988.

Legal recognition of unions did not settle who could speak on workers' behalf. An enterprise union could be formally registered yet dependent on managers or vulnerable to replacement when its leaders challenged wages or safety practices. Restrictions on outside assistance made it more difficult to obtain independent legal advice or cooperate across firms, even where workers shared a production chain. Employers could exploit turnover, dividing temporary and long-serving employees or distinguishing skilled men from lower-paid women. For activists, a demand for safer machinery or protection from dismissal might become a question of freedom of association when management called police or interfered with internal elections. Understanding labor conflict therefore requires studying institutional rules as well as wages and strikes, and distinguishing the official federation from independently organized workplace networks.

Industrial safety is equally difficult to capture through national totals. A factory's recorded accident rate depends on which injuries are reported, whether subcontractors are counted and whether workers have incentives to disclose illness. Chemical exposure might cause harm over years rather than during an immediately visible accident; a shipyard or steel plant presented different hazards from a garment loft. Workers and labor-oriented religious groups sometimes collected testimony precisely because official inspection had not established what employees experienced. The 1985 Guro solidarity action showed that mutual support could cross firm boundaries despite the rules designed to contain unionism within each enterprise. By the late 1980s a more experienced workforce had learned both the value and the limits of legal protections; that background is indispensable to Chapter 82's treatment of the later mass labor mobilization.

By the late 1980s, industrial workers entered a new period with accumulated grievances: low wages relative to productivity, long hours, unsafe conditions, and limited rights. They also had organizational experience, networks, and a sense of themselves as members of a class. The distinction that matters here is between the long formation of an industrial working class and the specific political events, such as the Great Workers' Struggle of July–September 1987, through which its collective power became visible. Those events, and their relationship to democratization, are the subject of Chapter 82.

Section 81.9 · Division III — Housing, Apartments, Transportation, and Urban Everyday Life

The Urban Housing Crisis: Informal Settlements, Tenants, and Displacement

Rapid migration produced a housing shortage. Many migrants rented single rooms in older houses, lodged in boardinghouses or workshops, or built makeshift dwellings on hillsides, riverbanks, and public land. These informal settlements, sometimes called "moon villages" for their position on high ground, lacked secure title, piped water, and sanitation. In winter, many households heated their rooms with coal briquettes, and carbon monoxide poisoning was a recurrent danger.

Rental arrangements varied. Monthly rent, often with a smaller deposit, was common among poorer households. A distinctive system, jeonse, required the tenant to provide a large lump-sum deposit, often a substantial fraction of the property's value, which was returned when the lease ended; the landlord invested the money and the tenant paid no monthly rent. Jeonse grew in a setting of scarce housing, high interest rates, and limited mortgage credit: landlords gained cheap capital, and tenants who could assemble a deposit gained a stable home. It is not an ordinary mortgage or a conventional lease, and it made tenants' security dependent on the landlord's solvency and on continually rising deposits.

Government housing targets repeatedly exceeded actual delivery of affordable units. Official statistics counted dwellings, but the number of units did not equal the number of households able to afford them, and the gap left many families in substandard housing. Authorities also acted against informal settlement. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, Seoul cleared hillside and riverside communities and relocated tens of thousands of residents to resettlement areas on the city's periphery, including the Gwangju Daedanji district, now part of Seongnam. Residents there, promised land and housing but facing high prices and few jobs, rose in protest in 1971, demonstrating that relocation could provoke open resistance.

Redevelopment brought further displacement. From the 1970s, and especially from 1983 with the "joint redevelopment" method, property owners in older districts partnered with private builders to replace houses with apartment blocks. Owners could receive new units, but tenants, who often outnumbered owners, had limited rights to compensation or replacement housing, and many were forced to leave. Disputes over compensation, demolition crews, and evictions became common, and advocacy groups, churches, and residents organized to resist.

The terms of tenancy could determine whether a household benefited from redevelopment. A registered owner might negotiate over a replacement unit, compensation or a rising land value, whereas a tenant often possessed no equivalent claim despite having lived in the neighborhood for years. In a jeonse arrangement the tenant had already committed substantial household savings, so recovering the deposit and finding another dwelling at a similar price could be difficult even without demolition. Relocation to a cheaper peripheral district altered commuting costs and access to schools, markets and relatives. The costs of clearance were thus not limited to the loss of walls and a roof; they included jobs reached on foot, informal childcare, credit from familiar shopkeepers and neighborhood networks that made low urban incomes survivable.

Gwangju Daedanji and the later redevelopment of Sanggye-dong should be studied as different episodes, not merged into one continuous eviction campaign. The former exposed the contradictions of rapid peripheral relocation in 1971, when residents faced inadequate employment, infrastructure and uncertain land terms. The latter involved the redevelopment policies of the 1980s, rising land values and more powerful alliances between property owners, government and private developers. In both settings residents organized because they regarded their position not simply as a private misfortune but as a question of public responsibility. Petitions, court records, neighborhood testimony and urban plans can establish different aspects of the dispute. A strong housing history should compare those records rather than accept either the redevelopment authority's promises or opponents' retrospective accounts as complete evidence on their own.

The effects were unequal. Property owners and developers often gained as land values rose; landlords benefited from jeonse capital; tenants and informal residents bore much of the cost of clearance. Housing insecurity was therefore as central to this history as new construction. Recognizing both is necessary to understand why housing became, by the late 1980s, a major political and social issue, with government announcing large housing programs in 1988 and 1989.

Section 81.10 · Division III

The Apartment Revolution: Gangnam, Residential Modernity, and Middle-Class Identity

Apartments became a defining feature of South Korean cities. Early examples appeared in the 1930s and the 1950s, but the Mapo apartment complex of 1962 was an early large-scale government-built development, and the 1960s saw additional projects. Quality varied: the Wawoo Apartment complex in Seoul collapsed on April 8, 1970, killing 33 and seriously injuring 39, a disaster that raised public concern about construction speed and supervision. Nevertheless, the state and private builders expanded apartment construction through the 1970s and 1980s, supported by legislation promoting housing construction and the supply of land.

Gangnam, the area south of the Han River, became the emblem of this transformation. Previously rural, it was developed from the late 1960s through land readjustment, in which landowners ceded a share of land for roads and public facilities in exchange for serviced lots. Government built bridges and highways, encouraged private developers, and from the early 1970s approved large complexes such as those in Banpo and Apgujeong. By the late 1970s and 1980s, Gangnam housed prestigious apartment estates, schools relocated from the northern city, commercial districts, and the offices and shops that followed.

Apartments appealed because they offered features older houses often lacked: central heating, indoor plumbing, reliable utilities, planned grounds and amenities, security, and convenient access to schools and shops. Their uniform layouts and shared facilities became a model of modern family life, emphasizing the nuclear household. French geographer Valérie Gelézeau has described Seoul's pattern of apartment complexes as characteristic of the city's modern landscape.

Ownership of an apartment became a route to wealth. As land and housing prices rose, especially in Seoul and Gangnam, owners saw large increases in the value of their property. Speculation intensified, with buyers purchasing for resale, and government repeatedly tried to dampen it with taxes, lotteries, and resale restrictions. The result was a division between those who held property and those who did not, and between desirable neighborhoods and others. Residential location also became tied to schooling, since families wanted access to well-regarded schools, further linking housing, education, and class.

The sequence of apartment construction reveals a change in who planners expected to house. The 1962 Mapo complex was an early demonstration of estate-style housing, but later projects such as Hangang Mansion, Yeouido Sibeom and Banpo Jugong increasingly attracted salaried and relatively prosperous buyers. Seoul Museum of History records connect this shift to the apartment-district policy of 1976, redevelopment along the Han River and the growth of Gangnam. The apartment complex became a managed environment with standardized floor plans, maintenance systems and shared grounds, contrasting with older neighborhoods where families relied on courtyard arrangements or informal services. Its architecture also embodied an idea of the modern nuclear family, although actual households continued to support parents, relatives and children living elsewhere.

Access depended on financial institutions and location as much as taste. Preconstruction sales and allocation systems encouraged households to treat eligibility for a new unit as an asset; those unable to assemble payments remained tenants, often putting large savings into jeonse deposits. Public investment in bridges, schools and roads raised the value of surrounding property, benefiting existing landholders and early purchasers disproportionately. When prestigious schools moved south, the relationship between residence and educational advantage strengthened, giving even families with no speculative intentions a reason to seek particular districts. The Wawoo collapse of April 8, 1970—33 people killed and 39 seriously injured—also demonstrates why speed of construction should not be mistaken for safety or uniformly improved living conditions. The apartment revolution was at once a housing transformation and a new mechanism for distributing wealth and opportunity.

The apartment thus became a symbol of security, mobility, modern family life, and aspiration. But the cultural appeal must be distinguished from financial reality: many households could not afford to buy, borrowed heavily, relied on jeonse, or faced rising prices that moved ownership out of reach. New apartments and informal settlements coexisted, sometimes within sight of one another, and apartment construction did not mean universally improved housing.

Section 81.11 · Division III

Roads, Subways, Commuting, Pollution, and the Infrastructure of Urban Growth

Urban growth required infrastructure. The Gyeongbu Expressway, opened in 1970, linked Seoul and Busan and became a symbol of national development. Within cities, authorities widened roads, built overpasses and Han River bridges, expanded bus services, and developed rail connections. Seoul's first subway line opened on 15 August 1974, linking the old city core with Seoul Station and areas to the east, and was connected with existing suburban rail lines; further lines followed in the 1980s, and Busan and other cities planned systems of their own.

Transport shaped access to work, schools, markets, and housing. Subways and buses allowed workers to live farther from workplaces, expanding the geography of residence. They also lengthened commutes, which in crowded buses and trains became a defining daily experience. Distance between home and workplace affected women's employment, children's schooling, and household budgets. Newly built districts at the periphery were often poorly served at first, and service quality varied sharply among neighborhoods.

Utilities expanded as well. Electricity reached most households by the 1970s and 1980s, piped water and sewerage improved in many urban districts, and household appliances became more common. Yet coverage was uneven, with hillside settlements and outlying districts receiving services later, and rural areas relying on village-level projects such as those of Saemaul Undong.

Industrialization carried environmental costs. Coal briquettes and industrial emissions polluted urban air, and Seoul's smog and sulfur dioxide levels worried residents and health officials. Rivers and coasts received industrial and household waste, and waste treatment lagged behind growth. In the Ulsan–Onsan industrial area, reports in the mid-1980s of illness among residents, popularly called "Onsan disease," drew attention to the connection between industrial pollution and health. Public concern about pollution, food safety, and water quality increased in the 1980s, and environmental disputes, a new kind of social conflict, emerged alongside rising prosperity.

The 1974 subway opening was only the beginning of an integrated metropolitan transport system. Seoul subsequently expanded rail lines and connecting bus services while bridges carried commuters across the Han River toward new residential and industrial districts. Such construction changed the economic meaning of distance. A household could consider a cheaper room farther from a workshop if transport remained affordable and reliable; a new apartment estate could become desirable when commuting time fell. But long trips were not costless. Crowded buses and trains, shift work, and early departures shaped meals, childcare and sleep. Construction itself disrupted established neighborhoods, and routes were often chosen to serve administrative or commercial priorities rather than the places of greatest need. Metropolitan mobility was therefore an achievement of infrastructure investment and a new source of differentiation among residents.

Environmental problems were distributed unequally too. Coal briquettes provided heat in many homes but posed carbon-monoxide hazards when ventilation was inadequate. Factories and power facilities contributed smoke and industrial discharge, while rapidly increasing traffic added noise and air pollution. Rivers receiving insufficiently treated sewage or industrial wastewater reflected the gap between urban expansion and environmental management. Residents of better-serviced districts could enjoy parks and modern utilities while those near industrial corridors, waste facilities or poorly drained streams faced different conditions. The official success of a road, waterworks project or industrial estate should therefore be evaluated beside evidence on health, commuting, access to services and ecological damage. By the late 1980s environmental complaints increasingly entered civic organizing, connecting the social consequences of urbanization to wider demands for public accountability.

Improvements in national transport capacity should be distinguished from the experience of individual neighborhoods. A new expressway or subway line benefited some residents far more than others, and the costs of pollution and construction were distributed unevenly, often falling on poorer districts near factories and highways.

Section 81.12 · Division III

Neighborhoods, Markets, Public Services, and the Social Organization of City Life

Everyday urban life was organized around neighborhoods. Traditional markets supplied fresh food and daily goods; small shops, public bathhouses, clinics, pharmacies, and tea rooms served as local centers. Churches, Buddhist temples, schools, and alumni and hometown associations structured social life, and neighborhood associations (bansanghoe and similar bodies) mixed local sociability with administrative functions. Urban residents also relied on informal rotating-credit groups (gye), through which members pooled savings and took turns receiving the sum, a form of credit in a society where bank loans were hard for ordinary households to get.

Experiences differed by dwelling type. Residents of apartments encountered planned courtyards, management offices, and, increasingly, neighbors who were strangers to one another. Residents of older neighborhoods often knew neighbors for years and relied on mutual help. Boardinghouse lodgers and workers' rental-room residents often shared kitchens and toilets and moved frequently. Residents of informal settlements built networks of mutual support and, at times, collective organizations to resist eviction or demand water and electricity.

Households managed scarce resources. Women often handled daily budgets, shopping, and food preparation, along with childcare, which was supplied by relatives, neighbors, and, later, nurseries and kindergartens. Households purchased food at markets and small shops, storing kimchi and staples through the winter. Medical treatment depended on income, location, and, after 1977, the gradual spread of health insurance; clinics, pharmacies, and traditional medicine all played roles.

Service provision improved but unevenly. Electricity, piped water, sanitation, healthcare access, and public transport reached more households each decade, yet differences between districts persisted. Neighborhood identity could supply social support but also mark differences in income, education, and residential status, and residents of certain districts faced stigma.

The organization of a neighborhood was shaped by the everyday work of provisioning households. Open-air and covered markets connected wholesalers, small merchants, customers and delivery workers; a credit relationship with a familiar vendor could be as significant to an irregularly paid family as the price of an item. Public baths and communal facilities supplemented homes without adequate plumbing, while pharmacists and neighborhood clinics often offered more accessible care than major hospitals. Women commonly coordinated shopping, childcare and care for relatives, and this unpaid labor allowed other household members to hold factory and office jobs. These arrangements were not remnants of an unchanging 'traditional' city: they adapted to new wages, television advertising, packaged goods, rental housing and the altered rhythms of commuting.

Religion and local association also became resources for adjustment. Churches, hometown groups, savings associations and school-parent networks could introduce migrants to landlords, employers or childcare, but benefits depended on who belonged and who could contribute. Apartment residents encountered new management rules and maintenance fees, while households in low-rise districts organized around alleys, shared courtyards and informal neighborhood watch. Such relationships could produce solidarity, yet also social surveillance or exclusion of tenants and newcomers. The expansion of public healthcare and utilities reduced some older risks, but the process was uneven: the arrival of a water line did not guarantee affordable connection, and national medical insurance did not mean every treatment was free. Oral history is particularly valuable here when a testimony's date, neighborhood and household circumstances can be identified rather than being treated as universal experience.

The history of informal services is also a history of unequal visibility. Tax records and building permits describe businesses recognized by government; they reveal less about homework sewing, informal loans, childcare exchanged among neighbors or street vending by households without secure premises. Newspaper photographs often highlight a renovated market or new commercial road but may overlook the families whose livelihood depended on the old alley. A careful student can compare a municipal photograph with a household-budget survey or an interview to identify both public improvements and hidden costs. Such comparison matters because city life was sustained by unpaid and semi-formal economic activity alongside factory wages and department-store spending. To understand modernization from below is to ask who maintained the household, whose time was counted as productive, and whose voice was absent from the planning record.

Historians reconstruct these experiences from oral histories, photographs, household surveys, local-government records, and contemporary journalism, sources that capture aspects missing from national economic statistics. They remind us that the emerging society was built not only in factories and government offices but also in apartments, streets, markets, schools, clinics, and homes.

Historical Vocabulary
Apartment Culture, Jeonse, and Residential Inequality

Apartment housing in Korea usually means large planned complexes (danji) of multi-story blocks with shared grounds. Jeonse is a rental arrangement in which the tenant provides a large refundable deposit instead of monthly rent. Homeownership became a main route to household wealth. Redevelopment is the demolition and rebuilding of older districts. Displacement is the forced or economically compelled departure of residents from their homes. Land speculation is the purchase of land or housing for expected price gains. Spatial segregation is the concentration of social groups in different districts.

Why it matters: Housing connected household security, wealth accumulation, schooling, and class identity.

Potential misunderstanding: Jeonse is not a mortgage and not a conventional lease. Nor did new apartments mean that informal housing disappeared.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Case Studies in Industrial Society

Section 81.13 · Division IV — Education, Gender, Family Life, and Demographic Transformation

The Expansion of Education: Schools, Literacy, Credentials, and Social Mobility

South Korea's education system expanded enormously during industrialization. Primary schooling was nearly universal by the early 1960s; middle-school, high-school, and university enrollment rose steadily through the following decades. Governments needed a literate, disciplined, and increasingly skilled labor force, and the 1968 National Charter of Education expressed national aims for schooling. But expansion also reflected households' own demand: parents, including poor ones, invested in children's schooling as a route to better jobs, and many sacrificed to keep children, especially sons, in school.

Policy changed the structure of access. Competitive entrance examinations for middle schools, which had made elite middle schools highly selective and fostered intense preparation among children, were abolished in stages from 1969 in Seoul, with nationwide coverage by 1971; students were instead assigned by lottery within districts. In 1974 a high-school equalization policy took effect in Seoul and Busan, assigning students by lottery to high schools within districts, and it was extended to other cities in following years. The goals were to reduce preparation pressure on younger children and to narrow differences among schools. These reforms were distinct, and neither abolished competition for university entrance.

Vocational and technical education expanded with heavy industry. Technical high schools trained machinists, welders, and electricians for the new industrial complexes, and the government created specialized institutes. Many workers, especially young women in factories, also gained credentials through company-attached "special classes" and night schools established from the late 1970s. Academic high schools and universities remained the more prestigious track, and vocational education carried lower status, which many families sought to avoid.

Opportunities depended on location, income, and gender. Rural schools lagged behind urban ones; poor families often could not afford fees, uniforms, and lost labor. Girls were more likely than boys to be withdrawn from school to work or marry, though the gap narrowed over time. University enrollment grew, especially after the early 1980s expansion of admission quotas, but elite universities in Seoul remained concentrated in places accessible to more advantaged families.

Equalization was not one nationwide switch. The government began replacing selective middle-school entrance examinations with allocation from 1969, while the 1973 high-school policy was implemented first in Seoul and Busan in 1974 and extended progressively to other cities. The high-school system retained distinctions between academic and vocational tracks and did not eliminate screening or competition for places in universities. Its stated aim was to reduce the concentration of advantage in a few schools and to ease the burden of entrance preparation on children and families. National Archives of Korea records include the 1973 policy formulation and subsequent implementation history. To evaluate whether it succeeded, students need enrollment, school-resource and family-spending evidence as well as the legislation's declared intentions.

For a rural household, educational expansion could change the family's economic plan. Sending a child to middle school required fees, supplies, transport and time not spent in fieldwork or a family business. Secondary-school graduates, in turn, could seek technical training, office employment or university admission. Yet an additional credential did not guarantee the same opportunities everywhere: urban schools more readily accessed teachers, facilities and nearby employers, while rural students might need boarding arrangements or long journeys. Vocational schools offered important industrial pathways but often carried a different social prestige from academic schools leading to top universities. Gender expectations shaped these choices as well; some families financed sons' longer schooling from daughters' earlier wage employment. The history of rising enrollment therefore needs to be read together with family budgets and the changing hierarchy of credentials.

Educational attainment became increasingly tied to occupational prestige, income, marriage prospects, and social standing. Education served as an instrument of national development and as a household strategy of advancement. These two purposes did not always coincide: the nation's need for skilled labor met families' desire for credentials that distinguished their children from others.

Section 81.14 · Division IV

The Examination Society: University Admissions, Hagwon, and Educational Competition

As secondary schooling expanded, competition shifted to university admissions. Entry to a prestigious university, especially in Seoul, promised access to leading firms, government posts, and professions, and the examination system gave credentials great weight. Historians sometimes use the term "education fever" for the intense popular commitment to schooling, a long-standing cultural feature that industrialization intensified, as in Michael Seth's study of the subject.

A large private education sector developed. Families paid for tutoring, supplementary lessons, and preparatory academies, known as hagwon, and for study materials. Parents allocated household income, and often a mother's labor and time, to children's education, sometimes at the expense of savings, housing, or other children's needs. Students faced long study hours, examination anxiety, and strong expectations of success. Differences in family income, parental education, and residence produced unequal access to private lessons.

Educational expansion did not automatically produce equal access to elite universities or prestigious jobs. As more students gained secondary and higher education, credentials lost scarcity, and families sought to maintain advantage through additional investment and through residence in districts with desirable schools. Competition thus continued despite policy efforts to equalize schooling.

The government of Chun Doo-hwan sought to curb this spending. Measures announced on 30 July 1980 banned most private tutoring for school students, limited tutoring by university students, and altered admission procedures, including an expansion of university quotas. The ban reflected concern about household burdens and inequality. But it was unevenly enforced, with exceptions and patterns of evasion: some families paid for clandestine lessons, and private instruction continued in less visible forms. UNESCO's study of private supplementary education in Korea records both the policy and the difficulty of enforcing it. The legal prohibition and the practice must be distinguished.

The pressure of examinations was reinforced by the changing labor market. If the largest corporations and prestigious government institutions recruited heavily through educational credentials, parents could interpret expenditures on tutoring as an investment in future household security. Competition consequently shifted rather than disappeared: eliminating selection at one school level increased attention to the next, and expanding university places did not erase the prestige differences among institutions. Academic and vocational routes also offered different expectations of employment, influencing how families assessed risk. A private tutor might provide specialized preparation that crowded classrooms could not, but access was governed by household finances and by the student's available time outside regular lessons. Educational achievement became both a possible ladder of mobility and a mechanism through which better-off families maintained an advantage.

The July 1980 restrictions on private tutoring illustrate the limits of administrative solutions to social demand. Authorities sought to reduce family expenditure and the perceived inequality produced by paid supplementary lessons, but the desire for scarce university places remained. Some lessons continued privately or shifted toward activities and arrangements not covered in the same way by the prohibition. Uneven enforcement could advantage households able to find discreet instruction or devote resources to alternative preparation. Rather than assume that law successfully eliminated the tutoring market, the chapter must distinguish the announced policy, the behavior of families, and the incentives of schools and universities. School districts also linked academic competition to housing: a household's residential address affected educational opportunity, and this connection helped sustain demand for apartments in particular neighborhoods.

Study schedules also changed the use of urban space. Libraries, classrooms, boarding rooms and commercial academies became sites of preparation extending well beyond ordinary school hours; mothers, fathers and siblings often adjusted their routines to accommodate students. Such activities created employment for instructors and supplied the city with specialized educational businesses, while simultaneously making school success more costly. A household that had migrated to secure its children's future could discover that access to better education required a second move to a more expensive neighborhood. This is why schooling cannot be separated from apartment prices and transportation. The state could allocate pupils among schools, but it had much less control over the strategies households adopted to preserve perceived advantages. The result was a social contest over scarce prestigious positions rather than an absence of educational progress.

By the 1980s the relationships among school competition, household income, residence, and class were clear. Families with greater resources could invest more in tutoring and move to districts with strong schools, and the educational advantage translated into future income and housing. Education thus both enabled mobility and helped reproduce class distinctions across generations, a process sociologists call social reproduction.

Section 81.15 · Division IV

Women, Marriage, Family Responsibilities, and Changing Gender Relations

Economic modernization changed women's opportunities beyond the factory. Women's schooling expanded; more women entered clerical, teaching, nursing, and service occupations, and a small but growing number entered professions. Women participated in neighborhood associations, church groups, savings circles, and, increasingly, civic and professional organizations. Yet expectations about marriage, childrearing, and elder care remained strongly gendered.

Marriage remained nearly universal, and many employers expected women to leave employment on marriage or childbirth. Married women often combined unpaid housework, care for children, and obligations to a husband's parents with paid work in family businesses, small shops, piecework, or factory jobs. Daughters-in-law bore heavy responsibility for caring for in-laws, even as nuclear households became more common. The "double burden" of paid and domestic labor was widely felt.

Legal institutions preserved male authority. The family-registration system (hojeok) organized families around a male household head; inheritance customs favored sons, and family law treated wives and daughters unequally, for example in parental authority and property after divorce. A December 31, 1977 amendment, effective January 1, 1979, introduced changes including joint parental authority and some marital-property and inheritance provisions, but left the male household-head system in place. A further substantial amendment was promulgated on January 13, 1990 and became effective January 1, 1991; that later reform strengthened inheritance equality and rights to property division after divorce, and is mentioned here only as an outcome beyond this chapter's period. The Equal Employment Opportunity Act of 1987 prohibited discrimination in hiring and treatment, though its enforcement was initially weak. Statutory change did not immediately produce equality in practice.

Women's organizations grew. Professional and church groups, long-standing associations such as those for women's welfare, and newer activist groups addressed discrimination, labor conditions, and family law. In 1987 several major groups were founded, including a national federation of women's organizations and an association of women workers, building on earlier networks. Women also organized within the labor movement and student movement.

Family law illustrates the difference between a reform's date and its effects on households. The December 31, 1977 amendment, effective January 1, 1979, recognized joint parental authority in important circumstances and made changes to marital property and inheritance provisions. It did not abolish the male household-head system or remove all distinctions among sons, daughters and spouses. The next major amendment was promulgated on January 13, 1990 and took effect on January 1, 1991, extending rights in inheritance and property division after divorce; it lies beyond this chapter's main period and is mentioned only as a subsequent consequence of prolonged reform campaigns. These distinctions are supported by the National Institute of Korean History's annotated account of the successive reforms. Treating legal change as a process helps explain why women continued to encounter restrictions even after public discussion of equality became more prominent.

The material demands of care made gender arrangements durable. A woman working in a family shop or factory might still be expected to supervise children's schooling, maintain kinship ties and assist her husband's parents. Married women's labor was often recorded as assistance in family enterprises rather than as an independent occupation, complicating comparisons of participation rates. Educated women entering teaching, nursing, banking or office work faced different pressures from wage workers in small factories, yet both encountered assumptions that marriage or motherhood should interrupt paid employment. Women's organizations drew connections among workplace discrimination, family-registration rules, violence and access to public services, but their positions were not uniform. Reform was the result of changing households and organized advocacy, not an automatic reward from economic growth. This section should preserve that plurality rather than treating a legal amendment as a complete social transformation.

Women's experiences varied by class, age, education, occupation, and marital status. A young factory worker, an educated office worker, a farm wife, a shopkeeper, and a professional woman faced different constraints and opportunities. The central question is how industrialization expanded women's economic participation while preserving elements of gender inequality. The answer lies in the difference between access to paid work and the status, pay, and security that work provided, which remained lower for women than for men, as well as in family institutions that assigned unpaid care to women.

Section 81.16 · Division IV

Family Planning, Population Change, Health, and the Transformation of Household Structure

South Korea experienced one of the fastest fertility declines recorded. The total fertility rate fell from about six births per woman around 1960 to roughly 1.7 by 1985. The change reshaped households, schools, housing needs, and the labor market, and its causes were several.

The government sponsored a national family-planning program from 1962, supported by the Planned Parenthood Federation of Korea, which distributed contraceptives through health centers and field workers and promoted small families through slogans and education. Incentives, such as preferences in housing or exemptions for men who underwent sterilization, were used in the 1970s. Abortion, formally restricted, was permitted under defined conditions by the Maternal and Child Health Law of 1973, and in practice was widely used. These policies mattered, but they did not act alone.

Urbanization, schooling, and household economics also lowered fertility. In cities, children cost more and contributed less labor than on farms; rising education costs encouraged parents to invest more per child in fewer children; women's education and employment raised the cost of childbearing; and marriage occurred later. Parents' own reproductive decisions, responding to these conditions, were central. Historians therefore avoid attributing the decline to government policy alone: economic development, education, reproductive choices, healthcare, and cultural expectations all contributed.

Health improved. Infant mortality fell sharply, life expectancy rose from the mid-50s around 1960 to around 70 by the late 1980s, and maternal and child health services expanded. National health insurance started in 1977 for employees of large firms and extended over the following years to other groups, reaching universal coverage in 1989. Datasets differ in definitions, and students should use consistent series when comparing figures.

The speed of fertility decline becomes clearer when measures are distinguished. The total fertility rate estimates the number of births a woman would have over a lifetime if prevailing age-specific rates continued; it is not the average number of living children in a household at one instant. International series place the rate around six in 1960 and near 1.7 by 1985. Government family-planning programs increased information and access to contraception, but schooling, urban housing costs, changes in marriage timing and lower demand for children's farm labor also shaped decisions. A couple in a rented urban room faced different expenses and expectations from a family operating a small farm. The shift thus reflects a combination of public intervention and private choices made under changing economic circumstances, not a simple order transmitted from the state to households.

Changes in survival and health reinforced the new demographic environment. Expanded vaccination, prenatal services, water systems and access to clinical care contributed to falling childhood mortality and longer life, although income and location continued to determine quality of care. Employee health insurance began in 1977 at larger workplaces and was extended in stages until population-wide coverage in 1989; insurance membership did not make all care free or ensure equal access to specialists. Smaller families could invest more per child in schooling, but they also carried growing expectations for parents to finance increasingly expensive education. Longer lives and rural outmigration left some older people separated from adult children, maintaining the importance of remittances and kin obligations. The emerging nuclear household therefore coexisted with continuing intergenerational financial dependence.

Household structure changed. Families became smaller and more nuclear, though many still supported aging parents; migration separated older relatives from adult children, leaving some elderly people in the countryside with limited support, while family responsibility for elder care remained strong. Son preference persisted in some households and, with ultrasound and abortion available in the 1980s, contributed to a sex ratio at birth that rose above the biological norm. By the late 1980s smaller households created new needs for housing, schooling, childcare, and, in the future, elder care, issues that would become more prominent in later decades.

Section 81.17 · Division V — Consumer Society, the New Middle Class, and the Social Consequences of Modernization

The New Middle Class: Office Workers, Professionals, Civil Servants, and Household Aspirations

As industry and government grew, so did occupations outside farming and manual labor: office workers, teachers, engineers, technicians, civil servants, managers, bank employees, nurses, doctors, and other educated groups. Expanded schooling supplied the people; large corporations, banks, public institutions, and universities supplied the positions. Salaried employment, once rare, became the aspiration of many families.

Stable wages, professional qualifications, apartment ownership, savings, and children's education came to define middle-class status. Households made deliberate decisions about marriage, residence, consumption, and schooling in pursuit of advancement. Working for a major corporation, bank, or government agency carried particular prestige and security, including benefits such as bonuses, housing assistance, and health coverage.

The new middle class was not uniform. Established property owners, including families who had held urban land or rural assets, differed from newly salaried households whose security depended on their jobs. Small business owners, shopkeepers, and self-employed workers belonged to a separate group, often described as the old middle class. Skilled industrial workers sometimes earned incomes close to those of white-collar employees but were socially distinguished by occupation. Many households with salaried members still rented, borrowed, and lived with limited savings.

Membership was often insecure. Housing prices outran salaries for many; the costs of education and housing consumed income; and layoffs or business failures could erase gains, risks that would become more visible in the later period examined in Chapter 84. Surveys in the 1980s showed that many South Koreans identified themselves as middle class, a self-identification that expressed aspiration as well as material position.

Class position during these decades cannot be identified through income alone. A civil servant or teacher might enjoy regular pay and occupational status but have little property, while a small merchant might hold valuable urban land despite uneven earnings. Apartment ownership supplied security and potential capital gains that a similar-salaried tenant could not necessarily acquire. Employment in a major corporation often offered welfare benefits and advancement channels absent from smaller firms. Education, occupational stability and housing tenure worked together to shape a household's prospects. Families therefore classified themselves in relation to a combination of schooling, savings, neighborhood and the future they expected for their children. The categories 'middle class' and 'working class' overlapped in lived experience and should not be treated as fixed census labels.

This social layer also played an important role in associational life. Teachers, professionals, religious participants and salaried parents helped sustain neighborhood activities, student support networks and, in some cases, civil-rights or environmental campaigns. But increased prosperity did not produce a uniform political attitude: some valued order, savings and property protection, while others opposed censorship or were troubled by housing speculation and educational inequality. Household anxieties remained acute because medical costs, mortgage or jeonse obligations, and examination expenses could absorb much of a salary. A strong account of the emerging middle class should therefore distinguish aggregate expansion in professional employment from individual economic security. In this sense, modern class formation involved aspirations and vulnerabilities as well as an enlarged consumer market.

Generational perspective is essential. Parents who had survived wartime displacement and rural poverty often equated a salaried job and a permanent apartment with extraordinary security; their children, increasingly educated in urban schools, might view the same standard as a starting point and aspire to professional positions or overseas study. Inheritance and housing ownership influenced whether the younger generation could reach those goals without starting from nothing. Household strategies could be cooperative—relatives pooled money for one child's schooling—or competitive when educational prestige and property values mattered. The distinction between upward mobility and the reproduction of advantage became especially visible as desirable schools and apartments concentrated in specific districts. Narratives of a single expanding middle class can obscure these divisions unless researchers look at property ownership, occupation, education and age together.

By the late 1980s, middle-class participation in civil society, through professional associations, churches, neighborhood groups, and consumer and environmental concerns, was changing the political climate. Teachers, journalists, lawyers, and office workers were more willing to voice grievances. This development is part of the background to democratization, but its political story belongs to Chapter 82. The central argument here is that the new middle class represented a real transformation in opportunities and identities, though it was unevenly distributed and often precarious.

Section 81.18 · Division V

Consumer Culture, Television, Department Stores, Leisure, and Popular Entertainment

Rising household incomes created consumer markets. Televisions, refrigerators, washing machines, fans, and later private automobiles entered homes, first among urban and higher-income households and then more widely. Domestic firms produced many of these goods, and the Hyundai Pony, launched in the mid-1970s, was an early Hyundai-developed mass-produced Korean passenger car, styled by Italian designer Giorgetto Giugiaro with Japanese-derived mechanical technology, though private car ownership remained limited until the late 1980s. Processed foods, ready-made clothing, and branded goods spread, and advertising taught households new expectations of comfort.

Retail changed. Department stores, such as Lotte's flagship opened in 1979 and later stores in Gangnam, offered fixed prices and displays that appealed to middle-class shoppers, while traditional markets, neighborhood shops, and street vendors continued to supply most daily needs. Fast-food and convenience-store chains began to appear in the 1980s. Consumption expressed status, family achievement, and gender roles, with women typically managing household purchases and advertising addressed to them, while young people formed distinct tastes in clothing and music.

Mass media expanded. Radio and newspapers reached wide audiences; television spread rapidly through the 1970s and 1980s, and color broadcasting began in December 1980. Dramas, news, and variety programs became shared national experiences. Cinema, popular music, and sports also expanded: professional baseball began in 1982 and professional football in 1983. Some critics have argued that the Chun government promoted sports, entertainment, and relaxation of social restrictions, such as the end of the nighttime curfew in 1982 and of school uniform and hair rules, partly to defuse political discontent, a claim that scholars debate.

Commercial growth coexisted with political control. In 1980 the government forced the merger and closure of many newspapers and broadcasters, and censorship governed film, broadcasting, and print. Journalists faced pressure, and critical expression was restricted. Consumers had a growing range of goods and entertainment but a restricted range of public speech. This coexistence is characteristic of the period and should not be overlooked.

The chronology of automobiles makes the interaction between Korean manufacturing and imported expertise clear. Hyundai's Pony was presented in 1974 and introduced for the domestic market in late 1975, with broader sales and exports beginning in 1976. It was a Hyundai-developed mass-produced Korean vehicle, but the exterior styling was commissioned from Italian designer Giorgetto Giugiaro and its development drew on Mitsubishi mechanical technology. It should not be described as wholly Korean-designed or technologically self-contained. For most urban families of the 1970s private car ownership remained an ambition rather than an everyday necessity, so the expansion of buses and subways mattered more immediately. The Pony's cultural symbolism nevertheless captured a changing national consumer horizon: domestic manufacturers increasingly supplied products once associated with wealthy foreign economies.

Television and retail changed the rhythm of the household. Color broadcasts beginning in 1980 added a new attraction to entertainment and advertising, while department stores offered displays, fixed prices and seasonal promotions that differed from personal bargaining in traditional markets. Yet shopping centers did not replace neighborhood vendors, and appliances diffused unevenly according to purchasing power, electricity, housing size and credit. Popular dramas, sports and music created common experiences across a nationally connected audience, even while censorship restricted the range of political interpretations allowed on air. Government promotion of recreation could serve political purposes, but consumers also brought their own tastes and desires to the market. The historian must therefore avoid treating entertainment as either pure state manipulation or unqualified freedom: mass culture developed through commercial expansion inside a constrained political environment.

Students should not project the global entertainment industry of the twenty-first century backward onto the 1980s. The mass culture of this period was largely domestic, shaped by censorship, state broadcasters, and imported American and Japanese influences, and it laid the commercial foundations for later developments examined in Chapters 88 through 90.

Section 81.19 · Division V

The 1988 Seoul Olympics: Urban Transformation, International Visibility, and Social Displacement

Seoul was awarded the 1988 Summer Olympics in September 1981, and the Games took place from 17 September to 2 October 1988, with participation from a large majority of the world's national Olympic committees. Preparations were already underway earlier, building on the 1986 Asian Games in Seoul and on the Han River development project of the early 1980s. They included sports complexes, in particular at Jamsil, new roads and the Olympic Expressway, subway extensions, hotels, telecommunications, and campaigns to beautify the city and educate residents about hosting visitors.

The Games displayed South Korea's industrial growth, large middle class, consumer culture, and urban infrastructure to a global audience, and they enhanced international recognition. Government agencies, construction firms, service workers, volunteers, merchants, athletes, and residents all took part. Many South Koreans took pride in the event, and it coincided with, though it did not cause, a period of political opening, treated in Chapter 82.

Modernization also had costs. Redevelopment of older districts accelerated in the 1980s, partly in connection with the Games and the Asian Games, through demolition of dilapidated neighborhoods, clearance of street vendors, and the relocation of poor residents. Joint redevelopment in places such as Sanggye-dong and Mokdong led to confrontations between residents and demolition crews. Tenants received limited compensation and often could not afford the new apartments. A contemporary Asian Coalition for Housing Rights investigation and subsequent scholarship cite an advocacy estimate of approximately 720,000 people affected by the wider Seoul redevelopment drive between 1983 and 1988; this is not a census-verified total, nor a count attributable solely to the Olympic Games. The figure depends on definitions of eviction, the period covered, and the redevelopment projects included.

Independent urban-history research documents a relationship between Olympic preparations and displacement, while emphasizing that demolition and redevelopment were part of a longer process of urban transformation that began earlier and continued afterward. Students should therefore avoid both extremes: the Olympics were not the sole cause of displacement, nor were they merely a national triumph. The same projects that improved infrastructure and international prestige imposed substantial costs on vulnerable communities.

Redevelopment research offers a way to quantify displacement without confusing different causes or populations. A contemporary 1989 Asian Coalition for Housing Rights report described evictions occurring on a very large scale in Seoul; later scholarship cites an advocacy estimate of approximately 720,000 people affected by redevelopment from 1983 to 1988. That is a reported estimate for a period and a set of redevelopment operations, not a census-confirmed count of people removed solely to build Olympic venues. The distinction matters because the joint redevelopment program had its own land and property incentives and continued beyond the Games. The Olympics nevertheless increased the urgency of urban presentation, transport projects and clearance, contributing to the pressure experienced in some neighborhoods. Students should identify who calculated any displacement figure and what counted as an eviction.

Sanggye-dong provides a more grounded view of this process. Some residents were themselves earlier migrants or people displaced by previous clearances, making the same household vulnerable more than once. Redevelopment threatened access to affordable rented rooms and everyday networks, while new buildings were frequently marketed to buyers with far greater resources. Tenant organizations, religious groups and housing advocates challenged compensation practices and the use of demolition contractors. Others welcomed new roads, sanitation, housing construction and international attention, illustrating why public support and resistance could coexist. Studies of the Olympics should examine both tangible infrastructure gains and the different rights of owners, tenants and informal residents. The social history of the Games is not completed by medal counts, visitor numbers or the televised appearance of a modern capital.

Contemporary witnesses also disagreed over what counted as adequate compensation. A landlord could value a replacement apartment, while a shopkeeper losing street frontage could lose customers even if a payment was offered. A tenant displaced from a central district might be unable to afford the new rent or the travel cost of continuing to work in the same area. Government accounts emphasized clearance of unsafe structures and improved urban order; housing-rights groups emphasized the threat of forced removal and the limited bargaining power of residents. Neither source category alone measures every household's experience. When teaching Olympic redevelopment, the strongest method is to pair a dated plan with a neighborhood-specific investigation and to specify the time period of any wider displacement statistic. That method avoids both celebration without social cost and the assumption that every project had the same effect.

The principal focus here is the changing city and its residents. Its political consequences, including the relationship between Olympic preparations and the 1987 democratic transition, belong to Chapter 82.

Section 81.20 · Division V

South Korean Society by 1989: Mobility, Prosperity, Inequality, and the Making of a Modern Nation

By 1989, industrialization had transformed employment, urban geography, education, housing, health, family structure, social mobility, and consumption. A society of farmers had become one of wage earners; a country of villages had become one of cities; a population with limited schooling had become one with widespread secondary and substantial university education; large families had given way to small ones; and health insurance and medical services reached nearly all residents. New occupational groups, including salaried employees, technicians, and professionals, had emerged.

Many groups contributed: rural migrants who moved to cities, factory workers who built the export economy, women who supported households and organized unions, teachers and engineers who staffed schools and plants, parents who invested in children, neighborhood organizations that supplied mutual aid, and civic groups that publicized grievances. The new society was made by their cumulative decisions and labor, not simply by policy.

Inequalities persisted. Cities outpaced rural areas, and Seoul and the southeast outpaced the southwest and mining regions. Men earned more than women and held more secure jobs. Large-firm employees fared better than those in small subcontracting firms. Housing and educational credentials increasingly determined household opportunity: homeowners in desirable districts gained wealth, while tenants and informal residents faced insecurity and displacement. Students should avoid treating national economic growth as an adequate description of every household's experience.

The experiences of workers, students, professionals, and middle-class families helped create a society increasingly capable of demanding institutional and political change. Workers entered the late 1980s with grievances and organizational experience; students and educated professionals had networks and expectations; middle-class households were concerned about rights, housing, and fairness; and women's and civic organizations were growing. These developments formed part of the background to the democratic transition examined in Chapter 82, though the political history of that transition requires its own account.

The transformation can be understood by comparing a set of household decisions rather than by placing all citizens on a single ladder of progress. A migrant factory worker weighed wages against rent and remittances; a small farmer chose whether to finance a child's move; a salaried family decided between a housing deposit and additional tutoring; a tenant faced the risk of demolition; an aging parent depended on children living far away. Each decision was shaped by national investment and policy, but also by gender, inherited assets, location and personal relationships. Such choices accumulated into larger patterns of urban concentration, smaller families, a more educated labor force and growing consumer demand. They also exposed people to different risks, even when average national indicators improved dramatically.

By the late 1980s the institutions of everyday life had become connected in new ways. A coveted school district could raise nearby housing prices; higher housing costs could increase the necessity of two incomes; industrial commuting shaped childcare; workplace injury or dismissal threatened the capacity to maintain a jeonse deposit or pay for private education. Social activism emerged within these interactions as residents organized against redevelopment and employees sought safer working conditions or representation. This chapter's contribution is to show the social foundations of that organizing rather than recite the political events of 1987, which Chapter 82 examines. Economic development had expanded opportunity, but the question of who controlled the benefits, whose work made them possible and who absorbed the risks remained unresolved at the end of the decade.

A final methodological distinction concerns evidence of success. Rising national income and life expectancy reveal important material changes but cannot establish whether a young garment worker was paid fairly, whether a tenant recovered a deposit or whether a rural daughter received the same educational support as her brother. Conversely, a memoir of hardship cannot prove that living standards failed to improve for everyone. Population statistics, family budgets, municipal plans, factory records and oral testimony answer different questions, and their limitations should be named rather than concealed. The most defensible history combines them to explain why prosperous new districts, poorly serviced settlements, universities, factories and older villages belonged to the same transformed society. The social changes of 1960–1989 formed an enduring legacy, but their long-term consequences belong to the chapters on later democracy and economic crisis.

By the end of the 1980s, South Korea was not merely an industrializing economy with a growing national income. It had become a fundamentally different society, with new forms of work, urban living, education, family life, consumption, and collective identity. Its achievements were substantial, but the unequal distribution of opportunity and security remained central to its social development.

Historical Debate

Did Industrialization Promote Broadly Shared Advancement?

The question: Did South Korea's industrialization produce broadly shared social advancement, or did its benefits depend strongly on class, region, gender, and government policy? Scholars differ on how to weigh rapid gains in income, schooling, and health against inequality, repression, and insecurity.

Interpretation A — Broadly shared gains: Some scholars emphasize that wages, schooling, health, and living standards improved for most households, that land reform and expanded education made opportunity unusually accessible, and that mobility was widespread.
Interpretation B — Gains structured by inequality: Others stress that benefits were distributed unequally by region, gender, firm size, and property ownership, that labor repression and weak enforcement held down wages and safety, and that housing and education reproduced class advantage.

Evidence: Both draw on census, wage, schooling, and housing data, together with labor records, surveys, and oral histories. They differ in which indicators they emphasize and in how they weigh the political context.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than basic facts, though some concerns, such as the extent of displacement or the effect of repression on wages, are matters of evidence.

Why it matters: It shapes whether development is remembered mainly as a shared achievement or as an unequal and coerced process, with consequences for later debates about the developmental state.

Student task: Construct the strongest case for each interpretation using this chapter's evidence, then identify what additional evidence, such as household income distribution over time or regional wage comparisons, could help decide between them.

Cause and Effect

How Employment, Migration, Planning, Education, Housing, and Family Decisions Reinforced One Another

Long-term causes

Land reform and small farms, population growth, a strong culture of educational investment, and export-oriented industrial policy.

Short-term causes

Factory expansion from the 1960s, new industrial complexes, school reforms, and subsidized infrastructure drew and shaped movement.

Immediate context

Authoritarian government controlled unions and the press, keeping wages low and public criticism limited.

Transformation

Migrants entered urban employment, funded schooling, sought housing, and reduced family size, producing a new urban society.

Immediate effects

Overcrowding, housing insecurity, educational competition, and worker organizing appeared alongside rising incomes.

Long-term consequences

A property- and credential-based class structure, a large urban middle class, and a mobilized population capable of demanding political change.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how South Korea's industrialization between about 1960 and 1989 transformed everyday life, social relationships, and class structure. Its central argument is that a predominantly rural society became an urban, industrial, educated, and consumer-oriented one through the cumulative decisions, labor, and aspirations of millions of ordinary people, and that this achievement was inseparable from persistent inequality.

Migration was the foundation. Households, not planners alone, decided who would leave farms, drawn by wages, schooling, and kin networks and pushed by small holdings and rural population growth. Seoul rose from about 2.4 million residents in 1960 to about 8.4 million in 1980, and industrial cities in the southeast grew around ports, steel mills, shipyards, and electronics and machinery complexes, while the southwest and mining regions developed differently. Saemaul Undong improved village infrastructure and living conditions with mixed voluntary participation and political pressure, yet the countryside continued to lose its young.

Factory work created an industrial working class. Conditions varied by industry, firm size, gender, and employment status, with long hours, low wages, weak enforcement of the Labor Standards Act, and workplace hazards. Women, especially young unmarried women from rural families, were central to export manufacturing, supporting parents and siblings while facing wage discrimination and the expectation that their work was temporary. They also organized, as in the Dongil Textile dispute, the early 1980s strikes, and the 1985 Guro solidarity strike.

The death of Jeon Tae-il in 1970 drew attention to conditions at Pyeonghwa Market and became a catalyst for activism, supported by churches, students, and journalists. Historians caution that labor activism had many roots. Under Chun Doo-hwan, unions faced renewed restriction, yet networks persisted, and by the late 1980s workers carried grievances and organizational experience into the political events treated in Chapter 82.

Housing was a central arena of inequality. Migrants lived in rented rooms and informal settlements, and jeonse served as a distinctive deposit-based tenure in a setting of scarce credit. Clearance, relocation, and redevelopment displaced poorer residents, while apartment complexes, above all in Gangnam, offered modern amenities and, for owners, wealth accumulation. Subways, expressways, utilities, and services expanded, but pollution, crowding, and unequal service followed. Neighborhoods, markets, and associations supplied the social organization of daily life.

Education was both a national development strategy and a household strategy. Middle-school entrance examinations were abolished between 1969 and 1971, and high-school equalization began in 1974, yet university competition, private tutoring, and hagwon spending continued. The 1980 tutoring ban was unevenly enforced. Educational credentials became tied to occupation, marriage, residence, and class reproduction.

Gender and family relations changed unevenly. Women gained schooling and paid work, but family law, household registration, and social expectations kept men's authority and women's unpaid care in place, with reforms in 1987 and 1989 only beginning to alter practice. Fertility fell from about six births per woman to about 1.7 by 1985 through the combined effects of family planning, urbanization, education, economic incentives, and parents' own choices, while health insurance reached universal coverage in 1989.

A new middle class of salaried employees, professionals, and homeowners emerged, together with consumer culture, television, department stores, and commercial leisure, all coexisting with censorship and media control. The 1988 Olympics displayed national achievement while redevelopment displaced low-income residents. By 1989 South Korea had become a fundamentally different society whose achievements were substantial and whose unequal distribution of opportunity and security remained central. That society, with its organized workers, educated professionals, and anxious middle-class households, forms the social background to Chapter 82.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the industrial transformation described in earlier chapters was a social process as well as an economic one. Growth in exports and production depended on the labor of migrants and women, the savings of households, the credential-seeking of families, and the willingness of tenants to bear insecurity. Policy created conditions, but people made the society that resulted.

It also shows how achievement and inequality grew together. The same decades that brought schooling, health coverage, and apartments to many households left others in cramped rooms, unsafe workshops, and neighborhoods marked for demolition. Housing and educational credentials, in particular, became mechanisms by which advantage was passed on, a pattern that would shape later South Korean society.

The chapter links forward to Chapter 82, where organized workers, students, professionals, and middle-class households entered political life, and to Chapter 84, where the security of middle-class households and the stability of employment would be tested. It also connects to Chapter 91, which examines educational competition, housing prices, and demographic change in later decades, while cautioning against reading those later conditions back into the 1980s.

Finally, the chapter offers a lesson about historical method. National statistics, official plans, and later commemorations all illuminate the period, but none captures the whole. Understanding South Korea's social transformation requires listening to the voices that surviving evidence often mutes, and recognizing that modernization was lived differently by farmers, factory workers, tenants, teachers, and apartment owners.
