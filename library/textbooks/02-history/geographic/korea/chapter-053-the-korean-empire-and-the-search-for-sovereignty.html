The Korean Empire and the Search for Sovereignty

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In October 1897, in a newly constructed round altar beside the palace he had made his own, a Korean king stepped into the ritual role of emperor. Within six years the government he led had surveyed farmland, opened a railway, run electric streetcars through Seoul, founded technical schools, built a new command structure for its army, signed a treaty of equals with Qing China, and sought international recognition of its neutrality. Within seven, its sovereignty would be gravely compromised. This chapter asks how both of those statements can be true.

The ceremony took place on 12 October 1897 at the Hwangudan, an altar to Heaven erected only weeks earlier on the site of a former guesthouse near Gyeongungung, the palace now known as Deoksugung. The man who performed it, Gojong, had ascended the Joseon throne in December 1863, while still a child, with government initially conducted under a regency in which his father, the Heungseon Daewongun, became the dominant figure. He had lived through the Imo Mutiny, the Gapsin Coup, the Donghak Peasant Revolution, a war fought on his territory between Qing China and Japan, the murder of his queen by Japanese-backed assailants, and a year of residence in the Russian legation. When he re-entered his own palace complex in February 1897, he did so as a monarch who had learned, at considerable cost, how thin the protection of a weak state could be. The proclamation of the Korean Empire (Daehan Jeguk) was his answer.

The title mattered because of the world in which it was claimed. In the 1890s, East Asian diplomacy was moving from a Sinocentric system, in which Joseon had been a tributary kingdom of the Qing while managing its own internal government, toward an international order of sovereign states defined by treaties, legations, and international law. The Treaty of Shimonoseki in 1895 had formally ended Qing claims over Korea, but it had not guaranteed that anyone else would respect Korean autonomy. By choosing the name Daehan, drawing on the ancient Samhan peoples discussed in Chapter 7 and in Chapter 1, and by adopting the imperial title previously reserved for Chinese, and in some contexts Japanese, sovereigns, Gojong claimed a rank equal to that of any other ruler. Historians debate how far this claim was ceremonial and how far it was a working program, and this chapter treats the proclamation as both.

The program that followed is usually called the Gwangmu Reforms, after the era name Gwangmu ("Bright Martial Power") that Gojong chose. It differed in character from the Gabo Reforms of 1894–1896 examined in Chapter 52. The Gabo cabinets had acted under heavy Japanese pressure and had often legislated by decree from above. The Gwangmu program was directed from the throne, drew on a vocabulary of "old foundations and new methods" (gubon sincham), and attempted to build the material and fiscal underpinnings of a state: a land survey and title system, a reorganized army, modern schools, hospitals, a railway, electric lighting, telegraph and telephone lines, and an enlarged imperial household economy. It was less a single set of reforms than a collection of initiatives of varying ambition, funding, and success.

Reform was also contested. Korea in these years produced its first sustained, public, urban political movement, the Independence Club, whose members shared Gojong's aim of national strengthening but not his understanding of how authority should be organized. In 1898 they held open assemblies at Jongno, the main commercial thoroughfare of Seoul, and proposed that the advisory council be partly elected. By the end of the year the movement had been suppressed, and in 1899 the Daehan Gukje, the empire's basic state code, declared the emperor's powers to be unlimited. How to weigh these two visions, and whether centralization strengthened or weakened Korea's chances of survival, remains one of the central historiographical questions of this period.

The chapter also insists on the limits of the evidence for what reform accomplished. Imperial decrees, ministry regulations, newspaper reports, and diplomatic correspondence document intentions and institutions well. They document implementation unevenly, and they say less about how farmers, tenants, porters, schoolgirls, and shop assistants experienced change. A streetcar ran in Seoul in 1899; that fact tells us about capital, concession contracts, and imperial patronage, and rather little about villages in the southern provinces. Part of the historian's task here is to hold the visible achievements of the Korean Empire and the unevenness of their reach in view together.

Finally, the chapter treats Korea as an actor in international politics and not only as an object of it. Korean officials negotiated a treaty with Qing China on equal terms, sent diplomats and exhibitors abroad, bargained with foreign companies over concessions, tried to administer disputed borderlands, and in January 1904 attempted to secure recognition of neutrality. Each of these was a deliberate choice among narrow options. The period covered runs from February 1897 to early 1904. The Russo-Japanese War, the 1905 protectorate, and annexation belong to Chapter 54, and this chapter ends at the threshold of those events, without assuming that they were bound to follow.

Students should read the chapter with one analytical distinction in mind: sovereignty as a legal claim, sovereignty as a diplomatic relationship, and sovereignty as practical capacity to administer, finance, and defend a state. The Korean Empire made rapid progress on the first, real progress on the second, and uneven progress on the third. The distance between them is the subject of this chapter.

Learning Objectives

  2. Distinguish the Gwangmu Reforms from the Gabo Reforms in agency, method, and objectives.

  4. Trace the stages of the 1898 popular assemblies, the Six Articles, the proposal for a partly elected Jungchuwon, and the suppression of the Independence Club.

  6. Assess the organization, aims, and limitations of the Wonsubu and the imperial military.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Korean Empire attempt to create a sovereign modern state, and why did its institutional and technological achievements fail to guarantee its independence?
  2. What did an imperial title add to Korea's position in an international system that had already recognized its independence on paper?
  3. Why did a government committed to national strengthening suppress a political movement that also sought national strengthening?
  4. How much of the Gwangmu program was Korean initiative, how much depended on foreign capital and expertise, and how can historians tell the difference?
  5. What does the difference between a decree and its implementation reveal about the capacity of the late Joseon and Korean Empire state?
  6. Who benefited from new infrastructure and institutions, and who experienced mainly new burdens or no change at all?
  7. How should historians treat documents such as Imperial Decree No. 41 and the neutrality declaration, whose wording is secure but whose later interpretation is contested?
  8. What choices were genuinely available to Korean policymakers between 1897 and 1904, and what limited them?
  9. How should the Gwangmu era be evaluated if historians avoid judging it solely by what happened after 1904?

Historical Context

By the end of Chapter 52, Joseon had passed through two years of reform administration shaped by Japanese pressure. The Gabo cabinets of 1894–1896 had abolished the civil-service examination system, formally ended legal distinctions of status, reorganized ministries, and introduced a new calendar and new administrative forms. These reforms had a real constituency among Korean officials, but they became politically toxic after the murder of Queen Min in October 1895 and the haircutting edict of December that provoked righteous-army uprisings in the provinces. In February 1896 Gojong escaped from the palace to the Russian legation, where he stayed for roughly a year and dismissed the reformist cabinet. Chapter 52 owns those events. This chapter takes them only as background: the king who returned in 1897 had regained a measure of initiative, but he governed a state whose fiscal base, army, and administrative reach were all weak.

The international environment was crowded. Japan had defeated Qing China in 1894–95 but had been forced by the Triple Intervention to give up the Liaodong Peninsula, which Russia then moved to dominate. Russia gained influence in Seoul in 1896 and 1897 through the king's presence in its legation and through military and financial advisers, but it was committed elsewhere in Manchuria. Britain, the United States, France, and Germany were also present, often as concession-seekers rather than as strategic patrons. In April 1898 Russia and Japan concluded the Rosen–Nishi agreement, in which each pledged not to interfere in Korea's internal affairs and Russia accepted Japanese commercial predominance there. This produced a temporary equilibrium, one that Korean policy tried to exploit.

Domestically, Joseon had inherited a land and tax system that was administratively fragmented and fiscally leaky. Government revenue relied on land taxes, tariffs administered by a customs service headed by the British official John McLeavy Brown, and income from various special sources. Land registers were old and unreliable, local officials and clerks held substantial discretion, and the royal household held lands and revenues separate from those of the ministries. Nineteenth-century unrest, discussed in Chapters 43–44 and 51, had left the countryside cautious toward fresh demands from the state.

Intellectually, the period saw a growth of Korean-language print, new schools, and a new vocabulary of "civilization and enlightenment" (munmyeong gaehwa), "independence," and "people" (min) as political actors. Missionary schools and hospitals, which had operated since the 1880s, formed part of this environment, as did the education of Korean students in Japan and the United States. The Independence Club, founded in 1896, transformed this vocabulary into practical politics.

Geographically, the empire's reach was uneven. Seoul, the port of Incheon, and the treaty ports of Busan and Wonsan were exposed to foreign commerce and technology. Large parts of the interior were not. The northern frontier along the Amnok (Yalu) and Duman (Tumen) rivers, where Koreans had been migrating into Manchurian lands, raised questions of citizenship and jurisdiction, and the eastern island of Ulleungdo had its own settlement and administration problems. These regional variations, introduced in Chapter 2, must remain visible throughout the chapter: "Korea" in 1900 was not simply Seoul.

Division I — From Royal Refuge to the Proclamation of Empire · Section 53.1

Gojong's Return from the Russian Legation and the Recovery of Royal Authority

Gojong's year in the Russian legation, which Chapter 52 narrates, had a double effect. It removed him from the control of the cabinet formed under Japanese influence, and it exposed the monarchy to the charge that a Korean king could be sheltered only by a foreign power. Even during his stay, officials and the growing circle of reformist writers pressed him to return. In the winter of 1896–97 the government prepared Gyeongungung, a smaller palace compound close to the legation quarter, as an alternative to Gyeongbokgung, where the queen had been murdered. On 20 February 1897 Gojong moved there.

The choice of site was practical and political. Gyeongungung lay next to the American, British, and Russian legations, which offered a degree of protection that Gyeongbokgung did not. It also kept the king near the diplomatic quarter in which his future maneuvering would take place. Gojong could not return to a world he had left; Queen Min's death meant that a major political partner of two decades was gone, and the court was now organized around the king himself, an expanding circle of advisers, and officials who owed their positions directly to him.

After his return Gojong rebuilt the government around the throne. He appointed ministers who had not been associated with the Gabo reformers, restored many court titles and offices, and relied on a mixture of conservative officials and men with some experience of foreign diplomacy. The cabinet system of 1895 was not abolished, but in practice policy was increasingly decided at court. At the same time the king did not simply repudiate all the Gabo measures. The abolition of the examination system, the new ministries, and the legal ending of status distinctions stayed on the books, and a number of Gabo-era officials continued to serve.

Gojong also moved to regain control of Korea's foreign relations, which Russia had to some degree directed in 1896. Russian military instructors and a Russian financial adviser were present during 1897, but the king sought to balance them against American, British, and Japanese interests. Historians have long debated how far this represented skilled diplomacy and how far improvisation; the most defensible description is that Gojong aimed to prevent any single power from dominating, while recognizing that Korea lacked the means to compel them. The political space for this approach depended on the temporary mutual restraint of Russia and Japan, which is why the rivalry between them, explored in Section 53.31, is central to the chapter.

The restoration of royal authority also depended on a symbolic argument. Many Korean officials and writers, including those later associated with the Independence Club, wanted the king to assert full independence in a form that foreigners could read. The Independence Gate, whose construction began in 1896 on the site of the old Yeongeunmun where Chinese envoys had been received, expressed this feeling. When petitions in the summer of 1897 urged Gojong to adopt the imperial title, they drew on a combination of motives: pride, a desire to end any lingering suggestion of subordination to China, and a belief that rank would strengthen Korea's diplomatic position. That pressure converged with the king's own inclinations, and in September 1897 the government began constructing the altar at which he would be enthroned.

Division I · Section 53.2

October 1897: The Proclamation of the Korean Empire

Between mid-1897 and October, a series of memorials from officials and scholars asked Gojong to take the title of emperor. Gojong declined several times in accordance with convention before accepting, a pattern familiar from Chinese and Korean court practice. On 12 October 1897 (according to the solar calendar then in use, introduced in 1896), he was enthroned at the Hwangudan. The following day he issued the proclamation confirming Daehan as the name of the new state and retaining the Gwangmu era name adopted in August. The distinction between the enthronement ceremony of 12 October and the formal proclamation and associated decrees of 13 October and adjacent days is worth keeping clear, because later accounts often compress them into a single date.

Several elements of the proclamation need explanation. The title hwangje (emperor) asserted equality with the emperors of China and Japan; the Korean court had addressed the Qing emperor as a superior in the tributary context, and Korea's own monarch had been "king" (wang). The claim was therefore a deliberate rejection of any hierarchy among them. The name Daehan, "Great Han," was chosen over Joseon in part to mark a break and in part to evoke the Samhan polities of the southern peninsula, as Chapter 1 explained, while also avoiding the associations of a dynastic name tied to a tributary past. The era name Gwangmu asserted calendrical independence but was not first selected in October: the court chose it in August 1897 and formally brought it into use on August 16, before the imperial enthronement. The previous era name, Geonyang, had been used since 1896. The October proclamation confirmed Gwangmu as the era name of the new empire, rather than introducing it for the first time.

Korean historical argument for the claim drew on tradition. Memorialists pointed to ancient states, Goguryeo among them, as having used their own titles and rites, and to the Samhan origins of the name Daehan. These precedents served to present the empire not as an imitation of Western or Japanese practice but as a recovery of an indigenous sovereign tradition. This does not mean the argument was purely antiquarian. The same officials who cited ancient precedent were also familiar with international law and with the fact that Japan, China, Russia, and the Western powers dealt with one another as empires. An imperial Korea could demand the diplomatic protocol of one.

Foreign reaction was cautious. Russia and Japan, whose mutual rivalry gave them an interest in not antagonizing Korea, accepted the change; the United States, Britain, and other powers recognized the new title in subsequent months and years. The Qing government, which had lost its claims by treaty in 1895 and had been a weak force in Korea since, did not contest the title in any effective way. Formal recognition was less than a guarantee, but it did establish that Korean sovereignty would be addressed in imperial terms. Domestically, the proclamation was received with a mixture of enthusiasm and reserve. Reformers welcomed the assertion of independence while some later argued that it enlarged the monarch's claims at the expense of the people. Conservative scholar-officials generally approved because it elevated the dynasty.

Primary Source Window

Gojong's Proclamation of the Korean Empire, October 1897

Creator: The court of Gojong, issued in the king's name through official channels · Date: 12–13 October 1897 · Type: Imperial edict recorded in the court and government record and reproduced in official gazettes

Context: The proclamation followed months of memorials urging the king to take the imperial title and completed the preparation of the Hwangudan altar. It was addressed both to the Korean population and, through diplomatic channels, to foreign governments.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation.

The edict presents the adoption of the imperial title as a response to the repeated requests of officials and people, rather than as the sovereign's own initiative. It affirms that the state's name is now Daehan and that the era name is Gwangmu, links the new title to Korea's ancient inheritance and to the state's independent standing, and calls on the realm to unite in the new era.

Analysis: The document shows how the court framed the change: as popular request, ancient precedent, and sovereign independence. The convention of first declining and then accepting reflects traditional ritual language. It also shows the dual audience, domestic and international.

Limitations: Edicts state official justifications, not motives. They cannot show how widely the title was welcomed, how far it changed administrative practice, or whether foreign governments regarded it as more than a courtesy.

Perspective: The court's. Missing are the voices of provincial communities, women, and the many officials who accepted the title without comment, and the private views of foreign diplomats, which survive in other archives.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the proclamation say about why the title was adopted?
  2. Who created it, and for which audiences?
  3. What purposes might the language of popular request and ancient precedent serve?
  4. What can historians learn about Korean ideas of sovereignty from it?
  5. What can they not establish from this source alone?
Division I · Section 53.3

Imperial Ritual, Symbols, and the Reconstruction of Sovereign Legitimacy

The Hwangudan was the centerpiece of the empire's ceremonial program. In Chinese and Korean tradition, sacrifices to Heaven were the prerogative of the emperor, so the construction of a round altar for this purpose was itself a statement. The site, close to Gyeongungung and to the foreign legations, made the ritual visible. Around it the government constructed a three-story octagonal pavilion (Hwanggungu), stone drums, and associated structures. Only parts survive today, notably the Hwanggungu and the stone drums, a point worth remembering when students encounter popular images of the site.

Other symbols reinforced the message. The ruler's dress, formerly that of a king, was modified to the imperial yellow robes and crown of an emperor. Seals and titles were reissued, the late queen was posthumously given the title of empress, and the crown prince, later Sunjong, was invested as imperial heir. The funeral of the empress, delayed to November 1897, became a major state ritual that tied dynastic memory to the new order. The national flag, the Taegukgi, adopted in the 1880s, was used in imperial ceremony, and in 1902 an imperial anthem was composed with the help of Franz Eckert, the German bandmaster serving the military. Photographs and portraits of the emperor were produced and circulated, a novel form of royal representation.

Ritual served distinct audiences. Toward Koreans, it affirmed continuity with dynastic tradition while announcing renewal; toward foreign powers, it communicated that Korea belonged among empires. These two purposes were not always in harmony. Some conservative Confucian scholars favored the title on traditional grounds but distrusted the adoption of foreign technologies and customs. Some reformers favored the technologies but regarded the elaborate ritual as an unproductive investment. The court responded by combining the two, sometimes in the same event: Western-style military bands accompanied traditional sacrifice.

Gyeongungung itself was transformed into an imperial center. Existing halls were rebuilt or repaired, new buildings in mixed styles were added during the following years, and the palace was surrounded by an expanding quarter of ministries and legations. The location of the new palace complex in Jeongdong, next to the diplomatic district, meant that the court was literally embedded in the international community of Seoul. This had defensive value, since a king surrounded by foreign legations was harder to remove, but it also tied the monarchy's security to the willingness of those powers to stay on its side, a dependency whose limits the events of 1904–1905 would reveal.

Historians disagree on how much weight ritual should receive. Some treat it as an expression of legitimate Korean state-building: an assertion of sovereignty in the vocabulary available to the age. Others view it as an expensive projection of authority that diverted resources from more pressing needs. A balanced assessment recognizes that, in the international society of the time, rank and ceremony carried real diplomatic weight, while also recognizing that symbolic assertion could not substitute for the revenues and military power examined in later sections.

Division I · Section 53.4

Gwangmu, State Strengthening, and the Meaning of Modernization

The Gwangmu era name gave its name to a program of state-strengthening measures that unfolded over roughly 1897–1904, though the era lasted until 1907. Korean historians commonly distinguish the longer era from the intensive reform period, which ended as the Russo-Japanese War approached. The program was not issued as a single plan; it emerged as a series of decrees, institutions, and ventures responding to opportunities and pressures. What gave it coherence was a shared principle summed up in the phrase gubon sincham: use the old as a foundation and the new as a reference.

Historical Vocabulary
Gwangmu and Gubon Sincham

Definition: Gwangmu was the era name of the Korean Empire (1897–1907), and "Gwangmu Reforms" denotes the state-strengthening policies of those years, especially up to 1904. Gubon sincham is a phrase, usually translated "old foundations, new references," that describes the principle of retaining existing institutions and values while selectively adopting new techniques.

Historical context: The formula distinguished the program from the more thoroughgoing institutional rebuilding of the Gabo era and reflected the monarchy's wish to modernize without disrupting the legitimacy of the dynasty.

Why it matters: It explains why Gwangmu policy combined imperial ritual and absolute monarchy with railways and electric light.

Potential misunderstanding: The phrase should not be read as a rejection of change or as a simple slogan of conservatism. Contemporaries used it to describe a program that included substantial technical and institutional innovation.

The program had four overlapping aims. The first was fiscal: to establish accurate knowledge of land and population so that the state could tax effectively. The second was military: to create forces loyal to the sovereign and capable of maintaining order. The third was economic and technological: to introduce railways, electricity, mining, and manufacturing that would make Korea productive and visible as a modern state. The fourth was diplomatic: to be treated as an equal, which required not only a title but also treaties, legations, participation in international organizations, and the ability to manage foreign concessions. Education, medicine, and communications supported all four.

Gojong's own role was central. Contemporary records show him intervening personally in appointments, financial arrangements, concession decisions, and the establishment of institutions such as the Hanseong Electric Company. His conception of leadership was that of a ruler who must unify the state's resources and direct them. This produced a pronounced concentration of decision-making that gave the program energy and also made it dependent on the emperor's attention and on court networks, a matter taken up in Section 53.5.

The program differed from the Gabo Reforms in three respects. In agency, the Gwangmu measures were driven by the throne and by officials who owed their positions to it, whereas the Gabo reforms had been framed under foreign pressure by a cabinet with limited royal support. In method, Gwangmu relied more on state-sponsored enterprises, foreign concession contracts, and imperial household resources than on abstract institutional redesign. In orientation, it placed greater emphasis on technological and infrastructural change than on legal-social restructuring. Still, the two programs overlapped in personnel and policies, and students should not treat the transition from one to the other as a clean break.

What Changed

  • Policy initiative shifted from cabinets operating under Japanese pressure to the emperor and his court.
  • The state's self-image shifted from a reforming kingdom to an empire claiming equal status.
  • Reform emphasis shifted from legal and administrative restructuring toward land records, infrastructure, military command, and state-sponsored enterprise.
  • Political participation changed: the Independence Club created a public political arena in 1896–1898, and it was suppressed by the end of 1898.

What Stayed the Same

  • The abolition of the examination system and of legal status distinctions remained in force.
  • Many ministries and some officials persisted across the transition.
  • The fiscal difficulty of an administratively fragmented state continued.
  • Foreign advisers and capital remained important to the government.

Pattern: Change was uneven and contested. Elite officials and urban residents experienced it most directly; rural populations experienced greater continuity, with mostly changed administrative procedures and tax demands.

Division II — Imperial Authority, Political Reform, and the Struggle over Representation · Section 53.5

The Emperor, State Council, Ministries, and Imperial Household

The institutions of the Korean Empire combined inherited and recently created offices. At the top stood the emperor, who after 1899 held legal authority over administration, legislation, the military, and diplomacy. Beneath him were the ministries created in 1895 and later adjusted, the State Council (Uijeongbu), which was restored as the central deliberative body, and the Privy Council (Jungchuwon), originally an advisory body created in the mid-1890s. Provincial government continued under governors and county magistrates, with the thirteen-province system of 1896 remaining in force.

Formal authority and actual influence did not coincide. Gojong preferred to rely on persons he trusted personally, including palace officials, court attendants, and officials in the Imperial Household Department (Gungnaebu), whose responsibilities steadily expanded. This created a layered system: ministers were responsible for departments, but important decisions might be made within the palace, often through informal consultation. Historians cannot always reconstruct how particular decisions were reached because informal court influence leaves fewer records than ministry regulations.

The imperial household became a major institution in its own right. It managed palace affairs, but also lands, mines, ginseng, enterprises, and revenues. Its growth was not unique to Korea; many monarchies kept household revenues distinct from state finances. In Korea during the Gwangmu years, however, the household's resources were enlarged in ways that blurred the boundary between the emperor's private means and public finance, a matter developed in Section 53.14.

Ministers varied in outlook. Some, trained in the 1880s and early 1890s in diplomacy or education, favored technical modernization; others were court conservatives who focused on ritual and dynastic stability. Turnover was frequent; the emperor's willingness to dismiss ministers reduced the ability of any official to build an independent power base, and it also meant that policy could change abruptly. Foreign advisers, including customs and legal advisers, held positions in various ministries, but their influence depended on the emperor's consent.

The result was a state with considerable executive energy and a limited system of internal checks. This was an advantage for launching initiatives quickly, as the streetcar and survey programs show. It was a disadvantage for sustained administration, since implementation depended on provincial officials and clerks who were less responsive to central direction than the court assumed. The tension between a powerful center and a leaky administrative apparatus, which dated back to nineteenth-century Joseon, remained unresolved.

Division II · Section 53.6

Seo Jae-pil, the Independence Club, and the Language of National Self-Strengthening

The Independence Club (Dongnip Hyeophoe) was founded in July 1896 by a group that included court-connected reformers and the returned exile Seo Jae-pil, known in the United States as Philip Jaisohn. Seo had participated in the 1884 Gapsin Coup as a young man, fled to Japan and then the United States, trained as a physician, and returned to Seoul in 1895 as an adviser to the government. In April 1896 he launched Dongnip Sinmun (The Independent), the first newspaper to be published in Hangeul, with an English-language edition. Its editorials urged independence, civil rights, education, and an end to deference toward foreign powers.

At its founding, the club included high officials, some of whom were aligned with the government. Its early activities were relatively modest: raising funds to convert the Yeongeunmun, where Chinese envoys had been received, into the Independence Gate, and refurbishing the nearby Mohwagwan guesthouse as an Independence Hall, where debates and lectures were held. These projects expressed a clearly nationalist but not antimonarchical message: they celebrated independence from China while praising the monarchy as its embodiment. Gojong himself contributed funds.

By 1897 the club was changing. Debates, held weekly, discussed practical subjects, from education to hygiene to constitutional government, and attracted students, merchants, junior officials, and others. Membership grew, and by 1898 the club had branches outside Seoul. Its language blended Confucian and new vocabularies: it spoke of the people's duty to the emperor and the nation, and also of the people's rights, legal equality, and the need for open discussion. This hybridity is significant. The club did not clearly distinguish between loyalty and criticism, and in practice its members interpreted the relationship between monarch and public differently.

Its politics developed in tension with the court. The club criticized officials it considered corrupt and opposed foreign concessions that seemed to compromise Korean control, while still supporting the monarchy. After Gojong proclaimed the empire in 1897, the club welcomed the step. Yet its emphasis on open debate and public petitioning introduced a new form of political action into a political culture in which petitioning the king had been the accepted channel but mass public assemblies had not. How the court would respond to this was an open question in 1897.

The evidence for the club's activities is relatively rich. Dongnip Sinmun and other newspapers reported meetings, and government records, diplomatic reports, and later memoirs add perspective. These sources have limitations: the newspaper was an advocacy organ with a particular perspective, and later memoirs sometimes projected subsequent nationalist ideas backward. Historians therefore use them alongside official records and foreign accounts.

Division II · Section 53.7

The Manmin Gongdonghoe and the Emergence of Public Political Participation

The Independence Club's turn to mass politics came in early 1898. In February and March, Russia pressed for rights including a coaling station at Busan's Jeolyeongdo and the establishment of a Russo-Korean Bank, while Russian military and financial advisers remained in Seoul. On 10 March 1898 the club held a mass public assembly at Jongno, the Manmin Gongdonghoe ("Joint Assembly of All the People"), attended by thousands according to contemporary reports, to demand that the government reject Russian demands and end its dependence on Russian advisers.

The speakers included members of the club and, notably, a butcher named Park Seong-chun, who according to accounts addressed the crowd and was cited afterward as a symbol of social inclusiveness. Participants included students, merchants, shopkeepers, officials, and members of various social groups. The meeting adopted a memorial and the government, in the following weeks, dismissed the Russian military instructors and financial adviser and ultimately closed the Russo-Korean Bank. Russia's strategic situation, including its preoccupation with Manchuria and its negotiations with Japan, was an important cause of this withdrawal. Korean sources and historians differ on how much credit the assembly deserves; the most reasonable interpretation is that it contributed to a result that diplomatic circumstances had also made possible.

The assembly was historically significant regardless. It showed that residents of Seoul could organize and assemble publicly in support of a political position, and it reflected the idea that the "people" as a body had a stake in the country's sovereignty. Merchants had a particular interest in resisting foreign monopolies and concessions, and students in the new schools identified with the nationalist message. Women's participation was limited, although a women's group, the Chanyanghoe, would petition for girls' education later that year.

Participation was more varied than a simple narrative of "the people against the government" suggests. Some officials joined; some supported the monarchy while opposing particular ministers; some in the crowd attended out of curiosity or for local reasons. The Independence Club's leaders were mostly educated urbanites with an interest in civic reform, while the broader crowd included lower-status urban residents for whom the issues were less direct. Historians therefore describe the movement as an urban phenomenon centered on Seoul, with weaker roots in the countryside.

Seo Jae-pil's position became increasingly difficult. Some conservative officials, and foreign diplomats, regarded him as an agitator. In the spring of 1898 the government, under pressure, arranged for him to leave Korea; he departed in May. Yun Chi-ho, an American-educated reformer, and other leaders carried the movement forward. The loss of its founder did not dampen its activity, which intensified in the autumn.

Division II · Section 53.8

The Government-People Assembly, the Six Articles, and Proposals for Representative Government

By the autumn of 1898, political tension had increased. Conflict between the club and conservative ministers over concessions and appointments, and an incident in September in which Gojong was reportedly poisoned with coffee, raised suspicion within the court. In October the club organized the Gwanmin Gongdonghoe, a "Joint Assembly of Officials and People," which met at Jongno over several days beginning October 28, with officials, club members, and members of the public. The assembly adopted a set of resolutions on October 29 known as the Six Articles (Heonui Yukjo) and sent them to the emperor.

Gojong, after some days, approved the articles in an edict, and in early November the government issued regulations for reorganizing the Jungchuwon into a body half of whose fifty members would be appointed by the government and half elected by the Independence Club. The plan was never fully implemented, but it marked a significant moment: the government proposed, however briefly, a legislative or consultative body with a substantial elected component.

It is important not to equate these proposals with modern parliamentary democracy. The envisioned Jungchuwon was advisory, not sovereign; members would have deliberated and given consent on specified matters within a monarchical order. Its electors would not have included the whole population, and the mechanism by which half the members would have been chosen by a particular association raised its own questions. Participants themselves described their aims in terms of accountability, public opinion, and cooperation between emperor and people, vocabulary that connected Confucian ideas of remonstrance with newer notions of public representation.

The political sequence matters because the reformers came closer to creating an institutional opening than a generic description of an assembly suggests. The meetings continued through the first days of November. The new Jungchuwon regulations were promulgated on November 4, and election of the proposed twenty-five popularly selected members was being prepared for November 5. The expression “popularly selected” needs qualification: the plan delegated the immediate election to the Independence Club rather than introducing a nationwide franchise. Even this limited proposal, however, changed the nature of advice to the throne. The council could become a place in which organized opinion was expressed through appointed and selected members, rather than simply an audience at which officeholders petitioned the emperor.

The proposed arrangement also left unresolved questions about legitimacy. Would members serve the emperor, the ministers, the club, or a wider public? Could a council influence fiscal decisions when imperial household revenues were handled separately? And would conservative officials accept its interventions in appointments and concessions? Such questions were not answered in the short interval between promulgation and repression. They help explain why political actors could use the same language of strengthening Korea while disagreeing sharply about the institutional means.

Division II · Section 53.9

The Suppression of the Independence Club and the Limits of Political Reform

The reform moment lasted only days. In early November 1898, anonymous placards appeared in Seoul claiming that the Independence Club intended to abolish the monarchy and establish a republic, with a named club figure as president. The authenticity and source of these accusations remain uncertain; historians generally regard them as a conservative provocation, and the club denied them. Gojong, already suspicious, authorized the arrest of seventeen club leaders in the November 4–5 crisis, and an initial dissolution order followed on November 5, aborting elections planned for the restructured Jungchuwon. The sequence is documented in contemporary materials, though some accounts date the decisions by different moments of announcement and enforcement.

The arrests provoked large demonstrations. Thousands of club members and supporters gathered at the Gyeongungung gates and in Jongno for days, demanding the release of the prisoners, and most were released. The conflict then escalated. The government mobilized the Hwangguk Hyeophoe (Imperial Association), an organization of peddlers and merchants backed by conservative officials, to counter the club, and violent clashes occurred in late November and December. By December 25, 1898, the government carried through the final suppression of the Independence Club and its assemblies. Some leaders were arrested, others fled, and the Jungchuwon reorganization plan was dropped.

Why did the court act this way? Several explanations operate together. The emperor viewed the club's proposals, particularly for a partly elected council and for ministerial consent to appointments, as incompatible with the imperial prerogative he had just asserted. Conservative officials, who were targets of club criticism, had direct incentive to portray the movement as dangerous. The rumor of republican plans, whether sincerely believed or exploited, gave the emperor a rationale for repression. The government was also sensitive to foreign diplomatic opinion, and some diplomats regarded mass assemblies as destabilizing. Finally, the emperor's experience of the 1884 coup, the 1895 murder of his wife, and other events had made him distrustful of organized political factions.

The suppression had consequences. It removed the most visible public forum for political discussion, and it affirmed that national strengthening would proceed under the throne's direction rather than through representative institutions. It also fed the later perception that Gojong's government regarded domestic reformers as a greater threat than foreign interests, a view that some historians consider unfair but that circulated widely. Reform-minded intellectuals, some of whom were imprisoned or exiled, did not disappear; many later participated in education, publishing, and patriotic enlightenment societies, discussed in Chapter 54 and later chapters. The question of why a government pursuing national strengthening restricted organized domestic participation is addressed in the debate in Section 53.10.

Division II · Section 53.10

The Daehan Gukje of 1899: Sovereignty and Imperial Absolutism

On 17 August 1899 the government promulgated the Daehan Gukje, usually translated "Grand Korean State Code" or similar. It consisted of nine short articles. Its first article declared the empire an independent state; its second described the imperial line as continuing for ten thousand generations; and the remaining articles set out the emperor's powers.

Students should distinguish a constitution in the modern sense from this document. A constitutional text typically allocates powers among institutions and sets limits on them. The Daehan Gukje defined the emperor's powers broadly and set no corresponding limits; it was, in effect, a legal statement of absolute monarchy. At the same time, it was also a declaration of sovereignty against external claims: the first article denied any subordination and the final articles assigned to the emperor the powers of war, treaty, and diplomacy that independence required. Its meaning is therefore double. Domestically, it defined the relationship between ruler and subjects; internationally, it declared that no other power could claim to direct Korea's internal or external policy.

Conflict between this code and the Six Articles reflects competing visions of strength. Reformers argued that a state in which officials were accountable, finances transparent, and public opinion consulted would mobilize more resources and legitimacy. The court argued that a divided and quarrelsome polity could not resist outside pressure and that unified command under the emperor was necessary. Both visions had internal logic, and each responded to real weaknesses.

Historical Debate

Did Imperial Centralization Strengthen or Weaken Korean Sovereignty?

The question: Did the concentration of authority in the emperor give the Korean Empire the coherence it needed to resist foreign pressure, or did it undermine the legitimacy and administrative effectiveness on which sovereignty depended?

Interpretation A — Centralization was a rational response to danger: Some historians argue that in a period of acute external threat and fragile institutions, concentrating authority allowed rapid initiatives such as the land survey, the military reorganization, and the introduction of electricity. They stress that proposals for representative bodies were also embryonic, and that the Independence Club itself had divisions and limited reach.
Interpretation B — Centralization narrowed the base of the state: Other historians argue that suppressing the Independence Club and legally enshrining unlimited authority deprived the empire of political support, transparency, and the cooperation of educated urban groups. They note that imperial household enterprises blurred public finance and that dependence on the emperor's attention made policy erratic.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same sources: imperial decrees, the Six Articles and Daehan Gukje, newspapers, financial records, and foreign diplomatic reports. They differ on the weight given to political legitimacy relative to administrative capacity.

Points of disagreement: Mainly interpretation and counterfactual reasoning. Whether a more representative government would have mobilized more resources, or been more vulnerable to factional conflict, is not directly answerable from the evidence.

Why it matters: The question bears on how historians judge the Korean Empire and on how later Korean political traditions drew on it.

Student task: Identify two types of evidence that would help assess whether centralization increased state capacity, and explain what each could and could not show.

The code is best read alongside the reform debates of the previous year, not as an isolated abstract proclamation. Its clauses placed the emperor at the center of legislation, appointments, military command, punishments, and foreign relations. This represented a choice about where the state’s authority should be gathered. The crown had experienced a foreign-backed cabinet, political assassination, and refuge in a legation; its determination to avoid divided executive control was grounded in real crises. But making imperial will the final source of law offered no independent institution capable of reviewing royal financial decisions or protecting political assemblies. The unresolved tension was not simply “tradition versus modernity”: both the code and the Independence Club invoked modern sovereignty while proposing different domestic distributions of power.

The code also leaves open a question that the later course of the empire makes particularly urgent. A ruler who formally controls war and treaty powers may be legally sovereign, but the exercise of those powers requires reliable information, effective ministries, revenue, and means of enforcement. The provisions did not themselves enlarge the tax base or secure outside military backing. The difference between legal attribution of authority and practical state capacity should therefore be carried forward into the chapters on finance, armed forces, and diplomacy.

Division III — Land, Finance, Property, and the Economic Foundations of Sovereignty · Section 53.11

The Gwangmu Land Survey and the Yangji Amun

Land taxation was the oldest foundation of the Joseon state, and by the late nineteenth century it was in disrepair. The last comprehensive survey had been conducted generations earlier. Registers listed plots that had changed hands, been subdivided, or reclaimed from waste; some land was hidden from the registers by owners or local clerks; and the measurement units used varied by region. The result was that the state did not know with confidence who owned or cultivated what, and revenue depended heavily on local negotiation. Reformers of different persuasions, including Gabo-era officials and later Gwangmu administrators, agreed that a new land survey was a precondition for effective government.

On July 6, 1898 the government established the Yangji Amun (Land Survey Office) under the Ministry of the Interior, soon placed under the emperor's direct authority and later transferred among agencies. The office began systematic work in 1899, initially surveying counties in Chungcheong Province and subsequently expanding to other regions. Survey teams, trained by officials and, from 1901, assisted by a foreign surveyor, the American engineer Raymond Krumm, measured plots, recorded boundaries, identified owners and cultivators, and compiled registers. The survey used modern surveying instruments and a standardized grading of land by productivity, intended to provide a uniform basis for taxation.

The survey's scope was substantial but incomplete, and two successive totals must not be confused. The Yangji Amun surveyed approximately 124 counties between 1899 and 1901. A severe crop failure interrupted the operation in late 1901; work resumed under reorganized institutions, and Korean historical research records a cumulative total of approximately 218 counties surveyed by 1903, or roughly two-thirds of the country's more than 330 counties. The first figure is an earlier phase, not 124 out of a total of 218 counties. These counts refer to counties in which surveying took place, not a direct measure of the land area accurately registered or the uniform completeness of each county's records. Work ended under the pressures surrounding the Russo-Japanese War. The outcome was a substantial but unfinished cadastral initiative, not a complete modern survey.

The survey met with difficulty. Local clerks and landowners who benefited from existing opacity had reasons to resist or manipulate the process. Measurement required labor, funds, and cooperation; surveyors had to be paid and monitored, and disputes over boundaries and ownership multiplied. Some contemporaries criticized the survey for placing burdens on farmers or for being conducted unevenly. Yet the survey also represented a genuine attempt to substitute documentation for customary negotiation as the basis of the state's relationship with land, and it was the largest administrative undertaking of the Gwangmu years.

Division III · Section 53.12

Landownership Certificates, the Jigye Amun, and Property Rights

A survey established plots; it did not by itself establish ownership. In October 1901 the government created the Jigye Amun (Land Title Office), which issued certificates, called jigye, recording the owner of surveyed land. The reform was intended to provide legal security for landholders, to make land transactions more reliable, and to link the survey to tax liability. The certificates were issued to owners identified through the survey, and the system applied initially to Seoul and certain areas where land sales were already active.

Definition: Yangji means "land measurement"; the Yangji Amun was the office that surveyed land. Jigye means a land certificate or deed; the Jigye Amun issued formal ownership certificates.

Property rights in Joseon had long been layered. A person might hold the right to cultivate, a different person the right to receive rent, and a third the state's claim to tax. Government, temple, palace, and lineage lands were additional categories. The jigye system tended to simplify this by recording one owner, which strengthened the position of those recorded and could weaken customary claims of cultivators. The consequences depended on who was recorded. Where an owner and cultivator were the same household, the certificate gave a documentary basis for transfer and security; where land was rented, the record could confirm the landlord's claim.

Legal modernization also raised problems the government could not fully resolve: overlapping claims where several people held documents, incomplete records in unsurveyed areas, and the inconsistent treatment of land that had been reclaimed or whose ownership was disputed. In addition, certificates created the possibility that land could be transferred more readily, including to foreigners in treaty-port areas, where foreign land ownership was restricted but sometimes achieved through proxies. The system was, therefore, both an assertion of state control over property and a source of new disputes.

Administrative categories shaped what the certificates could accomplish. In a landholding system with overlapping use rights, the person paying land tax was not always identical with the cultivator, and the cultivator was not necessarily the person entitled to collect rent. A new document could clarify some transactions while also privileging the claimant recognized by officials. The later transfer or reissue of certificates required procedures that depended on accessible offices and trustworthy records. Where surveys had not been completed, certificates could not simply substitute for missing evidence. Where an estate was contested, written registration could make the state an arbiter but could not by itself produce agreement among claimants.

The system thus touched sovereignty in a concrete way. By claiming the authority to define and certify private property, the empire strengthened its claim to regulate land transactions within its territory. Yet the initiative could be understood differently by landowners seeking secure documentation, tenants concerned about customary rights, and fiscal officials looking for a predictable tax base. These different stakes help explain why a technical-looking system of surveys and deeds was also a social reform with uneven effects.

Division III · Section 53.13

Taxation, Rural Administration, and the Limits of Fiscal Reform

The intended chain was clear: survey the land, certify ownership, and collect land tax from documented owners at a standard rate. The government had also moved, in the 1890s, toward collecting taxes in cash rather than in kind. In theory, the chain would reduce the opportunities for local clerks to inflate or skim from the tax and would increase central revenue.

In practice, the links were uneven. Because the survey covered only part of the country and was suspended in 1904, much of the revenue system continued to operate on older registers. Provincial governors and county magistrates, who were responsible for collection, were often paid irregularly and depended on local clerks, whose knowledge of land and households was indispensable. Clerks had incentives to preserve discretion. Where central officials demanded more, local administrators sometimes passed the burden to those least able to resist, such as smallholders without influence, while powerful households secured favorable assessments.

Costs of implementation were also significant. Surveying required funds for personnel, equipment, and travel, and revenue gains came only after surveys were completed and registers adopted. The government faced simultaneous demands from the military, the imperial household, and infrastructure projects, and therefore often borrowed against future revenue, issued coins of lower value, or turned to special levies. Contemporary newspapers and later scholarship record complaints about new taxes and about the debasement of the currency, which affected prices and fixed-income groups.

Evidence Comparison

Imperial Reform Decrees versus Administrative Implementation Records

Historians reconstruct the Gwangmu Reforms from two quite different kinds of evidence, and comparing them is essential.

Decrees and regulations state what the government intended. They are well preserved, typically clear in their objectives, and written for a general audience of officials. They show policy design, the vocabulary of reform, and the central government's priorities. They cannot show whether instructions were carried out.

Implementation records, including survey registers, county reports, petitions, accounts, and local complaints, show what happened or failed to happen. They are fragmentary and uneven in survival, written by local officials with their own interests, and often reflect compliance more than practice. Petitions and complaints preserve voices of farmers and local elites, but selectively.

What the comparison shows: Where decrees proclaim uniform nationwide change, implementation records show regional variation and delay. Where decrees portray a rational procedure, local records show negotiation and conflict. Neither source is reliable alone.

A more complete register was only the beginning of fiscal reform. Officials needed to determine which parcels bore which assessments, identify responsible taxpayers, transmit receipts to the center, and limit the deductions retained by local collectors. The government also had to decide how to handle arrears, land whose ownership was disputed, and revenue historically assigned to specialized public or royal agencies. Each decision affected established interests. Surveying might reveal formerly hidden land, but it could also provoke resistance from households that regarded the new measurement as a prelude to higher demands. A tax administration that could measure land without securing fair collection would achieve little of its promised improvement.

A central difficulty was the coexistence of a Finance Ministry claiming oversight of public revenue with an imperial household that increasingly managed its own lands, mines, rents, and concession income. The separation originally established in the Gabo period had not eliminated competition between these sources of authority. The central government could gain stronger documentation yet still have less discretionary income than reformers hoped. For that reason the success of fiscal modernization must be measured separately in administrative capacity, the stability of revenues, taxpayer burdens, and the ability to finance agreed public services.

Division III · Section 53.14

The Imperial Household, Naejangwon, and the Problem of Government Finance

In most monarchies, a ruler's household had separate revenues. In Korea during the Gwangmu years, the scale and scope of these revenues grew, and the imperial household became a major economic actor. The Naejangwon, an agency of the household, administered lands, mines, fisheries, and other assets, and during the late 1890s and 1900s additional categories of income were placed under household management, including ginseng, certain mines, and other revenue sources. Officials such as Yi Yong-ik, who rose to prominence in the household administration, played central roles.

Arguments for enlarging household resources were practical. The Finance Ministry's revenues were limited and subject to claims by ministries and foreign creditors, while the emperor needed funds for ceremony, palace reconstruction, military expenses, and ventures he wished to sponsor, such as the Hanseong Electric Company. A household with its own income could act quickly and independently of the ministerial bureaucracy and of the customs service, whose revenues were overseen by a foreign adviser.

The result, however, was a dual financial structure. Household and ministry competed for revenue sources, and the boundary between the emperor's private means and the state's public finance was porous. Funds for state purposes sometimes came from household resources and vice versa, and accounting was less transparent than the Independence Club's third article had demanded. Contemporary critics and later historians have noted that this arrangement made national finance difficult to plan, supervise, or reform. The coinage policy, including issues of new copper and nickel coins from the central mint, also drew criticism for contributing to inflation, although scholars differ in how much blame to attach to which decisions.

Strengthening the emperor's financial position therefore did not necessarily strengthen the state's fiscal capacity. It increased the monarch's room for maneuver, which mattered for rapid initiatives. It also reduced the visibility of expenditures and created channels for patronage and, according to critics, corruption. Foreign observers and Korean reformers alike remarked on the difficulty of ascertaining the government's true financial position. This was one of the structural limits on Gwangmu reform: it could mobilize resources for specific projects but struggled to create a stable national fiscal system.

Division III · Section 53.15

Farmers, Landlords, Markets, and the Uneven Effects of Reform

Most Koreans in 1900 lived in rural communities, and their experience of the Gwangmu years differed sharply from that of Seoul residents. Smallholders, tenants, laborers, and landlords all worked within an economy shaped by rice and other crops, local markets, and obligations of rent and tax. The land survey and certificate system affected them according to their position.

Landowners, especially large landlords with influence, tended to benefit from documentary recognition of their holdings, though some resisted measurement that might reveal hidden land. Small owner-cultivators gained a document that could protect their claim, but they also faced surveyors' fees, local demands, and the risk of being undercut by neighbors with better connections. Tenants, whose rights were customary, often appear least in the records. Rent levels in much of Korea were high, and nothing in the survey changed the underlying relationship between landlord and tenant.

Commercialization continued, particularly in rice, where exports to Japan through treaty ports such as Incheon and Busan expanded in the 1890s. Rising export demand benefited some landlords and merchants; it also contributed to price fluctuations that could harm consumers and poorer farmers in years of shortage. In the late 1890s harvest failures and food shortages affected parts of the country, and prices rose; historians must be cautious in attributing particular famines or price movements to a single cause, since weather, trade, and currency policy interacted.

Regional variation was marked. Areas near Seoul and the ports were more exposed to commerce, survey activity, and foreign influence. The south and southwest, with productive rice land, had strong landlord classes. The northern and eastern mountainous provinces had different patterns of settlement and economy, including slash-and-burn agriculture and, in border areas, migration across the rivers. The evidence for these varied experiences is incomplete, relying mainly on administrative reports, newspaper accounts, and petitions, and historians differ about whether the Gwangmu years improved or worsened rural security. A defensible conclusion is that reform altered the legal and administrative setting for some rural households, with benefits distributed unevenly, and that for many the most noticeable changes were new financial demands rather than visible improvements.

Division III · Section 53.16

Industrial Promotion, Mining, Manufacturing, and Commercial Enterprise

The Gwangmu government sought not only revenue but productive capacity. It established or encouraged enterprises in mining, textile weaving, glass, paper, and other fields, as well as commercial companies and banks. Korean entrepreneurs and officials formed joint-stock companies, and the government granted charters and sometimes capital. In 1897 the Hanseong Bank was established, and others followed, including the Daehan Cheon'il Bank in 1899, as Korean-owned financial institutions emerging alongside branches of Japanese banks.

The government also created institutions to promote technical development, including a Ministry of Agriculture, Commerce, and Industry, and technical schools, discussed in Section 53.19. Textile and weaving workshops in Seoul trained workers in machine weaving, and some enterprises were connected to the imperial household. Mining was a priority because gold and other minerals promised revenue; the government retained some mines directly while granting others, notably the Unsan gold mine, to foreign firms, an issue explored in Section 53.29.

Policy aspirations must be distinguished from output. The government declared goals of making Korea economically self-sufficient, but many enterprises were small, short-lived, undercapitalized, or dependent on imperial patronage. Competition from imported manufactured goods, particularly cotton textiles from Japan and Britain, constrained domestic producers. Capital was scarce, technical skills limited, and managerial accountability inconsistent. Some enterprises were successful on a modest scale; others failed. Historians therefore describe the period as one of important experimentation rather than sustained industrialization. They also note that Korean entrepreneurship, including among merchants and officials who invested in new ventures, laid foundations later drawn on in colonial-era business, discussed in Chapter 58.

The distinction among state investment, private enterprise, and foreign ownership matters for interpretation. Where the state financed ventures, it carried the risk and any benefit; where private Korean investors did so, the benefits could be uneven; where foreign firms held concessions, profits and control largely left the country. Judging the government's industrial policy requires asking which type of enterprise is under discussion and what happened to its returns.

The institutions behind industrial promotion were as important as the list of projects. A mine required secure rights, technical personnel, transport, working capital, and access to buyers; a workshop required machinery and reliable inputs; a commercial enterprise required enforceable contracts and accounts. State sponsorship might assist with a concession or import equipment, but that did not ensure sustained production. In many enterprises Korean officials negotiated with foreign engineers, financiers, and merchants whose objectives were commercial profit rather than the empire’s strategic independence. The state therefore faced a recurring choice between retaining control and securing the expertise or funds needed to proceed.

Nor did these initiatives affect all places equally. Mines and ports could link particular regions to international trade, while peasants elsewhere might experience modernization primarily through new registration or revenue procedures. Industrial capacity could not be inferred from the existence of a company charter. To evaluate the record responsibly, students should distinguish ventures merely proposed, ventures launched, those that remained in operation, and enterprises that developed a durable Korean workforce or productive base.

Division IV — Armed Forces, Education, Knowledge, and Public Institutions · Section 53.17

The Wonsubu and the Reorganization of Imperial Military Command

The army inherited by the Korean Empire had a troubled history. The Imo Mutiny of 1882 had begun among unpaid soldiers; the 1895 and 1896 events had shown that palace guards could be overwhelmed or divided; and the Japanese-trained units of the Gabo period had been dissolved or reorganized. Gojong wanted an army loyal to him that could secure the capital and, in principle, defend the state.

Historical Vocabulary
Wonsubu

Definition: The Wonsubu ("Marshal Headquarters"), established in June 1899, was the supreme command organ of the imperial armed forces, with the emperor as generalissimo (daewonsu) and the crown prince as marshal.

Historical context: It was created under the framework of the Daehan Gukje, which made the emperor commander of the military.

Why it matters: It institutionalized the emperor's personal command and reduced the role of the Military Ministry in policymaking.

Potential misunderstanding: Symbolic command did not equal operational capacity; the Wonsubu's authority on paper exceeded the army's capability.

In June 1899 the government established the Wonsubu. The emperor became supreme commander, and the headquarters took control of command, appointments, and planning, while the Military Ministry handled administrative matters such as budgets and supply. The arrangement reflected the principle of imperial command in the Daehan Gukje and resembled models of monarchical command seen elsewhere, including in Japan and Germany, whose officers Korean officials had observed.

The reorganization was intended to produce an army whose loyalty was to the sovereign state and its ruler, rather than to factions. It also reflected a perceived need: the previous decade had shown that a weak military meant exposure to coups, mutinies, and foreign pressure. Centralization of command created a clearer line of authority, but it also placed military affairs under the same personalized style of decision-making that characterized other areas of Gwangmu government, meaning that appointments and promotions might depend on court connections as much as on professional training.

The organization also shifted the balance inside the state. Under the rules established in June 1899, the Wonsubu exercised the authority to issue military orders and supervise forces under the emperor, while the regular Military Ministry increasingly handled administrative functions. Subsequent regulations reduced the ministry’s independent powers further. A modernizing army was therefore not simply added to the existing bureaucracy: the military chain of command was reorganized to concentrate loyalty and decision-making at court. The emperor and crown prince occupied the summit of this hierarchy, and key senior officers owed their position to imperial confidence.

Centralized command could theoretically improve coordination. It also carried political costs. When military promotions, procurement, and orders passed through a small imperial circle, rival institutions had fewer means to question expenses or assess preparedness. The reform illustrates a major theme of the Gwangmu program: expanding specialized state institutions while ensuring that the emperor retained direct control over their strategic decisions. Whether this produced a stronger army depends not only on the organizational chart but on training, supply, discipline, and available funds.

Division IV · Section 53.18

Training, Recruitment, Weapons, Fortifications, and Military Limitations

The Korean Empire's armed forces consisted of palace and capital guard units, regular infantry and other forces stationed in Seoul, and provincial garrisons. The government sought to expand and modernize them. A military academy trained officers; Russian instructors were present until their dismissal in 1898; thereafter Korean officers and some foreign advisers, including a German bandmaster for ceremonial music, continued the work. A conscription law was discussed and some recruitment reforms attempted, but the army remained a small, mostly volunteer and appointed force.

Military expenditure was a large part of the national budget. Estimates vary, but contemporary and later sources agree that spending on the army and palace guards consumed a substantial share of expenditure, a burden on a limited revenue base. Funds were spent on salaries, uniforms, rifles, artillery, and barracks; the government purchased weapons from foreign suppliers and attempted to establish an arsenal for domestic production, but domestic capacity remained small.

Authorized strength should not be confused with effective capability. Official troop numbers existed on paper, but pay arrears, uneven training, limited ammunition, and inadequate logistics reduced readiness. The army was suited to protecting the capital and suppressing local disturbances, as in the Bandit and Donghak remnants of the late 1890s, and it was not designed or equipped to resist the regular forces of Russia or Japan. Fortifications, including those planned for strategic points, were limited. Foreign observers, such as military attaches, typically regarded Korean forces as insufficient for external defense.

This did not make military reform pointless. The regular army secured the capital and gave the monarchy independent coercive means, and military ceremony contributed to the empire's presentation of sovereignty. But the gap between the army's size and cost and its capacity for external defense illustrates the broader gap between sovereignty as legal claim and sovereignty as practical power. In the absence of a credible military, Korean security depended on the diplomatic balance among foreign powers rather than on its own forces, which is why neutrality diplomacy, discussed in Section 53.32, became the government's main strategy.

Contemporary administrative measures reveal both ambition and uneven realization. The empire maintained central guard and line units, provincial detachments, military schools, and a widening assortment of specialized services. Officials discussed the purchase of horses, field artillery, and imported firearms, while decrees envisaged expansion beyond units already able to train and serve. A conscription ordinance was promulgated in 1903, but promulgation should not be mistaken for the existence of a fully functioning mass conscript army. Recruitment still required an effective census, procedures to select men, trained officers, barracks, equipment, and means to pay soldiers regularly. These requirements were costly even for states with stronger tax systems.

Military development also had two purposes that could pull against one another. The emperor wanted forces capable of defending Korea from outside pressure, but he also needed reliable troops to guard the court and maintain public order after the unrest of the 1890s. Concentrating capable men around Seoul could improve palace security without creating an army able to defend distant ports and borderlands. Students should accordingly compare the units authorized by law, those actually organized, and the military objectives the state could realistically support.

Division IV · Section 53.19

Modern Education, Technical Training, and the Formation of Skilled Personnel

Modern education in Korea had begun before the empire, with the Gabo government's 1895 education reforms, the establishment of a normal school and primary schools, and the pioneering work of missionary institutions such as Paejae and Ewha. The Gwangmu government built on this foundation while emphasizing technical and practical training for state needs.

Several institutions illustrate the pattern. The foreign-language schools trained interpreters and diplomats in English, Japanese, French, Russian, German, and Chinese. A commercial and industrial school, founded in 1899, trained students for commerce and technical occupations, and a medical school was established in 1899. A middle school opened in 1900 to provide secondary education. Mining, telegraph, and other technical training were also offered. Primary school attendance grew, though much remained limited to Seoul and larger towns, and the majority of Korean children, especially girls and rural children, had little access.

Private and missionary schools were important parallel actors. Christian missions ran schools that taught Korean, English, mathematics, and science to boys and girls, and some Korean-run private schools founded by reformers and officials joined them. In this way education was not solely a state project; it was a field of competition among the state, missions, and private initiatives, each with different aims.

The stated purpose of Gwangmu education was to produce technically trained officials and specialists who could operate a modern state. Graduates entered government offices, interpreter positions, telegraph services, and commerce. But numbers were small relative to the need, and funding, teachers, and textbooks were limited. Education also had political implications: students were among the most active participants in the 1898 assemblies, and the knowledge they acquired shaped expectations of the state. Education therefore both served and challenged the empire, a tension that would continue into the 1900s.

The educational program encountered a shortage of teachers, books, and students able to continue their studies without leaving needed household labor. The existence of a school ordinance did not guarantee a functioning classroom in every district, and schools established by the state, missionaries, or private reformers had different curricula and social constituencies. Technical training was especially demanding: knowledge of a foreign language was useful for translating manuals, but managing a mine, repairing a telegraph line, or keeping public accounts also required practical instruction and repeated experience.

Education carried competing political meanings. Gojong’s government hoped to train officials and skilled personnel for a stronger imperial state, while independent reformers connected literacy to public discussion and civic participation. Students sometimes entered demonstrations or newspaper circles, and the court viewed educational institutions as both sources of needed expertise and possible sites of opposition. The educational transformation therefore belongs to political and social history as well as to the history of schools.

Division IV · Section 53.20

Newspapers, Hangeul, Public Discussion, and the Changing Intellectual World

The late 1890s saw the first flowering of a Korean-language press. Dongnip Sinmun began in 1896 and ran until 1899, published in Hangeul and English. In 1898 several others appeared: Maeil Sinmun, a daily; Jeguk Sinmun (The Imperial Newspaper), founded by Yi Jong-il and published in Hangeul; and Hwangseong Sinmun, established by Nam Gung-eok and others and published in mixed Chinese characters and Hangeul. The Hangeul press reached a broader readership than the Chinese-character literary tradition and promoted literacy in the vernacular, which Chapter 27 identified as a long-term development since the fifteenth century.

Papers differed in audience and politics. Dongnip Sinmun was associated with the reformist circle. Jeguk Sinmun addressed wider and often popular readers in plain language. Hwangseong Sinmun, using mixed script, targeted educated readers and, though supportive of independence, was often more attuned to scholarly and official audiences. Their editorials discussed independence, civilization, education, foreign concessions, and the duties of officials and citizens. They also carried news from abroad, commercial advertisements, and letters from readers.

The press helped circulate concepts that shaped political discussion: that the people (baeksung or min) were part of the nation; that independence was a matter for all Koreans; and that civilization involved education, hygiene, and public responsibility. Translation of foreign works, including histories and textbooks, introduced new frameworks. Public lectures and debates, particularly those of the Independence Club, complemented print.

The press also faced restrictions. After the suppression of the Independence Club, the government placed limits on newspapers, and foreign-linked papers had somewhat more protection than others. Circulation was small by modern standards and concentrated in Seoul and treaty ports, though papers were read aloud in groups, extending their reach beyond the literate. Evidence for readership is therefore indirect, and historians must avoid assuming that newspaper opinion represented the views of all Koreans.

Division IV · Section 53.21

Hospitals, Medicine, Hygiene, and the Expansion of Public Services

Western-style medicine had arrived in Korea in 1885 with the establishment of Gwanghyewon, soon renamed Jejungwon, a government hospital operated with the American missionary physician Horace Allen. Over the following years, missionary hospitals and clinics operated in Seoul and other cities, while the government developed its own institutions. In 1899 a medical school was founded, and in 1900 the government hospital, reorganized from earlier bodies, emerged as the Gwangjewon, which provided Western medical care and served as a clinical training site.

Public health was a growing concern. The government and municipal authorities, with foreign advice, attempted to address sanitation in Seoul, including street cleaning and drainage, and responded to epidemic disease, notably cholera, which had struck repeatedly since the 1880s. Quarantine measures and vaccination campaigns, particularly for smallpox, were introduced in various forms. These efforts expanded the idea that the state bore responsibility for public health, a conception that grew in later decades.

The reach of these services was narrow. Hospitals concentrated in Seoul and a few cities, and most Koreans continued to rely on traditional medicine, herbalists, shamans, and household remedies. Fees, distance, and cultural unfamiliarity limited access. Missionary medicine, including women's hospitals that provided care for women who might not see male physicians, was particularly significant because it reached groups otherwise underserved. Yet in a country where most people lived in villages, the new hospitals had little direct impact outside privileged urban groups.

Historians therefore treat medical developments as meaningful institution-building with limited immediate social reach. They also note the interplay of state and missionary action, and the ways in which Korean practitioners and patients adapted, resisted, or combined Western and traditional medicine. These developments contributed to the empire's claim to be a modern state while illustrating, again, that state capacity extended furthest in the capital.

The development of Gwangjewon shows the value of distinguishing institutional continuity from a simple founding date. An Interior Ministry hospital had been established under regulations of 1899. In 1900 that institution was reorganized, briefly bearing the name Bosiwon before becoming Gwangjewon. Its remit included care for patients beyond the palace, with provisions for infectious illness and prisoners, and its practice retained traditional Korean therapeutic arrangements alongside newer medical administration. The transition was not an instantaneous replacement of indigenous medicine by Western medicine. Instead, officials and practitioners experimented with overlapping practices inside a changing government institution.

Public-health reform also depended on regulatory and fiscal capacity. A capital hospital might treat patients, train personnel, or provide evidence of changing ideas about contagious disease, but it could not by itself create sanitation infrastructure across the country. Local officials needed information, supplies, and cooperation from the population; many households continued to rely on familiar healers and remedies. The resulting system was plural and uneven, a useful reminder that a new state agency may be historically important even when most residents rarely encounter its services.

Division V — Railways, Electricity, Communication, and the Transformation of Seoul · Section 53.22

The Gyeongin Railway and the Beginning of Modern Railroad Transportation

In 1896 the Korean government granted the American entrepreneur James R. Morse a concession to build a railway between Seoul and the port of Incheon (then also called Jemulpo). Morse lacked the capital to complete it, and in 1898 he sold his rights to a Japanese syndicate that formed the Gyeongin Railway Company. The railway's first section, from Noryangjin on the south bank of the Han River to Incheon, opened in September 1899. The Han River bridge was completed in 1900, which extended service to the Seodaemun station inside the capital's walls.

The line of roughly thirty-odd kilometers shortened a journey that had previously taken a day by road or longer by boat, though students should note that precise travel times depended on conditions and service. Passengers included merchants, officials, foreigners, and increasingly Korean travelers; freight included rice, goods imported through Incheon, and construction materials. The railway thus linked the capital to its main treaty port and facilitated commerce, while also giving the operating company, and Japan, an important stake in Korean transport.

The Gyeongin Railway was the first of a wider railway-building program. Concessions had been granted for a line from Seoul to Uiju on the northwest border (the Gyeongui line), originally to a French company and later pursued by the Korean government itself, and for a line from Seoul to Busan (the Gyeongbu line), granted to Japanese interests in 1898, with construction starting in 1901. The government's own Railway Bureau attempted to build parts of the northwest line, but lack of capital limited its progress. Railways raised questions of ownership and strategy: lines connecting the capital to the southern port and the northern frontier had obvious military value, and foreign control of them meant foreign leverage. This chapter treats only the planning and early construction of these lines; their completion and wartime use belong to Chapter 54.

The railway illustrates the dilemma of Gwangmu modernization. Korea wanted modern transportation but lacked the capital to build it; foreign companies offered capital but expected control. The government sought to retain as much authority as it could, through concession terms, a Korean railway bureau, and attempts to build with domestic funds, but the outcomes were mixed. Evaluating it requires distinguishing Korean initiative in seeking railways from the ownership and operating arrangements through which they were actually built.

Division V · Section 53.23

The Hanseong Electric Company, Streetcars, and Electric Lighting

Electric light had appeared in Korea in 1887, when lamps were installed at Gyeongbokgung, but this was a palace novelty. Gojong's interest in electricity as a public and commercial technology led, in 1898, to the establishment of the Hanseong Electric Company, with the backing of the court and the technical and commercial involvement of two Americans, Henry Collbran and Harry Bostwick. The company was associated with imperial household interests, an example of the household's entrepreneurial role discussed in Section 53.14.

The company's first major project was an electric streetcar line, which began operating in May 1899 between Seodaemun and Cheongnyangni via Jongno and Dongdaemun, with a power plant near Dongdaemun. The streetcars carried passengers along the capital's central axis, and their novelty drew crowds. Contemporary accounts record early difficulties, including an accident soon after the line opened in which a child was killed, and an angry crowd damaged or burned a streetcar. Such reports indicate that the new technology provoked anxiety and resentment, as well as curiosity, though students should treat dramatic details as drawn from newspaper and later narrative sources.

Street lighting followed. In April 1900, three electric streetlights were installed in Jongno, which Korean historical research records as the first public electric street illumination. Over the following years electric service extended to government offices, the palace, foreign legations, large shops, and some private homes, as the company expanded its output. The evidence from company records and newspaper accounts shows that access was concentrated among institutions, merchants, and districts that could pay, and that for most Seoul residents, and nearly all rural residents, oil lamps and candles remained the normal sources of light.

Interpretation should distinguish deployment from universal access. Electricity and streetcars made Seoul a visible example of the Korean Empire's technological ambitions, and they symbolized the emperor's patronage. They also operated under a foreign-managed concession, and profits were shared with foreign partners. The streetcar fare was affordable for some residents and not others, and the vehicles served a limited route. They are best understood as evidence of what the empire sought to show and of what a concentrated urban investment could achieve, not as evidence of general prosperity.

Division V · Section 53.24

Telegraphs, Telephones, Postal Services, and the Acceleration of Communication

Communications infrastructure had begun before the empire, with the first telegraph line between Seoul and Incheon in the 1880s and extension of lines southward and northward in the following years. A modern postal service, interrupted after the Gapsin Coup of 1884, was restored in 1895. The Gwangmu government expanded and integrated these systems. A postal and telegraph administration, organized within the Ministry of Communications, extended offices and lines across the country, and Korea joined the Universal Postal Union in 1900, which allowed Korean mail to be exchanged internationally under agreed rules.

The telephone was introduced into the palace in the late 1890s, giving the emperor and his offices an instrument for rapid communication; public telephone service began in the early 1900s, starting with a line between Seoul and Incheon in 1902. Initially, users were government offices, wealthy merchants, and legations. Telegraph connections allowed officials in Seoul to receive reports from distant provinces and the treaty ports more quickly, aiding administration and military control, and they gave the court faster access to news of international affairs. Commercial users, including merchants and rice dealers, benefited from price information.

Foreign involvement was significant. Early telegraph lines had been built under Chinese auspices and later partly under Japanese influence, and the Japanese presence in the postal and telegraph systems in the treaty ports and along strategic lines grew. The Korean government worked to maintain control of its own communications, but limited funds meant continued reliance on foreign expertise and, in some cases, foreign-owned lines. Communications infrastructure therefore served both Korean state-building and, potentially, foreign strategic purposes, a duality that became more consequential in 1904.

For ordinary Koreans, the effects were uneven. A letter could travel faster than before, and rural post offices appeared in some districts, but telegraph and telephone use was largely limited to officials and urban elites. The communications network nonetheless contributed to the sense that Korea was becoming integrated into an international system, and to the speed at which news of political events circulated in Seoul.

Communications changed the temporal expectations of government. Under the older system, county reports traveled by courier, often passing through intermediate offices before they reached the capital. Telegraphy did not abolish written administration, but it enabled officials to notify the center of emergencies, trade developments, and military movements more rapidly. Quicker messages could help Seoul demand explanations from provincial officials, yet a wire was only useful when offices had trained operators, funds for maintenance, and confidence that messages would not be intercepted or delayed. Network ownership and control were therefore issues of sovereignty as well as of convenience.

Postal services raised a related problem at a different social scale. Regular routes and internationally recognized exchange standards could assist merchants, students, and families, but access depended on the location of offices and the cost of service. Expansion toward provincial communities proceeded unevenly. For historical evaluation the key comparison is between the government’s ability to communicate, the opportunities available to paying private users, and the strategic leverage gained by foreign powers controlling or operating particular lines.

Division V · Section 53.25

Gyeongungung, Jeongdong, Jongno, and the Imperial Capital

Seoul, called Hanseong in official usage, was physically reshaped in these years. The city had been laid out in the late fourteenth century around the palace of Gyeongbokgung and the main north–south and east–west axes, with Jongno as its commercial spine. In 1896 and after, a municipal improvement program under city officials, with advice from John McLeavy Brown, widened major streets, cleared encroachments, improved drainage, and created public spaces. The Hanseong Prefect's office coordinated this work. Pagoda Park (Tapgol Park), planned in the late 1890s around the old Wongaksa pagoda site, took shape through land clearance and construction beginning around 1899; scholars differ on the precise phases of completion and public opening. It should not be described as a fully operating public park in 1897.

The focus of imperial Seoul shifted toward the area around Gyeongungung. The palace was enlarged and surrounded by new buildings, including Western-style structures. Seokjojeon, a neoclassical stone building, began construction around 1900 under a British architect, and other buildings were added in the following years. Nearby Jeongdong housed the American, British, and Russian legations, as well as schools, churches, and the residences of missionaries and diplomats. Myeongdong Cathedral, a Gothic brick church for the Catholic community, was completed in 1898, a conspicuous marker of the visibility of Christianity in the capital.

Division V · Section 53.26

Everyday Life in an Unevenly Modernizing Society

The material changes of the Gwangmu years were visible but unevenly experienced. For residents of Seoul, new elements entered daily life: streetcars on Jongno, electric lamps in shops and offices, newspapers sold on streets and read aloud in teahouses, imported goods such as kerosene, cotton cloth, matches, and soap in markets, and wage employment in new enterprises and government offices. Schools offered new paths for boys and, in missionary institutions, for some girls. A new time discipline accompanied some of these developments, as schools and offices followed schedules.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Seoul Household and a Rural Household, c. 1900

Consider two illustrative households. These are composite sketches based on patterns recorded in contemporary sources, not individual historical families.

A Seoul household of modest means near Jongno might include a shopkeeper or clerk, his wife, and children. The household could have bought imported cotton cloth and kerosene at nearby shops, listened to a neighbor read a Hangeul newspaper, seen the streetcar pass, and paid rent in cash. A son might attend a new private or public school. Wage and price movements, including the effects of debased coinage, could squeeze the household budget. Women's activities were concentrated in the home and in small trades; a few girls had access to missionary schools.

A tenant household in a southern rice-growing county would have organized life around the agricultural calendar, with rent to a landlord in rice, taxes passed through local clerks, and local markets visited every five days. The household might have seen surveyors, been affected by rising rice exports to Japan through its price effects, and heard of the empire's changes through the market or the village headman. Electric light and the streetcar were unknown, and schooling was limited to a village school teaching Chinese characters, if anything. Women worked in the fields and household, spinning and weaving, and children's labor was important.

What the comparison shows: Class, region, and gender determined access to the empire's new resources. The sources are uneven: urban households appear in newspapers and foreign accounts, while rural households appear mostly in administrative records and later recollections.

Differences of wealth were pronounced. The affluent, including officials, landlords, and large merchants, could afford new goods, attend new schools, and benefit from new services. Poor urban laborers, porters, and day workers faced rising prices and competition for work, while some found employment in construction, railway work, and new factories. Foreign residents and treaty-port communities formed their own social worlds with imported amenities, which contributed to the impression of two cities: one Korean, one cosmopolitan.

Gender also shaped experience. The Gabo-era edict permitting widows to remarry and the founding of girls' schools, such as Ewha, created new possibilities, but social norms continued to restrict most women's public activity. Women's groups did emerge in 1898, petitioning for girls' education, and their appearance, though limited, indicated change. Evidence for women's experience is thinner than for men's, since most sources were produced by men, and historians have worked to recover women's voices through missionary records, petitions, and later memoirs.

For a small trader near Jongno, modernization might mean a tram bringing customers, a newspaper advertising goods, and an electric lamp illuminating a commercial street after dark. The same trader might also face competition from better-financed foreign firms or changing municipal fees. For a household in a Jeolla village, a land survey could be the most immediate encounter with the new state: unfamiliar measurements, questions about the name to be entered on a register, and uncertainty about whether the procedure would raise or regularize taxes. These are contrasting analytical examples, not reconstructed accounts of particular named individuals.

The differences were not only urban and rural. Wealthier families could purchase imported goods, pay school fees, or make use of commercial services earlier than households living from uncertain wages or tenancies. Girls and women encountered new schools and some expanding opportunities in medicine, but access was narrow and conventional expectations remained influential. Laborers helped build infrastructure without necessarily gaining lasting access to it. A history of technological change must therefore consider who financed, built, operated, used, and paid for each innovation. That framework protects the chapter from confusing photographs of modern streets with evidence of a broadly transformed standard of living.

Division VI — Diplomacy, Foreign Economic Pressure, and the Defense of Territory · Section 53.27

The Korean Empire, Qing China, and the Reordering of East Asian Diplomacy

The Treaty of Shimonoseki in April 1895 had required Qing China to recognize Korea's full independence, ending the tributary relationship in principle. But practical questions remained: the rights of Chinese merchants in Korea, the status of Chinese diplomats, and the terms of future relations. After the proclamation of the empire, Korea and the Qing government negotiated a commercial treaty on a basis of formal equality. The Korea–Qing Treaty of Commerce (also rendered Treaty of Amity and Commerce) was signed on 11 September 1899.

The treaty was modeled on those Korea and China had each signed with Western powers and Japan: it provided for diplomatic representation, commercial rights, consular jurisdiction, and mutual treatment. Its significance lay less in new commercial terms than in its form. The 1882 regulations between Qing and Joseon, by contrast, had been framed as rules for a superior-subordinate relationship, and Chinese officials had exercised special privileges in Korea, including intervention in Korean politics. A treaty between two empires on equal terms replaced that framework, and the Qing court's acceptance of it confirmed the end of the tributary order.

Korean diplomats and officials viewed the treaty as an important recognition of independent status, particularly since China had been the principal partner in the older system. The treaty also created practical issues. Chinese merchants, long active in Korean ports, continued to operate, and the treaty provided a framework for their rights. Questions about the border along the Yalu and Tumen rivers, and about the status of Koreans and Chinese living across them, remained unresolved and were taken up in later negotiations, as Section 53.30 shows.

Historians treat the treaty as evidence of treaty-based diplomatic recognition, distinct from tributary diplomacy. It did not change the strategic balance of power, since the Qing was weak and the real contenders were Russia and Japan. But the change from tributary to treaty relations marked a transition in Korea's place in the East Asian order, one that the Korean government had actively pursued.

Division VI · Section 53.28

Diplomatic Recognition and Korea's Engagement with the International State System

The Korean Empire pursued diplomacy through several channels. It maintained or sought legations and representatives in key capitals, including Washington, Tokyo, and several in Europe, though limited funds meant that representation was uneven and sometimes consolidated. In Seoul, a diplomatic corps of foreign ministers, consuls, and advisers interacted daily with the court, and the emperor gave audiences with elaborate protocol. These relations required Korean officials to learn international law, languages, and the practice of treaty negotiation.

Korea extended its treaty network. In addition to treaties from the 1880s with the United States, Britain, Germany, Russia, France, Italy, and others, the empire concluded treaties with Belgium in 1901 and Denmark in 1902. It participated in the Paris Universal Exposition of 1900, displaying Korean goods and culture, and took steps to join international conventions, including postal arrangements and, subsequently, conventions on the laws of war. These actions presented Korea as a participant in international society and aimed to secure recognition and support.

The government also tried to cultivate the interest of particular powers. The United States was regarded by many Korean officials as a friendly power that had no territorial ambitions in Korea, a view based on the 1882 treaty's provision for good offices, though in practice American policy gave priority to its own interests elsewhere. Britain was important for its naval power and commercial interests, and was attractive as a counterweight to Russia. France, whose missionary and commercial connections were considerable, and Germany were also approached. Russia and Japan, as the immediate neighbors, were the most consequential and the hardest to manage.

The record shows a persistent mismatch. Korea's diplomats could win formal recognition and sympathy, but not guarantees. International law of the period recognized sovereign states but gave weaker states little protection against stronger ones, a reality that Korean officials, who read international law texts, understood. The empire's diplomacy was therefore an effort to use the international system's rules and the rivalries among its powers, with the hope that no single power would be willing to act against Korea in the face of the others' objections.

Division VI · Section 53.29

Foreign Concessions, Financial Influence, and the Economic Costs of Independence

Korea lacked capital and technical expertise, and the Gwangmu government turned to foreign concessions: grants to foreign individuals or companies of rights to build railways, work mines, cut timber, or operate utilities in return for fees, shares, or other benefits. Concessions had begun in the 1880s and multiplied in the 1890s. Among the best known were the American Unsan gold mine concession, granted in 1896 to a syndicate that became the Oriental Consolidated Mining Company; the Russian timber concession along the Yalu and Tumen rivers; Japanese rights to build the Gyeongbu railway; French rights for the northwest railway; and various British, German, and other interests.

The government's aims were to attract capital and technology, to secure revenue, and to give foreign interests a stake in Korean independence by involving several powers rather than one. This strategy of balance, however, had costs. Concession contracts were often long, sometimes set for several decades, and granted valuable resources on favorable terms to the concessionaire. The Unsan mine, for example, became a major gold producer operated largely under foreign control, and Korean revenue from it was limited relative to its value. Disputes over concession terms, over jurisdiction, and over the treatment of Korean workers also arose.

Competition among foreign powers shaped these choices. Japan prioritized railways, commerce, banking, and fishing rights, and its economic penetration of Korea's ports and rice trade was deeper than that of any other power. American interests focused on mining and utilities, with the American government providing limited political support. British and European interests were smaller but present.

Korean officials were not simply passive. They negotiated terms, rejected some proposals, and attempted to use concessions as instruments of diplomacy. But the bargaining position of a capital-poor state was weak, and concession-granting could not create the independent economic base that sovereignty required. The cost of acquiring technology through concessions was the transfer of some control over key resources, which constrained later policy and gave concession-holding powers economic stakes in Korea that they would defend.

Division VI · Section 53.30

Northern Borderlands, Gando, Ulleungdo, and Territorial Administration

The Korean Empire also sought to make sovereignty a matter of territorial administration. On the northeastern frontier, tens of thousands of Koreans, driven by famine and economic hardship in the mid- and late nineteenth century, had settled in the area north of the Tumen River, in the region often called Gando (or Jiandao in Chinese). Its status was ambiguous: the Qing regarded it as Chinese territory, and its closed policy toward settlement there had broken down, while Korean officials and settlers argued about the location of the boundary as described in an early eighteenth-century survey of Mount Baekdu. Negotiations in the 1880s had failed to settle the matter.

After 1897 the Korean government took a more active position. In 1902 it dispatched Yi Beom-yun as an inspector to investigate the Korean community, and in 1903 he was given authority as an administrator to register settlers and protect them from abuse by Chinese local officials and bandits. His mission produced a census of Korean settlers, established some governing arrangements, and brought the status of the region into diplomatic discussion with Qing authorities. The Qing, for its part, contested Korean claims of jurisdiction. The dispute was unresolved when the war of 1904 began, and its later fate, including the 1909 settlement between Japan and China, belongs to Chapter 54.

In the east, the Korean government dealt with the island of Ulleungdo. Settlement of the island, which had been restricted for centuries, was formally opened in the early 1880s after an official inspection, and Korean settlers arrived in increasing numbers, as did Japanese loggers and fishermen operating illegally. Seeking to assert administration, the government issued Imperial Decree No. 41 on 25 October 1900.

Division VI · Section 53.31

Russia, Japan, and the Increasingly Dangerous Balance of Power

The relative calm of 1898, when Russia and Japan pledged mutual restraint in Korea, did not last. In 1900, the Boxer uprising in northern China led to the intervention of an international force and, separately, to Russia's occupation of much of Manchuria. Russian forces remained after the uprising ended, and Russia sought to turn its military presence into lasting influence, including along the Yalu River. To Japanese leaders, Russian control of Manchuria and a stronger position in northern Korea threatened Japan's strategic and economic interests, including the security of the peninsula as a buffer.

Japan's response was diplomatic and military. In January 1902 it concluded the Anglo-Japanese Alliance with Britain, an agreement in which each recognized the other's interests, Britain's in China and Japan's in Korea in particular, and pledged support if either were attacked by more than one power. This reduced the risk that Japan would face a Russo-French combination alone and strengthened its position in negotiations with Russia. In 1902 Russia agreed with China to withdraw its troops from Manchuria in stages, but the withdrawal stalled in 1903, and Russian activity on the Korean side of the Yalu, notably involving timber operations at Yongampo, increased Japanese concern.

Negotiations between Russia and Japan took place from mid-1903 and centered on Manchuria and Korea. Japan sought recognition of its predominant position in Korea in exchange for accepting Russian interests in Manchuria; Russia was willing to concede some influence in southern Korea but sought limits, including proposals for a neutral zone in the north. The details belong to Chapter 54, but their significance here is clear: by late 1903, Korea's future was being discussed between Russia and Japan, with Korea not a party.

Korean policy during these years reflected awareness of the danger. The government pursued neutrality proposals, balanced concessions, courted the United States and Britain, and sought assurances from both Russia and Japan. It could not, however, change the structure of the situation: two powers with incompatible aims and the means to fight had interests that overlapped on its territory. Students should resist two simplifications. Korea was not merely a passive arena, since its government actively tried to shape outcomes. But nor could Korean policy decide the matter: the choice between war and agreement lay mainly with St. Petersburg and Tokyo.

Division VII — Neutrality, the Limits of Reform, and the Future of Korean Sovereignty · Section 53.32

Korea's Neutrality Diplomacy on the Eve of the Russo-Japanese War

Neutrality was not a new idea in 1904. In 1900 and 1901, Korean officials and some foreign advisers had explored proposals by which Korea would be declared neutral by the powers, in the manner of Belgium or Switzerland, or by which Russia and Japan would jointly guarantee its status. These proposals did not mature, in part because neither power was willing to renounce its interests and in part because Korea's own diplomatic standing was not strong enough to compel agreement. The idea nonetheless remained part of the government's thinking, and as Russo-Japanese talks stalled in 1903, it returned with urgency.

During 1903 and January 1904 the court sought assurances through several channels. It communicated with foreign representatives in Seoul, sent messages to legations abroad, and approached governments it hoped would sympathize, notably France, which was allied with Russia but had commercial and missionary interests in Korea, and also Britain and the United States. The emperor and his advisers understood that a declaration alone would carry no force, and aimed to obtain, if possible, acknowledgment from the powers that would raise the diplomatic cost of violating it.

The gap between the declared position and Korea's capacity to defend it was the crux. Neutrality, as practiced by recognized neutral states, rested on either strong armed forces or powerful guarantors. The Korean Empire had neither: its army could not prevent a landing, and no power had pledged to defend it. Japan, preparing for war, regarded Korea's territory and communications as essential to military operations and was prepared to disregard a neutrality declaration. What followed, beginning in February 1904, is narrated in Chapter 54. For this chapter, the declaration is best read as the empire's last major attempt, before the war, to use the language of international law to protect itself, a serious initiative made from a position of deep weakness.

Division VII · Section 53.33

The Achievements and Contradictions of Gwangmu Reform

Taken as a whole, the Gwangmu program left a substantial record. In land administration, a survey covering a large part of the country, though incomplete, and a certificate system establishing documentary ownership. In finance, a more active state, though fragmented by the dual structure of ministry and household. In industry and infrastructure, a railway, electric power, streetcars, telegraph and telephone expansion, banks, and numerous experimental enterprises. In education and medicine, new schools, a medical school, and a government hospital. In the military, a centralized command and a larger army. In diplomacy, treaty equality with China, new treaties with other states, and participation in international exhibitions and conventions. In politics, the first mass public assemblies and the first proposals for a partly elected consultative council, followed by their suppression and a legal statement of unlimited imperial authority.

The contradictions are equally clear. The program aimed at independence while relying on foreign capital, advisers, and concession holders. It sought state strengthening while creating a household economy that competed with state finance. It cultivated public discussion through schools and newspapers while repressing the political movement that organized it. It invested in visible modern technologies in the capital while the countryside saw mainly tax collection and uncertain land documentation. And it built an army larger and more expensive than before without achieving the capacity to withstand a great power.

Historical Debate

Did the Gwangmu Reforms Constitute Substantial Modernization?

The question: Should the Gwangmu program be understood as meaningful, Korean-led modernization, as a limited or superficial effort, or as something in between?

Interpretation A — Meaningful Korean-led modernization: Many Korean historians, particularly since the late twentieth century, emphasize the institutions, legislation, surveys, infrastructure, and educational programs that the empire created, and argue that these demonstrate Korean initiative and capacity, countering older narratives that portrayed Korea as stagnant until colonial rule.
Interpretation B — Constrained by centralization and dependence: Other scholars argue that the program's effectiveness was limited by imperial absolutism, fiscal disorder, foreign dependence, and the narrow social reach of its benefits, and that many achievements were concentrated in Seoul or controlled by foreigners.
Interpretation C — Combined assessment: More nuanced approaches recognize both genuine achievements and structural constraints, and caution against judging the era solely by its outcome after 1904, or by the need either to defend or to condemn it.

Evidence: Decrees, registers, newspapers, company records, and diplomatic reports. Disagreement arises over weighting and over the standards of comparison.

Points of disagreement: Interpretation and terminology: what "modernization" means, whether Korean initiative or foreign influence should be emphasized, and what counts as success.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians assess Korean capacity before colonization, and how the later colonial period is understood, a question that returns in Chapters 55–58.

Student task: Choose two Gwangmu initiatives and evaluate each under interpretations A and B, noting what evidence supports each reading.

Multiple Perspectives

The Gwangmu Reforms as Experienced by Different Groups

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from any single named person.

The emperor and court: Reform meant unifying authority under the throne, securing independence through rank, ritual, treaties, and a loyal army, and adopting useful technologies without dismantling the dynasty's legitimacy.
Reform-minded activists and students: Reform meant independence, education, public discussion, and accountable government; they supported the monarchy but saw absolutism and concentration of power as obstacles to strength.
Seoul merchants and workers: New markets, wage work, and goods offered opportunities, but foreign competition, debased coinage, and rising prices created hardship; concessions and monopolies were particular concerns.
Rural farmers and tenants: Surveys and taxes were experienced mainly as new demands from officials and clerks, with benefits uncertain; landlords and tenants stood in different positions relative to documentation.
Foreign representatives and concession holders: Korea was a field for commercial and strategic interests; they supported reform where it served those interests, cooperated or competed with one another, and judged Korean capacity by their own priorities.

Note: These groups did not possess equal power or freedom of action; the emperor and foreign powers shaped outcomes far more than farmers or workers.

The strongest evaluation separates different kinds of achievement. Legal and institutional establishment can be demonstrated by laws, appointments, budgets, and offices. Administrative effectiveness requires additional evidence: surveys completed and maintained, taxes actually reaching public accounts, schools operating with trained staff, and military units equipped and paid. Social transformation requires still other evidence, especially records of landholding, household livelihood, education, and urban or rural mobility. A policy might succeed on the first measure and fail on the second, or produce benefits for a particular group without changing the broader population’s circumstances.

The program also did not present a single, unanimous conception of independence. Imperial officials often emphasized state coordination and the monarch’s ability to command resources. Independence Club activists viewed public accountability and representative consultation as ways to mobilize national strength. Entrepreneurs wanted reliable rules and access to capital; rural cultivators might prioritize fair assessment, land security, and protection from predatory collectors. Foreign lenders and concession holders could support specific modernizing projects while seeking privileges incompatible with Korean fiscal or territorial autonomy. These were not merely divergent attitudes toward “modernity”; they were competing expectations about who should govern and who should benefit.

A historically defensible judgment must therefore resist two retrospective temptations. The later establishment of Japanese colonial rule does not prove that every Gwangmu initiative was pointless or that Korean leaders had no strategic options. Conversely, the existence of electric streetcars, new ministries, and survey documents does not show that the empire had created a fully integrated modern state. Its accomplishments and weaknesses should be assessed at the scales at which the evidence is strongest, then connected to the international balance of power. That distinction is necessary to understand what the Russo-Japanese crisis threatened.

Division VII · Section 53.34

The Korean Empire at the Threshold of War

At the beginning of 1904, the Korean Empire possessed a recognizable set of modern state institutions and several visible symbols of technological change, set against deep structural weaknesses. Its legal claim to sovereignty was explicit: the Daehan Gukje asserted independence and the powers had recognized the imperial title. Its diplomatic relationships included treaties with major powers and a settlement of the old tributary relationship with China. In practical administration, it had advanced a land survey and certificate system that remained incomplete, a communications network concentrated in cities and along key routes, and a small number of modern enterprises.

Its weaknesses were equally evident. Revenue was insufficient and fragmented, with the imperial household and the Finance Ministry competing, and the currency had been strained. The army was costly but unable to defend against a great power. The most valuable concessions were in foreign hands. Domestic political participation had been closed after 1898, and many educated Koreans were frustrated with the court's policies, even as they shared the goal of independence. Rural conditions varied, with some areas experiencing hardship and local unrest.

The loss of independence was not inevitable in 1904, but the odds were poor. The empire's story is thus best told as one of real achievement and real vulnerability, with the outcome determined by a combination of Korean limitations and external power.

The chapter's governing conclusion is therefore neither that Gwangmu modernization was a failure nor that Korea was on a secure path to independence. The Korean Empire pursued a substantive program of national state-building and achieved meaningful institutional and technological change. It nevertheless remained vulnerable because the resources needed for effective sovereignty did not match the strategic power confronting it. Chapter Review · Section 53.35

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This review asks students to synthesize the chapter, and in particular to distinguish three meanings of sovereignty: as a legal claim (the Daehan Gukje, the imperial title, the 1899 treaty), as a diplomatic relationship (recognition by other states, treaty equality, legations, neutrality diplomacy), and as practical capacity to administer, finance, and defend a state (land records, taxation, the army, infrastructure). The Korean Empire advanced quickly on the first, made real progress on the second, and progressed unevenly on the third.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Korean Empire, proclaimed in October 1897, attempted to build a sovereign modern state and why its achievements did not secure independence. After the monarch's return from the Russian legation in February 1897, Gojong consolidated authority at Gyeongungung and, responding to petitions and his own strategic reading of the international system, accepted the imperial title at the Hwangudan. The name Daehan and the era name Gwangmu asserted equal standing with the empires of East Asia and the wider world.

The Gwangmu program combined ritual and technology under the principle of old foundations and new references. It differed from the Gabo Reforms in being directed from the throne and in emphasizing land, finance, military command, infrastructure, and state-sponsored enterprise rather than primarily legal and administrative restructuring. Its record can be assessed only by comparing decrees with implementation, since the two often diverged.

Politically, the empire faced the first sustained public political movement in Korean history. The Independence Club, led initially by Seo Jae-pil, organized mass assemblies in 1898, adopted the Six Articles, and secured the government's consent to a plan for a partly elected advisory council. Rumors of a republican conspiracy, conservative mobilization, and the emperor's insistence on his prerogative led to suppression in December 1898. The Daehan Gukje of August 1899 then declared Korea an independent empire and the emperor's powers unlimited, a document that expressed sovereignty externally and absolutism internally.

The land and fiscal reforms aimed to give the state reliable knowledge of land and ownership. The Yangji Amun surveyed a substantial share of the country's counties, and the Jigye Amun issued ownership certificates, but the survey was incomplete and suspended in 1904. Taxation remained dependent on local officials, and the imperial household's growing revenues blurred the boundary between private and public finance. Industrial and commercial enterprises were numerous but typically small, and their long-term results were limited.

The Wonsubu centralized military command under the emperor, and the army grew in size and cost, but its capability for external defense remained small. Schools, newspapers, and hospitals expanded the empire's institutional reach, particularly in Seoul and the treaty ports. A Korean-language press circulated ideas of independence, civilization, and public responsibility, while medical institutions extended the state's role in public health to a limited population.

Railways, electricity, streetcars, and communications transformed the capital's appearance and some features of urban life. The Gyeongin railway, the Hanseong Electric Company, and the telegraph, telephone, and postal systems were important innovations, but they depended heavily on foreign capital and expertise and were unevenly accessible. Everyday life varied sharply by class, gender, and region, as the comparison of a Seoul household and a rural tenant household illustrates.

In diplomacy, the empire achieved treaty equality with Qing China in 1899, expanded its treaties with other states, participated in international exhibitions, and attempted to administer territory at Gando and Ulleungdo, where Imperial Decree No. 41 of 1900 is secure evidence of an administrative act whose later interpretation is contested. Foreign concessions brought capital and technology, but also long-term foreign control over resources and new strategic stakes.

The strategic environment worsened after 1900, as Russian occupation of Manchuria, the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, and stalled Russo-Japanese negotiations raised the likelihood of war. Korean policy responded with balancing and, in January 1904, a neutrality declaration. This was a genuine initiative, but not an enforceable guarantee.

Historians assess the era in different ways, and this chapter has presented the main interpretations: as meaningful Korean-led modernization, as a program limited by centralization and dependence, and as a combination of the two. The most defensible judgment recognizes both achievements and structural constraints, and avoids reading the loss of independence back into the period as inevitable.

The chapter's central lesson is the distance between legal sovereignty, diplomatic recognition, and practical capacity. These three can diverge, and the history of the Korean Empire illustrates how a state may be recognized in law and in diplomacy without possessing the resources to defend its position. Chapter 54 examines the war, the 1905 protectorate, resistance, and annexation that followed.

Chapter Reflection

The Korean Empire matters because it complicates two familiar stories. One treats late Joseon as stagnant until outside forces imposed change; the other treats the loss of independence as the foreordained end of a failing state. The evidence of Gwangmu points elsewhere: a government actively built institutions, surveyed land, introduced technologies, sought treaty equality, and tried to defend its territory and neutrality, in a world where recognition and capacity did not coincide.

The chapter also reveals tensions that outlasted the empire. The competing visions of the Independence Club and the Daehan Gukje, one stressing accountable public participation and the other unified imperial command, anticipated debates about authority, legitimacy, and national strength that recur across Korean history, from the colonial period through the divided states of Volume IV. The reformist press, schools, and associations that grew in these years also became the foundation for later patriotic enlightenment and independence movements.

Material change in Seoul, the land survey, railways, and electric power left traces that later regimes, including the colonial government, would use, reinterpret, or claim. How historians understand their origins, as Korean initiative, foreign-dependent development, or both, affects how they understand the colonial period that followed, a question explored in Chapters 55 through 58.

Finally, the Korean Empire teaches a lesson about sovereignty itself. Legal claims, diplomatic recognition, and practical capacity can diverge, and a state may have the first two without the third. With that distinction in hand, students are prepared for Chapter 54, where the war between Russia and Japan, the imposed protectorate, and the resistance and annexation that followed test the empire's position.
