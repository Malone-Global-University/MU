The Manchu Invasions and a New East Asian Order

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the thirtieth day of the first lunar month of 1637 (February 24 in the Gregorian calendar), King Injo of Joseon left a mountain fortress southeast of Seoul and, according to Joseon court records, went to the bank of the Han River at Samjeondo, dressed as a supplicant rather than a sovereign, to submit to Hong Taiji, the ruler of the newly proclaimed Qing empire. The dynasty survived, its king kept his throne, and its territory was not annexed. Yet the ceremony ended a diplomatic order in which Joseon had understood itself as the loyal eastern ally of Ming China, and it placed that same state in a new, asymmetrical relationship with a power that most of its elite had long regarded as culturally inferior. How did a kingdom that had been rescued from invasion by Ming armies a generation earlier come to kneel to the Manchus, and what did that submission change?

Chapter 35 closed the narrative of the Imjin War: the Japanese invasions of 1592 and 1597, the destruction they caused, and the withdrawal of Japanese forces after the death of Toyotomi Hideyoshi in 1598. This chapter turns to the second great military crisis of the same half-century, one that came not from the sea but from the northwest, across the Amnok (Yalu) River. In the 1590s the Jurchen peoples of the Manchurian borderlands were a set of rival groups, many of them tied by trade and tributary titles to Ming China. By the 1630s a state organized by one Jurchen lineage, the Aisin Gioro, and built on a military-administrative system called the Eight Banners, had conquered most of Liaodong, brought much of the eastern Mongol world under its authority, adopted the name Manchu for its people, and proclaimed a new dynasty, the Great Qing. Joseon was caught between the declining Ming order to which its political culture was committed and the rising Manchu order that increasingly controlled its northern frontier.

This chapter is organized around two invasions: the campaign of 1627, led by the forces of the Later Jin state, and the campaign of 1636–1637, led by Hong Taiji in the name of the Qing. They were not identical events. The first ended in a negotiated agreement between nominally equal states and left Joseon's formal relationship with Ming intact; the second ended with a formal submission, the surrender of the Ming connection, the taking of royal hostages, and the incorporation of Joseon into a Qing-centered tributary hierarchy. Between and around these invasions lay the political upheavals of Joseon itself: the late reign of Gwanghaegun, the coup of 1623 that placed Injo on the throne, the rebellion of Yi Gwal in 1624, and the long argument at court between officials who favored accommodation with the Manchus and officials who insisted on resistance and moral loyalty to Ming.

Historians approach these events with a layered body of evidence. Joseon court records, notably the annals of the Injo reign, the diaries of the Royal Secretariat, and the records of the Border Defense Council, document debates, appointments, memorials, and military reports, but they were compiled by officials with strong positions on the questions at stake. Diaries and memoirs kept by participants, among them Na Man-gap's account of the siege of Namhansanseong, supply vivid detail but also hearsay and judgment. Ming records preserve the perspective of a dynasty that was losing control of its northeastern frontier, while Manchu-language and Chinese-language Qing documents, including archival records and the later veritable records of the early Qing rulers, show how a state in the making understood its own expansion. No single body of sources is neutral, and this chapter treats the invasions as events seen differently from Seoul, Shenyang, Beijing, and the villages of the Pyeongan and Gyeonggi countrysides.

Two cautions shape the chapter. First, the Manchu conquest of Joseon was neither inevitable nor the simple outcome of Joseon weakness after the Imjin War. Joseon's capacity to resist was constrained by devastation, factional conflict, and a military establishment still under reconstruction, but the outcomes of 1627 and 1636 also depended on Manchu institutional innovation, Ming collapse in Liaodong, defections of Chinese commanders, climatic and economic pressures on the Manchurian frontier, and decisions made by individuals who could not know how events would end. Second, the story must not be reduced to a contest between patriotic hardliners and cowardly accommodationists. The officials who argued for peace and those who argued for war were both appealing to Confucian ethical categories, to evidence about military capabilities, and to concern for the survival of the dynasty and its people.

The chapter also gives full weight to the human consequences. The campaigns of 1627 and especially 1636–1637 killed soldiers and civilians, devastated the roads and counties along the Qing armies' routes, carried off captives to Manchuria, and left families divided for years or permanently. The Joseon court's formal submission was settled in a ceremony; the experiences of captives, ransomed prisoners, returnees, refugees, and rural communities took far longer to resolve. These experiences shaped Korean historical memory of the invasions, including the very names by which they are known, a subject examined in the chapter's vocabulary features.

Finally, the chapter concludes Part VI. It explains how the seventeenth-century crisis altered Joseon's military institutions, its diplomatic position, and its political culture. The kingdom's formal allegiance moved from the Ming to the Qing, yet a strong strand of Ming loyalism persisted among Joseon elites after the Ming dynasty fell to rebellion and Qing conquest in 1644. This persistence, and the tension between practical subordination and ideological loyalty, set the terms for much of the political and intellectual history addressed in Part VII, beginning with Chapter 37.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the condition of Joseon after the Imjin War and why its relationship with Ming China carried both strategic and ideological weight.
  2. Trace the rise of the Jurchen-Manchu state from Nurhaci's consolidation of the Jianzhou Jurchen through the proclamation of Later Jin in 1616 and the Qing in 1636.
  3. Analyze the political, military, institutional, economic, and environmental foundations of Manchu expansion rather than attributing it to a single ruler.
  4. Evaluate Gwanghaegun's diplomacy during the Ming–Later Jin war, including the Battle of Sarhu in 1619, and distinguish interpretations that call it neutrality, pragmatism, or disloyalty.
  5. Assess the 1623 coup, the legitimizing arguments used to justify it, and the relationship between domestic legitimacy and foreign policy under Injo.
  6. Compare the causes, course, and settlements of the invasions of 1627 and 1636–1637, and distinguish Later Jin from the Qing dynasty.
  7. Interpret the debate at Namhansanseong between advocates of resistance and advocates of accommodation as a set of historically situated strategic and ethical arguments.
  8. Describe the terms of the Qing settlement of 1637 and explain why submission to Qing differed from territorial annexation.
  9. Identify the human consequences of the invasions, including civilian deaths, displacement, captivity, forced labor, ransom, and the experiences of families and communities beyond the royal court.
  10. Evaluate sources for the invasions, including a participant's diary, by distinguishing contemporary observation, retrospective composition, interpretation, and later memory.
  11. Connect the invasions to changes in Joseon military institutions, ideological loyalty to Ming, and the wider transition from Ming to Qing predominance in East Asia.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a state formed in the Manchurian borderlands within a few decades become capable of invading a kingdom that Japanese armies had recently overrun?
  2. What obligations did Joseon owe to Ming China, how did those obligations arise, and why did they limit Joseon's choices in the 1610s and 1620s?
  3. Was Gwanghaegun's diplomacy a coherent policy of balance, an improvised response to impossible circumstances, or something else, and how do the sources shape our answer?
  4. How did the legitimacy crisis surrounding the 1623 coup interact with foreign policy toward Ming and Later Jin?
  5. Why did the 1627 invasion end in a settlement between nominally equal states, while the 1636–1637 invasion ended in submission?
  6. How should historians evaluate the arguments of officials who favored resistance and officials who favored negotiation during the siege of Namhansanseong?
  7. What distinguishes a tributary or vassal relationship under Qing from annexation, and what did Joseon retain and lose in 1637?
  8. Who bore the heaviest costs of the invasions, and how did their experiences differ from the experiences recorded in court documents?
  9. How did formal subordination to Qing coexist with continuing ideological loyalty to Ming in Joseon political culture?

Historical Context

Joseon had entered the seventeenth century as a society recovering from the Imjin War. Chapters 32 through 35 describe the loss of cultivated land, the disruption of the tax and registration systems, the destruction of palaces and archives, and the human toll of occupation, military campaigns, and displacement. Reconstruction was incomplete when new pressures appeared in the north. At the same time, the war had changed Joseon's military practice. The Military Training Agency, established in 1593, trained musketeers, archers, and spearmen in new formations, and the Border Defense Council, originally a frontier planning body, had become a central wartime organ of government. Understanding these changes matters because Joseon's defenders in the 1620s and 1630s were not simply an unreformed army; they were a force reformed along lines designed for the Japanese threat and then confronted with a very different kind of enemy.

Joseon's political world was also defined by its relationship with Ming China. Since the founding of Joseon in 1392, the kingdom's rulers had sought Ming investiture, sent regular tribute missions, and used Ming reign names in their diplomatic documents, while governing their kingdom through their own institutions, laws, and officials. This arrangement, examined in Chapter 30, was reciprocal, ritualized, and politically useful. Ming recognition legitimated Joseon kings against internal challengers, while Joseon's loyalty gave the Ming court a reliable eastern neighbor. The Imjin War gave the relationship a new moral dimension, because Ming armies had fought on Joseon soil. Many Joseon officials spoke of an enormous debt of gratitude to the Ming emperor, a theme that would later be invoked against any policy that appeared to abandon Ming.

The northern frontier was a zone of diverse peoples. The Jurchen groups of the Manchurian forests and river valleys, whom Ming administrators and Joseon officials classified in several ways according to region and degree of political organization, had long traded horses, furs, and ginseng with Ming and Joseon, and Joseon had a history of frontier diplomacy and conflict with them, examined in Chapter 30. Liaodong, the region east of the Liao River and north of the Bohai Gulf, was a Ming-governed military frontier garrisoned by troops of varied origins and inhabited by Chinese, Jurchen, Mongol, and Korean-descended populations. Its fortunes, and those of the Ming armies that defended it, were central to what happened to Joseon. When the Ming position in Liaodong collapsed, Joseon lost the buffer that had protected its northwestern border and found itself adjacent to a militarized state with expanding ambitions.

A final piece of context concerns historical method. The textbook has so far treated Joseon's records as unusually rich, and they are. But in this crisis the records are also deeply partisan. The reign of Gwanghaegun was recorded after his deposition by the political faction that overthrew him, and the debates at Namhansanseong were remembered, recorded, and reinterpreted by participants who later justified their own positions. Qing materials, composed from the viewpoint of an expanding dynasty, cast the invasions in terms of Joseon's alleged breaches of promises. The task of the historian is to compare these accounts, identify their purposes, and reconstruct what can be known about events, causes, and consequences.

Section 36.1

Joseon after the Imjin War: Recovery, Vulnerability, and the Ming Connection

When the last Japanese forces withdrew in 1598, Joseon's government faced the task of reconstruction under conditions that Chapter 35 has described: depopulated and uncultivated districts, disrupted land and household registers, burned palaces, lost archives, and a population that had been displaced, enslaved, killed, or carried to Japan. Reconstruction under King Seonjo and then Gwanghaegun was real but uneven. The court re-surveyed land and revived registration, undertook the rebuilding of palaces, completed the medical compendium Donguibogam, and began the tax reform known as the Daedong law, introduced first in Gyeonggi Province in 1608. These measures show a state attempting to restore its fiscal base and political authority. They were not cheap, and the work of rebuilding palaces and fortifications added to burdens on already stressed communities.

Joseon's armed forces in these years were a mixture of old and new. The Military Training Agency (Hunryeon Dogam), founded in 1593 with the advice of Ming officers, trained salaried soldiers in the Ming-derived Zhejiang-style drill and in the use of firearms, and the court gradually developed other capital commands. The Border Defense Council, an institution that had begun as a small body for frontier matters, retained its wartime importance as a coordinating organ for military and diplomatic affairs, and its records are among the most valuable sources for the period. But the army's size, training, provisioning, and geographic distribution remained limited by finances and by the persistent difficulty of mobilizing manpower in a status society where many commoners were unwilling or unable to serve. The northern frontier, historically a region with smaller garrisons than the south and the capital, was particularly exposed.

Joseon's relations with Ming after the war combined gratitude, dependence, and friction. Ming armies had come to Joseon's aid in 1592 and 1597, but they had also quartered large numbers of soldiers in Korean towns and drawn on Korean grain supplies, and Ming envoys in the following years made demands that Joseon officials found burdensome. Even so, the idea that the Ming emperor had saved the kingdom, expressed in later vocabulary as the favor of "re-creating" Joseon, entered court rhetoric and eventually became a moral touchstone. It is important to distinguish the genuine historical roots of this sentiment from its later uses. The sentiment reflected real events; it also served the interests of officials and kings who needed legitimacy and wanted to resist unwelcome policies, as the vocabulary box on jaejo jieun below explains.

Students should resist two tempting inferences. The first is that the Imjin War caused the Ming decline or the Manchu rise. Ming fiscal and military strain had many causes, including the cost of the Wanli emperor's major campaigns of the 1590s in different theaters, tax problems, factional conflict at court, and corruption in frontier commands, and the Jurchen consolidation under Nurhaci began decades earlier and followed its own logic. The war of 1592–1598 was one among several burdens on Ming resources, and historians disagree about its weight. The second inference is that Joseon was helpless in the 1620s because of the Imjin War. Joseon was damaged, but it retained an organized state, productive agricultural regions, and military officials with recent experience of war. Its vulnerability on the northern frontier arose from the conjunction of that damage with the speed, mobility, and organization of the Manchu army.

The sources for this period include the annals of the Joseon kings (the Sillok), which are ordinarily the central court chronicle but, for Gwanghaegun's reign, take the form of a daily record (ilgi) compiled after his deposition, a distinction examined below. They include the diaries of the Royal Secretariat, the records of the Border Defense Council, and the collected works of officials. For the Ming side they include the Ming veritable records and the memorials of frontier officials. Together they allow historians to reconstruct a Joseon state under strain, a Ming state losing control of Liaodong, and a frontier world in which different powers sought alliances, supplies, and information.

Section 36.2

The Rise of the Jurchen-Manchu State

The people called Jurchen in Ming and Joseon sources were not a single political community in the sixteenth century. Ming administrators grouped them broadly into the Jianzhou, Haixi, and "wild" or eastern Jurchen, using terms that reflected geography and degree of integration into the Ming tributary system as much as ethnic or linguistic reality. Their economies combined farming, hunting, fishing, and gathering with trade in horses, furs, and ginseng, and many of their leaders held Ming military titles that came with trading privileges. Alongside them in Liaodong lived Chinese farmers and soldiers, Mongol groups, and people of mixed background. Political loyalty in this frontier world was fluid, and Ming officials often used rivalry among Jurchen leaders as a tool of frontier management.

Nurhaci, a leader of the Jianzhou Jurchen of the Aisin Gioro lineage, began his career in the 1580s in the aftermath of the deaths of his father and grandfather in fighting that involved Ming forces. Later traditions elaborate his early campaigns with legendary details, and students should treat the specific stories as tradition rather than documented fact. What is clear is that, over three decades, he defeated neighboring Jurchen leaders, incorporated their households and followers, and built alliances through marriage and negotiation, including with eastern Mongol leaders. He accepted Ming titles when they were useful, and for years his state operated within the framework of the Ming frontier order. The turning point came when his consolidation made him strong enough to challenge Ming control over trade and territory directly.

The institution that made this possible was the banner system. Nurhaci organized his followers into military-administrative units based on companies called niru, each originally responsible for a fixed number of households, and grouped those companies into larger divisions distinguished by colored banners. Four banners existed by about 1601; by 1615 the system had been expanded to eight. A banner unit combined functions that elsewhere were separate: it mustered soldiers, assigned land and labor, collected resources, settled disputes, and bound households to the state through hereditary service. The system allowed rapid mobilization, drew captured and incorporated populations into the state's structure, and gave the Jurchen leadership a way to turn conquest into administration. Alongside it came a writing system. Around 1599 Jurchen scribes adapted the Mongolian script to write Manchu, creating a vehicle for records, law, and diplomacy.

Political authority in the early Jurchen state was less personal than a focus on Nurhaci alone suggests. Nurhaci shared decision-making with a small group of senior princes called beile, including his sons and nephews, each of whom commanded banners and held a stake in decisions about war, tribute, and succession. Under Hong Taiji, who succeeded him in 1626 after Nurhaci's death, this collective leadership was gradually curtailed in favor of stronger central authority, and Hong Taiji also drew increasingly on literate Chinese and Mongol advisers and on the services of Chinese commanders who defected to the Manchu side. The state that attacked Joseon in 1627 and 1636 was therefore the product of institutional development as well as of individual leadership.

Historical Vocabulary
The Eight Banners and the Name "Manchu"

Definition. The Eight Banners were the military and administrative divisions of the Jurchen-Manchu state, each distinguished by a colored banner and made up of companies (niru) of households. Manchu (Manju) is the name that Hong Taiji officially adopted in 1635 for the people previously called Jurchen. The state name Later Jin (Aisin Gurun in Manchu) was proclaimed in 1616 and replaced in 1636 by Qing (Daicing Gurun, "Great Qing").

Historical context. Banner membership did not coincide perfectly with ethnicity. As conquests continued, Mongol and Chinese households were incorporated into banner structures, and separate Mongol and Han (Hanjun) banners were organized under Hong Taiji's rule as the empire grew. The decision to adopt the name Manchu and to forbid use of the older Jurchen name was part of a deliberate effort to define a new political community with its own identity.

Why it matters. The banner system explains how a relatively small population could field large, disciplined armies, administer conquered territory, and fuse soldiers, workers, and households into a single state apparatus.

Potential misunderstanding. The Eight Banners were not simply an "army of Manchus." The Qing armies that invaded Joseon in 1636 included Manchu, Mongol, and Han Chinese banner units, along with artillery and naval expertise contributed by Chinese defectors. Nor should "Manchu" be read back into earlier decades: the Jurchen of the 1590s and the Manchus of the 1630s were connected by continuity but were not the same political community.

Expansion followed a sequence of military and political steps. In 1616, at Hetu Ala, Nurhaci was enthroned as khan of a state that Chinese sources call the Later Jin, recalling the Jurchen Jin dynasty of the twelfth century. In 1618 he issued a proclamation known as the "Seven Grievances" against the Ming, listing alleged wrongs, and took the Ming garrison town of Fushun. The Ming response in 1619 ended in defeat at Sarhu, discussed in Section 36.3. In 1621 Later Jin forces took Shenyang and Liaoyang, the main Ming administrative and military centers in Liaodong, and Nurhaci moved his capital to Liaoyang and then, in 1625, to Shenyang. Treatment of the Chinese population of Liaodong during these conquests was harsh and inconsistent, with episodes of severe violence alongside efforts to settle and incorporate captured populations, and it produced flight, resistance, and mass movement of people, some of whom reached the Korean border.

Explanations of this expansion should avoid reducing it to the personality of Nurhaci. Structural conditions mattered. The banner system gave the Jurchen leadership an unusual capacity to mobilize labor and troops. Ming frontier command was fractured and short of funds, with factional politics at court shaping appointments and supplies. Liaodong's economy depended on trade and military supply networks that were disrupted by war and by Ming policies. Some scholars point also to climate stress in the early seventeenth century, a cooler period in which harvests in northeastern Asia suffered, as one factor pressing Manchurian communities toward raiding and conquest, though the weight of this factor is debated. Contingency mattered as well. In 1626 Nurhaci's army was repulsed at Ningyuan by a Ming garrison using Western-style cannon, and Nurhaci died later that year. The succession of Hong Taiji, accepted by the other beile, was not guaranteed in advance, and the shape of the later Qing state reflected his choices as well as the structures he inherited.

For Joseon, the significance of these developments was geographic and strategic. After 1621 the Ming presence in Liaodong, which had served as a buffer, had largely disappeared from the land route to the Amnok. Ming resistance survived at sea and on the Liaodong coast, and one Ming officer, Mao Wenlong, established a base on Pi Island, just off the Pyeongan coast and near the mouth of the Amnok, from which he conducted raids against Later Jin territory and drew on Joseon for supplies. Joseon thus found itself with a Ming ally on its coast, a hostile Manchu power across its northern border, and a diplomatic problem that would last for two decades.

Section 36.3

Gwanghaegun, Sarhu, and the Northern Crisis

Gwanghaegun, the second son of King Seonjo by a concubine, had become heir during the Imjin War, when Seonjo's designated heir was absent and his own wartime leadership gave him standing. His accession in 1608 was never fully secure. He faced challenges from supporters of Seonjo's son by his principal queen, Prince Yeongchang, who was born in 1606, and his reign was marked by factional violence. The Great Northerner faction supported him; his opponents, including many Westerners and Southerners, resented the executions and exclusions that followed, and the death of Prince Yeongchang in 1614 and the confinement of Queen Dowager Inmok, Seonjo's principal wife, in 1618 gave them moral arguments that would be used in 1623. His government nonetheless carried out significant projects of reconstruction, as noted above, and it also dealt with the first stage of the Ming–Jin war.

In 1618, after the fall of Fushun, the Ming court asked Joseon to send troops to assist an expedition against Later Jin. The request placed Gwanghaegun's court in a dilemma that officials debated in detail. On one side stood the obligations of the Ming relationship and the repeatedly expressed gratitude for Ming aid in the Imjin War. On the other side stood concerns that Joseon troops were few, poorly suited for operations on the Liaodong plains, and likely to provoke a Jurchen response against the kingdom itself. Many officials supported sending troops; some counseled caution; Gwanghaegun himself hesitated, and the Border Defense Council's deliberations show how divided the court was. Ming pressure and the force of loyalty arguments eventually produced a decision to send a contingent of roughly thirteen thousand soldiers under General Gang Hong-rip, though numbers in the sources vary.

The Ming plan of 1619 divided its forces into four columns that were to converge on Hetu Ala. The Joseon contingent joined the eastern column under Ming commander Liu Ting. Nurhaci, operating on interior lines and with highly mobile banner troops, defeated the columns one after another at and around Sarhu in the third lunar month of 1619. Liu Ting's column was destroyed, and the Joseon force, caught at a distance from the main Ming armies and heavily outnumbered, suffered heavy losses. Gang Hong-rip and other senior officers surrendered to Nurhaci. Joseon sources and Qing sources differ over the circumstances and the number of casualties, and modern historians treat troop figures for all sides with caution. Later Jin reportedly released many ordinary soldiers while keeping senior officers, and Gang Hong-rip subsequently served as an intermediary between Nurhaci and the Joseon court.

A traditional account, preserved in sources compiled after Gwanghaegun's fall, holds that the king had privately instructed Gang Hong-rip to observe the situation and act according to circumstances rather than fight to the end, which would mean the surrender was carried out in accordance with royal policy. The accuracy of this account is contested, partly because the sources that preserve it were produced by officials who overthrew Gwanghaegun and who had reasons to portray him as disloyal to Ming or as a manipulator, and partly because other evidence suggests that the court's attitude was more ambivalent than any single instruction. Historians therefore generally describe Gwanghaegun's policy as an attempt to maintain Joseon's obligations to Ming while avoiding a direct confrontation with Later Jin, an attempt whose practical success was limited.

After Sarhu, Gwanghaegun's court tried to manage relations with both sides. It maintained diplomatic correspondence with Later Jin through Gang Hong-rip and other intermediaries, using careful wording that avoided accepting Jin claims of superiority. It continued to send tribute to Ming, though the sea route was substituted for the overland route that Later Jin controlled. It assisted Mao Wenlong's forces when they appeared on the coast in 1621 and 1622, providing food and shelter while seeking to limit the consequences of the Ming presence. These policies drew criticism from Ming officials who suspected Joseon of half-hearted loyalty, and from some Joseon officials who regarded any dealings with Later Jin as a betrayal of moral principle. The sources for Gwanghaegun's reign are discussed in the next feature.

Historical Debate

Was Gwanghaegun's Diplomacy Balanced Pragmatism, Improvisation, or Disloyalty?

The question: Did Gwanghaegun pursue a coherent policy of balancing between Ming and Later Jin, and if so, was it wise, effective, or morally defensible? The question matters because the same sources that narrate his foreign policy were shaped by the political purposes of the faction that deposed him.

Interpretation A — Prudent pragmatism: Some historians argue that Gwanghaegun grasped the military reality of Liaodong earlier than his critics and tried to protect Joseon from a war it could not win. On this view, his dealings with Later Jin and his reluctance to commit fully to Ming were reasonable responses to a rapidly changing balance of power, and his overthrow replaced a flexible strategy with an ideologically rigid one that contributed to the invasions of 1627 and 1636.
Interpretation B — Improvisation under constraint: Other scholars emphasize that Gwanghaegun had limited options and that his policy was less a coherent doctrine than a sequence of improvised responses to pressure from both Ming and Later Jin. In this view, he did not abandon Ming, continued to provide support to the Ming general on Pi Island, and dealt with Later Jin chiefly because Gang Hong-rip's captivity and the geography of the frontier made some communication unavoidable.
Interpretation C — A policy exaggerated or distorted by its critics: A third approach focuses on the sources themselves. The record of Gwanghaegun's reign was compiled under his successors and carries the marks of political victory. The coup's leaders justified their action partly by charging him with ingratitude toward Ming, so the sources may amplify evidence of his supposed disloyalty. On this view, the more important historical questions concern how the coup's rhetoric shaped later policy, and how much of what we think we know about Gwanghaegun's diplomacy comes from texts written to discredit him.

Evidence: The principal evidence includes the daily record of Gwanghaegun's reign, the records of the Border Defense Council, diplomatic correspondence with Ming and Later Jin, and Ming and Manchu sources that describe Joseon's conduct. Each type of source has limitations: Joseon court records reflect factional compilation, Ming records reflect suspicion of allies, and Manchu records describe Joseon from the perspective of a rival state.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern interpretation of intent, the reliability of sources compiled after 1623, and counterfactual questions about whether a different policy under Injo could have avoided the invasions. They do not concern the basic sequence of events.

Why it matters: If Gwanghaegun's diplomacy was wise, the 1623 coup appears to have exchanged flexible policy for ideological commitment at a high cost. If it was improvised or exaggerated by critics, the coup appears more as a political event than as a decisive turning point in foreign policy.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that would help determine whether Gwanghaegun deliberately balanced between Ming and Later Jin. Then explain why the political position of the people who compiled the surviving sources complicates the use of that evidence.

Section 36.4

The 1623 Coup and Strategic Reorientation

In the third lunar month of 1623, a group of officials and military officers, most of them associated with the Westerner faction and including figures such as Kim Ryu, Yi Gwi, and the military officer Yi Gwal, carried out a coup against Gwanghaegun. The conspirators secured the backing of the confined Queen Dowager Inmok, whose authority as Seonjo's principal widow gave the action a form of legitimacy, and they placed on the throne Neungyang-gun, a grandson of Seonjo, who became King Injo. Gwanghaegun was removed from the throne, exiled, and moved after the Qing invasion of 1637 to Jeju Island, where he died in 1641. The Great Northerner officials who had dominated his government were purged.

The coup's justification rested on several charges. The first concerned the death of Prince Yeongchang and the confinement of the Queen Dowager, acts that the conspirators framed as violations of Confucian family morality, since a king who mistreated his half-brother and his father's widow could not claim to rule virtuously. The second concerned fiscal and labor burdens associated with Gwanghaegun's palace construction, which weighed on already damaged communities. The third concerned foreign policy: the conspirators accused Gwanghaegun of ingratitude toward the Ming dynasty, in other words of betraying the debt Joseon owed for the Imjin War, and of dealing with Later Jin. Historians debate how much weight the foreign-policy charge carried in the planning of the coup compared with factional interest and moral outrage. What is clear is that, once in power, the new government made Ming loyalty a central element of its public identity.

The relationship between domestic legitimacy and foreign policy was close. Injo was a king whose accession was irregular, and his government needed recognition both inside Joseon and from Ming, since Ming investiture of a new king was a normal part of legitimate succession. The Ming court delayed granting recognition until 1625, partly out of doubt about the circumstances of the coup. During that interval, Injo's court had a strong incentive to demonstrate its loyalty, and one way to do so was to cooperate with Mao Wenlong, whose forces on Pi Island were the most visible Ming presence near Joseon. This cooperation burdened Joseon communities, which supplied grain and endured the depredations of Ming troops, and it exposed Joseon to Later Jin charges that the kingdom was harboring an enemy.

The new regime did not remain stable. In the first lunar month of 1624, Yi Gwal, who had been a leading military figure in the coup and who commanded northern forces, rose in rebellion. He was dissatisfied with the rewards he received and was also affected by accusations of disloyalty. His forces advanced toward Seoul, and the court, including Injo, fled the capital before the rebels were defeated near the city. The rebellion exposed fault lines within the coalition that had overthrown Gwanghaegun, drew off and then destroyed some of the most experienced northern troops, and left a set of rebel survivors who fled to Later Jin and urged it to attack Joseon. The rebellion thus linked domestic instability directly to the northern threat.

In response to these pressures, Injo's government undertook defensive measures. It created new military commands for the defense of the capital region, including the Eoyeongcheong and the Chongyungcheong, and began work on fortifications, including the large-scale reconstruction of the mountain fortress of Namhansanseong, which was completed in the mid-1620s with the labor of Buddhist monks as well as conscripted workers. The Chongyungcheong was responsible for the Namhansanseong garrison. These institutions would be tested in 1636 and would then be reorganized in the aftermath, as explained in Section 36.11. The government also sought to strengthen Joseon's position at the border, but its resources were limited, and the Yi Gwal rebellion had reduced the northern army's strength at the moment when Later Jin's power was growing.

Historical Vocabulary
Jaejo jieun and Sadae

Definition. Jaejo jieun (the favor of re-creation) is the phrase Joseon officials used for the debt owed to the Ming emperor for helping to restore the kingdom during the Imjin War. Sadae ("serving the great") is the Korean term for the practice of maintaining a ritual and diplomatic relationship of deference toward a larger state, in this period Ming.

Historical context. Joseon's relationship with Ming had been in place since 1392 and combined practical diplomacy with Confucian principles of hierarchy. After 1598 the phrase jaejo jieun became a standard element of court rhetoric, and its use intensified after the 1623 coup, when it served the new government as an argument against Gwanghaegun and a justification for its own policies.

Why it matters. The concept explains why many Joseon officials experienced Qing demands in the 1630s not just as strategic challenges but as moral crises, and why ideological loyalty to Ming persisted after Ming's fall.

Potential misunderstanding. Sadae should not be translated simply as "servility." Under the Ming relationship Joseon kept its own government, laws, and domestic ritual calendar, and officials debated what the relationship required in practice. Nor should jaejo jieun be treated as pure ideology: the Ming armies had in fact fought in Joseon, and the memory of that aid was widely shared. But the same phrase was also a political instrument, used selectively by factions to argue for or against particular policies.

Section 36.5

The Invasion of 1627

The first invasion arose from several converging causes. Later Jin faced economic strain in the mid-1620s, with food shortages in Liaodong, disruptions to trade after the conquests, and the demands of supplying an expanding army. Mao Wenlong's base on Pi Island posed a persistent threat to the state's rear, since his forces could raid the coast and keep Ming resistance alive near Later Jin's southeastern flank. The rebels from Yi Gwal's circle who had fled to Later Jin encouraged an attack and offered information about Joseon's weaknesses. Politically, Hong Taiji, who had taken power in 1626 after Nurhaci's death and after the Ningyuan setback, needed a success that would consolidate his position among the beile, and a campaign against Joseon offered a chance to secure supplies, neutralize the threat on his flank, and prepare for operations against Ming. These causes operated together, and the sources do not allow historians to assign precise weights to them.

A Later Jin force commanded by Amin, a nephew of Nurhaci and a senior beile, crossed the Amnok in the first lunar month of 1627. Estimates of its size, which Joseon and Manchu sources do not agree on, are often placed at around thirty thousand, drawn mainly from banner units. The Jin army moved quickly against Joseon's northwestern border fortresses, taking Uiju and other positions with relative ease. At Anju, the commander Nam I-heung is recorded in Joseon sources as having set fire to the powder stores and died rather than surrender, a story that entered later memory as an example of loyalty. Pyeongyang was abandoned, and the Jin armies pushed south toward Hwanghae Province.

The court's response was to withdraw. Injo and the main court moved to Ganghwa Island, the island at the mouth of the Han River that had served as a refuge for the Goryeo court during the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century (Chapter 23). The island was a logical choice: Manchu forces, who lacked a navy and had little experience with amphibious operations, would find it difficult to attack. Crown Prince Sohyeon was sent south to Jeonju to head a subsidiary court (bunjo), a practice that preserved the dynasty's continuity if the king were captured. Irregular forces and volunteer fighters in the northwest, some of them organized by local officials and officers, harassed Jin foragers and supply lines, and their activity influenced Jin's calculations about how far it could advance.

Negotiations began quickly. Later Jin did not seek to occupy Joseon permanently; its goals were limited to extracting an agreement that would secure the rear, supply provisions, and establish Joseon's recognition of its status. Joseon, for its part, sought to end the campaign without conceding what its officials regarded as the essential principle, the Ming connection. The result was an agreement concluded in the third lunar month of 1627, in which Later Jin and Joseon were to treat each other as brother states. Joseon agreed to cease hostile actions against Later Jin and to allow trade and exchange along the border, and Later Jin agreed to withdraw its forces. Later Jin demanded a royal prince as a hostage; Joseon instead sent a royal relative, presenting him as the king's younger brother, a maneuver that satisfied the form of the demand without surrendering a member of the king's immediate family. The accounts of how the question of Joseon's use of Ming reign names in correspondence with Later Jin was handled differ, and Joseon sources emphasize that the court retained its Ming allegiance.

The settlement's character is important. It was an agreement between nominally equal states, a "brotherhood," in which Later Jin took the senior position of an elder brother in the language of some accounts, and it did not reduce Joseon to tributary status or establish any Qing-style hierarchy. Joseon retained its Ming relationship, continued to receive Ming officials, and continued to support Mao Wenlong, though at a cost. The settlement also left unresolved the issues that would reopen conflict: Joseon's continuing alignment with Ming, Later Jin's need for grain and goods, and Hong Taiji's ambitions. Students should resist reading the settlement as a failed first step toward submission in 1637. It was, at the time, a negotiated outcome that both sides understood in different ways and that left several futures open.

Historical Vocabulary
Horan, Jeongmyo, and Byeongja

Definition. Horan (胡亂) is the traditional Korean name for the Manchu invasions; ho was a term applied to northern and western peoples, and ran means disturbance or disorder. The invasions are named by the sexagenary cyclical year: the Jeongmyo Horan (1627) and the Byeongja Horan (1636–1637).

Historical context. Korean writing used the sixty-year cycle of paired terms to designate years, and wars and political events were conventionally named after the year in which they began. The name horan reflects the perspective of the Joseon court, which regarded the Manchus as barbarians in the Sinocentric vocabulary of the time, and the label is an example of how language records cultural attitudes.

Why it matters. Names for wars carry interpretations. Using horan without comment would adopt a seventeenth-century Joseon elite's view of the invaders, while using neutral terms such as "first Manchu invasion" and "second Manchu invasion" describes the events without importing that judgment.

Potential misunderstanding. Modern Korean usage sometimes treats horan as a neutral historical label, and Korean scholarship now often uses alternatives. This textbook uses "the invasion of 1627" and "the invasion of 1636–1637" and notes the traditional names where relevant.

Section 36.6

The Uneasy Interval, 1627–1636

The nine years between the invasions were not a period of stable peace. The brotherhood arrangement of 1627 depended on shared understandings that neither side held. For Later Jin, and from 1636 the Qing, the relationship was a means of securing Joseon's neutrality and supplies while it prosecuted the war against Ming. For Joseon's court, it was a temporary expedient that preserved the kingdom and left its fundamental loyalty intact. Disputes over border trade, the treatment of refugees and fugitives, the quantity of goods and horses Joseon was expected to provide, and the language of diplomatic correspondence arose repeatedly. Hong Taiji's government pressed for larger payments and for a more clearly hierarchical relationship, and Joseon's court responded by delaying, supplying less than requested, and sometimes complying under pressure.

Hong Taiji's wider campaigns changed the balance. In 1629 and again in 1632 Manchu armies bypassed the main Ming defenses at Shanhaiguan and raided deep into northern China. He pursued the submission of the eastern Mongol groups, and with the defeat of the Chahar Mongol leader Ligdan Khan and the surrender of his successors in 1634–1635, the Manchu state acquired a seal associated with the Yuan dynasty, which Hong Taiji used to support claims to universal rulership. Chinese commanders who had served under Mao Wenlong and in Ming coastal commands, including Kong Youde, Geng Zhongming, and Shang Kexi, defected to the Manchu side in the early 1630s and brought with them expertise in artillery and shipping. Mao Wenlong himself had been killed in 1629 by a Ming general who distrusted him, which removed the main Ming presence on the coast and left its followers divided. In 1635 Hong Taiji adopted the name Manchu, and in the fourth lunar month of 1636 he proclaimed himself emperor of the Great Qing at Shenyang.

Students should distinguish Later Jin from the Qing. Later Jin was the state founded by Nurhaci in 1616, still presenting itself in the frame of a Jurchen khanate and, in Chinese historiography, a successor to the earlier Jin dynasty. The Qing, proclaimed in 1636, claimed imperial status in the Chinese tradition and implied a rejection of the Ming emperor's primacy. The change of name was accompanied by institutional changes, including the expansion of the banner system to include Mongol and, later, Han units, and the adoption of Chinese-style administrative offices, and it altered the diplomatic vocabulary available for Joseon relations. A kingdom that had accepted a "brotherhood" with a khan could not easily accept the same relationship with an emperor who claimed to rule as the Ming emperor did.

Within Joseon, the interval produced a sharpening argument. Officials who favored accommodation, called the peace party (jumhwa) in later usage, argued that Joseon lacked the strength to resist a Manchu army, that the kingdom's survival required flexibility, and that Confucian thought allowed for expedient measures (gwon) in circumstances of necessity. Choe Myeong-gil became the best-known advocate of this position. Officials who rejected accommodation, the anti-peace party (cheokhwa), among them Kim Sang-heon and the censorial officials Hong Ik-han, Yun Jip, and O Dal-je, argued that submission to a barbarian ruler violated fundamental ethical principles, that it would betray the Ming benefactor, and that moral steadfastness was the foundation of any durable defense. These were not simple factions in the sense of fixed political parties. The two positions cut across the Westerner-led coalition, and individual officials changed emphasis as circumstances changed.

Joseon's military preparations during these years were real but limited. The court continued work on fortifications, maintained the new capital commands, and ordered provincial officials to prepare for war. Resources were scarce, and the northern garrisons remained thin. The government also faced pressure from Ming, which asked for continued support, from the Qing, which asked for goods and troops, and from the countryside, where tax collection and labor service continued to weigh on communities. In the spring of 1636 the situation reached a crisis. Joseon envoys sent to Shenyang, including Na Deok-heon and Yi Gwak, were present at the ceremonies surrounding the proclamation of the Qing and refused to perform the full kowtow before Hong Taiji, a gesture that Joseon sources record and that the Qing treated as an insult. Qing envoys who then went to Seoul found the court unwilling to receive their letters, and they departed in alarm. A Joseon instruction calling on provincial officials to prepare for war, which reportedly fell into Qing hands, strengthened the conviction in Shenyang that Joseon intended to resist. The Qing prepared a major expedition.

Section 36.7

The Qing Proclamation and the Second Invasion, 1636–1637

The Qing demands of 1636 were different from those of 1627. They asked not only for goods and cooperation but for the acceptance of a hierarchy in which Joseon would be a subordinate state, in which its king and crown prince would be sent to Shenyang, and in which the officials who had advocated war would be surrendered. Joseon's court rejected these demands. Its reasons included the ideological commitment to Ming, a belief that the kingdom's mountainous terrain and fortresses would allow it to hold out, and an expectation, which proved wrong, that the Qing would not attack in the depth of winter. Some officials also hoped that a firm stance would produce a negotiated compromise like that of 1627. The decision was made through a series of debates in which hardliners held the moral advantage, and in which advocates of negotiation, notably Choe Myeong-gil, tried to keep channels open.

Hong Taiji personally led the expedition, which departed Shenyang in the eleventh lunar month and crossed the Amnok on the eighth day of the twelfth lunar month of 1636 (January 3, 1637). The army included Manchu, Mongol, and Han banner troops. Its size is uncertain: modern estimates are commonly placed somewhere around one hundred thousand to over one hundred twenty thousand, while Joseon sources sometimes give higher figures that reflect alarm and rhetoric as much as counting. The Qing strategy differed sharply from the 1627 campaign. Rather than reduce border fortresses one by one, the army bypassed them and sent a fast-moving vanguard under the commander Mafuta directly toward Seoul. Joseon's border fortresses were left isolated, and its provincial forces were unable to concentrate before the vanguard arrived. The vanguard reached the vicinity of Seoul within about five days of crossing the border, according to Joseon records.

The court's plan was to repeat the 1627 evacuation to Ganghwa. Injo, the crown prince, and part of the court set out for the island, but the road was already threatened by Qing cavalry, and the weather, according to the sources, added to the difficulty. Joseon officials, including Choe Myeong-gil, were sent to the Qing vanguard to negotiate, partly in the hope of gaining time. The king turned instead toward Namhansanseong, the mountain fortress to the southeast prepared during the 1620s. The members of the royal family and officials' families who had gone ahead to Ganghwa, among them Prince Bongnim, remained on the island. The decision to take refuge in the mountain fortress rather than on the island placed the court in a location from which relief might be expected but from which escape was difficult.

The failure of the Ganghwa plan reflected several factors. The Qing advance was faster than the court's planning assumed; the Qing had learned from 1627 not to let the court slip away; and Chinese defectors in Qing service now provided artillery and naval skills that made an amphibious assault on the island possible. It also reflected the structural weakness of Joseon's defenses. The provincial armies were scattered, the northern garrisons were small, and the central capital forces, though reformed, were not capable of challenging the Qing army in open battle.

Cause and Effect

The Second Manchu Invasion of Joseon, 1636–1637

Long-term causes

Joseon's century-long ritual and ideological bond with Ming, intensified by the memory of Ming aid in the Imjin War; the collapse of Ming control in Liaodong; the institutional strength of the Manchu banner state; the weakness of Joseon's northern defenses after the war and the 1624 rebellion.

Short-term causes

The unresolved disputes of the 1627 settlement; Hong Taiji's conquest of the Mongols and proclamation of the Qing; Chinese defections that gave the Qing artillery and naval capacity; the refusal of Joseon envoys to kowtow in 1636 and the hostile atmosphere at the Joseon court.

Immediate trigger and event

The Qing demand for a ruler–subject relationship, Joseon's rejection, and the Qing crossing of the Amnok in the twelfth lunar month of 1636, followed by the bypassing of fortresses, the failure of the Ganghwa plan, and the siege of Namhansanseong.

Immediate effects

Mass casualties and displacement along the army's route; the fall of Ganghwa on the twenty-second day of the first lunar month of 1637; the king's surrender at Samjeondo; the taking of captives and hostages.

Long-term consequences: Joseon's formal subordination to Qing and the severance of its Ming ties; reorganization of its military commands; the persistence of Ming loyalism as a political and intellectual current; a long-lived memory of humiliation and suffering. Historical complexity: No single cause was sufficient. A different Joseon policy in 1636 might have changed the timing or terms of the war but would not necessarily have avoided Qing pressure, since Hong Taiji's strategic aims included securing his rear before the main campaign against Ming. Historians disagree about how much weight to give Joseon's decisions versus Qing strategy.

Section 36.8

Namhansanseong and the Struggle over Surrender

Namhansanseong stands on Mount Namhan, about twenty-five kilometers southeast of Seoul. Its walls follow ridgelines and enclose a sloping basin with limited level ground. The site had been fortified earlier in Korean history, and Injo's government substantially rebuilt and expanded it in the 1620s. It was a strong position against direct assault, but its military value depended on two conditions: the garrison needed enough food, fuel, and warm clothing to hold out, and relief armies needed to reach it from the provinces. Neither condition held in the winter of 1636–1637. The court entered the fortress on the fourteenth day of the twelfth lunar month (January 9, 1637) with a garrison commonly estimated at around thirteen thousand, though figures vary, and with stores of grain generally understood to be enough for roughly six weeks. The fortress held not only soldiers but the king, the crown prince, ministers, officials, servants, and refugees from the capital region.

The Qing army encircled the fortress and used its numbers and its mobility to screen the approaches. Its artillery, of the kind often called Hongyi cannon after a design of European origin adapted in China, and operated in part by Chinese defectors, was used against the walls and gates, though the fortress's position limited its effect. The Qing also made a point of defeating relief columns. Joseon provincial forces from Chungcheong, Jeolla, Gyeongsang, and other regions moved toward the capital area, but they were scattered, poorly coordinated, and often intercepted before they could concentrate. Some units were destroyed in engagements near the fortress; others halted at a distance. No Ming force could come: Ming armies were occupied with internal rebellions and the northern war, and the Ming court was in no position to send help. The siege thus took place in military isolation.

Inside the walls, conditions deteriorated. The weather was severe, with deep cold and snow, and soldiers on the walls had inadequate winter clothing; sources record frostbite and exhaustion. Food was rationed, and the fortress's garrison, drawn from the new capital commands, Buddhist monk-soldiers organized under their own commanders, and others, was stretched along the walls. Sorties against Qing positions had mixed results; in some cases defenders inflicted losses, in others they suffered them. The siege is commonly described as lasting about forty-five days; the Academy of Korean Studies chronology yields 47 days when the first and last days are counted inclusively, from January 9 to the surrender on February 24, 1637. Secondary works sometimes shift dates by weeks by mislabeling lunar dates as Gregorian.

The central political drama concerned whether to continue to resist or to negotiate surrender. Letters passed between Hong Taiji's camp and the fortress. Qing demands included the king's emergence from the fortress, the acceptance of ruler–subject status, and the handing over of officials identified as instigators of war. Debates at court were heated. Advocates of resistance, including Kim Sang-heon, argued that submitting to a barbarian ruler abandoned the Ming benefactor and violated the moral foundations of the dynasty, and that honor required fighting to the end if necessary. Advocates of negotiation, led by Choe Myeong-gil, argued that the fortress could not hold, that the lives of the king and the people were at stake, and that the Confucian tradition permitted flexibility in an emergency. Joseon tradition preserves an anecdote that Kim Sang-heon tore up a draft surrender letter and that Choe Myeong-gil gathered and reassembled the pieces. Whether or not the incident occurred exactly as described, it captures how the argument was remembered.

Treating these arguments as patriotism versus cowardice distorts them. Both sides were appealing to principles that their culture regarded as authoritative. The hardliners were not necessarily indifferent to suffering; they believed that moral fidelity was itself a condition of dynastic survival and that surrender would have consequences for Joseon's political and ethical life that physical survival could not compensate for. The accommodationists were not necessarily indifferent to principle; they argued that a ruler's primary duty was the preservation of the altars of the state (jongsa) and the lives of his people, and that rigid refusal in the face of overwhelming force could destroy both. Their disagreement concerned the weighing of values and the interpretation of military facts, not the existence of values.

The decisive event was the fall of Ganghwa Island. A Qing force under Prince Dorgon, Hong Taiji's younger half-brother, crossed to the island in boats, with Chinese defectors providing naval expertise, and overcame the defenders on the twenty-second day of the first lunar month of 1637 (February 16). The island's commander, Kim Gyeong-jing, is recorded in Joseon sources as having failed to organize an effective defense. Many women and officials' families were captured, including members of the royal clan; among those taken was Prince Bongnim. Joseon sources record deaths by suicide among women and officials who preferred death to capture, among them the former official Kim Sang-yong, who is said to have ignited gunpowder at the gate. When the news reached Namhansanseong, the court's remaining argument for continued resistance largely evaporated. The possibility of holding out for relief from the provinces had already faded, and the Qing now held hostages from among the royal family.

Primary Source Window

Na Man-gap's Byeongja Rok (Diary of 1636) on the Siege of Namhansanseong

Creator: Na Man-gap (1592–1642), a Joseon official who was at Namhansanseong during the siege · Date: events of the twelfth lunar month of 1636 to the first lunar month of 1637; composed in the following years · Type: participant's diary and memoir, with later arrangement and commentary

Context: Na Man-gap recorded the events of the invasion and siege from the perspective of an official within the besieged court. The text covers the departure from Seoul, the debates in the fortress, conditions in the garrison, the military situation, and the negotiations leading to surrender. Surviving versions of the text circulate in manuscript and printed forms, and scholars working with it use critical editions and compare it with other accounts.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of the diary's general content, not a direct quotation.

The diary describes the cold and hunger endured by soldiers on the walls, the repeated failure of expected relief forces to arrive, arguments among ministers over whether to send letters to the Qing camp, and the sense of dread that spread through the fortress as news of Ganghwa arrived. It also records judgments about particular officials and commanders, sometimes harshly.

Analysis: As an account by a participant, the diary preserves details unavailable in official records: how people experienced the cold, how rumors spread, how officials spoke about one another, and how the decision to surrender was reached. It is therefore valuable for social and political history, not just military narrative.

Limitations: A diary is not a neutral transcript. Parts of the work were evidently shaped after the events, and the author reports hearsay and expresses opinions, particularly about officials whose conduct he judged. Dates, numbers, and attributions of speech may reflect memory or later arrangement, so historians compare the text with the royal annals, the records of the Royal Secretariat, and Qing documents. The text does not by itself establish casualty numbers, troop figures, or the motives of Qing commanders.

Perspective: The author wrote as a member of the Joseon official class who shared the court's assumptions about hierarchy and legitimacy. The voices of common soldiers, monk-soldiers, women in the fortress, captives, and the Qing commanders themselves appear only indirectly or not at all.

Section 36.9

Samjeondo and the Qing Settlement

On the thirtieth day of the first lunar month of 1637 (February 24), Injo left Namhansanseong by the west gate and went to Samjeondo, a ferry crossing on the Han River where Hong Taiji had established a ceremonial platform. Joseon records describe the king wearing plain clothing rather than royal robes. Injo performed the ritual of three kneelings and nine head-knockings, the full kowtow required of a subordinate before the Qing emperor, and acknowledged the Qing ruler as his sovereign. Hong Taiji accepted the submission and allowed Injo to return. The scene entered Korean memory as one of national humiliation, and it is often referred to in Korean usage by the name of the site.

The terms imposed on Joseon, as reconstructed from Joseon and Qing sources, included the following. Joseon was to renounce its relationship with Ming, cease using Ming reign names, and surrender the documents and seals through which Ming had invested its kings. It was to recognize the Qing emperor as its sovereign and use Qing reign names in its diplomatic correspondence. It was to send the crown prince and another royal prince, along with the sons or relatives of senior officials, as hostages to Shenyang. It was to provide troops, ships, and supplies when the Qing asked for them for campaigns against Ming. It was to deliver regular tribute in goods and precious metals, and it was not to repair or build fortifications without Qing approval. It was to hand over the officials identified as instigators of the war. Specific tribute quantities are reported differently in different sources, and some were adjusted in later negotiations. Students should treat exact figures with caution.

The terms are best understood as establishing a relationship of subordination without annexation. Joseon remained a state with its own king, government, laws, and territory. The Qing did not station a garrison in Seoul or take over the administration of the kingdom, and the Qing's dealings with Joseon were handled through the Board of Rites rather than through the office that managed Mongol and other inner-Asian peoples, a distinction that scholars read as an acknowledgment of Joseon's place within a Sinitic ritual order. At the same time, the relationship was not nominal. The hostages, the tribute, the military obligations, the restrictions on fortification, and the requirement to break with Ming imposed real constraints and served real Qing interests.

The Qing used its new authority almost immediately. Joseon troops and ships were required to take part in the Qing campaign against Mao Wenlong's former base on Pi Island in 1637. Further requests for troops followed in the Qing campaigns of the following years. The three officials identified as leaders of the anti-peace position, Hong Ik-han, Yun Jip, and O Dal-je, were taken to Shenyang and executed there. Kim Sang-heon, who had opposed submission at the fortress, was later taken to Shenyang as well after he opposed further military cooperation, and he returned in 1645. Crown Prince Sohyeon and Prince Bongnim went to Shenyang as hostages with their households and retainers; after the Qing conquest of Beijing in 1644 the Joseon entourage moved to the new capital, and the princes returned to Joseon in 1645. In 1639 the Qing had a monument erected at Samjeondo, with inscriptions in Manchu, Mongolian, and Chinese, praising the Qing emperor's virtue and recording Joseon's submission; the monument was written by a Joseon official under Qing instruction, a fact that later generations remembered with discomfort.

Joseon's court did not accept the new order as a simple matter of fact. Officials debated how to describe it, how to implement its demands, and how to preserve what could be preserved. Ritual matters were especially contested, and some officials sought ways of maintaining internal commemorations of Ming. Others sought to reduce the tribute burden and to ransom captives. The relationship was therefore not a single moment of submission but a long process of negotiation, adaptation, and quiet resistance.

Section 36.10

The Human Consequences

The costs of the invasions were borne unevenly. In 1627 the armies passed through Pyeongan and Hwanghae Provinces, plundering for food and fuel and leaving destruction in their wake. In 1636–1637 the Qing army's route through Pyeongan, Hwanghae, and Gyeonggi, and its encirclement of the capital region, caused destruction along the major roads, the flight of populations, and the seizure of grain, livestock, and goods. Reliable counts of civilian deaths do not exist. The Joseon and Qing records emphasize military events and the fates of officials, and the people most affected, farmers, artisans, women, and children, left few records of their own. Historians infer the scale of the losses from references to depopulated villages, abandoned fields, famine, disease, and the long period of recovery that followed.

Captivity was among the most consequential experiences. The Qing army carried away large numbers of Joseon people at the war's end, including civilians seized in the countryside, people taken at Ganghwa, and soldiers. Estimates of the total vary widely. Figures of several hundred thousand, often five hundred thousand to six hundred thousand, appear in Korean sources and in much modern writing, but many of these figures trace back to contemporary memorials and rhetorical statements rather than to systematic counts, and some modern scholars argue for substantially lower totals. No exact number is known. What is clear is that the number was large enough to create a major social and political problem in Joseon and a significant labor resource in the Qing.

Captives were taken to Shenyang and the surrounding region, where many were assigned to banner households as bondservants, a status that carried obligations of labor and could be hereditary. Some were put to agricultural work on the Manchurian frontier, and others served in households or workshops. Captives had limited legal recourse, and runaway captives were pursued by banner owners and by Qing officials, who also pressed Joseon to return fugitives. A system of ransom developed in which families, and sometimes the Joseon government, paid to redeem captives, and a market for the buying and selling of captives operated near Shenyang. Ransom prices varied with the captive's age, skills, and social rank, and the wealthiest and best-connected families were better placed to redeem relatives. The poor often could not. The ransom trade thus reproduced the inequalities of Joseon society in a new setting.

Women's experiences were shaped both by captivity and by the way Joseon society treated returnees. Joseon's elite ideology placed great emphasis on female chastity, and the court and families faced difficult decisions about women who had been taken to Manchuria and who returned after ransom or escape. Officials debated whether men whose wives had been taken could remarry, and whether returning women should be treated as ritually and socially impure. The court is recorded as having discouraged the repudiation of returned wives, and later tradition tells of rituals of purification at a stream near Seoul. A folk etymology links the later derogatory term for women to the word for returning women, an explanation that scholars regard as doubtful, but its persistence shows how deeply the experience entered social memory. Joseon sources also record, and later Confucian commemoration emphasized, the suicides of women at Ganghwa and elsewhere, a body of evidence that reflects both real events and the way elite writers shaped them into moral narratives.

Everyday Life and Social History

War, Captivity, and Return in the Countryside

For most inhabitants of the northwest and the capital region, the invasions were not debates about ritual hierarchy but sudden disruptions of household life. Farmers fled before the armies, abandoning fields and stores; families were separated in flight; and those who stayed risked seizure of grain and animals. The northwestern provinces, whose people had long been regarded by the central court as peripheral and had limited access to high office, had already borne the burdens of frontier defense and of hosting Ming troops before the invasions began, and they suffered first and most heavily. Regional military garrisons, tax-farming households, and fortress laborers all faced new demands.

Captivity created households divided across a border. A farmer carried to Shenyang might labor for a banner owner for years; a wife left behind might manage the farm alone or be forced to remarry; children might grow up in a household in Manchuria. When ransom was possible, it often required the sale of land or livestock, deepening poverty even for those who recovered their relatives. For those who returned, reintegration could be difficult, with suspicion, stigma, and disputes over property and marriage. The hostage princes in Shenyang also had to deal with captive questions; Joseon records report that Crown Prince Sohyeon's household in Shenyang became involved in efforts to assist captives, though the details of this involvement come largely from later and partisan accounts.

The sources for these experiences are limited and skewed toward elite memory. Court records, diaries of officials, and later commemorative writings dominate. Historians supplement them with local records, genealogical compilations that note family losses, and later oral traditions, using them with care. The ordinary people who endured captivity and return appear mostly as numbers and as exemplars in moral narratives, which is itself a historical problem to be recognized.

Section 36.11

Aftermath: Military Institutions, Ming Loyalism, and a New East Asian Order

The invasions reshaped Joseon's military institutions. The capital defense commands created in the 1620s were retained and consolidated with the Military Training Agency into what later became the Five Military Commands of the capital: the Military Training Agency, the Eoyeongcheong, the Chongyungcheong, the Sugeocheong, and the Royal Guard Command, the last created later in the century. Namhansanseong and Ganghwa Island remained part of the defensive system, and the court gave continuing attention to musket training and fortress defense. The Qing prohibition on repairing or building fortifications constrained these efforts, and the court pursued them cautiously and with attempts at concealment. Enforcement of the Qing restrictions was uneven, and Joseon's capacity to resist remained limited while the Qing remained powerful. Later developments in military reform, including those of the eighteenth century, are addressed in Chapter 37.

Joseon's international position changed fundamentally. The kingdom became a tributary of the Qing, sending regular missions to Beijing after the Qing established its capital there in 1644, delivering tribute, and receiving the Qing calendar. Joseon was also expected to supply troops when asked, and in the 1650s Joseon musketeers were sent to join Qing forces against Russian expeditions on the Amur River. At the same time, Joseon retained its own government, laws, and social order. The envoy missions to Beijing became channels for trade, information, books, and ideas, and they would later be important sources for intellectual currents discussed in Chapter 38. The relationship was thus asymmetrical but also productive of new connections.

The collapse of the Ming in 1644 transformed the geopolitical setting. In the spring of 1644, rebel forces under Li Zicheng took Beijing and the last Chongzhen emperor died by his own hand. A Ming general, Wu Sangui, admitted Qing forces through the Shanhaiguan pass, and Qing troops entered Beijing on the second day of the fifth lunar month of 1644 (June 6). The Qing leadership subsequently decided to make Beijing its capital. The fall of the Ming court in Beijing did not end Ming resistance: Southern Ming regimes continued the struggle for years, and Ming loyalist movements persisted into the 1660s and beyond, although the Ming no longer ruled China as a unified dynasty. The Joseon hostage princes, who accompanied the Qing army to Beijing, returned to Joseon in 1645. Sohyeon died soon after his return, in circumstances that remain disputed; Prince Bongnim then became crown prince and, on Injo's death in 1649, King Hyojong.

Ming loyalism persisted in Joseon despite the formal submission. Many officials and scholars continued to regard the Ming as the legitimate source of civilization and the Qing as an alien conquest, and they found ways to express this view, including private commemoration of Ming rulers and, in some writings, the use of the last Ming reign name or cyclical year designations instead of Qing era names. Hyojong planned a military campaign against the Qing, often called the Northern Expedition, a plan that was not carried out but that shaped the rhetoric of his reign, and scholars such as Song Si-yeol became leading voices for Ming loyalist thought. Later developments, including the sense that Joseon had become the preserver of authentic civilization after the fall of Ming, belong to the political and intellectual history treated in Chapter 37 and Chapter 38. What matters here is the combination of practical subordination and ideological loyalty, a combination that shaped Joseon political culture for generations.

The Qing relationship also had effects on Qing policy. A Joseon that was reliably quiescent secured Qing's eastern flank during the conquest of China, and the Qing's treatment of Joseon, relatively light compared to its treatment of conquered Chinese territories, reflected that interest. For Joseon, the era of Ming predominance, within which it had defined its identity since 1392, ended. The new order was a Qing-centered tributary system in which Joseon had a defined place, and the kingdom's politics, intellectual life, and memory had to adjust. The invasions thus concluded a century of crisis that began with Japanese invasion, and they opened a period in which Joseon recovered and developed but remained marked by what it had endured.

The Question of Surrender in the Winter of 1636–1637

From the perspective of officials favoring accommodation: The fortress cannot be held, relief has failed, and the duty of the ruler is to preserve the dynasty and the lives of those under him. From the perspective of officials favoring resistance: Surrender to a barbarian ruler violates the ethical basis of the state and betrays the Ming benefactor. Moral steadfastness is itself part of how a dynasty endures, and a ruler who yields on principle cannot reclaim it later.
From the perspective of Hong Taiji's government: Joseon's rejection of the new imperial order and its continued ties to Ming threaten the Qing's rear, and a decisive victory will secure supplies, hostages, and an unambiguous acknowledgment of Qing sovereignty before the main campaign against Ming.
From the perspective of soldiers and commoners in and around the fortress: Cold, hunger, and fear dominate. For many, the question is not honor but survival, and the decisions of the court determine whether their families will be killed, enslaved, or left alone. Their views are rarely recorded directly.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Joseon's Place in East Asia, 1592–1650

What Changed

  • Joseon's formal suzerain shifted from the Ming to the Qing, with new tribute, hostage, and military obligations
  • The land border to the northwest moved from a Ming-governed buffer to a frontier with a rising imperial state
  • Capital-region military commands were created and consolidated
  • Joseon elites faced a new problem: how to live under a dynasty they regarded as culturally inferior

What Stayed the Same

  • The Joseon dynasty, its king, its laws, and its territory continued
  • The tributary form of diplomacy persisted, with a new center
  • Confucian political thought and the moral language of loyalty remained central
  • Rural communities continued to bear the heaviest burdens of war and taxation

Pattern and timing: The change was abrupt in 1637 for diplomacy but gradual and contested for ideology, since Ming loyalism persisted. Court officials, hostages, and families of captives experienced change most directly, while the structure of local government and agriculture showed greater continuity. The pattern matters because it shows that subordination and cultural identity can diverge, a theme that recurs in later chapters.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how Joseon confronted the transformation of Northeast Asian power from Ming predominance toward the emerging Qing order, and why that confrontation led to the invasions of 1627 and 1636–1637. The central argument is that the outcome was produced by the interaction of several processes: the institutional growth of a Jurchen-Manchu state, the collapse of Ming control in Liaodong, Joseon's postwar vulnerability and ideological commitment to Ming, and the political dynamics of a Joseon court whose legitimacy was entangled with foreign policy.

After the Imjin War, Joseon was a damaged but functioning state. It reconstructed its fiscal and administrative systems and reformed parts of its military, but its northern frontier remained thinly defended. Its relationship with Ming combined real gratitude for wartime assistance with a rhetoric of obligation that later factions used for political purposes. The chapter cautioned against two inferences: that the Imjin War caused Ming decline or Manchu expansion, and that it left Joseon helpless.

The Jurchen-Manchu state arose through the work of Nurhaci and his associates, but its power rested on institutions rather than a single personality. The Eight Banners combined military, administrative, and productive functions; collective leadership by senior princes gave way under Hong Taiji to stronger central authority; and the state incorporated Mongol and Chinese populations and drew on Chinese defectors for artillery and naval skills. Ming frontier weakness, economic disruption in Liaodong, and possibly climatic stress contributed to the context in which this expansion occurred.

Gwanghaegun's government, caught between Ming requests and Later Jin power, sent a contingent to the disastrous Ming campaign of 1619, whose commander surrendered at Sarhu. His subsequent diplomacy has been interpreted as pragmatic balance, improvisation, or disloyalty, and the debate is complicated by the fact that the sources for his reign were compiled by the faction that overthrew him. The chapter emphasized that neither perfect neutrality nor simple betrayal describes the surviving evidence.

The 1623 coup placed Injo on the throne with an explicit emphasis on Ming loyalty and moral critique of Gwanghaegun. The new regime's legitimacy depended partly on Ming recognition and partly on the ideological coherence of its foreign policy, which tied it to Mao Wenlong's presence on Pi Island and exposed Joseon to Later Jin retaliation. Yi Gwal's rebellion in 1624 further weakened the northern army and sent rebel survivors to Later Jin.

The invasion of 1627, led by Amin under Hong Taiji's new government, arose from Later Jin economic needs, the threat from Mao Wenlong's base, refugee encouragement, and Hong Taiji's political needs. It ended with a brotherhood settlement between nominally equal states that left Joseon's Ming allegiance intact but did not resolve the underlying tensions. The interval that followed saw tribute disputes, factional hardening between advocates of accommodation and resistance, Qing conquest of the Mongols, Chinese defections, and the proclamation of the Qing in 1636, which made the old compromise untenable.

The second invasion, led personally by Hong Taiji, bypassed Joseon's border fortresses and reached the capital region within days, frustrating the Ganghwa evacuation plan. The court's refuge at Namhansanseong lasted about forty-five to forty-seven days, depending on how days are counted, ending after the fall of Ganghwa Island and the capture of royal family members. The debate over surrender involved serious ethical and strategic arguments on both sides, and the chapter urged students to avoid reducing it to patriotism versus cowardice. Injo's submission at Samjeondo established Joseon's subordination to the Qing without annexation: its king, laws, and territory persisted, while it accepted tribute, hostage, military, and fortification obligations.

The human consequences included civilian deaths, flight, destruction along the armies' routes, captivity of a large but uncertain number of people, forced labor, ransom, and the difficult return of survivors, especially women, to a society with strict ideas about chastity. These experiences are poorly documented in elite sources, and historians must read them against the grain and with attention to who produced the records.

The aftermath reshaped Joseon's military institutions, its diplomatic position, and its political culture. The Qing replaced Ming as the center of a tributary order, the Ming dynasty itself collapsed in 1644, and Ming loyalism persisted among Joseon elites as a powerful ideological current alongside practical subordination. These developments conclude Part VI and set the terms for the political, intellectual, and cultural history of later Joseon examined in Part VII.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter concludes a century of military crisis that began with Japanese invasion and ended with Manchu predominance. Together, Part VI shows a state repeatedly tested by powerful neighbors, damaged but not destroyed, and forced to reconsider its place in the East Asian order. The Manchu invasions matter because they transformed Joseon's diplomatic position, exposed the limits of its military organization, and created a lasting problem of memory and identity.

The chapter also reveals how foreign relations and domestic politics intertwined. The 1623 coup made Ming loyalty a basis of legitimacy, which narrowed policy options in the 1620s and 1630s. The argument at Namhansanseong shows how Confucian ethical language could support opposite strategies, and how historians must treat those arguments as serious rather than as cover for cowardice or bravado. It likewise shows that the experiences of captives, refugees, and rural communities lie behind the court's decisions and require sources and methods beyond the official record.

The consequences extended well beyond 1637. Formal Qing suzerainty gave Joseon a stable if asymmetrical place in a new order and created channels, especially the missions to Beijing, through which goods and ideas moved. Ming loyalism, meanwhile, became a durable element of elite political culture, shaping debates about civilization, legitimacy, and the meaning of Joseon's own tradition. These themes lead directly into Part VII, which examines the political, intellectual, economic, and cultural life of later Joseon.

Finally, the chapter illustrates a general historical lesson. Subordination in diplomacy does not equal absorption in identity, and a society may adapt practically while holding to other loyalties. Understanding how Joseon lived with that tension, and how later generations remembered the winter of 1636–1637, is part of understanding the long course of Korean history that this textbook follows.
