Democracy under Stress, 2016–2025

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the night of December 3, 2024, soldiers and police moved toward the National Assembly building on Yeouido, an island in the Han River in Seoul, while lawmakers, staff members, and ordinary citizens converged on the same ground from the opposite direction. Within hours, a president elected under the 1987 constitution had declared emergency martial law, and the legislature created by that constitution had voted to demand its end. How could a democracy that had removed a president through constitutional procedure less than eight years earlier come so close to a suspension of its own institutions, and what does the speed with which that attempt was reversed reveal about the strengths and weaknesses of the order built after 1987?

The events of that night are the analytical centerpiece of this chapter, but they cannot be understood in isolation. In the autumn of 2016, hundreds of thousands of South Koreans had gathered in the same capital, many of them holding candles, to demand the resignation of President Park Geun-hye, the daughter of the former military ruler Park Chung-hee, whose government is examined in Chapter 75. Out of that movement came a parliamentary impeachment vote on December 9, 2016, and a unanimous Constitutional Court judgment on March 10, 2017, that removed Park from office. At the time, many observers inside and outside Korea read the sequence as a demonstration that the democratic system established by the June 1987 struggle (Chapter 82) was mature enough to discipline even its most powerful officeholder.

Eight years later, the same constitutional machinery had to be used again. In the interval, South Korea experienced a government that promised democratic renewal and reform of the prosecution service under Moon Jae-in (2017–2022), a global pandemic that tested public administration and civil liberties, an exceptionally narrow presidential election in March 2022, and a presidency under Yoon Suk Yeol in which an opposition-controlled National Assembly and a conservative executive treated one another with mounting hostility. The chapter follows these developments not as steps toward an inevitable crisis, but as a series of choices, institutional conflicts, and electoral outcomes in which other paths remained available at each stage.

The historical problem the chapter addresses can be stated simply: how could a democracy capable of removing presidents through constitutional procedures also remain vulnerable to extraordinary concentrations and abuses of executive power? The 1987 constitution created a strong, directly elected president limited to a single five-year term, a National Assembly with real powers of oversight, and a Constitutional Court able to adjudicate impeachments. These institutions functioned in 2016–2017 and again in 2024–2025. Yet the same constitutional design also gave the president command of the armed forces, appointment power over much of the state, and an emergency power that had last been used in the authoritarian period. The chapter treats both facts, the capacity for removal and the persistence of concentrated authority, as parts of one historical situation.

Writing the history of events this recent requires particular care. Many of the subjects of this chapter were still in litigation, investigation, or political dispute at the time of writing. The chapter therefore distinguishes four kinds of statement: political controversy, meaning disputes among parties and commentators that do not turn on legal findings; allegation, meaning accusations made by prosecutors, politicians, or the press that have not been adjudicated; established constitutional findings, meaning the conclusions of the Constitutional Court in impeachment cases; and criminal convictions, meaning findings of guilt that have been finally decided by courts of criminal jurisdiction. These categories are not interchangeable. An impeachment judgment determines whether a public official should be removed for constitutional or legal violations; it is not a criminal verdict. A criminal indictment is a charge, not a conviction. Where the chapter reports a charge, it says so.

The evidence available differs sharply across the chapter's periods. For 2016–2017 historians can draw on the Assembly's impeachment resolution, the judgment of the Constitutional Court, the reports of the special prosecutor, and the work of investigative journalists. For the Moon years, evidence consists of legislation, election returns, public-opinion surveys, official statistics, and the reporting and commentary of a highly polarized press. For December 2024 and after, the chapter relies on the text of the martial-law proclamation, the Assembly's votes, the Constitutional Court's judgment of April 4, 2025, the results of the National Election Commission, and contemporaneous reporting from Korean and international news organizations. Testimony given in criminal proceedings and in the impeachment hearing is attributed to its source rather than treated as established fact.

The chapter has a fixed endpoint of December 31, 2025. Its task is to give a complete democratic and constitutional narrative of South Korea across this decade; the broader synthesis of both Korean states in 2026 belongs to Chapter 96. Related subjects are treated in neighboring chapters: inter-Korean diplomacy during the Moon years in Chapter 93, the alliance and great-power relationships in Chapter 94, and the social and demographic background of inequality, housing, education, and gender conflict in Chapter 91. Those chapters supply context; this chapter asks what they meant for the conduct and stability of democratic government.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the institutional design of the 1987 constitution, including the single-term presidency, the National Assembly's powers, and the Constitutional Court's role in impeachment.
  2. Trace the development of the Choi Soon-sil scandal into the candlelight movement, the Assembly's impeachment of Park Geun-hye, and her removal by the Constitutional Court in 2017.
  3. Distinguish among political controversy, allegation, constitutional finding, and criminal conviction when evaluating contemporary political events.
  4. Analyze why Moon Jae-in's reform agenda, especially prosecutorial reform, became a source of polarization as well as a response to it.
  5. Assess how housing, employment, and fairness concerns affected public confidence in the Moon administration without reducing the period to a single economic explanation.
  6. Evaluate South Korea's pandemic governance, including its public-health successes and the privacy questions its methods raised.
  7. Explain the circumstances of the 2022 presidential election and the institutional conflict that followed between a conservative executive and an opposition-controlled legislature.
  8. Reconstruct the chronology of December 3–14, 2024, and explain the roles of the National Assembly, military commanders, the police, and civilians.
  9. Interpret Article 77 of the constitution and explain why the Assembly's authority to demand the lifting of martial law was decisive.
  10. Compare the Constitutional Court's 2017 and 2025 impeachment judgments, identifying both shared reasoning and important differences.
  11. Contextualize the June 2025 election and the restoration of ordinary presidential authority within the longer history of Korean democratization.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of the 2016–2025 decade as evidence of democratic resilience, institutional vulnerability, or both.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did institutional features of the 1987 constitution make both the removal of presidents and the concentration of presidential power possible?
  2. What role did citizens, journalists, and civic organizations play in 2016–2017, and what role did formal institutions play? How did the two interact?
  3. Why might a reform intended to make a powerful institution more accountable itself become a cause of political conflict?
  4. How should historians weigh economic grievances, perceptions of fairness, and partisan identity in explaining shifts in electoral support?
  5. What distinguishes a legitimate use of presidential authority from an unconstitutional one, and who decides?
  6. Why did a divided government, in which a president lacked a legislative majority, lead to confrontation rather than compromise?
  7. What was the significance of the actions of individual soldiers, officers, lawmakers, and citizens on December 3–4, 2024, relative to the actions of institutions?
  8. How should historians treat events that are the subject of ongoing criminal proceedings?
  9. In what ways did the 2017 and 2025 impeachment cases differ, and why does it matter?
  10. Does the rapid reversal of martial law in 2024 demonstrate the resilience of South Korean democracy, its fragility, or both?

Historical Context

South Korea's political system in 2016 was the product of a long and violent struggle over the nature of the postwar state. After the April Revolution of 1960 (Chapter 74), the military coup of 1961 and the Park Chung-hee era (Chapter 75), and the military seizure of power in 1979–1980 that culminated in the Gwangju Uprising (Chapter 79), South Koreans mobilized in June 1987 to demand direct presidential elections. The resulting constitutional revision, examined in Chapter 82, created the Sixth Republic. Its framework has remained in force since: a president elected by direct popular vote to a single five-year term, a unicameral National Assembly of 300 members, an independent judiciary, and a Constitutional Court, established for the first time in 1988, empowered to rule on the constitutionality of laws, to adjudicate disputes among state organs, and to decide whether impeached officials must be removed.

Three features of this framework matter especially for the events of this chapter. First, the president combines the roles of head of state and head of government, commands the armed forces, appoints the prime minister (with Assembly consent), and appoints or nominates the heads of many state institutions, including the prosecutor general, the justices of the Constitutional Court (subject to the Assembly's role), and the leaders of the military. Second, the single-term limit, designed in 1987 to prevent a return to personal rule, also means that presidents cannot seek reelection and may lose political discipline over their own parties as their terms advance. Third, the constitution's emergency provisions, including Article 77 on martial law, survived the 1987 revision, though that revision added a significant safeguard by allowing the National Assembly to require the lifting of martial law by a majority vote of its members.

The political landscape that emerged after 1987 was shaped by strong regional loyalties, particularly the traditional association of the southwestern Honam region with progressive parties and the southeastern Yeongnam region with conservative ones, though these patterns changed over time. Between 1987 and 2016, presidential power alternated among conservative and progressive figures: Roh Tae-woo, Kim Young-sam, Kim Dae-jung (Chapter 85), Roh Moo-hyun, Lee Myung-bak, and Park Geun-hye. The country also experienced one earlier impeachment. In March 2004 the National Assembly impeached President Roh Moo-hyun, but the Constitutional Court rejected the case in May of that year and he returned to office. That precedent shaped later debates about whether impeachment is an appropriate instrument and how courts should evaluate it.

The economic setting was also significant. South Korea had recovered from the 1997 financial crisis (Chapter 84), became a leading exporter of semiconductors, automobiles, and electronics (Chapter 87), and remained dominated by large family-controlled conglomerates, the chaebol (Chapter 76). Their size and political proximity to presidents gave corporate-state relations a central place in corruption cases. At the same time, the social strains discussed in Chapter 91, including competitive education, housing costs, youth employment, gender conflict, and demographic decline, increasingly shaped voter attitudes. Foreign policy, covered in Chapters 93 and 94, formed another axis of conflict, especially between governments seeking engagement with North Korea and those emphasizing deterrence and alliance cooperation.

Finally, the history of this chapter unfolds within a changing media environment. Cable and online news, social-media platforms, and video streaming enabled new forms of mobilization and disinformation alike. Political actors on both sides built audiences through partisan channels, and the shared factual ground that once underpinned compromise grew thinner. Students should keep in mind that mobilizations in this chapter, from the candlelight rallies to the conservative demonstrations of 2025, relied on those communications structures as much as on traditional organizations.

Section 95.1

The Crisis of the Park Geun-hye Presidency

Park Geun-hye took office in February 2013 as the first woman to be elected president of South Korea and the daughter of a former head of state. She won a narrow majority in the December 2012 election, running as the candidate of the conservative Saenuri Party. Her presidency began with strong inherited political capital in the southeastern Yeongnam region and among older voters who associated her father's rule with rapid economic growth (Chapters 75 and 76). It also began with a style of governance that critics described as closed and hierarchical, relying on a small circle of trusted aides and issuing directives through the Blue House with limited consultation with party leaders, ministers, or the legislature.

The 1987 constitution had given presidents formidable tools, and Park used them. The Blue House controlled the principal appointments of the state, the budget process, and the direction of the intelligence and prosecution services. Because the single-term limit meant no president could be held to account at the ballot box for a second term, the main institutional checks were the National Assembly, the courts, the press, and public opinion. The system functioned best when these checks operated at the same time. In Park's case, they did not for several years. Her party held a legislative majority after the 2012 parliamentary election, which weakened the Assembly's oversight role, and the gradual accumulation of grievances against the administration took time to reach a political breaking point.

One event deeply shaped public attitudes toward the state during these years. On April 16, 2014, the ferry Sewol capsized off the southwestern coast, and 304 people died, most of them high-school students on a school excursion. Investigations pointed to overloaded cargo, regulatory failures, and a slow rescue response. For many families and citizens, the disaster raised questions about the competence and accountability of the state, and the question of what the president was doing on the day of the sinking became a persistent source of controversy. The Sewol disaster did not itself cause Park's removal, a point the Constitutional Court would make explicit in 2017 (see Section 95.4), but it contributed to a climate of public distrust in which later allegations found a receptive audience.

The scandal that ended Park's presidency emerged in stages in 2016. In the summer and early autumn, Korean newspapers, notably the Hankyoreh, and broadcasters reported that two nonprofit foundations, Mir and K-Sports, had been funded by large donations from corporations, including major chaebol, within a very short period, and that a private citizen named Choi Soon-sil, later known as Choi Seo-won, played a central role in their operation. On October 24, 2016, the cable channel JTBC reported that it had obtained a discarded tablet computer containing files that appeared to show Choi had received drafts of presidential speeches and other confidential documents before they were delivered. The following day Park issued a televised apology acknowledging that she had sought Choi's opinion on speech drafts in the early period of her presidency. Over the next weeks the scope of the allegations widened to include claims about Choi's influence over personnel decisions, her daughter's admission to and treatment at a prominent university, and the use of presidential authority to induce corporate donations.

The institutional character of the scandal is crucial for understanding why it produced a constitutional crisis rather than a conventional political scandal. The central allegation was not simply that individuals close to the president had behaved corruptly. It was that a person with no official position had gained access to state secrets and exercised informal influence over public decisions, and that presidential authority had been used to induce private corporations to contribute to organizations associated with that person. Such conduct, if established, would involve the abuse of the office itself. It also touched the relationship between the presidency and the chaebol, which had long been a source of concern: large firms depended on government decisions about regulation, mergers, tax matters, and pardons, and presidents depended on firms for investment and political support. The special prosecutor who investigated the case later charged that corporate leaders, including the head of the Samsung group's de facto leadership, had made payments seeking favorable treatment, a charge that became the subject of lengthy litigation.

Reducing the crisis to the personalities of Park and Choi would obscure its structural character. Park's presidency had become insulated from the party structures and parliamentary bargaining that, in a more open system, might have exposed and corrected informal influence earlier. The presidency's powers over appointments and information were broad, and the legal definition of what a president may share with private advisers was in dispute only after the fact. At the same time, the press and the judiciary retained enough independence to disclose and investigate the matter, and the public could organize freely. The crisis thus revealed both the vulnerabilities and the corrective capacities of the 1987 system.

Historians rely on several kinds of evidence for this period. Contemporary journalism, particularly the JTBC reporting and investigative articles in print outlets, is indispensable for establishing how the story became public, though it was produced in a competitive and politically charged media environment. The Assembly's parliamentary investigation hearings in late 2016, the special prosecutor's findings, the subsequent criminal judgments, and the Constitutional Court's decision provide more formal records. The limitations are significant: media reports often relied on anonymous sources, parliamentary hearings were shaped by partisan questioning, and prosecutors' charges represent allegations until courts rule. The chapter's account accordingly relies most heavily on the findings that were later adjudicated.

Section 95.2

The Candlelight Movement: Citizens, Protest, and Democratic Mobilization

The first large demonstration of the crisis took place on October 29, 2016, in central Seoul, and by early November protests had spread across the country. Weekly Saturday rallies became the movement's pattern. The largest were held in Gwanghwamun Square and the surrounding streets in Seoul, but demonstrations also took place in Busan, Gwangju, Daegu, Daejeon, Jeju, and many smaller cities. The rallies were organized by a broad coalition of civic groups, labor unions, farmers' associations, religious organizations, and student groups, but a distinguishing feature of the movement was the extent to which participation was not tied to any single organization. Many attendees came as families, as groups of friends, or alone, and the electronic candles that were sometimes substituted for real ones gave the gatherings both their name and their peaceful visual character.

Attendance estimates differ sharply, as they often do for mass demonstrations. Organizers reported that the largest single rallies drew well over a million people in Seoul, and that cumulative attendance across all rallies from October 2016 to early 2017 exceeded sixteen million. Police estimates of individual rallies were substantially lower. Historians treat these numbers as indicative rather than precise: they show that the demonstrations were among the largest in modern Korean history, while the divergence reflects the difficulty of counting dispersed crowds and the political interests of both organizers and authorities. One feature of the movement drew wide comment: the demonstrations were large, sustained, and largely peaceful, with few arrests and limited property damage, contrasting with earlier eras of confrontation between protesters and riot police (Chapters 79 and 82).

The movement's composition reflected the breadth of public anger but not unanimity of aims. Some participants demanded Park's resignation, others her impeachment, and others a thorough investigation and punishment of all those involved. High-school and university students attended in large numbers, and many first-time protesters joined, including women and parents who brought young children. Cultural performances, speeches, and satirical signs became part of the events. Trade unions, notably the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions, organized sections of the demonstrations and tied the protests to broader concerns about labor rights and inequality, which meant that the movement carried grievances beyond the immediate scandal. The online dimension was equally important: livestreamed rallies, messaging applications, and social-media sharing allowed participants to coordinate and informed those who did not attend in person.

Not all South Koreans supported the demonstrations. Conservative groups, drawing many older participants and often carrying national flags, held rival gatherings in central Seoul, arguing that the allegations had been exaggerated by a hostile media and opposition, that Park deserved due process, and that the movement threatened constitutional stability. These counter-demonstrations were substantial in their own right, and their organizers and participants regarded themselves as defenders of legitimate government. Their existence is a reminder that the candlelight movement, though very large, was not the whole of the country, and that the divisions that later shaped the Moon and Yoon years were already visible in the winter of 2016–2017.

The movement's relationship to earlier democratic struggles was a theme in how participants understood themselves. Commentators drew comparisons with the April Revolution of 1960, the Gwangju Uprising of 1980, and the June Struggle of 1987, and the movement's name evoked candlelight vigils that had earlier been used in Korean protests, including those over U.S. beef imports in 2008 and the deaths of two schoolgirls struck by a U.S. military vehicle in 2002. There were important differences. The earlier struggles confronted authoritarian governments and often involved violence and martial-law threats. The 2016–2017 movement operated within a functioning democracy, and its goals were framed in constitutional terms: to push the National Assembly to impeach, and the Constitutional Court to remove, a lawfully elected president. This framing mattered. The movement did not seek to overthrow the constitutional order but to invoke one of its mechanisms.

Interpretations of the movement's character remain contested, and this chapter returns to them in the Historical Debate in Section 95.8. What is not in dispute is the sequence: sustained public pressure, applied peacefully and in the open, coincided with parliamentary investigations, with the work of a special prosecutor, and with a shift in the positions of political parties, including defections from the ruling party. It is difficult to assign relative causal weight to each of these. Historians generally agree that mass mobilization altered the political costs of defending Park and made it harder for lawmakers to avoid acting, while the legal and institutional process determined how that pressure was translated into formal outcomes.

Section 95.3

The National Assembly and Park Geun-hye's Impeachment

Impeachment under the 1987 constitution is a two-stage process, and the distinction between the stages is essential for understanding the events of 2016–2017 and 2024–2025. The National Assembly decides whether to impeach; the Constitutional Court decides whether to remove. For a sitting president, the Assembly's impeachment motion must be proposed by a majority of its members and passed by at least two-thirds, which in a 300-seat chamber means 200 votes. Once the Assembly passes the motion, the president's powers are suspended, the prime minister assumes presidential duties as acting president, and the Constitutional Court has up to 180 days to rule. At least six of the nine justices must vote to uphold the impeachment for the president to be removed.

The two-thirds threshold meant that the opposition parties could not impeach Park on their own in 2016. After the 2016 parliamentary election in April, the opposition parties held a plurality but not two-thirds of the seats, and the ruling Saenuri Party retained a substantial block of lawmakers. Impeachment therefore required defections from the governing party. This was initially not guaranteed. In the weeks after the Choi story emerged, the opposition Democratic Party, the People's Party, and the Justice Party debated tactics, with some leaders favoring negotiation over a managed transition and others pressing for immediate impeachment. Park offered a second apology in November and indicated that she would accept a parliamentary arrangement on her departure, but she did not resign and the opposition regarded the offer as an attempt to manage the outcome on her terms. Meanwhile an independent special prosecutor appointed under a law passed by the Assembly, along with the regular prosecution, was investigating the case. The prosecution named Park as a suspect and described her in an indictment of Choi and others as a participant in the alleged offenses, though it did not formally indict a sitting president, who has immunity from most criminal prosecution while in office.

Pressure on the ruling party's lawmakers mounted as demonstrations grew and public-opinion polling showed very low approval ratings for Park. A faction of Saenuri lawmakers who opposed Park, known as the anti-mainstream or non-Park wing, announced they would support impeachment. On December 3, 2016, opposition parties and independents submitted an impeachment motion supported by 171 lawmakers. The same day, the protests of that Saturday were the largest of the series by organizers' counts. The vote took place on December 9, by secret ballot, with 299 members voting. The motion passed with 234 votes in favor, 56 against, 2 abstentions, and 7 invalid ballots, well above the two-thirds requirement. Because the opposition parties and independents together held only about 170 seats, the result implies that roughly sixty members of the governing Saenuri Party voted in favor, although the ballot was secret and the exact number cannot be known.

The impeachment resolution accused Park of violating the constitution and laws in several respects. In summary, it asserted that she had allowed a private person to interfere in state affairs and to gain access to confidential information; that she had abused her authority by pressuring corporations to contribute to the two foundations and by arranging favors; that she had made improper personnel decisions; and that she had failed in her duty to protect citizens' lives in connection with the Sewol disaster. It was a mixture of allegations of corrupt conduct, abuse of power, and political failure, and its breadth would later matter, because the Constitutional Court did not accept all of it (see Section 95.4).

The immediate consequence was the suspension of Park's presidential powers. She remained in the Blue House as president but did not exercise authority, while Prime Minister Hwang Kyo-ahn assumed the role of acting president. Hwang, a former justice minister and prosecutor who had been appointed by Park, was regarded with suspicion by the opposition, and his role during the interim, including decisions on appointments and the question of when an election should be held, became a source of political friction. The transitional arrangement also tested a constitutional question that would reappear in 2024–2025: how much authority an acting president should exercise when the president's powers are suspended but a Constitutional Court ruling is pending.

The process showed how public pressure, party divisions, and legislative authority interacted. Mass demonstrations did not themselves impeach Park. The vote required lawmakers, voting by secret ballot, to act under a formal procedure, and it required the divisions within the conservative camp to widen. At the same time, the demonstrations changed the calculations of individual legislators who faced voters in the next election and who were aware that the public mood was overwhelmingly hostile to the president. After the vote, the Saenuri Party split. A group of lawmakers left in early 2017 to form a new conservative party, the Bareun Party, while the remnant was later reorganized as the Liberty Korea Party. The fragmentation of the conservative camp would shape the 2017 election and, years later, the conditions that led to the formation of the People Power Party.

Historical Vocabulary
Impeachment and Constitutional Adjudication

Definition. In South Korea, impeachment is a procedure in which the National Assembly accuses a high official, including the president, of violating the constitution or laws in the performance of official duties, and the Constitutional Court adjudicates whether the official must be removed. The two bodies perform distinct functions: the Assembly initiates and votes on the motion; the Court decides the case.

Historical context. The Constitutional Court was established under the 1987 constitution and began operating in 1988. Two presidents before 2024 had been the subject of impeachment motions that passed the Assembly: Roh Moo-hyun in 2004, whose impeachment the Court rejected, and Park Geun-hye in 2016, whom the Court removed. The impeachment of Yoon Suk Yeol in 2024–2025 was the third. For a president, passage in the Assembly requires two-thirds of all members, and removal requires at least six of the nine justices. When an impeachment motion passes, the president's powers are suspended until the Court rules.

Why it matters. The separation of roles means that an impeachment vote is a political and legislative act, while the Court's judgment is a legal determination about whether violations occurred and whether they are serious enough to justify removal. The Court's reasoning therefore provides a legal record of findings that political rhetoric cannot supply.

Potential misunderstanding. Impeachment is not a criminal trial, and removal is not a criminal conviction. A removed president can later face criminal prosecution in ordinary courts, as Park did, but that is a separate process with different standards, evidence, and outcomes.

Section 95.4

The Constitutional Court Removes Park, March 2017

The Constitutional Court received the case immediately and held hearings through the winter. The Court at that time had eight sitting justices, since the ninth seat had been vacant following the retirement of the chief justice in January 2017, and Justice Lee Jung-mi served as acting chief justice. Because six votes were needed to uphold an impeachment, the departure of any additional justice would have complicated the case. Park's lawyers contested the allegations, argued that the Assembly had not followed proper procedure, and sought to delay the proceedings. Park herself did not appear at the hearings. Meanwhile outside the Court, demonstrations for and against removal continued, with the rival camps rallying in central Seoul.

On March 10, 2017, the Court issued its decision. All eight justices voted to uphold the impeachment and remove Park from office. The unanimity was legally and politically significant: it deprived supporters of the president of an argument that the ruling had been the product of a divided and politicized court, and it gave the decision a clarity that the 2004 case, which the Court had decided against removal, had lacked. The reasoning, in summary, found that Park had violated the constitution and laws by allowing Choi Soon-sil to interfere in state affairs, by using presidential authority to induce companies to contribute to the two foundations, and by other related acts, and that these violations were serious enough that the benefits of protecting the constitution through removal outweighed the costs. The Court emphasized that the president holds public office on behalf of the people and that her conduct had betrayed the trust placed in her.

The judgment is also notable for what it did not decide. The Court did not accept the criticism of Park's conduct in connection with the 2014 Sewol ferry disaster as a sufficient, independent ground for removal. In its reasoning, it indicated that the constitutional duty of the president to protect the lives of citizens, while real, did not translate into a legal obligation whose breach by the president's particular conduct on that day could be established as a violation justifying impeachment. This distinction mattered for public memory: many who had joined the protests understood the Sewol disaster as central to their anger, but the legal basis of the removal rested on other grounds. The gap between popular grievances and the grounds the Court actually accepted is a reminder that constitutional adjudication applies legal standards that do not map neatly onto the full range of public criticism of an administration.

The ruling also shows how the Court approached proportionality. It did not treat every established violation as automatically requiring removal. It weighed the gravity of the violations and the damage to constitutional order against the loss resulting from the removal of an elected president, and concluded that removal was warranted. This standard, that a violation must be grave enough to justify overriding the electorate's choice, would be applied again in 2025, when the Court assessed far more direct attacks on the constitutional structure.

The consequences were immediate. Park lost her office and her presidential immunity at the moment of the decision. In the following days she left the Blue House, and later in March prosecutors questioned her and she was arrested. She was indicted on charges including bribery, abuse of power, and coercion. In 2018 a lower court sentenced her to a lengthy prison term, and after further appeals the Supreme Court finalized a sentence of twenty years in 2021, combined from separate cases. Choi Soon-sil was also convicted and sentenced to a long term. The case against Samsung's leadership proceeded through a complicated sequence of trials, convictions, and later developments over several years. In late December 2021, near the end of his term, President Moon Jae-in granted Park a special pardon, an act that drew mixed reactions: some supporters of reconciliation welcomed it, while some of the movement's participants regarded it as undermining the accountability the movement had demanded.

Under the constitution, when a president is removed, a successor must be elected within sixty days. The election was set for May 9, 2017. Hwang Kyo-ahn continued as acting president until the new president took office. The short timetable shaped the campaign: candidates had weeks rather than months to organize, parties scrambled to select nominees, and the conservative camp, divided and discredited by the scandal, entered the race at a disadvantage. The brevity and discipline of the process, a constitutionally specified route from impeachment to removal to election, would become a template that was used again, under very different circumstances, in 2025.

Section 95.5

Moon Jae-in's Election and the Promise of Democratic Renewal

The election of May 9, 2017, was held within sixty days of Park's removal and took place in an atmosphere of unusual political fluidity. The Democratic Party nominated Moon Jae-in, a former human-rights lawyer who had served as chief of staff to President Roh Moo-hyun and had narrowly lost the 2012 presidential election to Park. The conservative camp, split between the Liberty Korea Party and the breakaway Bareun Party, was represented by Hong Joon-pyo for the former. The centrist People's Party nominated Ahn Cheol-soo, a software entrepreneur turned politician. Moon won with 41.08 percent of the vote, ahead of Hong Joon-pyo with about 24 percent and Ahn Cheol-soo with about 21 percent, on a turnout of roughly 77 percent. It was a plurality, not a majority, a fact that reflects the multi-candidate field and a reminder that the mandate of the candlelight movement, however it was understood, was not a single unified electorate.

Because the election followed a removal, there was no transition period. Moon took office on May 10, 2017, immediately after the National Election Commission certified the result, and he appointed his prime minister and cabinet over the following weeks. His inauguration took place in the National Assembly rather than a lavish ceremony, and his early public gestures emphasized accessibility: he walked among citizens, announced plans to relocate the presidential office from the Blue House to Gwanghwamun, and pledged to speak more directly with the public. The relocation did not take place during his term, and the Blue House remained the presidential office until Moon's successor moved it in 2022. The pattern, of a bold symbolic gesture that was only partially fulfilled, was characteristic of a presidency whose expectations ran ahead of its institutional capacity.

Moon's political background shaped his appeal. He was identified with the democratization tradition, having been arrested as a student activist in the 1970s, worked as a human-rights lawyer in Busan, and been a close associate of Roh Moo-hyun, whose own presidency (2003–2008) and subsequent death in 2009 (after a corruption investigation of his family) remained powerful political memories for progressives. He also carried the suspicion, among opponents, that his government would pursue retribution against the conservatives. The expectations placed on him were extensive: supporters hoped for a thoroughgoing reform of the prosecution service and of the relationship between the state and the chaebol, for greater social fairness, for a less personalized presidency, and for renewed engagement with North Korea (examined in Chapter 93).

The inaugural address, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, laid out themes of fairness, opportunity, and accountability. Moon promised a president who would communicate and be accountable, a society in which opportunity was equal and process was fair, and a government in which power would not be exercised arbitrarily. He also spoke of reforming the institutions of power, including the prosecution and intelligence services, which had been used, in earlier governments, for political purposes. These commitments mattered because they defined the standard by which later actions would be judged, both by his supporters and by his critics.

Historians evaluating the Moon presidency need to distinguish among three kinds of evidence: electoral promises, implemented measures, and outcomes. Promises can be read from campaign documents and speeches; implemented measures from legislation, executive orders, and personnel decisions; outcomes from economic statistics, polling, and later electoral results. The three often diverged. A promise to reform the prosecution service, for example, resulted in legislation, but the effects on the institution's behavior and on public confidence were more ambiguous. The sections that follow keep these categories distinct.

Section 95.6

Prosecutorial Reform, Anti-Corruption Institutions, and the Cho Kuk Controversy

The South Korean prosecution service occupies a position unlike that of prosecutors in many other democracies. Prosecutors historically held a monopoly over the authority to bring criminal indictments, wide discretion over investigations, and the power to direct police inquiries. During the authoritarian era, prosecutors were widely seen as instruments of the executive; after 1987, their reputation became mixed, as they pursued powerful figures, including former presidents, while also being accused of selective enforcement and of protecting their own institutional interests. Progressive governments tended to regard the prosecution as an unaccountable concentration of power requiring reform, while conservatives often emphasized its role in fighting corruption and the importance of its independence from political direction. The competing assessments are developed in the Historical Vocabulary box below.

The Moon administration made prosecutorial reform one of its signature objectives. Its program had several components. First, it sought to establish an independent investigative agency, the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO), to investigate the conduct of senior officials, including prosecutors themselves. After years of debate, the Assembly passed the enabling legislation in December 2020, and the CIO began operations in January 2021. Second, it sought to rebalance the relationship between prosecutors and police, giving police greater authority to conduct and conclude investigations and limiting the prosecution's power to direct them, a set of reforms enacted in early 2020. Third, in 2022 near the end of Moon's term, legislation further narrowed the categories of crime that prosecutors could investigate directly, a move that was itself intensely contested.

The controversy that most sharply defined these reforms centered on Cho Kuk. A law professor at Seoul National University and a close adviser to Moon, Cho served as senior secretary for civil affairs in the presidential office and had been an advocate of prosecutorial reform. In August 2019, Moon nominated him as justice minister, the cabinet official responsible for the prosecution service. Almost immediately, allegations emerged concerning his family: questions about his daughter's admission to university and medical school, about documents submitted in support of those admissions, and about investments made by his wife and relatives. Cho denied wrongdoing. Moon appointed him on September 9, 2019, without the Assembly's confirmation, which the constitution does not require for ministers other than the prime minister, though a confirmation hearing is customary.

The prosecution service, then led by Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol, whom Moon had appointed in July 2019, launched investigations into the allegations, including searches of Cho's family residence while he was minister. The confrontation was intensely symbolic: the minister designated to oversee reform of the prosecution faced investigation by the prosecution. Supporters of Cho argued that prosecutors were abusing their authority to retaliate against a reformer and to pressure the government, and held large rallies in Seoul, notably near the Supreme Prosecutors' Office. Opponents argued that the allegations raised serious questions about fairness in a country where admission to elite universities was a matter of deep social anxiety, and held their own large demonstrations at Gwanghwamun. Cho resigned on October 14, 2019, after about five weeks in office.

Subsequent legal proceedings produced a mixed record that shaped how the episode was remembered. Cho's wife, Chung Kyung-sim, was convicted of crimes including forgery of documents related to their daughter's university application, and her conviction was finalized by the Supreme Court. Cho himself was convicted at trial and on appeal of charges including interference with a university's admissions process and other offenses, and in December 2024 the Supreme Court upheld a two-year prison sentence. Cho was granted a special pardon in August 2025. He and his supporters maintained that the prosecution had been politically motivated; critics regarded the convictions as vindicating the original concerns. The chapter notes the facts of the proceedings without adjudicating the competing interpretations of motive.

Why did institutional reform become a source of polarization? Several factors interacted. First, the targets of the reform, the prosecution service, were in a position to respond, and the Cho investigation showed that they could exercise their authority in ways that affected the government directly. Second, the two sides held different conceptions of accountability. For the government and its supporters, accountability meant subjecting prosecutors to external oversight; for critics, it meant preserving the prosecution's independence from executive pressure so that it could investigate the powerful, including the government. Both conceptions have legitimate grounds, and the conflict could not be resolved by evidence alone. Third, the controversy intersected with social concerns about fairness. Many younger South Koreans, who faced intense competition for educational and employment opportunities (see Chapter 91), perceived the Cho allegations as an example of privilege, and this perception cut across the usual partisan lines and eroded support for the government among some of its expected constituencies.

The conflict between the Justice Ministry and the prosecutor general continued after Cho's departure. His successor, Choo Mi-ae, clashed publicly with Yoon, issuing a suspension and disciplinary measures against him in late 2020. A court suspended the suspension, and Yoon remained in office until he resigned in March 2021, saying that the legal and constitutional order was being undermined. The episode turned him into a figure of national political prominence and, within months, into a presidential contender. In this sense, the dispute over prosecutorial reform was not merely a policy dispute; it reshaped the political field and produced the candidate who would defeat the governing party in 2022.

Historical Vocabulary
Prosecutorial Independence and Political Accountability

Definition. Prosecutorial independence refers to arrangements that protect prosecutors from improper political direction in deciding whom to investigate and charge. Political accountability refers to mechanisms by which prosecutors, who wield considerable coercive power, are held answerable to elected officials, courts, or the public. The two principles can pull in opposite directions.

Historical context. In South Korea, prosecutors historically held broad powers over investigation and indictment, and the prosecutor general is appointed by the president. Reform proposals in the 2017–2022 period aimed to limit the prosecution's investigative powers, shift authority toward the police, and create the CIO as an independent investigator of senior officials. Critics argued that these reforms would weaken a body that had investigated powerful figures and could leave corruption cases under-investigated; proponents argued that unchecked prosecutorial authority posed its own danger to democratic government.

Why it matters. The debate shaped the political careers of Cho Kuk, Yoon Suk Yeol, and others, and it determined the institutional environment in which the later martial-law investigation was conducted, since the CIO, a reform-era creation, led the first investigation of a sitting president.

Potential misunderstanding. The terms do not map neatly onto left and right. Both progressives and conservatives have favored prosecutorial independence when their allies were investigated by hostile governments, and both have favored oversight when prosecutors were investigating their own allies. Students should evaluate the arguments rather than assuming any one party's position is consistent over time.

Section 95.7

Housing, Employment, Social Policy, and Declining Confidence

The Moon government's economic agenda, often summarized by supporters as "income-led growth," aimed to raise wages and household incomes as a driver of demand, to expand social welfare, and to reduce reliance on conglomerate-led export growth. Its early measures included substantial increases in the statutory minimum wage in 2018 and 2019, a reduction in the maximum legal working week to 52 hours, the conversion of some temporary public-sector workers into permanent employees, and expansions of child allowances and health insurance coverage. The policies reflected real concerns about low wages, long hours, and precarious employment, issues examined in Chapter 91. Their effects, however, were contested. Supporters argued that they improved conditions for low-paid workers and shifted social norms; critics, including many small-business owners, argued that rapid wage increases raised costs for small firms and reduced hiring in some sectors. Economists disagreed, and the empirical evidence about employment effects was mixed and depended on the sectors and the period studied.

The issue that most damaged public confidence was housing. Apartment prices in Seoul and surrounding areas rose sharply during the Moon years, driven by low interest rates, limited supply in desirable locations, speculative demand, and the long-standing perception of apartments as the principal form of household wealth. The government responded with a long series of measures, widely reported as more than twenty packages, including higher taxes on multiple-home owners and capital gains, restrictions on mortgage lending, and designation of regulated zones. It also changed rental rules in 2020 to strengthen tenant protections. Critics argued that the taxes and restrictions failed to increase supply and sometimes worsened shortages; defenders argued that without them prices would have risen further. For households without property, particularly younger people, the price increases created a sense that effort and savings could not secure a home, while owners faced rising tax assessments.

A scandal in March 2021 compounded the damage. Reports and an official investigation alleged that employees of the Korea Land and Housing Corporation, a public agency, had bought land in areas later designated for housing development, using inside information. The allegations struck at the government's credibility on exactly the issue where it was seeking public trust. Within weeks, the Democratic Party suffered heavy defeats in the April 2021 by-elections for mayor of Seoul and Busan, both won by conservative candidates. Surveys indicated that voters in their twenties and thirties, in particular, had shifted away from the government, and that concerns about housing and fairness were prominent in their reasoning, though many other factors, including the circumstances that created the by-elections in the first place, also played a role.

This chapter does not duplicate Chapter 91's analysis of the structural sources of housing costs, educational competition, and generational frustration. The point here is political: the government's inability to make convincing progress on housing and its exposure to fairness controversies, from the Cho affair to the land scandal, eroded the moral claim on which it had come to power. Approval ratings, which had been very high in the first year, particularly after the 2018 inter-Korean summits, declined over time. Observers sometimes described a gap between the government's rhetoric of fairness and the perceived behavior of some of its own members.

Welfare reforms and labor policies also had uneven political effects. The expansion of social programs received broad support in principle but was criticized for cost; the 52-hour week was applauded by many workers and criticized by some employers; and the conversion of contract workers to permanent status, especially at a major airport operator, provoked resentment among job seekers who saw it as unfair to those waiting for entry-level positions. The pattern shows that policies intended to promote equality could produce competing perceptions of fairness among different groups, a recurrent feature of the period.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Citizens in a Decade of Constitutional Crisis

The institutional narrative of this chapter was lived by ordinary people in very different ways. In 2016–2017, many Saturday protesters were families with children, office workers, and students, who brought snacks and light sticks to rallies, shared hot drinks with strangers, and cleaned up after gatherings. Pro-Park demonstrators, often older and frequently veterans or members of religious congregations, came on chartered buses and understood themselves as defending the constitutional order against what they saw as a media-driven mob. Within families, political disagreements, particularly between generations, were frequent and sometimes painful.

The economic conditions discussed in Section 95.7 shaped daily calculations. Renters in Seoul faced rising deposits under the traditional jeonse system, in which tenants pay a large lump sum in lieu of monthly rent; young adults postponed marriage, children, or moving out of parental homes; small-business owners absorbed rising wage costs and, during the pandemic, restrictions on opening hours. During COVID-19, residents received text alerts about nearby infection cases, wore masks in public, and queued for testing, experiences that combined a sense of collective efficacy with unease about surveillance.

On December 3, 2024, ordinary citizens experienced the declaration through phones and televisions late at night. Many recalled messages from family members who had lived through earlier martial law, and parents contacted children in the military. Those who went to Yeouido included people who left their homes in the middle of the night, some traveling by taxi or subway before services ended. In the following weeks, workplaces, schools, and dinner tables were arenas of argument. Foreign residents, businesses, and Koreans abroad followed the events with concern about security and markets, and exchange rates and stock prices fell sharply in the days that followed.

Sources for this social history include surveys, oral histories, journalistic features, photographs, and social-media posts. They are rich but uneven: they capture those who spoke publicly or were interviewed, and they reflect the political leanings of the outlets that collected them. Historians should be cautious about treating the most vivid testimony as representative, and they should seek evidence about people who did not take to the streets, whose views and experiences are harder to recover.

Section 95.8

Pandemic Governance, the 2020 Parliamentary Election, and the Limits of Moon's Mandate

South Korea identified its first case of COVID-19 on January 20, 2020, and in February experienced a large outbreak centered on the city of Daegu, linked in part to a religious group. The government's response became internationally noted. Drawing on lessons from the 2015 MERS outbreak, in which delays and poor information had led to public criticism, authorities had revised the Infectious Disease Control and Prevention Act to give public-health agencies greater powers to collect data and require testing. They combined widespread testing, contact tracing, isolation of cases, and public communication, an approach often summarized as testing, tracing, and treating, without the extensive national lockdowns adopted elsewhere. Private companies developed diagnostic tests quickly under emergency authorization, and drive-through testing sites became a widely reported feature.

The methods raised significant privacy questions. Contact tracing used mobile-phone location data, credit-card transaction records, and surveillance-camera footage, and public health authorities issued alerts that sometimes disclosed details of infected individuals' movements. In some cases, these disclosures led to speculation and stigma, including toward people connected to particular venues or communities. Authorities subsequently revised the practice to limit personal information. Civil-liberties advocates, public-health experts, and ordinary citizens differed about the proper balance, and the experience illustrated how a democracy navigated the tension between public safety and individual rights through a legal framework that had been revised in advance, with statutory authority, rather than by emergency decree. That distinction is relevant to later developments, when a president invoked a far more drastic emergency power without statutory or constitutional grounds.

The parliamentary election of April 15, 2020, took place during the pandemic, with precautions including temperature checks, hand sanitizer, and disposable gloves at polling stations, and with a turnout of about 66 percent, the highest for a parliamentary election since 1992. The results strongly favored the governing camp. The Democratic Party and its affiliated satellite party together won 180 of the 300 seats, while the main conservative party, then called the United Future Party, and its satellite party won 103. The 2020 election followed a revised electoral law that introduced a partial proportional-representation system in which parties could create satellite parties to capture additional seats, a practice that complicated the interpretation of seat counts and would recur in 2024. Observers attributed the result to a mix of factors, including approval of the government's pandemic response, the opposition's difficulties, and the continuing effects of the candlelight movement's realignment.

The result gave the governing camp the largest parliamentary majority of any party since the democratization of 1987 and, in principle, the ability to pass legislation with little opposition cooperation. It used this majority to enact its reform agenda, including the CIO legislation and the prosecutorial-police reforms discussed above, and to pass numerous housing and labor measures. But institutional and electoral success did not eliminate political division. Conservative voters and the opposition leaders contested the legitimacy of some legislation, particularly the prosecution reforms, which passed with limited opposition participation. Public opinion, meanwhile, shifted after the election, as the pandemic persisted, housing prices rose, and the controversies of 2021 accumulated. The April 2021 by-elections, mentioned above, showed how quickly a very large mandate could erode.

The conditions surrounding the March 2022 presidential contest were therefore shaped by an accumulation of factors. These included disappointment with housing and perceived unfairness, the polarizing struggle over prosecutorial reform, the emergence of Yoon Suk Yeol as a symbol of opposition to the government, generational and gender divisions among younger voters, and also continuing public-health concerns and the unevenness of the economic recovery. None of these made the outcome of the election inevitable. The election was close, and different candidates, different campaign events, or different turnouts could have produced a different result. The explanation of Moon's party's defeat is a matter of weighing contributing factors, not identifying a single cause.

Historical Debate

The Candlelight Movement: Democratic Renewal or Incomplete Institutional Reform?

The question: Did the 2016–2017 protests produce lasting transformations in South Korea's political institutions, or did they primarily replace one governing coalition with another? Historians and political commentators differ about how to measure the movement's legacy.

Interpretation A — Democratic renewal: Some scholars argue that the movement demonstrated that citizens could use peaceful assembly and constitutional procedure to hold a president accountable, strengthened the legitimacy of the Constitutional Court as an arbiter of presidential power, and established a precedent that would prove decisive in 2024–2025, when citizens and institutions again acted in defense of the constitutional order. On this view, the movement is evidence of a mature and resilient democratic culture.
Interpretation B — Incomplete institutional reform: Others argue that the movement's institutional legacy was limited. The presidency's formal powers were not reduced; plans for constitutional revision, including proposals to change the single-term structure and to decentralize power, were not enacted; the prosecution reforms proved controversial and arguably deepened polarization; and the government that came to power in 2017 itself became subject to criticism about fairness and accountability. On this view, the movement removed a president but left the structures that concentrated power largely intact.
Interpretation C — Social change beyond formal institutions: A third approach emphasizes changes in political culture and expectations rather than formal institutions: the experience of mass peaceful mobilization, the normalization of demanding accountability from the powerful, and the movement's effects on women's and young people's participation. This view holds that institutional measures and cultural change should be assessed separately.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: the documented course of the demonstrations; the legal record of the impeachment; legislation passed or proposed in 2017–2022; polling data on trust in institutions and satisfaction with democracy; and the events of 2024–2025. They differ in the criteria used to measure legacy, and in how much weight to give later events as evidence about the movement's effects.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and the standard of evaluation rather than facts. It also involves chronology: whether the events of 2024–2025 should be read as a consequence of the 2016–2017 precedent or as a separate development.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they understand the relationship between popular mobilization and institutional design, and whether they see the 2024 crisis as testing a reformed system or an unreformed one.

Section 95.9

The Presidential Election of 2022: A Narrow Conservative Victory

Yoon Suk Yeol's route to the presidency was unusual. He had spent his career as a prosecutor and had never held elected or party office. He rose to national visibility in 2013 as a prosecutor who clashed with superiors during an investigation of alleged intelligence-agency interference in the 2012 election, and again as a member of the team investigating the Park-Choi scandal in 2016–2017. President Moon appointed him chief of the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office in 2017 and prosecutor general in July 2019. As explained in Section 95.6, his confrontation with the government over the Cho Kuk investigation and the Justice Ministry's disciplinary measures made him a symbol of resistance to the administration among its opponents. He resigned in March 2021, joined the main conservative party, the People Power Party, in July 2021, and won its presidential nomination in November.

His opponent was Lee Jae-myung, the Democratic Party's nominee, a former labor lawyer, mayor of Seongnam, and governor of Gyeonggi Province, who had built a national profile with a populist, welfare-oriented platform and a reputation for decisive administration. Lee had come from a poor family and worked as a teenager in a factory, a biography he used to contrast with Yoon's elite legal career. Both candidates carried heavy negative ratings. Lee faced investigations and allegations over a land-development project in Seongnam, among other matters, which he denied; Yoon faced questions about his wife, Kim Keon-hee, and about statements made during the campaign. The campaign was widely described by commentators as a contest between two unpopular candidates, though that characterization simplifies the choices made by voters.

Several issues dominated the campaign. Housing costs and the perceived failure of government policy were central, as were fairness and corruption, with each side accusing the other. Generational differences were pronounced, and so was gender politics: Yoon's campaign appealed strongly to young men, partly by promising to abolish the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family, while many young women and older progressive voters moved toward Lee. Survey analyses suggested that voters in their twenties were divided sharply by gender, a pattern that Chapter 91 treats in its social context. Regional patterns persisted, with the Honam region heavily supporting Lee and the Yeongnam region heavily supporting Yoon, while the capital region and the Chungcheong provinces were closely contested.

On March 9, 2022, Yoon won with 48.56 percent of the vote to Lee's 47.83 percent, a margin of approximately 0.73 percentage points, or roughly 247,000 votes out of more than 34 million cast, the narrowest margin in a direct presidential election since 1987. Turnout was about 77 percent. Neither candidate had obtained a majority, and the outcome meant that a president took office with the barest of popular mandates and facing a National Assembly in which the Democratic Party held a clear majority, elected in 2020. The closeness mattered for later events. It left roughly half the electorate unreconciled to the result, it gave the new president limited political capital, and it created incentives for a combative approach by both camps, since each could plausibly claim that victory in the next election was within reach.

Section 95.10

The Yoon Administration: Presidential Authority, Prosecutors, and Institutional Conflict

Yoon took office on May 10, 2022, after a transition of two months. His stated governing priorities included restoring what he called a market-oriented economy, strengthening the alliance with the United States and cooperation with Japan (Chapter 94), taking a firmer line toward North Korea, and reducing regulatory burdens. He also emphasized the rule of law and fairness, themes drawn from his critique of the previous government. Many of these priorities generated debate, but this section concentrates on how he used the instruments of presidential authority and how institutions responded.

Yoon's appointments drew attention. His administration placed a notable number of former prosecutors in senior positions, including key roles in the presidential office, the Justice Ministry, the Financial Supervisory Service, and other bodies. Supporters argued that these were competent and honest professionals suited to restoring the rule of law; critics argued that the practice created what they called a "prosecutors' republic," in which a single institutional culture dominated the executive. Whether that culture shaped decision-making in specific cases is a matter of interpretation. The pattern is documented, but its significance is contested. The administration's relations with other institutions, including the Board of Audit and Inspection, the Constitutional Court, the public broadcasters, and the Korea Communications Commission, also became subjects of dispute, with opposition lawmakers alleging political interference and the administration denying it.

One of Yoon's first decisions, announced during the transition, was to relocate the presidential office from the Blue House to the compound of the former Defense Ministry in Yongsan. He argued that the Blue House symbolized an imperial and isolated presidency and that moving to Yongsan would bring the president closer to citizens. The Blue House was opened to the public. Critics questioned the cost, the speed with which the relocation was carried out, and the practical and security implications of placing the presidential office next to the military's former headquarters; supporters regarded it as a break from a distant past. The move had a broader significance for the way governing practices shaped perceptions of executive authority. The relocation was presented as democratization of space but was also carried out by executive decision, with limited consultation, a pattern that critics saw as characteristic.

The administration also faced challenges in managing public safety and accountability. On October 29, 2022, a crowd crush in the Itaewon district of Seoul during Halloween celebrations killed 159 people, many of them young. The tragedy led to public mourning and a prolonged dispute over responsibility. Investigations examined police and local-government preparedness; the interior minister was impeached by the Assembly in February 2023, though the Constitutional Court rejected the impeachment in July 2023. The episode reinforced, for critics, a perception that the government was resistant to accepting responsibility, while the government maintained that it had cooperated with investigations and that the Court's decision vindicated the minister. As with the Sewol disaster, the underlying question of how a democratic state assigns accountability for disaster was politically and emotionally charged.

Allegations concerning the president's wife became a recurring theme. Opposition lawmakers and some media reported claims that Kim Keon-hee had been involved in stock manipulation linked to a car dealership, had falsified credentials in academic and professional applications, and had accepted a luxury handbag from a pastor. The presidential office and Kim's representatives denied wrongdoing or disputed the characterization of the events. Prosecutors reached decisions in some of the matters, including decisions not to indict in certain cases, which the opposition criticized as lenient and the government defended as based on the evidence. The Assembly passed bills authorizing a special counsel to investigate the allegations, which Yoon vetoed. This chapter treats the allegations as allegations, noting that they were politically consequential in shaping perceptions, regardless of the legal outcomes, a subject that continued into 2025 under independent investigators.

The result, by 2024, was a presidency whose approval ratings were persistently low, whose party held a minority in the legislature, and whose relations with the principal opposition were characterized by mutual distrust. It would be misleading to attribute this to Yoon alone. Opposition parties used their legislative majority aggressively, and the media environment rewarded confrontation. But the president's governing practices, including reliance on appointees from a single professional background, reluctance to negotiate with the opposition, and use of the veto, shaped the way executive authority was perceived and the incentives for compromise.

Section 95.11

Political Polarization, Legislative Deadlock, and Competing Claims of Legitimacy

Between 2022 and 2024, the opposition Democratic Party, led from August 2022 by Lee Jae-myung, controlled the National Assembly with a majority sufficient to pass legislation but not sufficient to override presidential vetoes, which require a two-thirds majority. This structure created a persistent pattern: the Assembly passed bills; the president vetoed them; and the vetoes could not be overridden. By late 2024, the number of vetoes was reportedly in the range of two dozen, by far exceeding the number used by most recent presidents. The vetoed bills included measures on labor relations, often called the Yellow Envelope Act, which would limit companies' ability to sue unions for damages from strikes; measures on agricultural support; and the special-counsel bills concerning the president's wife and the investigation of the death of a marine in 2023. The president and the governing party characterized these bills as partisan or unconstitutional; the opposition characterized the vetoes as obstruction of the legislature and protection of the president's circle.

Budget disputes illustrated the deadlock. The Assembly's authority over the budget includes the power to reduce, but not increase, items proposed by the executive. In 2024 the opposition proposed substantial reductions in the budget for the following year, including reductions in contingency funds and in the budgets of certain agencies such as the prosecution and the Board of Audit and Inspection. The president and his party argued that the cuts were punitive and would hamper government functions; the opposition said the funds were poorly justified. The disagreement was a normal feature of divided government, but its tone and consequences intensified. Disputes of this kind later figured in the president's explanation for his actions in December 2024, an explanation the Constitutional Court did not accept as a justification for martial law.

The opposition also used the Assembly's power of impeachment with unusual frequency. Between 2022 and late 2024 it initiated or passed impeachment motions against numerous officials, including the interior minister, the head of the Korea Communications Commission, and a number of prosecutors, as well as motions concerning the Board of Audit and Inspection chair. The Constitutional Court rejected some of these, and in other cases officials resigned. Supporters of the opposition argued that these were legitimate exercises of oversight over officials who had violated the law; the government and its supporters argued that the opposition was weaponizing impeachment to paralyze the executive. The historian's task is to assess each case on its merits and note the general pattern, in which a constitutional safeguard intended for exceptional cases became a routine instrument of partisan conflict.

Investigations of political figures added to the tension. Lee Jae-myung himself was the subject of multiple prosecutions concerning alleged election-law violations, the Daejang-dong development project, and other matters. He denied the charges and characterized them as politically motivated; prosecutors said they were pursuing evidence of crimes. In January 2024, Lee was stabbed in the neck during a visit to Busan by an attacker, an event that underscored the volatility of the political atmosphere. In November 2024, a Seoul court convicted Lee of violating election law in a first-instance ruling, imposing a suspended sentence, and the case continued on appeal. At the same time, allegations against the president's family and associates remained the subject of dispute. Each side cited the other's legal troubles as evidence of bad faith, and the line between legitimate law enforcement and political contention became progressively harder for citizens to judge. The chapter reports these matters without adjudicating them beyond what courts have decided.

Structural factors in the media and political culture sustained polarization. Partisan television channels and online video platforms offered audiences consistent reinforcement of their beliefs. Conspiracy theories about election fraud, circulated particularly among some conservative online communities, and about government persecution, circulated among some progressive ones, eroded trust in institutions. Political identities, aligned with region, generation, and gender, hardened. Compromise became costly for politicians within their own camps, since negotiating with the other side risked accusations of betrayal. These factors do not by themselves explain why martial law was declared. They help explain why, by late 2024, each side regarded the other not as a legitimate competitor but as an existential threat, a condition in which constitutional norms of mutual forbearance weakened.

The question of legitimacy ran in both directions. The government's position was that it held a mandate from a presidential election and that an opposition majority was frustrating the will of voters who had elected the president. The opposition's position was that it held a mandate from the Assembly elections and that the president was refusing to accept the constitutional role of the legislature. The constitution settles this in principle: both branches are elected and both have powers. In practice, the system relies on negotiation, which in a polarized climate failed to occur. Importantly, the constitutional remedy for such conflict is not the suspension of one branch by the other. It is election, litigation before the Constitutional Court where appropriate, and compromise.

Section 95.12

The 2024 National Assembly Election and the Breakdown of Executive–Legislative Relations

The parliamentary election of April 10, 2024, was widely seen as a referendum on Yoon's first two years in office. Both major parties used the mixed electoral system, in which 254 seats are elected in single-member districts and 46 through proportional representation, and both created satellite parties to maximize proportional seats, a practice permitted by the electoral law and critiqued by commentators as undermining the intent of proportional representation. The ruling party campaigned on economic management and attacked the opposition leader's legal problems; the opposition campaigned on the government's alleged incompetence, allegations concerning the president's wife, and cost-of-living concerns. Turnout was about 67 percent, high for a parliamentary election.

The result was a significant defeat for the ruling camp. The Democratic Party and its electoral ally secured 175 seats, while the People Power Party and its ally won 108. A new party led by the former justice minister Cho Kuk, the Rebuilding Korea Party, won 12 seats, and a few other seats went to minor parties. The Democratic Party alone held a clear majority of seats, and with its allies and other opposition parties, the combined opposition fell short of the 200 seats needed to override a presidential veto by itself, but it was close enough to make the government's position difficult. The result retained for the opposition the control of the Assembly it had held since 2020 and, with it, the committee chairs, the speakership, and the legislative agenda.

After the election, the ruling party experienced internal turmoil. Its leader resigned, and it went through a period of leadership changes before Han Dong-hoon, the former justice minister and a prosecutor-turned-politician who had led the party's campaign, was elected leader in July 2024. Han's relationship with Yoon, initially close, deteriorated as he publicly pressed the president on the special-counsel issue concerning Kim Keon-hee and other matters. This tension within the conservative camp would matter later: when the martial-law declaration came, Han publicly opposed it, and some members of the party voted with the opposition.

Over the summer and autumn, the confrontation deepened. The opposition passed bills the president vetoed; it pursued impeachment motions and investigations; and in the autumn the budget dispute for the following year escalated. Yoon's approval ratings in public opinion polls remained low, in some surveys approaching the lowest of any president at that stage of his term. At the same time, the political and security environment was complex: North Korea had sent balloons carrying trash across the border, the war in Ukraine and the Pyongyang-Moscow relationship were developing (Chapters 92 and 94), and economic conditions were mixed. These contexts bear on the president's statements about threats, but, as the Court later found, they did not amount to a national emergency of the kind that justifies martial law.

The central point for historians is the distinction between a political defeat and a constitutional emergency. The election restricted Yoon's legislative agenda: he could not pass major bills without opposition support, and he could not prevent opposition bills except through vetoes. That is a normal consequence of divided government in a presidential system, and the constitution provides tools for coping: negotiation, vetoes, executive orders within the law, and the next election. It does not provide for the suspension of the legislature's powers by the executive because the legislature is hostile. Whether the executive and legislature could have found a cooperative path in 2024 is a counterfactual question that historians can discuss, but the record shows that neither side made sustained efforts to reach one. The crisis of December was a choice, not an inevitability, and it was a choice made by one actor against the constitutional framework that both branches operated under.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The South Korean Presidency, 1987–2025

What Changed

  • Presidents came to be subject to removal and, once out of office, to criminal prosecution, a pattern that expanded accountability beyond what existed in the authoritarian era
  • Civil society, the press, and the courts acted as active checks, and mass peaceful assembly became an accepted instrument of political pressure
  • Party competition shifted from regionally anchored coalitions toward generational and gender-inflected polarization
  • Institutions created to restrain power, the Constitutional Court, the CIO, and special counsels, became central actors in presidential politics

What Stayed the Same

  • The formal powers of the presidency, including command of the armed forces, appointments, and emergency authority, remained largely as designed in 1987
  • The single five-year term continued to produce late-term weakness and high stakes for each election
  • Proximity between presidents and large corporations remained a source of scandal and reform proposals
  • The memory of earlier authoritarian rule continued to shape public reactions and institutional responses

When and for whom: Change was uneven and typically came through crises rather than gradual reform. Citizens, journalists, and legislators experienced the changes most directly through mobilization and institutional action; the formal structure of presidential power experienced greater continuity.

Why the pattern matters: Accountability mechanisms improved while the concentration of power persisted, which helps explain how the same system could remove a president and, years later, nearly allow an attempt to suspend the legislature.

Section 95.13

December 3, 2024: Yoon Suk Yeol Declares Emergency Martial Law

On the evening of December 3, 2024, shortly before 10:30 p.m., President Yoon Suk Yeol appeared on television without prior public warning and announced that he was declaring emergency martial law. Speaking from the presidential office, he said the measure was necessary to protect the country from "anti-state forces" and to eliminate threats posed by North Korean sympathizers, and he accused the opposition-controlled National Assembly of paralyzing the government through its use of legislative power, impeachment motions, and budget cuts. He described the opposition as a "den of criminals" and as a force that had turned the legislature into a tool for its own ends. The quoted phrases are drawn from contemporary press translations of the address, and students should consult the official transcript for exact wording (see the Primary Source Window below).

What preceded the announcement is known partly from the Constitutional Court's findings and partly from later testimony, much of which remains subject to criminal proceedings. The following account relies on the points that were widely reported and that the Court addressed. Earlier in the evening, Yoon convened a meeting with several cabinet members at the presidential office. Accounts given later by participants indicate that most ministers present were surprised or expressed reservations, and that the meeting was brief and not a formal deliberation of the kind the constitution requires. Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, who under the constitution must countersign presidential acts of this kind, later stated that he opposed the decision; the Court examined the procedural question and found deficiencies in the process. The Army chief of staff, General Park An-su, was named martial-law commander, and Proclamation No. 1 was issued under his name in the early moments of martial law.

The proclamation imposed sweeping restrictions. It prohibited the activities of the National Assembly and of political parties and political associations; banned assemblies, demonstrations, and political gatherings; barred what it described as fake news, manipulation of public opinion, and false propaganda; placed the press and publications under the control of the martial-law command; and ordered striking medical workers, in the context of an ongoing dispute over medical-school admissions, to return to their posts within forty-eight hours or face punishment under martial law. Violators were subject to arrest, search, and seizure without warrants. The measure was framed as temporary, but its terms, if enforced, would have suspended the legislature, the political parties, and the free press, which are core institutions of constitutional democracy.

The constitutional standards for such a declaration are set out in Article 77. The president may proclaim martial law when there is a need to meet military requirements or to maintain public safety and order by use of military force in time of war, armed conflict, or a comparable national emergency. Martial law may be declared only in accordance with procedures set by law; the president must notify the National Assembly without delay; and if the Assembly demands the lifting of martial law by a majority of its members, the president must comply. The Martial Law Act, enacted in 1949 and amended since, further regulates the process, and the constitution requires that a presidential decision of this kind be deliberated by the State Council, the cabinet, and countersigned by the prime minister and relevant ministers. The Primary Source Window and Vocabulary box below explain these provisions in more detail.

Yoon's stated justifications consisted principally of political allegations about the opposition and claims of threats to the constitutional order. He did not point to a war, an armed conflict, a natural disaster, or an imminent security threat of the kind that martial law provisions historically contemplated. He spoke of paralysis of government through opposition actions and referred to risks associated with North Korea and with domestic political forces. Later, in his impeachment defense and public statements, he said the declaration was intended as a warning to the public about the opposition's conduct, a characterization that the Court rejected as inconsistent with the facts of the troop deployments and the terms of the proclamation. He also made claims related to election integrity, with supporters pointing to concerns about the security of the electoral system; the deployment of soldiers to National Election Commission facilities became one of the matters examined by the Court.

It is essential to distinguish between the allegations made by the president and the evidence of an actual national emergency. The president's claims that the opposition was an "anti-state" threat were assertions of political judgment, not findings of fact; they were not supported by evidence that a national emergency existed, and the Constitutional Court concluded in April 2025 that none did. The legal question was not whether the president disliked or distrusted the opposition's conduct, a matter on which citizens held very different views, but whether the constitutional conditions for martial law were present. The distinction between political controversy and constitutional authority runs through the rest of the chapter. A president may argue that the legislature is acting unwisely or even irresponsibly; the constitution does not authorize the president to suspend it on that basis.

The announcement was received with disbelief. Martial law had not been declared in South Korea since the period of military rule that ended with the transition of the 1980s; the last nationwide martial law, proclaimed in 1979 and extended in May 1980, was associated with the Gwangju massacre (Chapter 79). For many South Koreans, particularly those old enough to remember, the declaration evoked that history; for younger citizens, it was something encountered mainly in textbooks and films. Public reactions in the first minutes, as people watched the announcement or heard of it through messaging applications and online news, were shaped by that memory and by uncertainty about what the military would do.

Historical Vocabulary
Martial Law and Constitutional Emergency Powers

Definition. Martial law is a measure by which military authorities assume certain governmental functions in a defined emergency. Under Article 77 of the South Korean constitution, there are two forms: emergency martial law and precautionary martial law. Both require conditions of war, armed conflict, or a comparable national emergency and procedures prescribed by law, including deliberation by the State Council, notification of the National Assembly, and the Assembly's power to require lifting.

Historical context. The provision appeared in earlier constitutions, and martial law was used repeatedly in the authoritarian era, including after the 1961 coup, in the 1970s, and in 1979–1980. The 1987 revision added the Assembly's authority to demand lifting by a majority of its members, a safeguard that reflected the memory of those uses. The Martial Law Act regulates the powers of martial-law commanders, including limits on restricting the legislature and the courts.

Why it matters. The distinction between constitutional emergency governance and unconstitutional coercion rests on the conditions, the procedures, and the scope of the measures. A declaration that fails the conditions, bypasses the procedures, or suspends the institutions designed to check it falls outside the constitution, however it is labeled.

Potential misunderstanding. Martial law is not simply a strong policy tool available to a president who faces political opposition. It is an exceptional measure for military emergencies, and the constitution places it under legislative oversight precisely because it concentrates power in unelected commanders.

Section 95.14

Troops, Police, and the Confrontation at the National Assembly

Within minutes of the announcement, military and police units began moving. Special-forces troops and other soldiers arrived at the National Assembly compound by helicopter and vehicle, and police units cordoned off the grounds and sought to restrict entry. Soldiers also went to the offices of the National Election Commission and, according to later reports and findings, to other sites. Accounts from the scene, from journalists, and from lawmakers describe soldiers in combat gear with weapons entering the Assembly building, breaking windows, and moving through corridors, while staff members used furniture and fire extinguishers to slow their progress. The number of troops sent to the Assembly was reported to be in the low hundreds; the precise numbers, orders, and objectives are subjects of testimony and prosecution rather than settled fact.

The most serious factual disputes concern what the troops had been ordered to do. Testimony by some commanders, given to the Assembly and in court, indicated that they had been told to prevent lawmakers from entering the chamber or to remove lawmakers from the building so that a quorum could not be reached; other testimony and defense statements said the orders were limited to securing the building. The Constitutional Court in 2025 concluded that the deployment was intended to obstruct the Assembly's activities, and the criminal courts were considering the more specific allegations, including whether orders were given to detain particular politicians, as of this chapter's endpoint. The chapter attributes such claims to their sources and relies on the Court's findings for the constitutional conclusions.

The Assembly's response depended on the actions of several groups. Lawmakers from the Democratic Party and others rushed to Yeouido, many climbing the Assembly's perimeter fence when police had restricted the gates. Opposition leader Lee Jae-myung livestreamed his journey to the building and urged colleagues and citizens to go to the Assembly. Speaker Woo Won-shik also climbed the fence and, once inside, convened an emergency session. Assembly staff and aides, including those who barricaded doors, played a significant role in delaying the entry of troops. Han Dong-hoon, the leader of the ruling party, publicly called the declaration mistaken and said it must be stopped, and a number of ruling-party lawmakers made their way to the chamber, though most of the party's members were absent from the vote.

Citizens went to the Assembly as well. Within an hour or two, crowds of several hundred, then more, gathered outside the building, confronting soldiers and police, shouting for the troops to withdraw, and in some cases physically blocking vehicles. Journalists broadcast live and the images circulated rapidly through television and online platforms. The scene at the Assembly gates, in which unarmed citizens, some of them young, faced armed soldiers, drew explicit comparison with earlier democratic struggles. Many accounts describe soldiers who appeared hesitant, who did not use force against civilians, or who did not carry out their orders aggressively. The reasons for this restraint are disputed: some commanders and soldiers later said they had doubts about the legality of the orders; others pointed to the speed of events and the chain of command. The significance of this restraint for the outcome should not be exaggerated or reduced to a single explanation.

The episode raises questions about orders, compliance, and constitutional duty that historians will continue to analyze. Military officers and police officials are bound by the constitution and by laws that require obedience to lawful orders and, at the same time, prohibit unlawful acts. Where an order is manifestly unlawful, the duty to obey does not apply, though the practical difficulty of making that judgment in minutes under pressure is considerable. In the weeks and months after the declaration, the defense minister, the police chiefs, and several commanders were arrested and charged; some officers testified that they had refused or slow-walked orders, and others defended their actions as carried out under the command structure. Individual accountability, including the role of mid-level officers, remained under litigation. For the purposes of this chapter, the main point is that the outcome of the night depended on decisions by many people, in and out of uniform, rather than on institutions operating automatically.

Section 95.15

The National Assembly Rejects Martial Law: December 4, 2024

By about midnight, enough lawmakers had entered the Assembly building to meet in the main chamber. The Assembly has 300 members, and a vote to demand the lifting of martial law under Article 77 requires a majority of the total membership, meaning 150 votes, not merely a majority of those present. This threshold was the practical test of the night: the opposition and its allies needed at least 150 members physically in the chamber to vote. At about 1:00 a.m. on December 4, Speaker Woo Won-shik called the vote. The resolution demanding that martial law be lifted passed with 190 votes in favor and none opposed, among the 190 members present. The 190 included the Democratic Party's members and other opposition lawmakers as well as a small number of members of the People Power Party, including the party's leader, Han Dong-hoon, who participated in the vote.

The resolution constituted a formal demand under Article 77. Under the constitution, the president was then required to lift martial law. The legal effect did not depend on the military's compliance or the president's agreement, but on the president's constitutional duty. Nevertheless, the interval between the vote and the actual lifting is historically significant. Reports indicated that troops remained at the Assembly for a period after the vote and then withdrew; Yoon convened a cabinet meeting, and at about 4:30 a.m. he appeared on television to say that he had accepted the Assembly's demand, that the troops had been withdrawn, and that martial law would be lifted after the cabinet met. The cabinet approved the lifting later that morning. The declaration thus lasted roughly six hours from announcement to withdrawal, though its formal lifting occurred somewhat later.

Why did the Assembly's vote prove decisive? Several elements converged. First, the constitution had been amended in 1987 specifically to give the legislature the authority to reverse martial law, and the National Assembly invoked that authority. Second, the technical requirement of 150 members was met because enough lawmakers reached the chamber; had troops succeeded in sealing the building or detaining members, the vote might not have been possible. The time taken by soldiers to reach the chamber, the barricades set by staff, the speed of lawmakers' movement, and the hesitation of some commanders collectively determined the outcome. Third, public communication was open: livestreams, television coverage, and messaging platforms ensured that citizens knew what was happening and that the world could see it. The declaration did not succeed in controlling the press, despite the proclamation's terms. Fourth, the presence of citizens outside the Assembly raised the political cost of using force against them.

Counterfactuals are an uncertain basis for historical conclusions, but they illustrate the stakes. If the Assembly had not been able to meet, the constitutional safeguard would have been bypassed in practice, and the question of how the system would have responded, through courts, public protest, the military's own decisions, or international pressure, would have remained open. Scholars who emphasize the contingency of the outcome point to these possibilities; scholars who emphasize institutional resilience point out that opposition came from many directions, including from within the ruling party, the armed forces, and the public. Section 95.20 and the Historical Debate on this subject consider these interpretations. Students should avoid both a triumphalist account that treats the outcome as guaranteed and a pessimistic one that dismisses the genuine strength of the institutions.

Section 95.16

Impeachment, Public Demonstrations, and the Suspension of Presidential Authority

Opposition parties filed an impeachment motion against Yoon on December 4, the same day martial law was lifted, and the Assembly scheduled the vote for December 7. By then the ruling party was divided. Some members called for the president's resignation or an orderly departure; Han Dong-hoon initially sought an early, negotiated exit for the president short of impeachment; others stood firmly behind Yoon. On the morning of December 7, Yoon issued a televised apology, saying he had caused anxiety and would leave questions about his term to his party. That evening, as the Assembly met, most People Power Party members walked out of the chamber, leaving the vote short of the 200 members required for a valid vote on a presidential impeachment. Only a small number of ruling-party lawmakers, three by most accounts, remained and voted; with 195 members present, the ballot box was not opened, and the motion lapsed. Hundreds of thousands of people were demonstrating outside, and the failure intensified public anger.

The opposition refiled the motion, and on December 14, 2024, it passed. The vote was 204 in favor and 85 against, with 3 abstentions and 8 ballots declared invalid, a margin well above the 200-vote threshold, indicating that roughly a dozen members of the ruling party voted in favor, since the opposition parties together held about 192 seats. The motion's grounds, summarized in the Primary Source Window above, centered on the martial-law declaration and its implementation. On passage, Yoon's powers were suspended, and he remained in the presidential residence in Hannam-dong while the Constitutional Court considered the case. Prime Minister Han Duck-soo assumed the duties of acting president.

The public demonstrations of December 2024 resembled, and were consciously compared with, the candlelight rallies of 2016. Large crowds gathered near the National Assembly and in central Seoul, waving light sticks, many of them young people who had adopted K-pop fan culture's light sticks and music in place of candles, in what commentators noted as a distinctive feature of the mobilization. Many participants were women in their twenties and thirties. The crowds sang popular songs and held creative banners, and there was little violence. Meanwhile, supporters of Yoon, including many conservative groups and some religious organizations, held their own large rallies, arguing that the opposition and the courts were persecuting the president and that claims about election fraud had been ignored. These rallies became larger and more intense during the following weeks, including at the Constitutional Court and the president's residence.

The ruling party fractured further. Han Dong-hoon, who had announced before the December 14 vote that he would support impeachment, resigned as party leader on December 16 after the motion passed, and the party's leadership was reorganized under an emergency committee. The conservative movement, which had been partially defined by its anti-communism and its respect for law-and-order institutions, divided over whether to defend a president who had used the army against the legislature. Some conservatives denounced the declaration; others defended it as a response to opposition obstruction; and still others sought a middle position, accepting the impeachment process but opposing removal.

The crisis then spread to the executive's second line. Prime Minister Han, as acting president, resisted opposition demands to appoint three justices to the Constitutional Court, whose bench had three vacancies out of nine, and to accept special-counsel bills concerning the martial-law investigation. On December 27, the Assembly impeached Han as acting president, with 192 votes, on grounds that included alleged involvement in the martial-law decision and the refusal to fill the Court's vacancies. A dispute arose over whether the vote threshold for impeaching an acting president should be that for a president (two-thirds) or for a prime minister (a majority); the Assembly's speaker applied the lower threshold, and the question later figured in the Constitutional Court's decision in Han's case. Finance Minister Choi Sang-mok became acting president. In the following days, he appointed two of the three Court vacancies, leaving the third unfilled. The institutional dynamics, in which a president, then his acting successor, were both suspended within two weeks, led observers to ask whether the constitutional order could function amid a prolonged contest, and also how much authority a caretaker official should exercise.

Cause and Effect

The Martial-Law Crisis of December 2024

Long-term causes

A constitutional design that combined a strong president, a single term, and unresolved emergency powers; regional and ideological divisions; the politicization of the prosecution and the judiciary; and a media environment that rewarded confrontation.

Short-term causes

A narrow 2022 election; an opposition-controlled Assembly after 2020 and 2024; repeated vetoes, impeachments, and budget conflicts; allegations concerning the president's family; and very low presidential approval.

Immediate trigger and event

The budget dispute and the president's judgment, contested by the Court, that opposition actions amounted to a national crisis; the declaration of martial law on December 3.

Immediate effects

The Assembly's 190–0 vote; withdrawal of martial law; the arrests of senior officials; two impeachments within two weeks; and mass demonstrations on both sides.

Long-term consequences

The Court's April 2025 removal of Yoon; an early election; renewed debate over constitutional reform, emergency powers, and prosecutorial and military accountability.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the declaration. Presidential decisions, institutional design, polarization, and contingent circumstances interacted, and different historians weigh them differently.

Historical complexity: The crisis was a deliberate choice by one actor, but the conditions that made the choice thinkable were produced over years by many actors.

Section 95.17

Investigations, Arrest, and the Constitutional Crisis of Early 2025

Three separate legal tracks ran in parallel after December 14, 2024, and keeping them distinct is essential. The first was the impeachment adjudication before the Constitutional Court, which would decide whether Yoon must be removed. The second was the criminal investigation and prosecution, which concerned whether Yoon and others had committed crimes, principally the offense of leading an insurrection. The third comprised procedural and jurisdictional disputes about which agency could investigate, which court could issue warrants, and how detention periods were calculated. Outcomes in one track did not decide outcomes in another. A charge is not a conviction, and a court's ruling on detention is not a ruling on guilt or on constitutional violation.

The investigation was divided among several agencies: the police, the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials, the prosecution, and, later in 2025, independent special counsels authorized by new legislation. The defense minister, Kim Yong-hyun, and several military and police commanders were arrested and indicted in December on charges related to insurrection and abuse of power. The CIO summoned Yoon for questioning three times in December, and he did not appear. On December 31, a court issued a detention warrant. Yoon's lawyers argued that the CIO lacked jurisdiction over the charge of insurrection and that the warrant was invalid; the CIO, along with the court that issued it, maintained that its authority over senior officials' abuse of power extended to related offenses. The jurisdictional question would continue to be litigated for months.

On January 3, 2025, investigators attempted to execute the warrant at the presidential residence in Hannam-dong. They were blocked by members of the Presidential Security Service, who stood with soldiers assigned to the compound, and after a prolonged standoff the investigators withdrew. The episode raised acute concerns: it showed state security personnel resisting a judicial warrant against the president, and it prompted debate over the limits of the security service's duties. The acting president at the time, Choi Sang-mok, was criticized by the opposition for not intervening. Outside the residence, supporters of Yoon gathered in large numbers, some camping in the cold, and there were clashes with those opposing him. Yoon issued statements to supporters in which he described the country as endangered by hostile forces and said he would fight to the end.

On January 15, the CIO and police returned in force with hundreds and eventually reportedly more than a thousand personnel, entered the residence compound, and detained Yoon, who surrendered himself after releasing a recorded statement in which he said he was complying to avoid bloodshed but still considered the process unlawful. He thereby became the first sitting president in South Korean history to be taken into custody. A court issued a formal arrest warrant on January 19, an event followed by a night of violence in which supporters broke into the Seoul Western District Court, leading to the arrest of dozens of people. Prosecutors indicted Yoon on January 26 on the charge of leading an insurrection, which under the criminal code carries a sentence of death or life imprisonment upon conviction. As with any indictment, this was an accusation; the criminal trial would test it.

Procedural controversies continued. In March, the Seoul Central District Court, in a decision that surprised many, canceled Yoon's detention on the ground that the indictment had been filed after the legally permitted detention period had expired, depending on whether the period was calculated in hours or days. Prosecutors chose not to appeal, and Yoon was released on March 8, after fifty-two days in custody. His supporters celebrated; critics argued that a technicality was being used to release a person accused of a grave offense. The ruling did not affect the impeachment case, which proceeded independently, but it illustrated how multiple legal systems operating at once could produce results that were hard for the public to reconcile. Yoon was later re-arrested in July 2025 at the request of an independent special counsel on additional charges, including obstruction of the warrant execution, and the criminal trials were continuing as of this chapter's endpoint.

Meanwhile, the Constitutional Court heard Yoon's impeachment case in a series of hearings that concluded on February 25, 2025, during which witnesses, including military and police officials and members of the Assembly, testified. The Court also had to decide the separate case of Prime Minister Han Duck-soo. On March 24, 2025, it rejected the impeachment petition, restoring Han’s powers as acting president. Five justices voted to dismiss the impeachment on the merits, two justices would have rejected the petition on procedural grounds, and one justice would have upheld the impeachment and removed him. The Court’s disposition was therefore not a simple 7–1 merits vote; the different opinions reached the same immediate outcome by different legal routes. The decision was widely read as showing that the Court treated each official's case separately and that a decision for Han carried no implication for Yoon, whose actions were far more directly at issue. After the ruling, Han resumed his role, and a long period of uncertainty, in which the country had effectively had a series of acting leaders, continued until the Court's decision on Yoon.

Public mobilization intensified on both sides in February and March. Large rallies for removal were held at Gwanghwamun and near the Court; large rallies for reinstatement, among them demonstrations by groups of conservative Christians and by online communities, were held in the same districts. Opinion polls showed a majority in favor of upholding impeachment, but a stable minority opposed it, and the polarization of public attitudes was visible in the difficulty of finding common ground even on the facts of the night of December 3. As the Court's decision approached, authorities prepared for possible violence, closing roads and deploying large numbers of police. The preparation underscores how seriously officials took the risk of a breakdown of order, a risk that did not materialize on the day of the decision.

Section 95.18

April 4, 2025: The Constitutional Court Removes Yoon Suk Yeol

On the morning of April 4, 2025, the Constitutional Court announced its decision, delivered by Acting Chief Justice Moon Hyung-bae, in case number 2024Hun-Na8. At about 11:22 a.m., the Court declared that all eight justices upheld the National Assembly's impeachment of Yoon. Its judgment took effect immediately: Yoon ceased to be president at that moment, and under the constitution, an election had to be held within sixty days. The Court had taken 111 days from the filing of the case and 38 days after the final hearing to decide, longer than the period in the Park case, a delay that had caused public anxiety and allowed speculation, particularly among Yoon's supporters, about possible outcomes. Individual justices offered separate opinions on some procedural questions, but no justice dissented on the central finding.

The Court addressed procedural objections first. Yoon's lawyers had argued, among other things, that the Assembly had skipped a committee process before voting, that the impeachment grounds had been altered by shifting the charge from insurrection to constitutional violations, and that the Court's composition was improper. The Court rejected these arguments and proceeded to the merits. On the substance, it found that Yoon's declaration of martial law violated the constitution and the Martial Law Act. Its reasoning, as reported in the Court's announcement and in legal commentary, can be summarized in several parts. First, no situation existed that met the constitutional condition of a national emergency comparable to war or armed conflict; the political and administrative difficulties the president cited, including opposition legislation and budget actions, did not qualify. Second, the procedures were deficient, including the failure to hold a proper cabinet deliberation and the improper handling of the required countersignature. Third, the proclamation's restrictions on the Assembly and on political parties exceeded what the Martial Law Act permitted, and its restrictions on the press and its threats against strikers infringed fundamental rights.

The Court also held that the deployment of troops and police to the National Assembly infringed the Assembly's authority and its right under Article 77 to demand the lifting of martial law, and that the deployment of troops to National Election Commission offices violated the principles of electoral administration and the political neutrality of the military. It accepted, in addition, findings concerning attempts to detain political figures. Reports indicated that the Court acknowledged all five grounds set out in the Assembly's impeachment resolution. The Court rejected the president's characterization of the declaration as a warning or as a symbolic act to alert the public, concluding that the facts of the military deployment were inconsistent with that description. It characterized the violations as grave violations of the constitutional governance structure, and it held that the benefits of removal to the protection of the constitution outweighed any national loss caused by removing the president.

The reasoning can be compared with the Court's 2017 decision removing Park Geun-hye. Several continuities are evident. Both decisions were unanimous; both applied the standard that the violation must be serious enough to justify overriding the electoral mandate; both stressed the president's duty to uphold the constitution; and both were delivered live, with the country watching. But the differences are equally important. The Park case concerned the abuse of presidential authority through improper relations with a private individual and the solicitation of corporate contributions, acts that were corrupt but that did not directly attack the structure of constitutional government. The Yoon case concerned the use of the armed forces against the legislature, the electoral administration, and the press, an attack on the institutions through which the constitution functions. In the Park decision, the Court declined to treat the Sewol disaster as an independent ground; in the Yoon decision, it accepted each of the principal grounds. The two cases also differ in what preceded them: one resulted from months of revelations and protest, the other from a sudden decision by the president himself.

Yoon's reaction was limited at first. His office said it would respect the decision, and he left the presidential residence a week later, on April 11, apologizing to supporters in a statement. The ruling party said it accepted the ruling. Supporters protested, and the Court's neighborhood was closed to traffic and heavily policed, but the day passed without the large-scale violence that some had feared. Crowds outside the Court who favored removal celebrated; others, in tears, said they could not accept the judgment. In Gwangju, residents gathered at May 18 Democracy Square, where the city's 1980 uprising is commemorated, to watch the broadcast. The scene is worth noting because it connected the 2025 judgment to the longer history of the democratic struggle, though the connection was drawn by participants and commentators, not by the Court.

The ruling did not end the legal process. Impeachment determined only the question of office; the criminal case, in the ordinary courts, would determine guilt or innocence and any punishment. Because a removed president loses immunity, prosecutors were free to proceed with a trial, which began in April 2025. This chapter, ending on December 31, 2025, does not narrate the trial's outcome; students should consult later sources for subsequent judgments. What the April 4 decision did establish, as a matter of constitutional law, were the grounds on which the nation's highest constitutional tribunal concluded that the president had violated his oath and the limits of his office.

Section 95.19

The June 2025 Presidential Election and the Victory of Lee Jae-myung

The constitution required an election within sixty days of Yoon's removal, and acting president Han Duck-soo announced that it would take place on June 3, 2025. The compressed schedule, which gave parties about two months to nominate candidates and campaign, was reminiscent of 2017, but the political atmosphere was different. The central question was how voters would weigh the martial-law crisis against other concerns, including the economy, which faced uncertainty about United States tariffs (Chapter 94), the cost of living, and fatigue with political conflict.

Han Duck-soo's own position complicated the transition. After the Court restored him as acting president in March, he resigned on May 1, 2025, to seek the presidency himself, which passed the acting role first to Finance Minister Choi Sang-mok and then, after Choi's own resignation, to Education Minister Lee Ju-ho, who held it until the election. Han ultimately did not remain in the race after the conservative camp failed to unify behind him. Counting Yoon's suspension, the country was led by a succession of acting officeholders for nearly six months, an indication of the extent of the disruption.

The Democratic Party nominated Lee Jae-myung in a primary contest in which he defeated rivals by large margins, and he campaigned on restoring stability, reviving the economy, and holding accountable those responsible for the martial-law declaration. His legal troubles were an issue: in March 2025, an appeals court had acquitted him in the election-law case for which a lower court had convicted him in November 2024, and in May the Supreme Court sent the case back for retrial, which was subsequently postponed. The electoral and legal questions that surrounded him were contested, with opponents arguing that he should not be eligible and supporters arguing that the charges were politically driven. This chapter notes that the proceedings were unresolved as of the election and that, under the constitution as commonly interpreted, a sitting president's criminal trials are not conducted during the term, a point of controversy in its own right.

The conservative camp's succession struggle was prolonged and bitter. The People Power Party nominated Kim Moon-soo, a veteran politician and former labor minister who had been prominent in opposition to the impeachment, after a primary process in which several candidates, including Han Dong-hoon, were eliminated. There was then an unsuccessful attempt to replace Kim with Han Duck-soo as a unity candidate, which created internal conflict. The party faced the challenge of distancing itself from the martial-law declaration without alienating its base, many of whom defended Yoon. Kim declined, in the view of critics, to sufficiently repudiate the declaration; supporters said he was respecting the sentiments of the party's voters. A third conservative candidate, Lee Jun-seok of the Reform Party, appealed particularly to younger men.

On June 3, 2025, South Koreans voted with a turnout of 79.4 percent, the highest in a presidential election since 1997. Lee Jae-myung won with 49.42 percent of the vote, receiving about 17.29 million votes, the largest number ever cast for a winning candidate in a presidential election in South Korea. Kim Moon-soo received 41.15 percent, and Lee Jun-seok 8.34 percent. Kim conceded that night, saying that he accepted the people's decision. Lee did not win a majority, and the vote shares of the conservative candidates combined exceeded 49 percent, a reminder that the conservative electorate remained substantial despite the crisis. Regional patterns persisted, though Lee also won in the capital region and Chungcheong, areas decisive in earlier elections.

Lee's term began on June 4, 2025, at 6:21 a.m., at the moment the National Election Commission certified the result, without the usual transition period, as in 2017. He was formally inaugurated in an abbreviated ceremony at the National Assembly within hours. In his early speeches, he pledged to unify a divided country, to revive the economy, and to ensure that the martial-law declaration was addressed through accountability. The new administration's early measures, including special-counsel investigations, are noted in Section 95.20, which addresses the end of 2025. The election restored the ordinary authority of the presidency and completed the constitutional process, from impeachment to Court judgment to election, that the constitution prescribes.

Section 95.20

South Korean Democracy at the End of 2025: Resilience, Polarization, and Institutional Limits

Assessing the decade from Park's impeachment to Lee's election requires holding several facts together. The constitution's emergency power was invoked in a way the Constitutional Court held to be unlawful; the legislature voted in the early hours of the morning to reverse it; the president was impeached by the Assembly and removed by a unanimous Court; and an election was held on schedule, with a high turnout and a conceded result. At the same time, troops were sent to the Assembly, an acting president was impeached, a sitting president resisted a judicial warrant, a court building was attacked by rioters, and a substantial part of the public continued to defend the former president. Both sets of facts are part of the historical record.

The institutional safeguards worked at multiple levels. The constitutional provision allowing the Assembly to end martial law functioned within hours. The Court demonstrated independence and reached a clear judgment, though it did so over a period during which uncertainty weighed on the country. Civil society, including journalists who broadcast live, labor and civic organizations, and individual citizens, acted in ways that raised the cost of repression. Military and police personnel in many cases exercised restraint. The election administration, whose offices had been targeted, completed its task. These elements are not independent: each depended on others, and the failure of one might have changed the outcome.

Yet significant weaknesses were also exposed. The time available to the Assembly was short and depended partly on physical access to the building. The president retained the ability to command the military and to appoint its leadership, and the loyalty of certain commanders was a central factor in the planning, according to the Court's findings and prosecutors' allegations. The impeachment process, which requires two-thirds of the Assembly, proved vulnerable to boycott, as the failure on December 7 showed. The leadership gap, with multiple acting presidents, tested a part of the constitution that had not been designed for prolonged use. Questions about the Presidential Security Service's role, the limits of the CIO's jurisdiction, and the interplay of courts and investigative agencies remained unresolved.

Polarization persisted. The conservative movement's defense of Yoon, which included allegations about election fraud that were not supported by the Court's findings or by election authorities, represented a refusal by part of the public to accept the Court's account. The Democratic Party's large majority after June 2025, with control of both the executive and the legislature, raised a different concern, voiced by critics: that a government with such a majority could reshape institutions to its advantage, including through new special counsels, changes to the judiciary or prosecution, and pursuit of political opponents. Supporters of the government replied that the measures were needed to complete accountability for the martial-law attempt and to reform institutions that had failed to prevent it. Both sets of claims should be understood as political arguments that history will test.

Proposals for reform circulated across the political spectrum. They included amendments to the constitution to change the president's single five-year term to a four-year, renewable term, which would allow voters to judge performance; reforms to emergency powers, requiring prior parliamentary or judicial review of martial law; changes to the electoral system to reduce winner-take-all dynamics; restructuring the presidential security and military command; and further reform of the prosecution, with various proposals for the allocation of powers among prosecutors, police, and independent bodies. Political discussion of constitutional revision has recurred since 1987 without agreement, and the experience of 2024–2025 added urgency, but the end of 2025 found no consensus and no enacted constitutional change on these points.

The preservation of constitutional government in 2024–2025 was consequential. It prevented a possible long-term suspension of institutions, it demonstrated to public and officials that the constitutional limits were enforceable, and it established a legal record, in the Court's judgment, of what the constitution forbids. But it did not remove the underlying tensions. The causes of polarization, including the media environment, regional and generational divisions, and the winner-take-all character of presidential politics, remained. So did the structural features of the presidency. A fair assessment is that South Korean democracy showed substantial resilience and substantial vulnerability, and that these were not contradictory but related aspects of the same constitutional system.

This chapter has kept its focus on South Korea's democratic development. The broader condition of the divided peninsula in 2026, including how these events relate to the regime in the North, the strategic environment, and changing attitudes toward unification, is the subject of Chapter 96.

Historical Debate

The December 2024 Crisis: Evidence of Democratic Strength or Institutional Vulnerability?

The question: Does the failure of the martial-law declaration show that South Korea's democratic institutions are strong, or does the fact that the declaration was attempted, and came close to success in the view of some analysts, show that they are fragile? Scholars, jurists, and commentators in Korea and abroad have offered several answers.

Interpretation A — Successful constitutional resistance: Emphasizing the speed of the Assembly's vote, the restraint of some security personnel, the open press, the cross-party participation, the Court's unanimous judgment, and the orderly election, this view holds that the system performed as designed. It sees the episode as evidence that democratic norms had been internalized by citizens, officials, and the military, and notes that the attempt was reversed within hours.
Interpretation B — An attempted suspension of democratic institutions that exposed vulnerabilities: Emphasizing that troops reached the Assembly, that senior officials took part, that the outcome depended on narrow margins of time, and that a substantial minority defended the declaration, this view holds that the system was tested closely. It points to the continuing powers of the presidency, the fact that the Assembly depended on physical access to the chamber, and the persistence of polarization.
Interpretation C — Both, with attention to contingency: A third approach regards the two interpretations as complementary. The outcome was produced by institutions and individual decisions together, and different circumstances, such as a faster troop deployment or different command decisions, might have produced a different result. On this view, historians should neither celebrate the outcome as inevitable nor treat it as proof of collapse.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the timeline of the night, testimony and court findings about military orders, the vote counts, the Court's judgment, survey data on public attitudes, and comparative evidence from other democracies. Many details remain subject to criminal proceedings.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and counterfactual reasoning, and in part the evidence about the president's intentions and the plan's feasibility, which was still being established in court. It also concerns the standard for evaluating resilience: whether a system is resilient if it survives, or only if the threat is never seriously posed.

Why it matters: The answer affects what reforms seem necessary. If the system worked, reform is a matter of refinement; if it nearly failed, structural change in emergency powers, command of the armed forces, and the presidency may be urgent.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how South Korea experienced two presidential impeachments within a decade and what they reveal about the achievements and vulnerabilities of the democratic order built after 1987. Its central question was how a democracy capable of removing presidents through constitutional procedure could also remain vulnerable to extraordinary concentrations and abuses of executive power. The answer developed here is that both capacities arose from the same constitutional design: accountability mechanisms were real and effective, while the formal powers of the presidency remained broad.

The first crisis grew from the 2016 revelations about Choi Soon-sil's influence over state affairs and corporate fundraising for foundations connected to her. Investigative journalism and the broadcast of documents from a discarded tablet turned allegations into a national crisis, and weekly candlelight rallies, large, peaceful, and organized by a wide range of groups and individuals, pressed the National Assembly to act. The Assembly impeached Park Geun-hye on December 9, 2016, with 234 votes, requiring defections from the ruling party, and the Constitutional Court unanimously removed her on March 10, 2017, declining to treat the Sewol ferry response as an independent ground.

Moon Jae-in, elected in May 2017, entered office with expectations of democratic renewal, fairness, and institutional reform. His government created the Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials and reallocated investigative powers between prosecutors and police, but reform of the prosecution became a source of intense conflict, symbolized by the 2019 Cho Kuk controversy and by the confrontation between the Justice Ministry and Prosecutor General Yoon Suk Yeol. The chapter showed that attempts to make a powerful institution more accountable can themselves become polarizing, because competing conceptions of accountability and independence were both defensible.

Housing costs, employment and wage policy, and perceived unfairness eroded public confidence, culminating in the 2021 by-election defeats, even as the government earned international attention for its pandemic response and won a large parliamentary majority in April 2020. The pandemic experience showed a democracy using statutory authority and public communication to manage a crisis, while raising questions about privacy. The persistence of division despite electoral success demonstrated the limits of a mandate.

Yoon Suk Yeol's narrow victory in March 2022, by about 0.73 percentage points, produced a presidency that confronted an opposition-controlled Assembly from its first day. Reliance on former prosecutors, the relocation of the presidential office, controversies over disaster accountability and allegations about the president's wife, repeated vetoes, a high number of impeachment motions, and budget conflicts deepened mutual distrust. The April 2024 election gave the opposition 175 seats against 108 for the governing camp and its ally. The chapter argued that this was a political defeat, not a constitutional emergency, and that the constitution offers negotiation, vetoes, litigation, and elections, not suspension of the legislature, as remedies.

On December 3, 2024, Yoon declared emergency martial law, issuing restrictions on the Assembly, political parties, assemblies, and the press. The Constitutional Court would later find that no national emergency existed and that the declaration violated the constitution and the Martial Law Act. Troops and police were sent to the Assembly and to election offices. Lawmakers, staff, journalists, and citizens converged on Yeouido, and some soldiers acted with restraint. At about 1:00 a.m. on December 4, the Assembly voted 190–0 to demand the lifting of martial law under Article 77, and Yoon withdrew the declaration roughly six hours after announcing it.

An impeachment motion failed on December 7 when ruling-party members left the chamber, and succeeded on December 14 by 204 votes to 85. Prime Minister Han Duck-soo, as acting president, was himself impeached on December 27, and Finance Minister Choi Sang-mok became acting president. Mass demonstrations on both sides, a divided conservative movement, and an investigation involving the CIO, prosecutors, and later special counsels followed. Yoon was detained on January 15, 2025, formally arrested on January 19, indicted for leading an insurrection on January 26, released in March on a procedural ruling, and re-arrested in July; the criminal proceedings remained unresolved at this chapter's endpoint.

On March 24, 2025, the Court declined to remove Han (five justices dismissing the impeachment, two rejecting it on procedural grounds, and one favoring removal), and on April 4 it unanimously removed Yoon. The judgment found violations of the constitution and the Martial Law Act, interference with the Assembly's authority and with election administration, and infringements of fundamental rights, and it rejected the description of the declaration as a mere warning. Comparison with the 2017 Park decision showed shared standards, particularly proportionality and the duty of the president to uphold the constitution, and a difference of kind: the Park case concerned abuse of office, the Yoon case concerned an assault on the institutions of constitutional government.

Lee Jae-myung won the early election of June 3, 2025, with 49.42 percent against Kim Moon-soo's 41.15 percent, on a turnout of 79.4 percent, and took office on June 4 without a transition period. The result restored ordinary presidential authority while confirming continued regional and ideological division and a large conservative electorate.

At the end of 2025, South Korean democracy had demonstrated substantial resilience: legislative reversal of martial law, judicial independence, a free press, civic mobilization, and an orderly transfer of power. It had also exposed vulnerabilities: the closeness of the margin on December 3–4, the breadth of presidential command powers, the unresolved question of how emergency powers should be limited, persistent polarization, and the risk that large majorities might reshape institutions to partisan advantage. The preservation of constitutional government was consequential, but the tensions that produced the crisis had not disappeared, and the broader condition of the peninsula in 2026 is taken up in Chapter 96.

Key Terms

Impeachment
A constitutional procedure in which the National Assembly accuses a high official of violating the constitution or laws; for a president, passage requires two-thirds of members and suspends the president's powers pending the Court's ruling.
Constitutional Court
The nine-member court, operating since 1988, that adjudicates impeachments, reviews laws, and resolves disputes among state organs; six votes are required to remove a president.
Acting president
The official, normally the prime minister, who exercises presidential duties while the president's powers are suspended or the office is vacant.
Sixth Republic
The constitutional order in force since 1988, created by the 1987 revision following the June democratic struggle.
Candlelight movement
The mass peaceful demonstrations of October 2016 to early 2017 demanding Park Geun-hye's accountability and removal.
Choi Soon-sil scandal
The 2016 affair concerning a private individual's alleged influence over state affairs and corporate donations to two foundations, which led to Park's impeachment.
Sewol disaster
The April 2014 ferry sinking that killed 304 people and became a symbol of state failure; not accepted by the Court in 2017 as an independent ground for removal.
Special counsel
An independent investigator authorized by legislation to examine a specific matter, used in 2016–2017 and again in 2025.
Prosecutorial independence
Protection of prosecutors from improper political direction in deciding whom to investigate and charge, in tension with mechanisms of accountability.
Corruption Investigation Office for High-ranking Officials (CIO)
An investigative body created by legislation in 2020 and operating from 2021, with authority over senior officials, which led the January 2025 detention of Yoon.
Cho Kuk controversy
The 2019 dispute over the appointment and resignation of a justice minister amid allegations about his family, which intensified conflict over prosecutorial reform.
Income-led growth
The Moon government's policy approach emphasizing wage increases and social spending as drivers of demand.
Divided government
A situation in which the president's party lacks a majority in the legislature, as in 2022–2025.
Satellite party
A party formed by a larger party to capture additional proportional-representation seats under the mixed electoral system, used in 2020 and 2024.
Veto
The president's power to return a bill to the Assembly, which can be overridden only by a two-thirds vote.
Martial law
An exceptional measure under Article 77 permitting military authorities to assume certain governmental functions in war, armed conflict, or a comparable national emergency, under legislative oversight.
Article 77
The constitutional provision governing martial law, including the requirement to notify the Assembly and to lift martial law when a majority of its members demand it.
Martial Law Act
The statute regulating martial law procedures and the powers of martial-law commanders, which the Court found Yoon had violated.
Countersignature
The requirement that the prime minister and relevant ministers co-sign certain presidential acts, which the Court found improperly handled in the declaration.
Quorum
The minimum number of members needed for a valid vote; the December 7, 2024 impeachment vote failed because fewer than 200 members voted.
Insurrection
A criminal offense under Korean law of rising to subvert the constitutional order, punishable by death or life imprisonment; Yoon was indicted for leading one, an accusation distinct from the Court's impeachment finding.
Presidential Security Service
The agency responsible for the president's protection, whose personnel blocked execution of a detention warrant on January 3, 2025.
Early election
An election required within sixty days after a president is removed, held on June 3, 2025.
Polarization
The sharpening of political divisions along partisan, regional, generational, and gender lines, reducing willingness to compromise.
Civilian control of the military
The principle that armed forces are subordinate to constitutional civilian authority and must not be used against democratic institutions.
Rule of law
The principle that officials, including the president, are bound by law and subject to judicial review.
Democratic backsliding
A scholarly term for erosion of democratic institutions, applied in debates about whether and how the 2024 episode fits the concept.
Jeonse
A Korean rental system in which the tenant pays a large refundable deposit instead of monthly rent, sensitive to housing-market and interest-rate changes.
Yongsan relocation
The 2022 move of the presidential office from the Blue House to the former Defense Ministry compound.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows democratic institutions under direct test rather than in abstract design. The constitution of 1987, the product of a long struggle against authoritarian rule, was used in 2016–2017 to remove a president who had abused her office, and again in 2024–2025 to reverse and then judge an attempt by a president to suspend the legislature. Seeing both episodes together prevents two simple conclusions: that democracy was secure because it worked, or that it was failing because it was challenged.

The decade also reveals how democratic accountability can generate its own conflicts. Reforms aimed at powerful institutions, the use of impeachment and investigation as instruments, and the high stakes of single-term presidencies all contributed to a politics in which each side saw the other as illegitimate. The events of December 2024 were the choice of one actor, but they unfolded in a climate that many actors helped to create, and the chapter has tried to keep that distinction clear.

The chapter also connects South Korea's recent history to its earlier struggles. The memory of Gwangju, the Sixth Republic's safeguards against martial law, and the repertoire of peaceful assembly were all inherited from earlier periods and mattered on the night of December 3. At the same time, new elements, digital communication, the mobilization of younger citizens, and the role of independent courts and investigative agencies, show that democratic practice changed. What survived beyond the chapter was an unsettled question: whether the institutions that passed the test will be reformed to reduce the chance of another, or whether the underlying tensions will persist.

Chapter 96 takes the narrative to its endpoint by considering both Korean states in 2026. This chapter contributes to that synthesis a detailed account of how one of them preserved constitutional government through a crisis, and why that outcome should be understood as consequential, contingent, and incomplete.
