The Chaebol and the Miracle on the Han River

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1974, on the shore of Mipo Bay near Ulsan, a shipyard that had been under construction for little more than two years delivered its first very large tanker. The ship was a monument to industrial ambition. The loans, the foreign designs, the training of thousands of newly recruited workers, and the debts that made it possible were far less visible than the steel hull itself.

The yard belonged to the Hyundai group, led by Chung Ju-yung, and it has become one of the most frequently retold episodes in the economic history of the Republic of Korea. Ulsan had been, in large part, a district of farms and fishing communities until the early 1960s, when the government designated it as an industrial site. By the mid-1970s it contained a refinery and petrochemical works, an automobile assembly plant, and a shipyard whose docks were designed for ships larger than almost any Korean firm had ever built. A visitor in 1974 could see, in a single place, what the South Korean government and its business partners meant by industrialization: ground levelled, docks excavated, cranes erected, and a launch ceremony attended by the head of state.

What the visitor could not see were the arrangements behind the spectacle. The yard depended on a foreign shipowner willing to place an order with a builder that had never built a large ship, on British and other European technical partners, on loans arranged through international banks and backed by export-credit guarantees, and on a domestic banking system that the state could direct. It depended on engineers and foremen who learned shipbuilding while building, on welders and assemblers who worked long hours in dangerous conditions for wages that were low by the standards of the countries that bought Korean ships, and on suppliers of steel, machinery, and components. It also depended on a judgment, shared by Chung and by senior officials, that the risk of failure was worth taking. If the venture had failed, the cost would have fallen on a family-controlled corporate group, on state-controlled banks, and ultimately on the national economy.

This chapter studies the institutions that made such ventures possible: the chaebol, the family-controlled, diversified business groups that became the dominant corporate organizations of South Korea's industrialization. Chapter 75 explained how Park Chung-hee's government organized planning, credit, infrastructure, diplomacy, and industrial policy. This chapter turns to the firms themselves. It asks how Samsung, Hyundai, LG, SK, Daewoo, and other groups were formed, how they raised capital, how they negotiated with the state, how they entered industries as different as textiles, steel, ships, automobiles, and electronics, how they managed and sometimes disciplined their workers, how they depended on thousands of smaller firms, and how the same structures that accelerated growth also concentrated economic power and accumulated financial risk.

The chapter's central argument is that South Korea's conglomerates grew from entrepreneurial firms, many of them founded before or during the colonial period and the 1950s, through a combination of state-supported credit, export competition, diversification, technological learning, and very large industrial investments. Their growth helped transform the national economy. But their reliance on borrowing, their concentrated family control, their close political relationships, and their dominance of key markets also produced unequal opportunities and systemic financial vulnerabilities. Neither the state nor the conglomerates deserve exclusive credit for the transformation often called the "Miracle on the Han River." Workers, engineers, smaller businesses, household savers, foreign lenders, public enterprises, and international commercial partners were indispensable, and historians of the period, including those writing for the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, have emphasized this broader set of causes.

The evidence available is uneven, and students should learn its character from the start. Corporate histories, founder memoirs, and company anniversary publications supply dates, product milestones, and executive statements, but they are often written to celebrate founders and to present a coherent narrative of inevitable success. Government decrees, statistical series, and economic surveys document policy and aggregate performance but say little about shop-floor experience. Labor testimony, petitions, trial records, and the work of historians of labor supply that missing perspective, though they too reflect particular purposes and positions. Independent scholarship by economists, political scientists, and historians provides comparison and critique. This chapter uses all of these kinds of sources, and its Primary Source Windows ask students to compare them.

The chapter also observes boundaries set by the textbook's division of historical labor. The political and administrative history of economic planning belongs to Chapter 75; the comprehensive social history of urbanization, housing, education, and consumption belongs to Chapter 81; the political narrative of 1987 belongs to Chapter 82; the 1997 financial crisis and its restructuring belong to Chapter 84; and the later history of semiconductors, broadband, and smartphones belongs to Chapter 87. Chapter 76 identifies the financial and governance risks that matured in the 1990s without narrating the crisis itself, because to read the whole of the earlier period as merely a prelude to 1997 would be an act of hindsight, and would obscure the real achievements and the real choices facing people at the time.

Understanding this history matters for more than economic reasons. The chaebol shaped employment, regional development, family life, education, politics, and national self-image in South Korea, and they continue to shape how Koreans and outside observers think about the relationship between private enterprise, public power, and social fairness. Arguments about whether the large business groups served the nation or captured it began during their rise, and they continue in contemporary South Korean politics. A careful historical account must therefore explain both the accomplishment and the cost, and must resist both the founder legend and its mirror image, the story of unmitigated corporate exploitation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define chaebol and distinguish a diversified, family-controlled business group from an individual incorporated company and from Japanese zaibatsu and keiretsu.
  2. Trace the commercial and manufacturing origins of Samsung, Hyundai, LG, SK, and Daewoo, distinguishing the founding of individual firms from later group identities.
  3. Explain how preferential bank credit, government guarantees, foreign borrowing, and tax incentives shaped corporate balance sheets and investment decisions.
  4. Analyze the reciprocal relationship between export targets, state financing, and corporate performance, distinguishing performance discipline from political favoritism.
  5. Compare the corporate trajectories of Samsung, Hyundai, LG, and SK, identifying how founders, industries, and timing produced different paths.
  6. Distinguish POSCO, a state-backed enterprise, from the family-controlled chaebol, and explain how the two complemented one another.
  7. Assess the development of shipbuilding, automobiles, and electronics as processes of technological learning rather than simple copying.
  8. Evaluate the role of trading companies and international commercial partners in connecting Korean manufacturers to world markets.
  9. Interpret labor in the chaebol economy, including the different conditions of women and men workers, employer discipline, state restriction, and worker organization.
  10. Explain the importance of subcontractors, suppliers, engineers, and small and medium-sized enterprises, and the unequal bargaining positions between them and large firms.
  11. Analyze family ownership, cross-shareholding, group coordination, and the difference between legal ownership and effective control.
  12. Evaluate the achievements and distortions of chaebol-led capitalism, including debt, concentration, political influence, and the first regulatory responses of the 1980s.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What made the chaebol a distinctive form of business organization, and why did diversified family-controlled groups, rather than specialized or widely held companies, come to dominate South Korean industry?
  2. To what extent did the business foundations of the Miracle on the Han River exist before the 1961 coup, and what did the state add?
  3. How did preferential credit work in practice, and how did the state try to distinguish firms that earned continued support from those that did not?
  4. Why did firms accept projects, such as shipbuilding or heavy industry, that their own executives sometimes regarded as risky?
  5. How did South Korean firms move from assembly and licensed production toward engineering, design, and research, and what role did foreign partners play in that movement?
  6. Whose labor and whose business independence sustained corporate growth, and how did workers and subcontractors respond to the terms offered to them?
  7. What is the difference between corporate growth and national welfare, and how can historians measure each?
  8. Why could the same structures of concentrated control and heavy borrowing produce both rapid expansion and systemic vulnerability?
  9. How should historians weigh company-produced founder narratives against independent scholarship, labor testimony, and government records?

Historical Context

South Korea's corporate sector did not begin in 1961. During the colonial period (1910–1945, examined in Chapters 55 to 62), Japanese capital dominated large-scale industry, and the heavy chemical, metallurgical, and hydroelectric investments of the late colonial empire were concentrated disproportionately in the northern peninsula and Manchuria. The southern peninsula remained more agricultural, though it contained textile mills, rice-milling, brewing, and light manufacturing. Korean entrepreneurs were not absent: some operated textile firms, banks, trading houses, and retail enterprises under colonial regulation, and their experience of managing businesses in a controlled and unequal environment shaped the first generation of postwar business leaders. Colonial industrial organization also offered a model of the large, family-controlled business group, the Japanese zaibatsu, whose name and ideograph the Korean word chaebol inherited.

Liberation in 1945 and the division of the peninsula (Chapters 64 to 66) removed much of the industrial base from the southern economy and removed Japanese owners and technicians from the factories of the south. The U.S. Army Military Government in Korea took control of formerly Japanese-owned property, called "vested property," and the Republic of Korea government continued to sell and lease these enterprises after 1948. Individuals with capital, connections, or managerial experience acquired mills, plants, shops, and warehouses, often at prices and on payment terms that proved advantageous. The Korean War (Chapters 67 to 72) then destroyed or damaged much of the physical economy, but it also created demand for construction, transport, supplies, and services, and it brought a large volume of American military and economic assistance.

During the 1950s under Syngman Rhee (Chapter 74), a business class formed within an economy shaped by foreign aid, import licensing, and controlled foreign exchange. Domestic industries that processed imported raw materials, especially flour, sugar, and cotton textiles, the so-called "three white" industries, prospered, and access to aid-financed materials, bank loans, and official licenses often depended on political connections. Historians use the term rent-seeking to describe the pursuit of profit through such privileged access rather than through productivity alone. The period nevertheless built manufacturing experience, trained workers and managers, and accumulated capital in a small number of firms.

Park Chung-hee's seizure of power in 1961 and the developmental state of the 1960s and 1970s (Chapter 75) changed the incentives. The government targeted exports, controlled the banking system, took over foreign-borrowing guarantees, and measured private firms against export performance. In the 1960s the economy turned toward labor-intensive manufactured exports such as textiles, garments, wigs, plywood, and electronics assembly; after the declaration of the Heavy and Chemical Industrialization drive in January 1973, the state pushed firms toward steel, nonferrous metals, shipbuilding, machinery, electronics, and petrochemicals. International conditions assisted this strategy: the 1965 normalization of relations with Japan brought compensation funds, loans, and technology links; the Vietnam War brought contracts, remittances, and foreign exchange to Korean firms and workers; and the open markets of the United States and Western Europe provided buyers. In the 1970s the oil shocks and Middle Eastern construction boom reshaped the environment again, and the Korean companies that had learned construction at home and in Southeast Asia went to the Gulf.

This was the setting in which the chaebol emerged as the principal corporate institutions of industrialization. The chapter that follows treats them neither as inevitable products of Korean culture nor as mere creations of the state, but as firms whose founders, managers, and workers responded, sometimes brilliantly and sometimes at heavy cost to others, to incentives and constraints that changed over four decades.

Division I — The Rise of the Chaebol and the Institutions of Corporate Capitalism · Section 76.1

What Is a Chaebol? Family Enterprise, Diversification, and Corporate Organization

The Korean word chaebol (재벌, written with the same Chinese characters as the Japanese zaibatsu) is usually translated as "business group" or "conglomerate." Both translations capture something, but neither is sufficient. A chaebol is a collection of legally separate companies, called affiliates, that operate in many industries and are subject to the strategic direction of a single controlling family. A single group might own firms in construction, insurance, trading, chemicals, machinery, and electronics while keeping them linked through ownership stakes, shared financing, shared personnel, and a central staff office that answered to the group chairman. The group was a management and ownership structure, not usually a single incorporated company, and that distinction is essential for understanding both its strengths and its opacity.

The term also carries a history of judgment. In Korean public discussion chaebol has often had a critical tone, implying concentrated wealth and political privilege, whereas the more neutral term gieop jipdan ("enterprise group") is used in official and statistical contexts. Historians use chaebol because it is the established term, while recognizing that it bundles description with evaluation. It is also important not to project the fully developed groups of the 1980s back onto the small firms of the 1940s and 1950s. Samsung in 1938 was a modest trading business; Hyundai in 1946 was an automobile repair shop. The groups became chaebol through decades of expansion.

Three features distinguish the chaebol model from a conventional large manufacturer. The first is diversification: groups expanded into unrelated or loosely related industries, often at the government's urging or in response to opportunities created by new credit and licenses. The second is family control: the founder and his descendants retained strategic authority through direct shareholding, ownership of key affiliates, and the chairmanship, even when their equity in individual companies was modest. The third is group-level coordination: a central planning or secretariat office moved capital, managers, and strategic priorities among affiliates in ways that a market of independent firms could not. Samsung's central secretariat, for example, was established in the late 1950s and grew into a powerful group staff.

Students should distinguish legal ownership of an individual company from effective control of a group. A family might hold only a small share of equity in the group's largest company yet direct it through ownership of other affiliates that held shares in it. This difference, examined in detail in 76.17, was less elaborate in the early decades than in the 1980s and 1990s. It nevertheless explains why Korean public debate has focused so persistently on the gap between a family's ownership stake and its authority. Entrepreneurial management, in the sense of rapid decisions by a founder willing to take risks, coexisted with concentrated control and weak influence for minority shareholders, and each of these was a source of both speed and vulnerability.

Historical Vocabulary
Chaebol, Zaibatsu, and Keiretsu

Definition. A chaebol is a diversified, family-controlled group of affiliated firms. The Japanese zaibatsu were pre-1945 family-controlled groups, such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi, usually organized around a family holding company and dissolved by the Allied Occupation after 1945. The keiretsu were the looser postwar networks of Japanese firms linked by cross-shareholding, a main bank, and trading relationships, typically without a controlling family at the center.

Historical context. Korean business leaders knew the Japanese examples, and the word chaebol was borrowed from the older term. But Korean groups developed under different conditions. They were built by Korean founders in a postwar economy short of capital, relied heavily on state-directed bank credit rather than on an internal group bank, and kept the founding family at the center. For long periods, Korean competition law also prohibited pure holding companies, so family control was exercised through other arrangements.

Why it matters. The comparison clarifies what was distinctive. Historians debate how much Korean groups drew on Japanese models and how much they were improvised responses to Korean circumstances.

Potential misunderstanding. The three terms are not synonyms. Calling a chaebol a "Korean keiretsu" obscures the family-control feature, and calling it a "Korean zaibatsu" suggests a direct continuity that the evidence does not establish.

Section 76.2

From Trading Houses and Small Manufacturers to Industrial Conglomerates, 1930s–1960s

The major groups began as small and varied enterprises. Lee Byung-chul, born in 1910 in what is now South Gyeongsang Province, founded Samsung Sanghoe ("Three Stars Trading Company") in Daegu in 1938, trading in dried fish, produce, and other goods. Chung Ju-yung, born in 1915 into a farming family in what is now North Korea, worked in Seoul in a rice shop and an automobile repair business before founding Hyundai Auto Service in 1946 and Hyundai Civil Industries in 1947. Koo In-hwoi, a Jinju-born merchant, founded a chemical enterprise in Busan in 1947 that began with cosmetic cream and plastic goods. Chey Jong-gun acquired and rebuilt the war-damaged Sunkyong textile factory in 1953. Kim Woo-choong, who founded Daewoo in 1967, belonged to a later generation and began in export trading. None began as an industrial conglomerate, and several had been traders or builders rather than manufacturers.

Three conditions shaped the first generation. The first was colonial economic organization. Colonial rule had trained some Koreans in commerce, textile production, and small-scale industry while excluding most from large enterprises and technical positions. When Japanese owners left in 1945, there were few Korean managers with experience running large plants, but those who did had a considerable advantage. The second was the disposal of vested property. Former Japanese-owned factories, shops, and warehouses were sold or leased under the American military government and then the Republic of Korea. Individuals who could raise the necessary funds, or who held privileged access, acquired productive assets at low prices and on favorable terms. Historians differ over how much of the postwar business class was created by these transfers, but there is agreement that they were a significant source of capital for some firms. The third was American assistance. The reconstruction after 1953 was financed in part by U.S. aid, which supplied raw cotton, wheat, and sugar to processors. Samsung's Cheil sugar refinery (1953) and Cheil textile mill (1954) are examples of firms that prospered by processing aid-supplied or imported materials in these "three white" industries.

The war itself was also a commercial opportunity. Chung Ju-yung's construction firm worked on projects for the U.S. military and for reconstruction, including bridges and buildings, and learned contracting on a larger scale. Lee's trading company, moved to Busan during the war, made profits that later funded manufacturing. The experience of war and reconstruction trained business owners in dealing with foreign officials and procurement systems, and left a legacy of political entanglement. Access to licenses, foreign exchange, and bank credit under the Rhee government depended on political relationships, and the business class that prospered in the 1950s was acutely aware of this dependence.

This history matters because it corrects a common simplification. The business foundations of the Miracle on the Han River preceded Park Chung-hee's 1961 coup. By 1961 there existed a small group of experienced entrepreneurs with capital, managerial skills, and political connections, as well as a modest manufacturing base in textiles, food processing, and chemicals. The coup government initially treated the business class with suspicion; after the coup, prominent businessmen were investigated under a law against illicit wealth accumulation, and, according to widely repeated accounts, Lee Byung-chul and others proposed that they build industrial plants in place of paying penalties. Whatever the precise details, the outcome was a working relationship in which the government needed firms capable of executing its export plans and the firms needed access to credit and protection. The state did not create the entrepreneurs. It selected among them, financed them, and set the terms of their growth.

Some early arrangements also reveal the political risks of the relationship. In 1966 a scandal involving smuggling by a Samsung-linked fertilizer company, Hankook Fertilizer, forced Lee Byung-chul to step back from management and to transfer shares in the company to the state, according to historical accounts. The episode shows that closeness to the government protected firms only conditionally and that political authority could discipline powerful business leaders. It is a useful early example of the pattern, discussed in later sections, in which both sides could threaten and reward each other.

Section 76.3

State-Directed Credit, Banking, and the Financing of Corporate Expansion

The central question of corporate finance in the 1960s and 1970s was how firms with little capital could expand so quickly. The answer lay largely in credit. After the 1961 coup the government gained control over commercial banks, which had been privatized under Rhee but were brought back under state ownership and remained effectively under state direction even after nominal privatization in the early 1980s. Banks lent not according to a commercial assessment of each borrower, but according to priorities the government set. Specialized institutions, such as the Korea Development Bank, financed industrial investment, and from 1974 the National Investment Fund channeled savings into heavy and chemical industries.

The mechanisms were various. Policy loans carried interest rates below what a free market would have set, sometimes negative in real terms when inflation was high. Export credit was extended against export letters of credit, which tied financing directly to documented sales abroad. Government payment guarantees on foreign loans, authorized in the early 1960s, allowed Korean firms to borrow from foreign banks and suppliers that would not have lent to them on their own credit. Tax incentives, tariff exemptions on imported inputs for export production, and favorable depreciation rules reduced costs. Access to foreign exchange, which was scarce and controlled, was itself a privilege. Chapter 75 described these policies from the government's perspective. From the perspective of a firm, they changed balance sheets, investment decisions, and risk.

The most important effect was on leverage. Because credit was cheap and abundant for favored firms, they financed expansion mostly with borrowed funds rather than with equity. A World Bank historical comparative table based on manufacturing-sector financial statements reports a Korean debt-to-equity ratio of 487.9 percent in 1980 (compared with 377.1 percent in 1979), still above 300 percent in several subsequent years. This is a sector-wide accounting ratio, not a balance-sheet figure for every chaebol or a debt-to-assets percentage, and its international comparison depends on consistent definitions. The consequences were double-edged. High leverage enabled large investments, rapid construction, and fast learning. It also made firms sensitive to interest rates, exchange rates, and bank decisions, and it gave lenders, and through them the state, continuing leverage over borrowers.

Access was not equal. Large firms with strong export records and political standing received large shares of preferential credit, whereas small enterprises relied more heavily on the unregulated "curb market" of private lenders, where interest rates were far higher. On August 2, 1972, an emergency presidential decree was proclaimed, effective August 3, that froze and restructured existing curb-market debts and converted them into longer, lower-interest loans, a measure that relieved indebted firms (examined further in the Primary Source Window below). This unequal access is among the structural reasons why larger firms grew faster than smaller ones and why the economy became so concentrated.

Subsidized finance was not simply free money. Loans had to be repaid, and firms were generally expected to deliver specified results, such as export volumes, completed plants, or production targets. Nor was every favored investment successful: some projects lost money, some firms failed, and the government sometimes had to arrange rescues or consolidations. The financial system was best understood as one that rewarded firms for meeting state-defined goals while shielding them from some market discipline, and the quality of the state's selection varied over time and across industries.

A letter of credit did more than document an export order: when a bank discounted it, an exporter could obtain working capital before a shipment was paid for. That timing mattered to firms that had to buy imported components, pay wages, and meet delivery deadlines weeks or months before foreign buyers remitted payment. Equipment loans raised a different problem. A shipyard or petrochemical plant required large outlays long before it earned revenue, and repayment schedules could be more demanding than the project's early cash flow allowed. Corporate finance thus joined short-term production credit to long-lived investment debt. A firm's ability to obtain a guarantee was only a first step; it also needed foreign currency for imports and eventually enough export receipts to service its liabilities. These distinctions help explain why fast-growing firms could face liquidity crises despite showing expanding sales.

Historical Vocabulary
Policy Loans, Payment Guarantees, and the Curb Market

Definition. A policy loan was a bank loan directed to a sector or firm the government wished to promote, typically at a favorable rate. A payment guarantee was a state commitment to repay foreign lenders if a Korean borrower defaulted. The curb market was the informal, unregulated network of private lenders that supplied credit, at high interest rates, to firms and households that could not obtain bank loans.

Historical context. These institutions grew from a financial system in which banks were few, capital was scarce, and the state sought to turn household savings into industrial investment. They reflect the developmental state's belief that finance should serve production.

Why it matters. Together they explain how firms could borrow on a scale far beyond their own capital and why the largest firms enjoyed such advantages over small ones.

Potential misunderstanding. A policy loan was not a gift, and a guarantee was not unconditional approval of every project. Equally, state-directed credit should not be assumed to have been allocated purely on technical merit.

Section 76.4

Export Targets, Business-State Negotiations, and Corporate Performance

Exports were the principal measure by which the government judged private firms. South Korean exports, which were small at the beginning of the 1960s, reached US$100 million in 1964 and passed US$10 billion in 1977, a pace that official statistics and later scholarship both document. Beginning in the mid-1960s, President Park chaired regular Export Promotion Expansion Meetings, attended by ministers, bankers, and business leaders, at which targets were reviewed and obstacles discussed. Firms received recognition for performance: awards, honors, and public praise for exceeders, and, more consequentially, better access to loans, licenses, and import privileges. Underperformers risked losing credit.

For firms, the system offered both incentives and pressures. The incentive was concrete: a firm that exported successfully gained access to cheap export credit, tax concessions, and raw material imports, and a reputation with officials that could be converted into approval for new ventures. The pressure was also real. Firms were pushed to enter priority industries, sometimes ones in which their executives saw considerable risk. Business leaders negotiated with officials, accepting projects they considered uncertain in exchange for financing, political support, or the chance for future expansion. Hyundai's entry into shipbuilding and Samsung's later entry into semiconductors both involved calculations of this kind, as did the government's request to Daewoo in 1978 to take over a troubled shipyard (see 76.9).

Historians distinguish two aspects of the system. One was performance discipline: the export test was relatively objective, measurable, and hard to evade, because success was determined by foreign buyers. Scholars who emphasize this feature argue that it forced Korean firms to meet world standards of price and quality, and that it separated the system from other developing economies in which protected firms never had to compete abroad. The other aspect was political favoritism: access to credit and licenses also depended on relationships, and support sometimes continued for firms that performed poorly but were politically useful or too large to fail. Evidence for both exists, and it is a mistake to treat either as the whole story. The relationship was reciprocal, but not equal. The government held greater power, because it controlled credit and licenses, though large firms became increasingly important to the state's own goals and developed influence of their own.

The International Monetary Fund's later historical treatment, along with other economic studies, identifies preferential credit and export performance as especially important features of the corporate growth system. Neither can be reduced to the other. Exports were the objective test, credit was the reward, and both depended on a political arrangement in which firms and officials needed one another.

Export promotion functioned through a repeated relationship rather than a one-time government order. Ministries and banks assessed applications for import licenses and credit, enterprises negotiated targets they believed could be met, and performance was reviewed as shipments and foreign-exchange receipts materialized. The arrangement could reward managers able to identify reliable overseas customers and meet delivery standards, but it did not eliminate bargaining. Firms argued for favorable exchange rates, lower borrowing costs, and exceptions when world demand weakened. Officials could also revise priorities when an industry proved expensive or foreign markets narrowed. These encounters were consequential for smaller exporters: a subcontractor might produce the goods sold by a larger trading company without gaining the same direct access to policymakers or subsidized loans. An export total recorded under a major group's name therefore concealed a larger network of independent production and contractual dependence.

Division II — The Major Conglomerates: Founders, Corporate Cultures, and Industrial Expansion · Section 76.5

Samsung: From Trading and Manufacturing to Electronics and Diversified Enterprise

Samsung's history shows how a trading firm became an industrial group by following opportunities created by aid, protection, credit, and the state's export strategy. After founding Samsung Sanghoe in 1938, Lee Byung-chul re-established the business in Seoul and Busan after the war under the name Samsung Mulsan (Samsung Trading Corporation), and used trading profits to enter manufacturing. Cheil Jedang, a sugar refinery, was established in 1953 and Cheil Mojik, a woolen and cotton textile mill in Daegu, in 1954, at a time when imported raw materials and sheltered domestic markets made processing profitable. Samsung also entered finance, with insurance companies in the 1950s and, over time, securities and other businesses. By the 1960s, the group controlled firms in food processing, textiles, insurance, retail, and trade.

Samsung Electronics, founded in 1969, was an early product of the government's attempt to build an export-oriented electronics industry. The firm began with black-and-white televisions, radios, and household appliances, and its first years depended on technical partnership with Japanese companies, notably Sanyo and NEC, which supplied designs, components, and training. A joint venture with Corning in the 1970s produced picture tubes, and Samsung expanded production of consumer electronics for both domestic and export markets. This was learning through partnership: the firm learned to assemble, then to manufacture components, and then to design.

The group's entry into semiconductors came in two steps. In 1974, Samsung bought a half interest in a small Korean chip-making firm, Korea Semiconductor, giving it a foothold in the technology, and later in the decade it took full control. Then, in 1983, the aging Lee Byung-chul publicly announced the group's commitment to memory-chip production, an announcement often called the "Tokyo Declaration" because of where it was made, and the group rapidly developed a 64K DRAM memory chip, relying on licensed designs and process know-how from American and Japanese partners, including the U.S. firm Micron. Samsung then developed more advanced memory chips of its own in the mid-1980s. The investment was enormous and risky; the semiconductor business lost money for several years before it became a core profit center. This chapter treats it as an example of the shift from imported technology toward greater domestic technical capability, and leaves the later global semiconductor and smartphone history to Chapter 87.

Management, family control, and capital allocation help explain Samsung's trajectory. A central group staff coordinated strategy and moved capital among affiliates, and the founder made investment decisions that individual public companies might have hesitated over. Samsung also built a reputation for systematic recruitment, introducing open competitive hiring of university graduates from 1957, a practice the company has described as pioneering, though claims of precedence should be treated with caution. After Lee Byung-chul's death in November 1987, his third son, Lee Kun-hee, became group chairman, an episode of family succession that preserved continuity of control.

The group's expansion also illustrates the risks of concentrated, diversified growth. Samsung entered heavy industry, including petrochemicals and shipbuilding at Geoje, in the 1970s partly in response to the government's drive. These ventures consumed capital and management attention and did not all prove profitable. Samsung's history is therefore not a story of smooth, planned progress but of repeated bets, some of which paid off spectacularly while others strained the group.

Samsung's semiconductor milestones require separation into several institutional stages. Its initial 1974 acquisition was a partial stake in Korea Semiconductor, a small producer whose capacity was not equivalent to the later large-scale memory business; Samsung's official corporate history records completion of that acquisition in 1977. The 1983 commitment to very-large-scale integration was a new strategic decision, involving specialist recruitment, foreign process knowledge, expensive clean-room facilities, and tolerance of losses while manufacturing yields improved. Samsung's published chronology identifies development of a 64-kilobit DRAM in 1983 and operation of the first Giheung line in 1984. These are company-reported milestones that establish sequence, not proof that all design knowledge or machinery originated domestically. The case is particularly useful for distinguishing purchase of an existing enterprise, acquisition of technical capability, and development of an internationally competitive manufacturing system: they were connected steps, but they did not occur on a single founding day.

Section 76.6

Hyundai: Chung Ju-yung, Construction, Shipbuilding, and Industrial Ambition

Hyundai began as an automobile repair shop and became, through construction, the largest of the heavy-industry groups. Chung Ju-yung's Hyundai Auto Service (1946) and Hyundai Civil Industries (1947) merged in 1950 as Hyundai Construction. During and after the Korean War, the firm worked on construction projects for U.S. forces and for the reconstruction. In the 1960s it expanded rapidly through domestic infrastructure contracts, including work connected to the Gyeongbu Expressway and to dams, and through overseas work, beginning with a highway project in Thailand in the mid-1960s and continuing with construction for U.S. forces in Vietnam. In the 1970s, Hyundai became a major contractor in the Middle East, taking on port and industrial projects in Saudi Arabia valued at close to a billion dollars in one case, and employing Korean workers abroad in large numbers.

Construction taught lessons that Hyundai carried into other industries. It required mobilizing equipment, labor, and finance quickly, accepting fixed-price contracts with penalties for delay, and managing large projects under uncertainty. It also generated cash and foreign exchange. Chung developed a management style that emphasized speed, direct supervision, and a willingness to begin projects before every technical problem was solved, relying on foreign partners and on rapid learning to fill gaps. This approach produced remarkable achievements and sometimes expensive failures. Accounts of Chung's career, including his own memoir, stress his persistence and tolerance for risk, and they should be read as celebratory narratives, as the Primary Source Window below discusses.

Hyundai Motor Company was established in December 1967 and began assembling Ford Cortinas under a partnership with Ford in 1968. Although Hyundai and Ford considered a deeper joint venture in 1970, negotiations over production and export control continued; the South Korean government withdrew approval of the proposed venture in 1973. Hyundai consequently pursued its own vehicle and integrated manufacturing program, including the Pony, with design from the Italian stylist Giorgetto Giugiaro's firm, engines and technical support from Mitsubishi, and experienced engineers recruited from Britain. Hyundai's entry into shipbuilding in the early 1970s, covered in 76.11, was an even larger gamble. The group also entered machinery and other heavy industries, and in 1983 Hyundai Electronics was established to compete in electronics.

Hyundai is best understood as an integrated group rather than the product of one man's will. Its affiliates fed one another: the construction arm provided cash, expertise in managing large projects, and a captive customer for steel and equipment; the shipbuilding and motor divisions provided new industrial platforms; and group financing supported all of them. The founder's family, including several brothers and, later, sons, held key positions, and succession and the division of the group became issues in later decades. Engineers, supervisors, and workers, who carried out the projects and absorbed technical knowledge, receive less attention in founder narratives than they deserve. Hyundai's growth was possible because large numbers of employees were willing or compelled to work under demanding conditions, a point taken up in 76.15.

The failed Ford negotiations illustrate why Hyundai's independence was a corporate and political choice rather than merely an engineering achievement. As a local assembler, Hyundai could learn production routines and build a dealer system, but a foreign partner might retain control over model selection, design, export territories, and the most valuable components. Hyundai wanted a basis for a Korean-branded car that could enter markets beyond the domestic economy. The proposed deeper joint venture discussed in 1970 never fully resolved those interests; negotiations continued, and the government withdrew its approval in 1973. That left Hyundai to combine Italian styling, Japanese components and engineering, British automotive management expertise, and Korean assembly and supplier development for the Pony. The resulting product embodied substantial international collaboration, yet control over the venture's commercial direction rested in Korea. The choice brought greater scope for learning and greater financial exposure if the new model failed.

Section 76.7

LG: Lucky, GoldStar, Chemicals, Consumer Products, and Electronics

LG's history is a story of chemical-based diversification and of consumer products. Koo In-hwoi began with a cosmetic cream business in Busan, and in 1947 established a chemical enterprise, initially under the name Lak-Hui Chemical Industrial Corporation and later called Lucky Chemical Industrial Corporation. It made cosmetics and then plastic goods, such as caps and household items, using the chemical know-how and the early supply of plastics that the cosmetics business made possible. Koo worked in partnership with Huh Man-jung and his family, whose shared management of the group became a distinctive feature, and whose descendants later separated the GS group from LG in 2005.

GoldStar was established in 1958 to manufacture electrical and electronic goods, and in 1959 it produced the first Korean radio. This was a natural extension of the plastics business, since early radios needed plastic cases, and it was also an example of an entrepreneur identifying a protected domestic market. GoldStar moved from radios into televisions (the first Korean-made television appeared in the mid-1960s), refrigerators, washing machines, and air conditioners, relying on technical agreements with Japanese firms such as Hitachi. Through the 1960s and 1970s the group integrated chemicals and electronics, producing materials and finished goods, and entered petroleum refining through a partnership with the American company Caltex.

The corporate identity changed over time and students should use the names appropriate to each period. The enterprises were separately known as Lak-Hui, Lucky, and GoldStar; the group came to be called Lucky-GoldStar in the early 1980s; and the name LG was adopted in 1995. After Koo In-hwoi's death in 1969, his eldest son Koo Cha-kyung led the group, and in 1995 the leadership passed to the next generation. The group's pattern of succession from eldest son to eldest son, and of shared management with the Huh family, distinguished it from Samsung and Hyundai, where succession was more contested.

LG's trajectory differed from that of Samsung and Hyundai in several respects. It was strongly consumer-oriented and developed more gradually, without the dramatic gambles on shipbuilding or large-scale construction. Its early position in electronics, however, was eventually overtaken by Samsung's more aggressive investments in memory chips, and its development of semiconductors was less successful. The comparison shows that corporate outcomes depended not only on state policy, which was broadly similar for all groups, but on founders' strategies, risk preferences, and timing.

An industrial account of Lucky-GoldStar should track the relationships between materials and finished products. Plastic cases, electrical components, and chemical inputs were not glamorous achievements, but a supply of affordable, consistent parts was essential to making radios and appliances commercially viable. GoldStar's own retrospective chronology identifies a domestically produced radio in 1959, refrigerator in 1965, and black-and-white television in 1966; these company assertions should be checked against manufacturing records when comparing claims of national 'firsts.' Its development also involved overseas sales channels, technical licensing, and factory investment. According to company materials, GoldStar's electronics exports exceeded US$100 million in 1978, and it opened a United States production operation in the early 1980s. These milestones show a pattern different from Hyundai's shipbuilding gamble: product improvements and successive manufacturing platforms were linked to a steadily enlarging network of distributors, suppliers, and technical partners.

Section 76.8

SK: Sunkyong Textiles, Synthetic Fibers, Petrochemicals, and Energy

In 1953 Chey Jong-gun acquired the damaged Sunkyong textile works in Suwon and rebuilt it after the Korean War, establishing the Sunkyong enterprise from an existing factory site rather than founding the predecessor mill from nothing. The firm made woven textiles and exported fabrics, and its early growth illustrated the importance of textile exports in the 1960s. In 1969 the group established Sunkyong Synthetic Fibers, entering the production of man-made fibers, which required more capital and technology than weaving and which were supported by Japanese and other foreign technical knowledge. Through the 1970s the group integrated vertically, moving from fibers toward the petrochemical raw materials from which synthetic fibers were made, and expanded into trading and related activities.

Chey Jong-gun died in 1973, and his brother Chey Jong-hyun became chairman, leading the group during the period of its greatest transformation. In December 1980 Sunkyong acquired the Korea Oil Corporation, a formerly state-controlled oil refiner; the Yukong name followed in July 1982. The acquisition was a major turning point: it gave the group a position in energy, one of the largest sectors in the economy, and broadened its corporate structure and influence. It also reflects a pattern, familiar from the case of Daewoo's takeover of a troubled shipyard, in which the government transferred strategic enterprises to private groups it judged capable of running them.

The group's family succession followed a brother-to-brother and then father-to-son pattern, with Chey Jong-hyun's son Chey Tae-won assuming leadership in 1998. Management institutions, including a group-level coordinating office and a distinct emphasis on professional management in some affiliates, developed over time, though the Chey family retained strategic control. The group adopted the name SK in the late 1990s, and the students should be careful not to apply that name retrospectively to the Sunkyong enterprises of the 1950s through 1980s.

SK's later entry into telecommunications, semiconductors, and digital industries belongs to Chapter 87. For the purposes of this chapter, the key point is that earlier industrial diversification, especially the move into energy and chemicals, gave the group the capital, scale, and political position that made subsequent expansion possible. SK also illustrates how a group that began in a light industry, textiles, with no obvious technological advantage could, through successive acquisitions and state-supported transitions, become a major presence in sectors very different from its origins.

The 1953 origin also shows the difference between taking possession of a productive asset and making it productive again. SK's own heritage account describes Chey acquiring the damaged Suwon textile factory, salvaging machinery from the wreckage, and restoring looms rather than opening a newly equipped mill on an empty site. That account is corporate-produced and celebrates the founder, but its description of reconstruction fits the broader postwar context of scarce machinery and capital. Later moves from weaving to man-made fibers required access to chemical feedstocks, production expertise, and larger pools of finance, so integration was a response to practical dependencies in the supply chain, not simply diversification for its own sake. The 1980 petroleum acquisition again altered those dependencies: access to refining and related chemical production offered potential advantages to textile and petrochemical affiliates, while exposure to energy markets introduced a different pattern of price, procurement, and capital risk.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Small Firms of the 1940s and 1950s to the Diversified Groups of the 1980s

What Changed

  • Scale and scope: firms with a few dozen employees became groups of dozens of affiliates and, in the largest cases, hundreds of thousands of employees across many industries.
  • Technology: from repair, trade, and simple processing to shipbuilding, automobiles, and electronics.
  • Finance: from small private capital and aid-linked windfalls to large-scale bank debt and foreign borrowing.
  • Market: from sheltered domestic demand to world markets.

What Stayed the Same

  • Family control remained central, passing from founders to relatives and sons.
  • Dependence on the state for credit, licenses, and political protection persisted, though in changing forms.
  • Reliance on workers' long hours and on subordinate suppliers continued.

When and how: Change was gradual in the 1950s, accelerated in the 1960s with the export drive, and reached its most rapid phase in the 1970s with heavy industry. It was uneven across groups and sectors. Who experienced it: Founders and managers experienced rising status and wealth; workers experienced the change as new factories, new discipline, and migration, and many experienced continuity in long hours and low pay. Why it matters: It shows that the chaebol of the 1980s were the result of cumulative decisions over decades, not an instantaneous creation of the state.

Division III — The Expansion of Heavy Industry and the Diversification of Corporate Enterprise · Section 76.9

Daewoo and the Other Major Business Groups: Alternative Paths to Expansion

The large-business sector was never confined to Samsung, Hyundai, LG, and SK. Daewoo offers the clearest example of an alternative path. Kim Woo-choong founded Daewoo Industrial in 1967 as an export trading company, initially selling textiles and garments to the United States. Its growth depended on the apparel export quotas that the U.S. imposed on Asian producers, on commercial relationships with American buyers, and on a willingness to move quickly into new products and markets. Through the 1970s Daewoo used trading profits and bank credit to acquire or create manufacturing operations. In 1976 it took over a troubled machinery maker, which became Daewoo Heavy Industries, and in 1978 it acquired control of the Saehan Motor Company, which became Daewoo Motor, bringing a partnership with General Motors. In the same year, at the government's request, Daewoo took over the unfinished and financially troubled Okpo shipyard on Geoje Island, which it developed into a large shipbuilding operation.

These transactions illustrate a distinctive feature of Daewoo's expansion: growth by acquisition, often of enterprises the government wished to rescue or complete. Daewoo expanded into construction, finance, electronics, and overseas ventures, and became known for rapid diversification and a strongly export-oriented culture. The group's later difficulties, which belong to Chapter 84, should not be read back into these years; in the 1970s and 1980s Daewoo was widely seen as a dynamic example of Korean enterprise.

Hanjin began in transportation. Cho Choong-hun founded a trucking company in 1945, and the firm's fortunes were transformed by contracts transporting supplies for U.S. forces, including in the Vietnam War. In 1969 Hanjin took over Korean Air Lines, which the government had operated and then privatized, and in the 1970s it entered shipping with Hanjin Shipping. The group shows how government-related contracts and the privatization of state-run enterprises could provide the basis for a major group. Lotte had a different origin. Shin Kyuk-ho founded it in Japan in 1948 as a confectionery business, and after the 1965 normalization of relations it expanded into Korea, establishing Lotte Confectionery in 1967 and later building hotel, retail, and department-store businesses. Lotte's story shows the role of the Korean business community in Japan and of consumer markets rather than heavy industry. Ssangyong grew from cement, founded by Kim Seong-gon in 1962, and expanded into oil refining, paper, construction, and finance, and later acquired a motor company. Other groups followed still other routes: Kia, which grew from a bicycle-parts maker founded in the 1940s into motorcycles, small trucks, and passenger cars; Doosan, with commercial origins reaching back much further; and Hyosung, Kolon, and Dongkuk, in synthetic textiles and steel.

These examples support three conclusions. First, the paths into the large-business sector were varied: manufacturing, trade, construction, transportation, retail, cement, and government-related contracts each served as a base. Second, groups that began outside manufacturing, such as Hanjin and Lotte, drew their initial advantages from contracts, privatization, or consumer markets rather than from industrial policy alone. Third, the sector's composition was not static; some groups rose and others declined, and the government's selection of firms to support shaped these movements.

Daewoo's trajectory is particularly useful for studying acquisitions as an alternative to building every capability internally. Trading expertise generated relationships with foreign purchasers and the knowledge needed to arrange shipments, documentation, and payments. Taking over existing industrial assets offered an opportunity to enter production quickly, but also brought the obligations of older equipment, established workforces, and sometimes weak balance sheets. The move into the Okpo shipyard exemplified this trade-off: possession of a partly developed industrial site could accelerate entry, while incomplete construction and uncertain world orders demanded further investment. Compare this acquisition route with Hyundai's decision to build a new large yard at Ulsan. Neither was automatically superior; the risks depended on the condition of acquired assets, access to orders, labor skills, and financing. Daewoo's rapid accumulation of different enterprises should therefore be examined as a management problem as well as a story of corporate reach.

Section 76.10

Steel, POSCO, and the Industrial Foundations of Corporate Expansion

Steel was the material base of heavy industry. Shipbuilding, automobiles, machinery, construction, and appliances all required large and reliable supplies of steel, and importing it at world prices would have imposed a burden on every downstream industry. The Korean government had long wished to build an integrated steel mill, but early plans failed: a multinational consortium of Western firms organized to build one in the mid-1960s collapsed, and international lenders, including the World Bank, were skeptical that Korea could operate such a plant economically.

The Pohang Iron and Steel Company (POSCO) was established as a state-backed enterprise in April 1968, with Park Tae-joon, a former army general and the manager of a state-owned tungsten firm, as its first president. Its financing relied substantially on funds that Japan provided under the 1965 normalization agreements, and its construction depended on technical assistance from Japanese steel makers, particularly Nippon Steel and its predecessors. The Pohang integrated steelworks was built on the coast near Pohang, and the first blast furnace began operation in June 1973, with a ceremonial opening in July. Engineers and managers drilled their workforce in discipline, and the company developed a reputation for efficient operation and strict management. Later expansions, including the Gwangyang works begun in the 1980s, increased capacity substantially. Stories about Park Tae-joon's determination, such as claims that he and his colleagues pledged to drown themselves in the sea if the project failed, are widely repeated but are anecdotes that should be treated cautiously.

A fundamental institutional distinction must be made: POSCO was not a family-controlled chaebol. It was a state-backed enterprise whose shares were held by the government and public institutions, and it was managed by professional executives without a controlling founding family. Its importance in this chapter derives from the productive capacity it supplied to the wider economy, not from its resemblance to the groups. POSCO was privatized only later, in the early 2000s, a development beyond this chapter's scope.

The relationship between POSCO and the private conglomerates shows how public enterprise and private groups could complement one another. By producing steel at competitive prices, POSCO lowered costs for Hyundai's ships and automobiles, for Daewoo's machinery, and for construction firms, and its growth required huge investment that no private group might have been willing or able to finance. The arrangement also had a price: domestic users depended on a state-controlled supplier, and policy debates continued over steel pricing and over whether public and private enterprise were properly balanced. Historians generally treat POSCO as one of the clearest examples of the developmental state's capacity to organize a complex industrial project, while noting that it too depended on foreign finance, foreign technology, and the labor of thousands of workers.

The steelworks' place in the chaebol story lies above all in its position within the production network. A car manufacturer needed sheet steel of sufficiently consistent quality, a shipyard needed heavy plate delivered on schedule, and a machinery producer needed standardized inputs that could be machined and welded reliably. Importing those materials exposed firms to shipping costs, currency shortages, and dependence on foreign supply. POSCO's development reduced some of those constraints, but did not transform a public company into a private family-controlled group. Its investments depended on government choices, external finance, technology transfer, and specialized workers. Success also required coordination with downstream buyers: the grade and size of steel supplied mattered as much as headline tonnage. The comparison makes a broader point about industrial ecosystems. Some of the capital-intensive foundations of chaebol success were provided by state-owned enterprises rather than by the groups themselves.

Section 76.11

Shipbuilding: Hyundai, Daewoo, Foreign Contracts, and the Creation of a Maritime Industry

Shipbuilding was among the most ambitious industrial ventures in the chapter. Hyundai Construction announced the start of the Ulsan shipyard in 1970, and construction began in March 1972 at Mipo Bay. The project was unusual in that the group sought customers and finance before it had a shipyard. Chung Ju-yung obtained technical assistance from British shipbuilding firms, including a design arrangement and training support, and secured, in 1971, orders from the Greek shipowner George Livanos for two very large crude-oil carriers. The financing of the yard and of the vessels depended on loans from international banks, supported by British export-credit guarantees, together with Korean government support. Construction of the yard and the first ships proceeded in parallel, a practice that compressed time but increased risk.

The yard's first vessel was delivered in 1974, and the facility came to include very large docks capable of building the largest tankers then in service. Hyundai Shipbuilding and Heavy Industries was established as a separate company in 1973, and a related dockyard at Mipo followed. Thousands of workers had to be recruited and trained. Many came from rural areas or from other industries, and they learned welding, assembly, and heavy-lift operations quickly, often under foreign instructors and supervised by engineers who had themselves been trained only recently. Equipment suppliers, design specialists, classification societies that certified the ships, and foreign shipowners all played roles. The relationships among Korean shipyards, foreign owners, lenders, and the state were intricate, and none of the parties alone could have created the industry.

Daewoo's takeover of the Okpo yard in 1978 and Samsung's yard at Geoje expanded the industry and made Geoje a major shipbuilding center. The industry grew rapidly in the late 1970s and 1980s, and Korea became one of the world's leading shipbuilding nations. Success was not smooth. Shipbuilding is a highly cyclical, capital-intensive industry, with orders fluctuating with world trade and oil demand. The oil shock of 1973 and the subsequent glut of tankers depressed orders in the mid-1970s, and Korean yards moved toward bulk carriers, product tankers, and other vessels to keep their docks occupied. Competition from Japanese and European yards, and later from others, kept pressure on prices. The financial risk was borne by the group, the banks, and the state, and shipbuilding's heavy debts illustrate how an investment that increased national productive capacity could simultaneously increase financial vulnerability.

Historians have asked whether the shipbuilding drive was an example of visionary investment or of overreach rescued by favorable timing and state support. Both elements were present. The yard was built with unusual speed and used foreign knowledge intelligently, and it became profitable and competitive over time. But it also depended on extensive financial support, and its early years were precarious. The lesson is not that the risk was unwise, but that its success depended on conditions, such as finance, foreign partners, and a workforce willing to work long hours, that cannot be credited to one entrepreneur.

A peer-reviewed study based on British shipbuilding records offers an unusually concrete picture of how technical skills crossed national borders. In 1972 Hyundai sent trainees to Scott Lithgow's Scottish yards, where they observed production sequencing, yard organization, and the coordination of trades. The research identifies an initial group arriving in April of that year; the experience was not merely the purchase of blueprints. Welders, planners, supervisors, and engineers had to learn how activities on a large vessel fitted together, how quality inspection was organized, and how rework could delay an entire delivery. Foreign supervisors also worked in Korea, and the effectiveness of these exchanges depended on translation, workplace authority, and practical repetition. This evidence gives a better account of technological transfer than a famous founder anecdote about persuading bankers. It also reveals a limitation of the archival record: contracts and management correspondence document formal arrangements more readily than the everyday learning and hazards experienced by the wider workforce.

Section 76.12

Automobiles, Machinery, and the Development of Domestic Manufacturing Capacity

The Korean automobile industry began with assembly. In the early 1960s, firms assembled vehicles from imported kits, and the government, through a 1962 plan, sought to encourage domestic production while limiting imports. Hyundai Motor's establishment in December 1967 and its assembly of Ford Cortinas from 1968 illustrate this stage: the firm mastered assembly, procurement of parts, and dealer networks, but the design, engines, and much of the engineering remained foreign. Kia, which had produced motorcycles and small trucks, and the General Motors-linked Saehan Motor Company (later Daewoo Motor), followed variations of the same model, working with licensing agreements.

The government's long-term automobile promotion plan of the early 1970s pushed firms toward a domestically developed passenger car with high local content. Hyundai's answer was the Pony. The firm turned to the Italian designer Giorgetto Giugiaro for styling, to Mitsubishi for engines, transmissions, and technical support, and to a team of experienced engineers recruited from Britain, including former British Leyland executive George Turnbull, to guide product development and production. The Pony was introduced in the mid-1970s, with production starting in 1975, and the first exports, to Ecuador, occurred in 1976. Hyundai's later entry into the United States market with the Excel in 1986 marked a further step.

This history should not be reduced to the claim that Korean firms merely "copied" foreign cars. It was a process of technological learning: assembling imported kits, localizing parts, developing manufacturing processes, designing models with foreign help, and, over decades, building independent engineering and research capacities. Economists studying Korean technological development have described a progression from duplicative imitation to creative imitation and, eventually, to innovation. In the Pony's case, the combination of foreign design and engines with Korean manufacturing and management was characteristic of the intermediate stage: the car was not wholly Korean, yet it required considerable Korean capability to produce.

Difficulties abounded. Building competitive automobiles required not only an assembly plant but a network of domestic parts manufacturers, and many of these were small firms with limited capital and technical capability. The quality and cost of parts constrained the final product, and large firms often had to help their suppliers upgrade, a theme discussed in 76.16. The heavy machinery sector, developed at Changwon and elsewhere, presented similar problems: it required precision engineering, capital equipment, and a base of skilled workers and designers that took years to build, and it often depended on foreign licensing. The difference between assembling imported components and developing independent engineering capabilities was the central challenge for the Korean manufacturing sector in the 1970s and 1980s, and its gradual resolution was one of the chapter's key achievements.

A domestic vehicle industry also required a chain of suppliers able to meet repeatable tolerances rather than deliver merely approximate parts. Bodies, brakes, wiring, castings, tires, glass, and interior fittings had different technical requirements; inconsistent components increased warranty costs and could undermine export reputation. Large assemblers supplied specifications, inspected parts, and sometimes helped smaller manufacturers secure machinery and technical guidance. Suppliers gained regular orders, but they could become dependent on a small number of buyers with considerable power to demand price reductions. The Pony therefore demonstrates two different kinds of industrial learning: Hyundai coordinated design, assembly, sales, and quality systems, while a wider population of component firms developed manufacturing techniques that could serve several customers. The automobile industry's transformation was gradual, not a single achievement associated with the launch of one national model.

Long-term causes

A postwar economy with scarce capital and a business class formed in the colonial period, the 1950s, and the war; a state committed to export-led growth; and an international environment of open markets, Japanese and American capital and technology, and Cold War alliances.

Short-term causes

State control of banks, export targets, and the 1973 push toward heavy and chemical industries; cheap credit and payment guarantees; the oil shocks and the Middle Eastern construction boom.

Immediate trigger or context

Government requests and incentives to enter shipbuilding, steel, automobiles, machinery, and electronics, and firms' own calculations of opportunity and the risk of exclusion from future support.

Event or transformation

Rapid construction of yards, plants, and foundries; recruitment and training of large workforces; and a shift from assembly and licensing toward domestic engineering.

Immediate effects

Rising exports and industrial capacity, expanding employment, and increased borrowing and group size.

Long-term consequences

A competitive manufacturing base and globally active firms, together with concentrated economic power, high leverage, and dependence on state and bank support.

Cheap credit without export discipline might have produced waste; export pressure without credit might have been unattainable; credit and targets without skilled workers, foreign technology, and entrepreneurial effort would have yielded little. The mechanism was an interaction, and its results depended on timing, industry, and the particular firm.

Division IV — Electronics, Global Markets, Workers, and Industrial Organization · Section 76.13

Consumer Electronics, Semiconductors, and the Beginnings of Technological Competition

Electronics offered a path from simple assembly toward technological sophistication. In the 1960s, GoldStar and, from 1969, Samsung Electronics produced radios, black-and-white televisions, and appliances, first for the domestic market and increasingly for export. Much early export production was assembly of components under foreign licenses or on contract for foreign retailers and brands. The government supported the sector through an electronics promotion plan, designated industrial complexes such as Gumi, and tariff and credit policies. South Korean manufacturers began producing color televisions for export in 1974; domestic color-television sales were generally authorized only in August 1980, and regular domestic color broadcasting began on December 1, 1980. The industry also expanded into videocassette recorders, microwave ovens, and computers during the 1980s. Korean firms also faced trade friction, including U.S. antidumping action on color televisions in the early 1980s.

Technical learning followed a sequence. Firms licensed designs and process technology, sent engineers for training abroad, imported machinery, and gradually developed their own product designs and manufacturing improvements. Quality control and manufacturing efficiency became competitive strengths. Research and development, which was minimal in the 1960s, expanded in the 1970s and 1980s, supported by government institutes such as the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (founded in 1966), the Korea Advanced Institute of Science (1971), and the Daedeok science complex, and increasingly by in-house laboratories. Private-sector research spending grew markedly in the 1980s as firms recognized that licensing alone would not allow them to compete at the technological frontier.

Semiconductors were the most demanding test. Samsung's memory-chip investments, discussed in 76.5, were matched by Hyundai Electronics, established in 1983, and by GoldStar's semiconductor affiliate. The government supported the effort through a national joint development project in the mid-1980s on advanced memory chips, bringing together public research institutes and the major firms. Samsung's early chips were based on licensed designs, and the firm quickly moved toward its own designs, coming to produce increasingly advanced memory chips by the late 1980s. The semiconductor business required enormous capital, high-skilled engineers, precise manufacturing, and tolerance for losses during development, and only large groups with access to credit and cross-subsidy from other affiliates could afford it.

This chapter concentrates on the corporate and institutional foundations of technological competition: group finance, state support, foreign licensing, and technical training. It does not narrate the subsequent global semiconductor industry, the spread of broadband networks, mobile telecommunications, smartphones, or the broader digital transformation, which Chapter 87 addresses. The essential point here is that the move from assembly toward design and process innovation was a product of deliberate investment, foreign partnership, and sustained learning, and that the chaebol structure, by pooling finance and risk, shaped which firms could attempt it.

The chronology of color television illustrates the difference between domestic manufacturing capability and domestic consumer access. The National Archives records Korean National's first production in January 1974, initially for export, and the entry of GoldStar and Samsung into the field during the 1970s. Domestic retail sale was broadly permitted only in August 1980, and national color broadcasting began on December 1 of that year. Before then, firms could manufacture sets for foreign markets while most Korean households continued to watch black-and-white broadcasts. This distinction matters because export strategy could develop a technical industry before its products became routine domestic consumer goods. In semiconductors the lag between first involvement and competitive maturity was even more important: producing a component, reproducing a licensed design, improving yields, and sustaining large-scale world-market production were separate problems requiring different expertise and investment.

Historical Vocabulary
OEM, Licensing, and Technological Learning

Definition. Original equipment manufacturing (OEM) means producing goods to a foreign customer's specifications, to be sold under the customer's brand. Licensing is the purchase of the right to use another firm's technology or designs. Technological learning is the process by which firms acquire, absorb, improve, and eventually generate technology. Scholars of Korea have described a movement from OEM production toward design manufacture and, in some sectors, toward selling under firms' own brands.

Historical context. Korean exporters in the 1960s and 1970s often produced for American retailers and foreign brand owners, which gave them access to markets, standards, and technical advice. Over time, many firms invested in design and branding so as to capture more of the value of their products.

Why it matters. These concepts show that industrialization involved more than assembly, and that competence was built in stages.

Potential misunderstanding. Licensing and OEM production are sometimes dismissed as mere copying. In practice, they were vehicles of learning, though the degree of learning varied by firm and industry, and not every OEM supplier became an innovator.

Section 76.14

Trading Companies, International Markets, and the Global Organization of Korean Enterprise

Manufacturers needed channels to reach foreign buyers, and the government encouraged the creation of general trading companies in 1975, modelled partly on Japanese trading houses. Samsung, Hyundai, and Daewoo established such companies, which gathered export orders, arranged shipping and finance, and handled overseas marketing, licensing, and negotiations with foreign suppliers. These trading arms, together with corporate export departments and overseas offices in North America, Europe, the Middle East, and elsewhere, gave Korean groups a commercial reach that individual small manufacturers could not have achieved. Trading companies also helped smaller firms export, by purchasing their products or by acting as intermediaries, and, with their group's backing, they could assume the risks of long-distance trade.

Groups entered markets by different routes. Daewoo's apparel exports to the United States grew within the framework of textile quotas and relationships with American buyers; Hyundai's construction firm entered the Middle East through bidding on large infrastructure projects; Samsung and GoldStar sold electronics to foreign retailers. A crucial distinction concerned the form of participation. Contract manufacturing meant producing to a buyer's specifications and being paid per unit; selling under foreign brands meant supplying the finished product that the foreign firm sold under its own name; establishing independent Korean brands required marketing, distribution, and service networks of one's own, and carried higher risk and higher potential returns. Korean firms in the 1970s and 1980s were mostly in the first two categories, and only gradually, and unevenly, moved toward the third.

International competition encouraged learning. Foreign buyers imposed quality standards, delivery schedules, and design requirements, which pushed Korean firms to improve. At the same time, exposure to world markets brought hazards: exchange-rate movements, protectionist measures such as quotas and antidumping duties, recessions in buyer countries, and fluctuating demand. The 1973 and 1979 oil shocks affected costs and demand, and trade friction with the United States increased in the 1980s as Korea's surpluses grew. Firms that borrowed in foreign currencies were particularly sensitive to exchange rates, a vulnerability that foreshadowed later difficulties without determining them.

The global organization of Korean enterprise was also a matter of people. Overseas posts employed managers who often spent years abroad, and the construction boom in the Middle East sent large numbers of Korean workers, well over a hundred thousand at its peak, to the Gulf, whose wages and remittances contributed to national foreign-exchange earnings. These workers, like those at home, worked long hours under tight supervision, and their labor links the history of corporate expansion to the broader movement of Korean migrants across the world.

The work of overseas sales offices was less visible than factories but crucial to commercial learning. Buyers demanded delivery dates, packaging specifications, service commitments, financing terms, and consistent quality, and companies had to manage differences in currencies and legal systems. Firms selling under a foreign customer's brand might receive steady orders but gain little recognition for their own name; firms developing independent brands had to pay for distribution, advertising, inventories, and after-sales support. General trading affiliates could aggregate shipments from different Korean producers and provide documentation that small suppliers could not easily handle alone. These arrangements spread some commercial risk while placing considerable influence in the hands of the group controlling the overseas channel. Expansion abroad was thus not simply the opening of a distant office: it required a commercial infrastructure that linked engineering decisions at home to changing preferences and standards in export destinations.

Section 76.15

Labor, Management, Gender, and the Human Foundations of Corporate Growth

Corporate growth rested on workforces that firms recruited, trained, supervised, and organized. In the 1960s and 1970s, the labor-intensive export industries, such as textiles, garments, wigs, and electronics assembly, depended heavily on young women, many of them from rural families, who migrated to Seoul, Incheon, Gumi, Masan, and other industrial centers. They lived in company dormitories or crowded rented rooms, worked long shifts, and received wages that were low relative to those of male workers and to the profits of the firms. Their remittances supported families in the countryside and, in many cases, the education of brothers. Employers and officials often described these women through a rhetoric of family duty and national service, while the women themselves were sometimes derided in the pejorative term "factory girl."

The heavy industries, including shipbuilding, steel, construction, and heavy machinery, employed predominantly male workforces, including skilled welders, machinists, and foremen as well as many unskilled laborers. Conditions varied: shipyard and steel workers could earn higher wages and enjoyed greater job security than textile workers, and large firms developed company housing, welfare benefits, and a paternalist corporate culture to bind a workforce considered valuable. Yet hours were long, among the longest in the world, with manufacturing workers commonly working well over fifty hours a week through the 1980s, and industrial accidents were frequent. Management practices combined military-style discipline, group exercises, and strict hierarchy with promotion based on seniority and loyalty.

The legal framework restricted labor. Korea's Labor Standards Act of 1953 set limits on hours and working conditions, but enforcement was weak, and the government treated labor as a resource for export growth. Unions existed but were constrained: the state recognized official federations while limiting independent organizing and strikes, and laws in the early 1970s further restricted collective action, particularly after the Yushin system (Chapter 75). Employers often resisted unions, and some groups, notably Samsung, maintained non-union management for decades. Even so, workers organized. The self-immolation of the young garment worker Jeon Tae-il in November 1970, in protest against violations of the labor law, became a landmark. Women workers at textile mills such as Dongil Textile led union struggles in the 1970s, enduring violent employer and police repression, and historians of labor, including Hwasook Nam in her studies of workers in shipbuilding and other industries, have shown that male workers in the heavy industries also organized, sometimes within the constraints of official unions and sometimes outside them.

Labor conflict became especially consequential in the 1980s. After the political opening of 1987 (examined in Chapter 82), a wave of strikes spread through the manufacturing and shipbuilding sectors, with 3,749 recorded labor disputes over the full year of 1987, compared with 276 in 1986; these national totals cover workers beyond the chaebol affiliates and are not restricted to the second half of 1987. Workers at large firms in Ulsan, Changwon, Masan, and elsewhere formed independent unions and demanded higher wages, shorter hours, and respect. The result was significant wage increases and a renegotiation of the terms of industrial authority within the largest firms, though employers resisted, and later conflicts continued. Workers were not merely inputs into production. Their skills, productivity, organization, and resistance shaped corporate development, and the discipline and the demands of the heavy industries, the low wages of the export sectors, and the eventual strength of organized labor in the 1980s were all part of the same story.

Labor history also warns against assuming that one prominent activist or one factory represents all Korean industrial workers. Jeon Tae-il's Pyeonghwa Market organizing took place among small garment shops, not inside a chaebol affiliate. At the Korea Shipbuilding and Engineering Corporation, a different workforce developed sustained union organization before Hyundai's Ulsan yard rose to prominence; historian Hwasook Nam's archival study documents that earlier shipyard union's struggles for dignity, representation, and a family-supporting wage. These experiences illuminate distinct institutions and should not be merged into a fictional single shipyard testimony. The later wave of recorded labor disputes also had a national scale broader than the groups profiled here. Official historical data count 276 disputes in 1986 and 3,749 in 1987, with participation varying by industry, firm size, and locality. Such comparisons show both the longstanding grievances of industrial labor and the effect of changing political opportunities for organization.

Everyday Life and Social History

Corporate Workforces, Factory Conditions, and the Experience of Employees and Their Families

The daily life of corporate workforces varied by sector, sex, age, and employer. A young woman in a Gumi electronics plant or a Seoul textile mill typically worked shifts of ten or more hours, lived in a dormitory or shared room, and sent money home; marriage often ended her factory career. A male worker at a large shipyard or steel mill in Ulsan or Pohang might live in company housing, receive company-provided meals and medical care, attend firm-sponsored training, and expect, in return, to give long hours and loyalty. Managers and white-collar employees, recruited from universities, entered a hierarchy of seniority in which a group employee's identity, with the group's name on his business card, carried social status. Company outings, uniforms, morning exercises, and company songs created a corporate culture that fostered cohesion and discipline.

Families were part of this world. Wives of male employees managed households and, in some cases, informal work, while children's educational prospects depended on parental income and employer benefits; company-linked schools and foundations in cities such as Ulsan reinforced the social presence of the group. Small subcontractors' employees had fewer benefits and less security than those at the groups themselves, and the experience of a worker at a small supplier could differ sharply from that of an employee at the parent firm. Because most surviving records were produced by firms and officials, historians reconstruct these lives from oral history, labor testimony, memoirs, literature, and surveys, and must remember that the broader social history of urbanization, housing, and education belongs to Chapter 81.

Section 76.16

Industrial Clusters, Subcontractors, Engineers, and Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises

The Miracle on the Han River cannot be understood solely through the largest corporations. Behind each automobile, ship, or television stood hundreds or thousands of smaller firms: machine shops, foundries, parts makers, transport companies, packaging firms, and repair services. Small and medium-sized enterprises employed the majority of the manufacturing workforce, and many produced components or performed processes for the large groups. Engineers and technicians, trained in technical high schools, vocational institutes, and universities, moved among firms, carried knowledge, and sometimes founded their own enterprises. The government supported industrial training and, from the mid-1970s, passed laws and measures intended to promote subcontracting relationships between large and small firms.

Industrial geography gave these networks a spatial form. The Seoul–Incheon region, with the Guro export industrial complex and later Banwol and Ansan, concentrated light manufacturing and electronics; Gumi in North Gyeongsang developed as an electronics center beginning in the late 1960s; the southeast coast around Ulsan, Pohang, Busan, and Masan formed a heavy-industry and port corridor; Changwon became a planned machinery city; Geoje Island became a shipbuilding center; and Yeosu and others hosted petrochemicals. The Gyeongbu Expressway, opened in 1970, and rail and port investment tied these zones together. Historians debate the regional consequences, including the extent to which the southwest was left behind, a contested question that touches politics discussed in later chapters.

Coordinated networks offered advantages. Large firms could transfer technology, orders, and technical standards to suppliers, sending engineers to improve processes and guaranteeing orders that allowed small firms to invest. Proximity reduced transport costs and encouraged specialization. But the relationship was unequal. Large firms held strong purchasing power, could demand low prices and rapid delivery, delayed payment, and shifted risk and inventory costs onto suppliers during downturns. Some subcontractors depended on a single customer and had little ability to bargain or to develop their own brands or technologies. This asymmetry was a persistent source of grievance and one of the reasons for the later fair-trade measures aimed at subcontracting.

The point is not that small firms were mere appendages. Many developed real technical capacities, supplied several customers, and became successful in their own right, and the dense network of machine shops and parts makers was an economic asset that few other developing economies possessed. Still, the structure meant that the benefits of growth were distributed unevenly between the groups and the firms that served them, and between workers at the center and those at the periphery of the production chain.

Supplier relations were particularly consequential when industry shifted from textiles and assembly toward machinery and electronics. A defective component could require expensive rework at the final factory, so lead firms developed inspection procedures and technical specifications while expecting smaller contractors to invest in new equipment. Some suppliers gained expertise that later allowed them to sell to multiple customers or to export directly. Others remained dependent on annual contracts, narrow margins, and delayed payments. Industrial estates helped by bringing specialized firms near assemblers, workers, ports, and training institutions, but proximity did not eliminate differences in bargaining power. A geography of industrial clusters is therefore also a geography of dependence: the same network that enabled rapid knowledge diffusion could allow an anchor company to shift cost and uncertainty onto less well-capitalized subcontractors.

Division V — Family Ownership, Economic Power, and the Contradictions of the Miracle · Section 76.17

Family Ownership, Corporate Governance, Succession, and Internal Control

The governance of the chaebol was the mechanism that turned a founding family's moderate equity into command over a large and diversified group. Several tools were involved. Direct shareholding by the founder and relatives gave a core of voting power. Affiliate ownership allowed one company in the group to hold shares in another, so that the family's direct stake in a key firm could control a chain of subsidiaries beyond what its own capital would have purchased. Cross-shareholding, in which affiliates held one another's shares, and pyramidal arrangements, in which a family controlled a top company that controlled lower tiers, extended the reach of control. The chairman's office, a group-level staff, coordinated strategy and finance across affiliates, though legally no such group-level entity existed.

The distinction between legal ownership of a company and effective control of a group is crucial. Corporate law recognized each affiliate as a separate company with its own shareholders, directors, and obligations; in practice, a chairman could direct investments, appointments, and financing across companies where he held modest formal stakes. The ownership structures of the earliest decades were simpler than those of the 1980s and 1990s, when diversification and acquisitions multiplied affiliates and cross-holdings, and it would be anachronistic to project the elaborate later structures onto the small firms of the 1950s. Pure holding companies were prohibited under Korean competition law from 1987 until 1999, which pushed groups toward other forms of control and, some scholars argue, encouraged more complex cross-holding arrangements.

Group-wide coordination had real advantages. A chairman could move capital from profitable affiliates to new ventures, such as Samsung's semiconductors or Hyundai's shipbuilding, ventures that would have been difficult for an independent firm to finance during years of losses. Coordinated decisions could be made quickly, and managers could be rotated among companies. These features helped explain the speed of Korean industrial expansion. But the same features reduced transparency, since external investors could not easily see how resources moved within the group, and weakened the position of minority shareholders, whose interests might be subordinated to those of the controlling family or to the group's strategy. Questions of whether affiliates were being used to subsidize weaker members, or to transfer wealth to family members, became recurring objects of criticism.

The histories of the major groups illustrate different arrangements. Samsung centered power in a central secretariat and passed leadership from Lee Byung-chul to Lee Kun-hee in 1987; Hyundai, built by Chung Ju-yung and a network of brothers and sons, was run with a highly personal style, and its later succession was contested among family members; LG practiced a collective family management with the Huh family and a pattern of eldest-son succession; SK's leadership passed from Chey Jong-gun to his brother and then to the next generation. The relationship between owners and professional managers also varied. Groups recruited capable managers and engineers, and some professional executives wielded considerable authority, but strategic decisions about acquisitions, major investments, and succession remained with the family. This arrangement combined entrepreneurial energy with limited accountability to outside shareholders, a feature that would come under sustained pressure in later decades (Chapter 84).

Governance arrangements also shaped whose losses counted and whose interests received attention when a new venture demanded funds. A group's coordinating office could instruct one affiliate to invest in, lend to, or guarantee another even where the transaction was not clearly best for the first affiliate's outside shareholders. A professionally trained manager might recognize those risks yet lack the authority to contradict the controlling family's strategic decision. The distinction did not mean that all family-directed investment was irrational; pooled resources sometimes financed promising projects before outside capital markets would support them. It did mean that consolidated group success and individual-company accountability could diverge. The 1987 restrictions on cross-shareholding were an acknowledgment that coordination through ownership links raised questions beyond ordinary competition between independent companies. The chapter should judge each period's arrangements against the law then in effect rather than apply later governance standards mechanically.

Section 76.18

Corporate Debt, Cross-Guarantees, Financial Risk, and the Problem of Overexpansion

Borrowing was not an incidental feature of the chaebol model; it was central to it. Because preferential credit was available and equity was scarce, the groups financed expansion mostly through bank loans and foreign borrowing, producing high ratios of debt to equity. Firms also owed obligations in foreign currencies, a source of exposure when exchange rates moved. Rapid expansion into capital-intensive industries, such as shipbuilding, steel-using manufacturing, and semiconductors, required large and continuing investment, which in turn required continued access to credit and continued growth to service the debt. A firm that stopped growing risked difficulty repaying, and thus the system rewarded expansion and penalized caution.

Intra-group financial links amplified this dynamic. Affiliates often guaranteed one another's loans, so that a bank lending to one company in effect relied on the credit of the others. Weak affiliates could therefore receive financial support through stronger members, which allowed unprofitable ventures to continue and allowed groups to bet on future growth. These cross-guarantees also meant that losses at one affiliate could be transmitted through the group, because the failure of one company threatened the creditworthiness of others. The arrangement increased the group's capacity to invest and also increased its vulnerability. The government later moved to limit cross-guarantees in the 1990s, a reform belonging to the discussion in Chapter 84.

Expectations of rescue are a controversial part of this history. Banks and groups may have assumed that the government would not allow strategically important enterprises to fail, and episodes such as the 1972 curb-market decree, the 1970s support of heavy-industry projects, and the later industrial rationalization measures of the 1980s support the belief that the state sometimes intervened. But historians should distinguish documented interventions from assumptions of automatic guarantees. In 1985 the government allowed the Kukje Group, then among the large conglomerates, to be dismantled when banks refused to continue financing it, an episode that many observers connected to political relationships and which showed that rescue was selective and discretionary rather than assured. Support depended on the firm's standing, performance, and relationship with the authorities, and it is the uncertainty of that standing that gave the state leverage over private groups.

The fundamental analytic point is that investment which increased national productive capacity could simultaneously increase financial vulnerability. Without heavy borrowing, Korea could not have built shipyards and steel mills at the pace it did; with it, firms were exposed to interest-rate changes, to downturns in export markets, and to credit conditions. The International Monetary Fund's historical research identifies concentrated ownership, complex financial linkages, and heavy leverage as important vulnerabilities of the pre-1997 corporate system. This chapter treats them as antecedent conditions, risks that were present and recognized but whose consequences depended on later circumstances, and it does not narrate the crisis, which is the subject of Chapter 84. In the 1980s, favorable conditions, including low oil prices, low interest rates, and a weak currency in the mid-1980s, gave firms respite and produced the first sustained surpluses in the balance of trade, and risks that would later matter were less visible.

Historical leverage figures help make the mechanism concrete. A World Bank study reproduces a manufacturing debt-to-equity ratio of 377.1 percent in 1979 and 487.9 percent in 1980, before declining to 451.5 percent in 1981 and 385.8 percent in 1982. These are accounts for manufacturing enterprises as a category, not the balance sheets of a particular group. A ratio near 488 percent means recorded liabilities were about 4.88 times book equity, not that liabilities equaled 488 percent of assets. A large project with little equity cushion could absorb a temporary loss if banks refinanced it, but it could also face rapid deterioration when interest rates increased or orders were cancelled. Foreign-currency borrowing added a second problem: even profitable domestic operations might struggle to obtain the currency needed for debt service. These mechanisms make the chapter's later discussion of cross-guarantees intelligible without treating a future crisis as foreordained.

Section 76.19

Market Concentration, Political Influence, Competition Policy, and Reform in the 1980s

As the largest groups grew, so did public and governmental concern about their economic influence. The groups accounted for large shares of output, exports, and assets, and they dominated many markets in which a few firms competed or colluded. Preferential financing gave them advantages over smaller rivals, and the close relationships between business leaders and political officials raised the risk of corruption and favoritism. Barriers to entry limited the ability of new firms to challenge established groups, and many small enterprises were dependent on a single large customer. Some critics inside the government itself argued that excessive concentration threatened competition and that the state's own policies had contributed to it.

The early 1980s brought a reorientation. Under President Chun Doo-hwan, whose government came to power after the political crisis of 1979–1980 (Chapter 79), economic policy shifted from state-led credit allocation toward stabilization and some liberalization: banks were nominally privatized, inflation was brought down, and the government sought to reduce overlapping investments in heavy industry. A 1980 industry adjustment sought to rationalize duplicative investments in sectors such as power-generation equipment and passenger cars, in part because the heavy-industry drive of the 1970s had produced excess capacity and weak finances in some firms. Those measures show the state still intervening directly in corporate decisions, even as it spoke of competition.

The Monopoly Regulation and Fair Trade Act, enacted in December 1980 and effective in 1981, was South Korea's first general competition statute, and it created the Fair Trade Commission. It addressed abuse of dominant market position, restrictions on mergers, cartels, and unfair trade practices. Amendments adopted in 1986 and taking effect in 1987 introduced stronger measures aimed at the concentration of economic power: the government began to designate the largest business groups for special oversight and to limit cross-shareholding and the voting power of financial affiliates, and, as noted above, pure holding companies were banned. The aim was to prevent groups from expanding control through intricate cross-holdings and to encourage specialization in core businesses.

Regulation did not eliminate the groups' importance or the state's reliance on them. Enforcement was uneven, exemptions and reinterpretations eroded some provisions, and the groups found new ways to extend their influence, while the government continued to depend on large firms for exports, employment, and national prestige. The 1980s thus saw a partial rebalancing rather than a transformation: competition policy gained a legal foundation and institutional presence, and public debate about concentration became permanent, yet the structure of the economy remained one in which a handful of groups held great power. This section describes efforts to regulate and rebalance chaebol-led capitalism, and the comprehensive restructuring that followed the 1997 crisis belongs to Chapter 84.

Competition policy developed in layers rather than through a single law that immediately dismantled the business groups. The statute enacted in December 1980 provided rules against certain anticompetitive conduct; the amendments adopted in 1986 and operative in April 1987 focused more explicitly on concentration inside designated large groups. OECD's historical regulatory chronology lists restrictions on cross-shareholding, voting rights of financial and insurance affiliates, and creation of holding companies from April 1987. It records the holding-company restriction's later removal in 1999, outside this chapter's principal narrative. These measures did not retroactively invalidate every preexisting ownership link, and the practical effect depended on designations, exemptions, enforcement capacity, and corporate responses. Regulation was therefore simultaneously a legal development, an administrative task, and a negotiation with organizations that remained central to investment and exports. Distinguishing the formal rule from its implementation is essential to evaluating reform.

Historical Debate

Did the Chaebol Accelerate Development at an Acceptable Economic and Social Cost, and What Alternatives Existed?

The question: Scholars agree that South Korea industrialized with extraordinary speed and that large groups were central to the process. They disagree about whether concentrating resources in family-controlled conglomerates was necessary to that speed, whether the costs in inequality, concentrated power, and financial risk were justified, and whether alternative paths, such as the more small-firm-centered path of Taiwan, were realistic for Korea.

Interpretation A — Guided concentration worked: Scholars who emphasize the developmental state, such as Alice Amsden in her study of Korean industrialization, argue that selective support for large firms, tied to performance standards, allowed Korea to enter capital-intensive industries and to learn technology at a pace unreachable through small, dispersed firms. On this view, scale was a means of overcoming barriers of capital and technology, and the discipline of exports kept the system accountable.
Interpretation B — Growth was market-driven and the costs were avoidable: Other economists, including the authors of the World Bank's 1993 study of East Asia, stressed that export orientation, high savings and investment, and human capital drove growth, and that heavy industrial targeting and preferential credit were less decisive and sometimes wasteful. On this view, the chaebol's dominance generated rent-seeking, debt, and concentration that a more competitive system might have avoided.
Interpretation C — Entrepreneurship and political economy together: Historians and political scientists have also argued that Korean outcomes depended on the relationship between a strong state and entrepreneurial firms that were neither mere instruments nor independent actors, and that labor repression and the unequal distribution of gains are part of the explanation, not an unfortunate side effect. Studies of labor, such as Hwasook Nam's, emphasize that workers' contributions and struggles shaped the outcome.

Evidence: Debaters draw on aggregate economic statistics, case studies of individual firms and industries, comparative studies of Taiwan, Japan, and other economies, and labor and social history. Counterfactuals, such as how Korea might have grown with different institutions, cannot be tested directly.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation (how much each factor mattered), interpretation (whether costs were necessary), and comparison (whether other models were feasible), rather than the basic facts of rapid growth.

Why it matters: The answers shape how later reforms, including the post-1997 restructuring, and contemporary debates over conglomerate power are understood.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, assess the strengths and weaknesses of each interpretation, and identify evidence that would help decide between them, such as comparative firm-level performance, data on credit allocation, or labor records.

Multiple Perspectives

How Different Participants Understood Chaebol-Led Growth

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named person. These groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

From the perspective of a business founder: Growth demanded bold investment in projects that cautious lenders and foreign experts doubted; speed and concentrated decision-making were seen as necessary to catch up, and state support was a means of making risks bearable.
From the perspective of a professional manager: Responsibility for executing ambitious plans fell on salaried managers and engineers, who gained prestige and stability while working under a chairman whose decisions could not be challenged and whose family retained ultimate authority.
From the perspective of a government planner: Large firms were instruments for meeting export and industrial goals, to be rewarded for performance and disciplined for failure, though planners also worried about concentration and about the groups' growing influence.
From the perspective of a banker: State-directed banks lent as the government's priorities required, bearing credit risk on large borrowers while depending on official tolerance, and some bankers became uneasy about how much exposure to a single group had accumulated.
From the perspective of an engineer: Foreign licenses and partners supplied the starting knowledge, but progress depended on technicians who studied, adapted, and solved problems on the job, often under severe deadlines and with limited recognition.
From the perspective of a subcontractor: Orders from large groups made production possible and offered technical help, but dependence on one customer, pressure on prices, and delayed payment limited independence and profit.
From the perspective of an industrial worker: Factory jobs offered wages and a route out of rural poverty, particularly for young women, but the conditions of long hours, danger, strict discipline, and restrictions on organizing led many workers to seek better terms and, eventually, to form unions.
Section 76.20

The Miracle on the Han River: Achievements, Distortions, and the Legacy of Chaebol-Led Capitalism

The achievements were substantial. From a very low base in the early 1960s, when annual per capita income was below US$100 by most estimates, South Korea reached a per capita income of about US$1,000 in 1977 and sustained real growth averaging roughly eight percent a year across the following decades, though the figures vary with the series used and should be treated as approximate. Exports rose from tens of millions of dollars to tens of billions. Manufacturing came to dominate output and employment, and industries once unimaginable, shipbuilding, steel, automobiles, electronics, and petrochemicals, became internationally competitive. Large firms built infrastructure, trained workers, acquired technology, and opened overseas markets. The chaebol's capacity to mobilize capital, manage large projects, and move quickly was a central factor, along with the efforts of workers and engineers who made the plants run.

A fair account assigns credit broadly. State policy created incentives, set targets, and directed credit; private entrepreneurship supplied initiative and risk-taking; international markets and partners provided buyers, technology, and finance; public enterprises such as POSCO supplied basic materials; small businesses and subcontractors supplied parts and services; technical specialists made foreign knowledge usable; and industrial workers, whose wages stayed low and hours long for years, provided the labor on which competitiveness rested. Household savers, whose deposits fed the banking system, were another part of the story. World Bank and IMF historical research supports this broader interpretation rather than any single-actor explanation.

The costs were equally real. Market concentration restricted competition and gave a small number of families great economic and political influence. Preferential access to credit created unequal opportunities between large and small firms. Workers, particularly in labor-intensive industries, endured long hours and unsafe conditions, and labor conflicts, repressed for years, erupted in the 1980s. High debt and cross-guarantees left groups exposed to adverse conditions. Close relationships between business and political elites created risks of corruption and favoritism. These were not accidental by-products. They arose from the same structures, namely concentrated family control, dependence on state-directed credit, and heavy borrowing, that enabled fast growth. This is why the chaebol model could produce both rapid industrial development and serious institutional distortions.

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, South Korea entered a new phase. Wages were rising after the strikes of 1987, the won had appreciated, labor-intensive exports faced competition from lower-wage countries, and firms were moving into more technology-intensive products. The groups were larger and more diversified than ever, with global operations and ambitions, while governance arrangements, financial structures, and regulatory frameworks remained those developed in an earlier stage. It is possible, without anticipating what followed, to note that financing and governance arrangements that had served well during fast growth and favorable credit conditions might become liabilities under adverse conditions. Whether and how they did so is the subject of Chapter 84, and students should resist reading the 1990s as the predetermined consequence of the 1970s.

The final lesson of this chapter is that the Miracle on the Han River resulted from the interaction of institutions, capital, technology, labor, markets, and government, not from the actions of a few industrial leaders alone. The founders deserve study, and so do the employees, public institutions, foreign partners, subcontractors, and households whose contributions the founder narratives often leave out. The chaebol era was at once a history of corporate entrepreneurship, state capacity, technological learning, international competition, labor, and concentrated economic power, and a student who holds all of these together will be prepared for the later chapters on the transformation of South Korean capitalism (Chapter 84), the digital economy (Chapter 87), and the global rise of Korean culture and industry (Chapters 89 and 90).

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how South Korea's family-controlled conglomerates became the principal corporate institutions of rapid industrialization. A chaebol, as defined here, was a diversified group of legally separate affiliates directed by a founding family through ownership, a chairman's office, and group-level coordination. It was different from a single manufacturer, from the Japanese zaibatsu on which its name was based, and from the looser keiretsu networks. Understanding the structure was the key to understanding both its speed and its opacity.

The groups did not originate in 1961. Samsung began as a trading firm in 1938, Hyundai as an auto repair and construction business in the 1940s, Lucky Chemical in 1947, Sunkyong Textiles in 1953, and Daewoo as an export trader in 1967. Their founders drew on colonial-era commercial experience, vested property disposed after liberation, wartime and reconstruction contracts, and the aid-supplied industries of the 1950s. The state under Park Chung-hee did not create these entrepreneurs, but it selected, financed, and disciplined them on new terms.

Finance was the central mechanism. State control of banks, policy loans, export credit, payment guarantees on foreign borrowing, and tax incentives allowed favored firms to expand far beyond their own capital. The same mechanisms produced high leverage and unequal access, since large firms received cheaper credit than small ones. Subsidized finance was conditional on performance, especially exports, which provided a relatively objective test, but political relationships also shaped who was supported and for how long.

The four principal groups followed different paths. Samsung moved from trade and processing into electronics and, in the 1980s, memory chips; Hyundai from construction into automobiles, shipbuilding, and heavy industry; LG from chemicals and consumer products into electronics; and SK from textiles and synthetic fibers into petrochemicals and energy. Daewoo, Hanjin, Lotte, Ssangyong, and others show alternative routes through trading, transportation, retail, and cement. The variety demonstrates that policy did not produce identical outcomes and that founders' strategies and timing mattered.

POSCO, founded in 1968 and producing steel from 1973, was a state-backed enterprise rather than a family-controlled chaebol, and it supplied the basic material on which shipbuilding, automobiles, and machinery depended. Shipbuilding, at Ulsan and Geoje, and the automobile industry, from Ford-licensed assembly to the Pony, illustrate technological learning: the progression from assembly and licensing through domestic production toward engineering and design, with foreign partners, imported technology, and Korean workers and engineers all indispensable.

Electronics and semiconductors provided a further test of capability. Consumer electronics grew from licensed assembly to export manufacturing, and in the 1980s, Samsung, Hyundai, and GoldStar invested in memory chips with state support and foreign licenses. Trading companies and overseas offices connected manufacturers to world markets, and the move from contract manufacturing toward independent brands proceeded unevenly. International competition encouraged improvement while exposing firms to protectionism, exchange-rate shifts, and fluctuations in demand.

Workers and small firms were central to the story. Young women in textile and electronics plants and men in shipyards, steel mills, and construction supplied labor under long hours, strict discipline, and limited rights, and they organized and resisted, with significant consequences in the 1970s and especially after 1987. Thousands of subcontractors and small enterprises, clustered in industrial regions from Seoul–Incheon to Ulsan, Gumi, Changwon, and Geoje, supplied parts and services, benefiting from technology transfer while bearing the weaker bargaining position in their relationships with the groups.

Family ownership, cross-shareholding, and group-wide coordination allowed founding families to control vast enterprises with modest equity, accelerating investment but reducing transparency and weakening minority shareholders. Heavy borrowing, cross-guarantees among affiliates, and exposure to foreign-currency obligations made the groups simultaneously engines of expansion and sources of financial vulnerability. Rescue by the state was selective rather than automatic, as the dismantling of Kukje in 1985 shows.

The 1980s brought partial responses: the Monopoly Regulation and Fair Trade Act of 1980 and the amendments effective in 1987 created competition policy and restrictions on cross-shareholding and concentration, but did not remove the groups' centrality. The era's achievements, including a transformation in exports, manufacturing capacity, technology, and employment, and its costs, including concentration, unequal opportunity, labor conflict, and debt, arose from the same institutions.

The enduring lesson is that the Miracle on the Han River resulted from the interaction of institutions, capital, technology, labor, markets, and government. This chapter has taken care not to read the later crisis back into earlier decades, while identifying the financing and governance arrangements that would later come under strain. Chapter 84 takes up that history, and Chapter 87 the later technological ascendancy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how an economy transformed itself within a generation through institutions that were neither purely public nor purely private. The chaebol were the point at which state credit, family enterprise, foreign technology, and mass labor met, and the history of that meeting helps explain why South Korea changed so quickly and why its society continued to argue about fairness, power, and responsibility.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs elsewhere in this textbook: developments that solved one generation's problems became the next generation's constraints. Concentrated control and heavy borrowing enabled speed; the same arrangements limited transparency, competition, and resilience. Workers whose labor made growth possible organized and, after 1987, changed the terms on which firms operated, linking this chapter to the democratization examined in Chapter 82 and the social history of Chapter 81.

The chapter shaped later developments in several ways. The industrial base and technical capacities built in these decades underlay the digital and semiconductor economy of Chapter 87, and the financing and governance arrangements identified here form the background to the restructuring of Chapter 84. It also contributed to the cultural and commercial self-confidence from which Korean brands and, eventually, popular culture reached global audiences (Chapters 89 and 90). Across the divide, the contrast with the North Korean economy treated in Chapter 77 shows how differently two Korean states organized production after 1945.

Finally, the chapter models a way of thinking about economic history: crediting institutions, people, and circumstances in proportion to the evidence, distinguishing celebration from analysis, and refusing to read outcomes backward as inevitable. Students who carry those habits forward will be better prepared to evaluate claims about economic success and failure in any society.
