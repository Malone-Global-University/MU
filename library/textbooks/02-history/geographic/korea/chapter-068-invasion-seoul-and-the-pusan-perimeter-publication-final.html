Invasion, Seoul, and the Pusan Perimeter

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Between the early hours of Sunday, June 25, 1950, and mid-September, the front line of the Korean War moved some three hundred kilometers south, from the 38th parallel to a perimeter around the southeastern corner of the peninsula. Seoul changed hands in three days. The campaign that followed was less a single battle than a race between an army that had prepared to win quickly and a coalition that had to be improvised while it was already losing.

Before dawn on June 25, 1950, artillery of the Korean People's Army (KPA) opened fire along much of the 38th parallel, and infantry and armored columns moved south. The attack came after years of fighting along the border, a rebellion in the south, and an arms buildup in both Korean states, all examined in Chapter 67. It was not, however, another border clash. It was a coordinated offensive, planned in advance with Soviet assistance, in which several divisions advanced on separate axes toward a single strategic objective: the capital of the Republic of Korea (ROK) and, with it, the collapse of the southern government.

The opening campaign has often been told in English as a story of American arrival and American rescue. This chapter treats that version as incomplete. The ROK Army, outgunned and in many places surprised, was the first force to meet the invasion and the force that fought throughout the retreat; it was driven out of Seoul but did not disintegrate, and by August it held a large share of the line that finally stopped the KPA. American, British Commonwealth, and other United Nations forces were decisive in different ways, but they entered a war that Korean soldiers and civilians had already been fighting for days and that Korean formations would continue to fight beside them.

The chapter's central question is why a campaign that began with such a lopsided advantage ended in a stalemate at the southeastern corner of the peninsula. The answer has several parts. The KPA had armor, artillery, a coherent operational plan, and many experienced officers and soldiers, and the ROK Army lacked tanks, heavy artillery, and antitank weapons effective against the KPA's T-34/85 tanks. The first American ground forces to arrive were too few, too weakly equipped, and too lightly trained for the task they were given. Yet the KPA's advance also slowed as casualties mounted, supply lines lengthened, and the opposing forces concentrated in a shrinking area with short interior lines, a major port, and growing air and naval support. By September the KPA had conquered most of South Korea but had not destroyed its army.

The war was also experienced by civilians as a catastrophe of flight and violence. Seoul's residents were trapped north of the Han River when the bridges were destroyed, and refugees from the capital and other cities filled the roads and rail lines southward. They were exposed to occupation by the KPA and its political apparatus, to the suspicion of South Korean and American military authorities, and to attack from the air and ground by forces of both sides. This chapter introduces that experience, including the documented killing of refugees at No Gun Ri, while the fuller examination of atrocities and refugee crises belongs to Chapter 71.

The evidence for this period is unusually varied and unevenly distributed. U.S. Army official histories, written from American records and interviews, provide detailed operational narratives. South Korean military histories, memoirs, and civilian testimony record a Korean experience that those histories often underrepresent. Records of the United Nations, the Truman administration, and, increasingly, Soviet and Chinese archives illuminate decisions made far from the front. KPA records are comparatively scarce and survive largely in captured documents and later interviews. Throughout the chapter, students are asked to consider whose records they are reading and whose are missing.

This chapter follows Chapter 67, which examined the political rivalry and military preparations leading to June 25, and precedes Chapter 69, which begins with the Incheon landing on September 15. It covers June 25 to September 14, 1950, and ends with the Pusan Perimeter under heavy pressure but holding. It is organized in five divisions of four sections each: the invasion and the fall of Seoul; international intervention and the first American battles; the July retreat and the civilian crisis; the establishment and defense of the perimeter; and the September offensive, followed by a comprehensive review.

Editorial Note on Place Names

This chapter uses the place-name spellings found in the English-language operational histories and in contemporary records for battles, so that students can consult those sources easily: Pusan, Taegu, Taejon, Chonan, Chochiwon, Naktong, Kum River. The corresponding modern Revised Romanization forms are Busan, Daegu, Daejeon, Cheonan, Jochiwon, Nakdong, and Geum River. Seoul, Incheon, Uijeongbu, and Osan are given in current forms. Korean personal names follow Revised Romanization except where an individual's own preferred English spelling is well established, as with Paik Sun-yup.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Reconstruct the KPA's offensive of June 25–28, 1950, and distinguish it from the earlier border clashes of 1949–1950.
  2. Explain the military factors behind the rapid fall of Seoul, including KPA armor and artillery, ROK weaknesses, and communication failures.
  3. Analyze the destruction of the Han River bridges and distinguish what is known, estimated, and disputed about its consequences and responsibility.
  4. Assess the continuing role of the ROK Army after Seoul's fall rather than treating the campaign as an American defense of a disarmed country.
  5. Distinguish among United Nations Security Council Resolutions 82, 83, and 84 and between UN authorization and the operational chain of command.
  6. Trace President Truman's decisions of June 25–30 and the stages by which air, naval, and ground forces were committed.
  7. Evaluate the defeat of Task Force Smith and the July delaying campaign without reducing them to individual failure.
  8. Interpret documentary evidence for civilian displacement and the No Gun Ri killings, identifying the strengths and limits of each type of source.
  9. Describe the geography, command arrangements, and main battles of the Pusan Perimeter, including Korean contributions.
  10. Trace the KPA's late-August offensive and explain how the defenders prevented a breakthrough.
  11. Compare competing explanations for why the perimeter held and evaluate them against the evidence.
  12. Contextualize the opening campaign within the wider Korean civil conflict and the international Cold War.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a numerically comparable South Korean army fail to hold its initial positions against the KPA's offensive?
  2. How should historians describe responsibility for the invasion when earlier border violence involved both sides?
  3. Why was the loss of Seoul not equivalent to the destruction of the ROK as a state and army?
  4. How did the United Nations, the United States, and individual member states each contribute to the legal and military intervention, and why should those roles be kept distinct?
  5. Why did the first American ground forces fail to stop the KPA, and what does that reveal about military readiness and about the KPA?
  6. How did the civilian population experience the retreat, and whose records allow historians to reconstruct that experience?
  7. What roles did terrain, geography, and interior lines play in making a defensible perimeter possible?
  8. Why did the KPA's offensive lose momentum between July and September?
  9. Why do historians disagree about the relative importance of Korean resistance, American command, air power, logistics, and KPA weaknesses?
  10. What does it mean to say the perimeter "held," and why does that not imply that the war's outcome had been decided?

Historical Context

By June 1950 two rival states had existed on the peninsula for twenty-two months. The Republic of Korea, proclaimed in August 1948 under President Syngman Rhee, claimed jurisdiction over the whole peninsula; the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK), proclaimed in September 1948 under Premier Kim Il Sung, made the same claim from Pyongyang. Each state had been built under the occupation and sponsorship of a superpower (Chapters 65–66), and each regarded the other as illegitimate. Both rested on a society that had been mobilized, divided, and radicalized by colonial rule, liberation, and the political violence of 1945–1950, including the Jeju uprising and the Yeosu-Suncheon rebellion examined in Chapter 66.

Chapter 67 examined the decisions that led to June 25. In summary: Kim Il Sung repeatedly sought Soviet approval for an offensive in 1949 and early 1950; Soviet archival evidence shows that Stalin gave consent in the spring of 1950 on condition that Mao Zedong agree, and that Mao did so in May. The Soviet Union supplied weapons, including T-34/85 tanks and aircraft, and advisers helped with operational planning. Tens of thousands of Korean veterans of the Chinese Civil War were transferred from the People's Liberation Army into the KPA in 1949–1950. The United States, by contrast, had withdrawn its combat troops from South Korea in 1949, left a small Korean Military Advisory Group (KMAG), and supplied the ROK Army with light weapons while withholding heavy ones, in part because it feared that Rhee might attempt a northward attack of his own.

The terrain on which the campaign was fought shaped it throughout. The peninsula is mountainous, with the Taebaek range running down the east and the Sobaek range crossing the south, so that armies tended to move along a small number of road and rail corridors. The principal western corridor ran from the 38th parallel through Uijeongbu to Seoul, crossed the Han River, and continued south through Suwon, Osan, Chonan, and Taejon. In summer, heavy monsoon rains swell the rivers; rice paddies and narrow roads limit the use of tanks; and the heat and disease environment burdens soldiers who are unaccustomed to it. The Naktong River, the longest river in the southeast, formed the natural western edge of the area where the war would finally stabilize.

The campaign also took place within an international system that was just beginning to understand the stakes. The Soviet Union was boycotting the United Nations Security Council in 1950 over the question of China's seat. The People's Republic of China, founded in October 1949, had not yet entered the conflict. Japan, under Allied occupation, was the base from which American forces would be committed. These factors made possible the rapid international response that is examined in Division II, and they shaped the political and military meaning of every battle described in this chapter.

Invasion and the Fall of Seoul

Section 68.1

June 25, 1950: The North Korean Invasion Begins

The offensive began on the morning of Sunday, June 25, 1950, with artillery fire along much of the 38th parallel at about 4:00 a.m., followed within hours by infantry and, on the main axes, armor. Accounts differ by a few minutes on the exact timing at different points along the front, as should be expected in a bombardment spread across more than two hundred kilometers, but the sequence is not in doubt. The KPA attacked on at least five axes: the Ongjin Peninsula on the west coast; the Kaesong–Munsan route toward Seoul from the northwest; the Uijeongbu corridor, the main effort, running from Chorwon and Pochon toward the capital; the central mountain route toward Chuncheon; and the east coast road toward Gangneung, supported by amphibious landings by a KPA unit on the coast farther south.

The KPA force that crossed the parallel is usually estimated at between roughly 90,000 and 135,000 troops, depending on how reserves and second-echelon units are counted. It was organized into two corps and an armored brigade, and it was supported by about 150 T-34/85 tanks, self-propelled guns, divisional and heavier artillery, and an air force of Soviet-built Yak fighters and Il attack aircraft. The principal strike force consisted of the 3rd and 4th Divisions, spearheaded by the 105th Armored Brigade (soon expanded into a division), directed down the Uijeongbu corridor. Other divisions struck at Kaesong, at Chuncheon, and along the east coast. The plan, prepared with Soviet advisers, aimed to destroy the ROK Army's main forces in the area around Seoul, capture the capital within days, and bring down the government before outside help could arrive.

Students must distinguish the June 25 offensive from the border violence of the preceding year. Chapter 67 examined the clashes in 1949 and early 1950 around the Ongjin Peninsula, Kaesong, and the central front, in which units of both Korean armies initiated attacks and probes and which killed hundreds of soldiers. That violence contributed to the political climate of the war and has been important to historians who emphasize the civil character of the conflict. But the June 25 offensive differed in scale, coordination, and kind. It involved the simultaneous advance of multiple divisions with tanks and heavy artillery, along axes planned in advance, toward objectives far inside the territory of the other state. It was a planned invasion, and it is not possible, on the evidence now available from Soviet and Korean archives, from captured documents, and from the contemporary reports of UN observers, to describe it otherwise.

The DPRK's immediate public account was the opposite. Pyongyang's radio announced that South Korean forces had attacked northern territory and that the KPA was conducting a counteroffensive, and Kim Il Sung's broadcast of June 26 described a war against the "traitor" government in Seoul. This claim was repeated by the Soviet Union and the People's Republic of China and rejected by the United Nations. The question of who bore responsibility for the invasion must not be confused with the separate historiographical debate, examined in Chapter 67, over the origins of the conflict. Historians who emphasize the long civil struggle, the abuses of the southern regime, and the role of American policy do not generally dispute that the KPA launched the offensive on June 25; their argument is about how that act relates to a longer chain of events.

The evidence for the invasion's opening hours comes from several kinds of sources: reports by KMAG advisers and by UN military observers; ROK Army accounts; captured KPA operations orders and later interviews with KPA officers; and, for decisions in Moscow and Pyongyang, Soviet documents released after 1991. Each has limits. KMAG reports reflect an American advisory perspective; UN observers could report only what they saw along the sectors they visited; captured KPA orders cover only some units. Taken together they establish a coordinated offensive on multiple axes beginning in the early morning of June 25, and not a response to a southern attack.

Section 68.2

The KPA's Initial Offensive and the ROK Army's Defensive Crisis

The imbalance that June 25 exposed was not primarily one of numbers. On paper the ROK Army had approximately 95,000 to 100,000 men organized in eight divisions, a total comparable to the KPA force that crossed the parallel. Its weakness lay elsewhere. It had no tanks and almost no weapons capable of stopping them: its standard antitank weapon was the 2.36-inch rocket launcher, which could not penetrate the T-34/85's frontal armor, supplemented by a few 57-mm antitank guns. Its artillery consisted mainly of 105-mm howitzers in small numbers, with no medium artillery. Its air arm possessed trainer and liaison aircraft but no combat planes. These deficiencies were partly a consequence of American policy, which had supplied infantry weapons and light artillery while withholding armor and heavier equipment out of concern about Rhee's public statements on marching north.

Historical Vocabulary
Combined-arms warfare

Definition: The coordinated use of different kinds of military force, typically infantry, armor, artillery, engineers, and aircraft, so that the strengths of each cover the weaknesses of the others.

Historical context: In June 1950 the KPA employed tanks to break through defensive positions, infantry to clear and hold ground, and artillery to suppress defenders, and it moved around positions that resisted. Facing ROK infantry without armor or antitank weapons, the combination was very difficult to counter.

Why it matters: The KPA's early success is often described as the result of "tanks," but the tanks mattered because they were integrated with infantry and artillery. Conversely, when KPA tanks later advanced without supporting infantry or were exposed to air attack, they became vulnerable.

Potential misunderstanding: The term does not mean the KPA possessed overwhelming modern equipment across its whole force. Much of its infantry was equipped with Soviet and older weapons, and its logistics could not sustain a long offensive.

The KPA, by contrast, had been built over two years into a force that combined Soviet equipment with real experience. A significant proportion of its soldiers and officers, tens of thousands in all, were Korean veterans of the Chinese Civil War who had fought with the People's Liberation Army and were transferred to the north in 1949–1950; the 5th and 6th Divisions, among others, were built largely around such veterans. Soviet advisers had helped train the army and plan the offensive. The KPA's doctrine emphasized rapid penetration by tank-supported columns, the avoidance of frontal assaults on strongpoints in favor of flanking movements, and the use of infiltration through hills to reach the rear of defenders. Its weaknesses, which would become apparent later, were chiefly logistical: its supply system depended on a limited number of roads and railways, and it had planned for a short campaign.

The ROK Army's problems were organizational as well as material. It had expanded rapidly since 1948; many of its officers were young and many of its units were new. Several divisions, among them the 2nd at Daejeon, the 3rd at Daegu, and the 5th at Gwangju, were stationed in the south, where they had been engaged in anti-guerrilla operations that followed the rebellions of 1948–1949. Only four divisions, the 1st, 6th, 7th, and 8th, held the main frontier sectors, with the Capital Division in the Seoul area. On June 23 the army had lifted an alert, and on the weekend of June 24–25 a substantial proportion of soldiers were on leave or on pass, many for the rice-planting season, and a number of senior officers had been at a social event the night before the attack. The commanding structure was also uneven. The Chief of Staff, Chae Byong-duk, and the Defense Ministry were not always in step; relations with KMAG were uneasy; and the headquarters in Seoul had difficulty gathering reliable information from the front.

Despite these weaknesses, the picture of a ROK Army that simply fled is not accurate. Several units fought effectively. The 1st Division under Paik Sun-yup held the Imjin River line northwest of Seoul against the KPA 1st and 6th Divisions for several days and withdrew in good order. The 6th Division at Chuncheon resisted so effectively that the KPA 2nd Division was delayed there for days, upsetting the timetable for the planned envelopment of Seoul from the southeast. On the Ongjin Peninsula, the isolated ROK 17th Regiment withdrew by sea. In several places soldiers attacked tanks with satchel charges and explosives, at heavy cost, because they had no other weapons that could stop them. The ROK Army's early defeat was real and severe, but it was a defeat of an army without the means to resist an armored attack and not of an army without the will to do so.

Section 68.3

The Uijeongbu Corridor and the Battle for the Approaches to Seoul

The decisive fighting of the first three days took place along the corridor from the parallel to Seoul. The KPA 3rd and 4th Divisions, with the 105th Armored Brigade, advanced from the Pochon–Dongducheon area down the road toward Uijeongbu, a market town and road junction about twenty kilometers north of the capital. They were opposed by the ROK 7th Division and, as reinforcements, elements of the Capital Division and the 2nd Division, which were committed piecemeal. KPA artillery and tanks broke through the forward positions on June 25 and 26. ROK units could not stop the tanks, and the defenders were frequently outflanked by infantry moving through the hills on both sides of the road.

On June 26 the ROK Army attempted a counterattack at Uijeongbu with the Capital and 2nd Divisions, which were supposed to retake the town. The counterattack failed. Units arrived at different times, without adequate artillery or antitank support; tanks again proved decisive; and communications failures meant that commanders often did not know where their neighboring units were. Telephone lines were cut or overloaded, radios were scarce, and many orders did not arrive or arrived too late. By the afternoon and evening of June 26, KPA forces controlled Uijeongbu, and the ROK 7th Division, which had borne the first shock, had largely disintegrated as a cohesive unit. The road to Seoul lay open.

Meanwhile, on the flanks, the KPA gained ground more slowly. Kaesong fell within hours of the opening bombardment, but the ROK 1st Division regrouped behind the Imjin River and held for most of June 25–27 before withdrawing across the Han west of Seoul. At Chuncheon, the 6th Division held until June 27 and inflicted heavy losses on the KPA 2nd Division. These delays mattered. The KPA plan assumed that the 2nd and 7th Divisions would advance rapidly through the central mountains and reach the Han River southeast of Seoul, enveloping the capital's defenders. They arrived too late to do so. That the main drive down the Uijeongbu corridor succeeded without the full envelopment shows that the KPA's superiority in armor and firepower was enough, in the western corridor, to achieve the objective even when its timetable was disrupted elsewhere.

Within three days, then, the capital had become vulnerable for reasons that can be stated precisely. The KPA had concentrated its best armor and its strongest divisions along the shortest route to Seoul. The ROK defenders had no way to destroy tanks. The divisions that might have reinforced Uijeongbu were stationed far to the south and arrived in fragments. The commands could not communicate reliably. And the geography of the corridor, a narrow valley bounded by hills but with a road and railway on which armor could advance, favored the attacker. None of these factors alone explains the collapse; together they did.

The corridor's decisive importance was also institutional. Dispatch riders, telephone operators, and officers at local headquarters had to interpret reports about armored columns moving faster than the written orders designed to stop them. When a battalion lost contact with a neighboring formation, its commander could not know whether a withdrawal would expose a flank or whether holding position would lead to encirclement. Calls for help from the capital competed with requests from threatened sectors farther east. On a map these are arrows and place names; in practice they were decisions made with delayed information, exhausted soldiers, and little time for reconnaissance. A coherent defense required more than bravery at each roadblock: it required knowing which roads remained open and which reserve could reach them before KPA armor. The western command could not reliably provide that coordination in the opening forty-eight hours.

Section 68.4

The Fall of Seoul, June 28, and the Han River Disaster

Seoul in June 1950 was a city of well over a million people and the center of the ROK's government, economy, and communications. As the KPA approached on June 26 and 27, the government's position became increasingly difficult. President Rhee, urged by Ambassador Muccio and by his own officials, decided to leave. He departed by train in the early hours of June 27 and went first to Daejeon; the cabinet and National Assembly followed in confusion. A recorded radio message attributed to Rhee, broadcast during the day, told listeners that the government remained in Seoul and that the army was holding. By then Rhee had left, and the broadcast contributed to the confusion of residents who were trying to decide whether to flee. Meanwhile, American civilians were evacuated by sea from Incheon and by air from Gimpo, with the first American air operations of the war flown to cover those evacuations.

On the night of June 27–28, with KPA tanks entering the northern outskirts of the capital, the ROK Army demolished the bridges across the Han River south of the city. The explosions occurred at about 2:30 a.m. on June 28. They destroyed the main highway bridge and the railway bridges. At the time of the demolition the bridges were crowded with retreating soldiers, military vehicles, and civilians, and the number of people killed in the explosions and in the panic and drowning that followed has never been established. U.S. Army historical accounts estimate between roughly 500 and 800 dead; South Korean sources have given higher figures, in some accounts exceeding a thousand, and some popular claims go higher still. Because no one counted the dead on the bridge and in the river, these figures must be treated as estimates rather than as established totals.

The decision itself has been a subject of long argument. The demolition was ordered through the ROK Army's command, and the army's chief engineer, Colonel Choi Chang-sik, carried it out. Accounts differ on who gave the final order, on what the officers involved understood about the number of people still on the bridge, and on whether the order was given prematurely. The military rationale was clear: the KPA's tanks could not cross the river without the bridges, and the army needed time to establish a defensive line on the south bank. The cost was that most of the ROK Army's forces still north of the river, along with their vehicles and heavy weapons, were cut off, and that civilians who had not yet left the city were trapped. Colonel Choi was court-martialed and executed in September 1950; a reopened proceeding in 1962 and a subsequent acquittal reported in 1964 cleared him of criminal responsibility for the premature demolition. This sequence suggests that the military and political leadership found a subordinate to blame for a decision made at a higher level, but the evidence on the chain of command remains disputed.

The KPA entered Seoul on June 28, and the capital fell with little organized resistance. ROK units north of the Han that had survived the previous days' fighting crossed the river as best they could, by ferry, boat, and swimming, abandoning most of their vehicles and heavy weapons. U.S. Army historians, drawing on ROK reports, give an estimate of about 22,000 ROK soldiers accounted for south of the river immediately after the fall of the city, rising to about 54,000 as reports accumulated in the first days of July and stragglers rejoined their units; these are approximations from a period of chaotic record keeping, but they suggest that the loss was less a destruction of soldiers than a dispersal and loss of equipment. The ROK 1st Division, for example, crossed the Han at Haengju, west of the capital, and remained a functioning formation.

This is a critical teaching point. The loss of Seoul was a serious military and political blow: it removed the ROK's capital and much of its industrial base, destroyed its army's equipment, and gave the DPRK a powerful propaganda victory. But the ROK Army was not destroyed. It reorganized south of the river, continued to fight at every stage of the retreat, and would later hold half or more of the Pusan Perimeter. The idea that American troops arrived to rescue a state whose army had vanished is a misreading of the first weeks of the war. It also underestimates the effort and sacrifice of Korean soldiers who continued to resist.

For the people who remained in Seoul, the consequences were immediate. The KPA brought with it a political apparatus: People's Committees were organized in neighborhoods, officials of the ROK government, police, and anyone identified as a supporter of the Rhee government were sought, and many residents were pressed into labor or service. Over the summer, according to ROK government estimates, tens of thousands of civilians, including politicians, intellectuals, and professionals, were taken north, whether they went voluntarily or by force varying with the individual case. Killings of officials and of people regarded as enemies of the new order occurred in Seoul and elsewhere in the occupied areas. The detailed treatment of these events belongs to Chapter 71, but students should understand that the KPA's conquest of the capital was accompanied by a political transformation as well as a military one.

Division II · Sections 68.5–68.8 · Late June–Early July 1950

International Intervention and the First American Battles

Section 68.5

The ROK Army Regroups and Attempts to Defend the Han River Line

In the days after Seoul fell, the ROK Army's first task was to find out what was left of it. Units that had crossed the Han in disorder had to be collected, reorganized, and given orders; weapons had to be found for men who had lost theirs; and commanders had to establish contact with their superiors and neighbors. On June 30, President Rhee replaced Chae Byong-duk as Chief of Staff with Chung Il-kwon, a more senior officer who would hold the post through the critical summer and who proved better able to reorganize the army under pressure. A defensive command was improvised south of the river, and the army's headquarters moved south, first to Suwon and then, as the front moved, to Daejeon and Daegu.

The ROK defenders attempted to hold the south bank of the Han and, behind it, a line running through Yeongdeungpo, Siheung, and the approaches to Suwon. They had very few heavy weapons. Many soldiers had lost their rifles in the retreat, others had no ammunition, and the artillery that had survived was scarce. The reorganized units, drawn from the 1st, 2nd, 7th, and Capital Divisions and others, were of uneven quality. Mobilization was extended: the government called up reservists and volunteers, organized student and youth units, and began to press able-bodied men into service, sometimes by rounding them up in towns and on the roads. These improvised measures supplied manpower but not training or equipment, and many of the new soldiers were inexperienced when they first entered combat.

The KPA did not cross the Han immediately. It paused for several days after taking the capital, reorganizing its forces, repairing the damage to its own communications, and bringing up bridging equipment, and its main units began to cross the river on about July 3. Historians continue to discuss why the KPA paused. Explanations include the need to regroup after the rapid advance, the destruction of the bridges, the difficulty of moving tanks across the river, and the leadership's expectation that the South Korean government would collapse and that the population would rise in support, which made urgent exertion seem unnecessary. Whatever the reason, the delay of a few days gave the ROK Army time to organize its first improvised defense and gave the United States time to commit its first troops. Whether a more rapid pursuit would have taken Pusan before the Americans could arrive is a counterfactual that cannot be answered, but it is clear that the KPA's pause was consequential.

The contribution of Korean soldiers in these first weeks of the retreat has received less attention in English-language histories than it deserves. ROK units fought delaying actions at the Han crossings, at Suwon, at Pyeongtaek, and in the hills along the road toward Chungju and Cheongju. These actions seldom stopped the KPA for more than a day, but together they cost it time and casualties and helped the ROK Army to preserve some of its units. Their significance lies less in any single engagement than in the fact that the army continued to exist as a fighting force, which determined that there would be Korean divisions to hold the line in August.

The distinction between a surviving army and a coherent defensive front was crucial. Reassembled soldiers might be available for a head count but still lack radios, company commanders, mortars, ammunition, field kitchens, and the trucks needed to move as a unit. Regimental designations therefore should not be read as proof that every formation retained its prewar strength. Korean commanders improvised composite units and shifted battalions from place to place, while liaison officers attempted to discover which bridges, roads, and river crossings remained usable. The process also imposed costs on civilians: trains, carts, workshops, and food supplies could be requisitioned in the emergency. The soldiers and the populations who supported them were both moving south, often along the same already congested road system.

ROK reorganization was not simply an American accomplishment. Korean officers determined where stragglers could be assembled, restored local chains of command, and reconnected formations that had escaped intact from the north. Some of the most effective surviving divisions retained experienced officers and small-unit cohesion even after losing weapons. Others required extensive reconstruction from scattered men. American advisers could provide liaison and eventually matériel, but they could not instantly recreate the Korean command relationships on which a sustained defense depended. This difference helps explain why aggregate figures for ROK manpower recovered more quickly than actual fighting efficiency, and why the army's ability to hold a sector in August represented substantial institutional recovery from the June disaster.

Section 68.6

The United Nations Security Council and the Decision to Resist Aggression

The international response began within hours. On Sunday, June 25, in New York, the United States requested an emergency session of the United Nations Security Council, which met that afternoon. The Soviet Union, which had been boycotting the Council since January 1950 to protest the seating of the Republic of China (Taiwan) rather than the People's Republic of China, was absent and therefore could not cast a veto. The Council acted on three occasions in the first two weeks, and the three resolutions did different things. Students should keep them distinct.

Resolution 82 (June 25) determined that the armed attack on the Republic of Korea by forces from North Korea constituted a breach of the peace. It called for the immediate cessation of hostilities, called upon the North Korean authorities to withdraw their forces to the 38th parallel, requested the UN Commission on Korea to observe and report, and called upon all members to assist the United Nations in carrying out the resolution and to refrain from assisting the North Korean authorities. It was adopted by a vote of nine in favor, none against, and one abstention (Yugoslavia), with the Soviet Union absent.

Resolution 83 (June 27) noted that the North Korean authorities had not ceased hostilities or withdrawn and had not complied with Resolution 82, and it recommended that members of the United Nations furnish such assistance to the Republic of Korea as might be necessary to repel the armed attack and to restore international peace and security in the area. This was a recommendation, not an order, and it did not establish a command. It was adopted by seven votes to one (Yugoslavia against), with Egypt and India abstaining and the Soviet Union absent.

Resolution 84 (July 7) welcomed the willingness of members to assist, recommended that all members providing military forces and other assistance make them available to a unified command under the United States, requested the United States to designate the commander of those forces, and authorized the unified command to use the United Nations flag. It was adopted by seven votes to none, with Egypt, India, and Yugoslavia abstaining and the Soviet Union absent. On July 8 President Truman named General Douglas MacArthur as the commander. The resulting United Nations Command was therefore an American-led coalition authorized and recommended by the Council, not a standing UN army recruited and directed by the Secretary-General. The Security Council did not control the command's operations; the United States did, with reports to the Council.

The legal and political implications were contested at the time. The Charter's provisions for Council-directed military force, which would have required agreements between members and the Council under Article 43, had never been implemented, so the Korean operation proceeded on the basis of recommendations rather than binding orders. The Soviet Union argued that the resolutions were invalid because a permanent member had not concurred; the response, which became the prevailing practice, was that absence did not constitute a veto. The DPRK and the People's Republic of China denounced the resolutions as illegitimate. When the Soviet Union returned to the Council in August, it could no longer block action but could use the Council as a forum for protest. In the end, sixteen member states sent combat forces during the war and others sent medical and other assistance, though in the period covered by this chapter only a small number had arrived in Korea.

International participation was neither instantaneous nor interchangeable with a Security Council vote. Governments had to decide what forces they could release, obtain transport, determine how those forces would be supplied, and agree to operate within an American-led command. Some states initially offered ships, aircraft, medical assistance, or small detachments; their contingents reached Korea at different stages of the war. Diplomatic support for the resolutions therefore preceded the formation of a genuinely multinational battlefield coalition. Even among the Council's members there were different understandings of how far military action should go: defeating an invasion was a more limited objective than pursuing the KPA north of the old parallel. The latter question would become central after the events of this chapter. For June and July, the immediate political achievement was a recommendation for collective defense that American decisions could put into effect rapidly, not the creation of a self-sufficient United Nations military establishment.

Section 68.7

Truman, MacArthur, and the First American Military Response

President Harry Truman was at his home in Independence, Missouri, when the news arrived on June 25 and returned to Washington that evening. At meetings at Blair House (the White House was then being renovated) on June 25 and 26, and again on June 27, he met with his senior diplomatic and military advisers. The decisions that followed were taken in stages, and it is a common error to treat them as a single act. On June 25 and 26 Truman authorized General MacArthur, who commanded American forces in the Far East from Tokyo, to ship ammunition and equipment to the ROK Army and to use air and naval forces to protect the evacuation of American dependents. On June 26, in Washington, he went further and authorized the use of air and naval forces to support South Korean troops south of the 38th parallel. On June 27 he announced that decision publicly and also ordered the Seventh Fleet to prevent any attack on Taiwan, a step that linked the Korean crisis to the Chinese civil conflict and would long complicate relations with the People's Republic of China.

Responsibility was divided across multiple headquarters. Truman and the Joint Chiefs determined the scope of American commitments; MacArthur commanded the Far East forces and, after July 8, the unified command; Walker directed the main American ground forces inside Korea; and Korean officers continued to command their formations while coordinating with American advisers. Each level possessed different information. Washington followed an international crisis with consequences for Europe and Taiwan, whereas commanders in Korea needed ammunition and units before the next river crossing. The gap between policy authorization and the arrival of a usable combat formation explains why American intervention could begin decisively in political terms yet remain militarily inadequate for weeks. The fighting force and its logistical apparatus had to be transported from occupation bases, distributed across Korean railways, and organized under fire.

Events at the front moved faster than the decisions in Washington. On June 29 MacArthur flew to Suwon and, from the south bank of the Han, observed the retreating ROK troops and the burning capital. He reported that the ROK Army could not hold without American ground forces. On June 30 Truman first approved the use of a limited ground force to secure the port and airfield area around Pusan and then, within hours, authorized the commitment of American ground troops from Japan to combat in Korea. Those June 30 decisions are therefore distinct from the June 27 announcement of air and naval support, and the American ground war began as an improvised response, not as a long-prepared plan. Authority to bomb military targets in North Korea was extended in the same days.

MacArthur's position was unusual. He was already the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers governing occupied Japan and the commander in chief of American forces in the Far East; on July 8 he was added as commander of the new United Nations Command, a title that came with a flag, a headquarters in Tokyo, and a role as the channel between the United States and other contributing governments. On July 14 Rhee wrote to MacArthur placing the ROK's armed forces under his operational command, an arrangement that made the ROK Army subordinate to an American commander and shaped the allocation of Korean units and of American support to them for the rest of the war.

Japan was essential to everything that followed. The Eighth Army, which Lieutenant General Walton Walker commanded, was composed of the four divisions then on occupation duty there: the 24th, 25th, and 7th Infantry Divisions and the 1st Cavalry Division. Airfields in Kyushu launched the first air operations; Japanese ports supplied ships; Japanese industry repaired and produced equipment; and Japanese workers manned much of the logistical system. The occupation of Japan, in other words, was both the source of American troops and the support structure for them, a relationship that gave Japan a new economic role and that Chapter 88 revisits from the perspective of later Korean–Japanese relations.

American assumptions about the war's duration and difficulty were optimistic. Many officers in Tokyo believed that the appearance of American troops would cause the KPA to stop or that a small force could delay it until reinforcements arrived. This underestimation affected how the first units were committed, and it helps explain why the first American ground force, a small task force flown from Japan, was sent forward as a delaying and demonstration element. The commitment of limited forces on the assumption that the opponent would be deterred or defeated by a show of force would prove a costly error.

Section 68.8

Task Force Smith and the Battle of Osan, July 5, 1950

The first American ground unit in action was a force assembled from the 24th Infantry Division in Japan, the division nearest Korea. Lieutenant Colonel Charles B. Smith led an infantry detachment from the 1st Battalion, 21st Infantry Regiment: approximately 400 men were flown from Japan to Pusan on July 1 and moved north by train. The supporting artillery was not flown in with them. Elements of A Battery, 52nd Field Artillery Battalion, under Lieutenant Colonel Miller O. Perry, crossed from Japan by landing ship on July 2, reached Pusan, and traveled by rail and road to join the infantry around Pyongtaek by July 3–4. The combined force, including roughly 100–135 artillery personnel and six 105-mm howitzers, numbered approximately 540 men, depending on the roll used. Smith met the American advance headquarters at Taejon, where General Dean instructed him to delay the KPA on the road near Osan. Dean told Smith to move north and block the road at Osan, south of Suwon, and to delay the KPA. The task force was a token force; it was intended to show the flag and to slow the enemy, not to defeat him.

The task force took up positions on a ridge north of Osan in the early hours of July 5, in rain. The infantry held the high ground astride the road, with the artillery behind them. The KPA 4th Division, supported by the 107th Tank Regiment of the 105th Armored Division, approached from the north in the morning. Around 7:00 a.m., the first column of T-34 tanks came into view, and the American howitzers opened fire. Only a few high-explosive antitank rounds, six by common accounts, were available, and the artillerymen destroyed or damaged the first tanks at the head of the column. The American infantry fired their 2.36-inch rocket launchers and the available 75-mm recoilless rifles, but the weapons, either obsolete or in insufficient numbers, failed to stop the tanks. Some three dozen tanks, in successive groups, passed through the American positions in the course of the morning, firing on the infantry as they went, and continued south toward Osan and beyond.

The KPA infantry arrived later in the morning, with more tanks and trucks, and began to envelop the positions. The Americans had little ammunition left, their communications to the rear were cut, and their flanks were exposed. Around midday Smith ordered a withdrawal. It disintegrated: units left under fire, wounded men and weapons were abandoned, and groups of soldiers made their way south independently. U.S. Army accounts record about 150 killed, wounded, or missing out of the task force, including some taken prisoner; estimates differ, and a substantial portion of the casualties occurred in the withdrawal and the following days as stragglers were captured. The task force's artillery lost most of its guns. KPA casualties were comparatively light, and a few tanks were disabled; the force had delayed the 4th Division by several hours at most.

Task Force Smith's defeat has become a symbol, and it is important to understand what it did and did not demonstrate. It is not adequately explained by the failure of individual leaders or by a lack of courage. The task force's soldiers were mostly young men in occupation units who had been recruited for peacetime service, had received limited training in combined operations, and had never fired at a tank. Equipment was largely left over from the Second World War; ammunition was limited; the units sent to Korea were below authorized strength, with regiments lacking one of their three battalions. The Army's budget had been cut sharply after 1945, its doctrine and training had emphasized other contingencies, and it had assumed that its tactical air power and the atomic bomb would deter or deal with any major aggression. A small infantry force with weapons that could not stop armor, positioned on an open road, was in the situation of a ROK unit with the same weapons. KPA combined-arms tactics did the rest.

The battle's wider significance was both military and political. Militarily, it showed that small delaying forces could not stop a coordinated armored offensive and that more men, better antitank weapons, and artillery were needed. The 3.5-inch rocket launcher, which was effective against the T-34, was rushed to Korea by air in the following days. Politically, it ended the assumption in Tokyo that the mere appearance of American troops would stop the KPA, and it showed the American public that the war would not be short. The phrase "No more Task Force Smiths" later became a rallying cry for American military preparedness. For the history of the Korean War it is equally important that the defeat took place on the same ground where ROK units had been trying to do the same thing for a week. The ROK soldiers had no better answer to the tanks, and they had been fighting for far longer.

The arrival sequence mattered to the battle's outcome. The men flown from Japan were infantry with portable weapons, limited ammunition, and few vehicles; their accompanying artillery had to come by ship and road and then deploy separately. Six 105-mm howitzers were not equivalent to the full artillery support of a division, and they were furnished with only a handful of rounds suitable for attacking heavy armor. The defenders could choose a defensible ridge, but they had no means to prevent KPA armor from continuing south while infantry fixed them in place. Once enemy troops worked around the American flanks, Smith faced the choice of holding until surrounded or withdrawing under fire. The withdrawal itself became disorganized as communications broke down, men were separated from officers, and wounded soldiers had to be abandoned or carried without adequate transport.

The defeat also exposed a mistaken expectation about deterrence. The mere presence of American uniforms did not halt troops who had orders, a timetable, and the firepower to keep moving. Yet the action cannot be assessed solely by asking why Smith failed to defeat a superior force. He had been ordered to delay an offensive with a detachment drawn from a division in the middle of an emergency deployment. The military question is whether his short resistance contributed useful time and information to the arrival of larger forces, and whether commanders learned quickly enough from its limitations. Later recollections sometimes turned Osan into a cautionary symbol of American unpreparedness; the actual engagement also demonstrates how the KPA combined tactical movement, armor, and infantry pressure during the first weeks.

Division III · Sections 68.9–68.12 · July 1950

The July Retreat, Taejon, and the Civilian Crisis

Section 68.9

The American and South Korean Delaying Campaign: Chonan, Chochiwon, and the Kum River

After Osan, General Dean's mission was to trade ground for time. The 24th Infantry Division, with its three regiments (the 19th, 21st, and 34th) understrength and arriving piecemeal, was to delay the KPA along the main road and rail line from Osan through Pyeongtaek, Chonan, and Chochiwon to the Kum River and Taejon, long enough for the 25th Infantry Division and the 1st Cavalry Division to arrive from Japan and for the Eighth Army to establish a position. The strategy of exchanging territory for time had a clear logic, but its execution was costly because the units available were not strong enough to impose a serious delay on a force that could flank and outpace them.

The roads south of Osan were not a sequence of prepared fortresses. The retreating defenders repeatedly selected river lines and high ground close to road junctions, but there was rarely enough time to dig substantial positions, reconnoiter alternative routes, or register artillery fire. When one regiment withdrew, its neighbor could discover too late that the road behind it was open to infiltration. A delaying battle could still serve its purpose if it forced the KPA to deploy infantry, maneuver guns, repair roads, and organize another assault; it did not have to hold the town indefinitely. Yet such actions consumed the defenders' own trained soldiers and vehicles. By the Kum River line, the 24th Division had been fighting with insufficient replacements, and its commanders had to decide which bridges to deny, where to hold reserves, and whether a withdrawal would be possible after daylight.

Historical Vocabulary
Delaying action

Definition: A defensive operation in which a force fights from successive positions, yielding ground only when threatened with envelopment, in order to slow an enemy and gain time for other forces to prepare, while avoiding decisive engagement.

Historical context: In July 1950 the 24th Division and ROK units attempted delaying actions at Pyeongtaek, Chonan, Chochiwon, the Kum River, and Taejon, with the aim of gaining time for reinforcement.

Why it matters: A delaying action is judged by the time it buys relative to its cost. The July actions bought days at the price of heavy casualties and lost equipment, and their value depended on what the defenders did with the time.

Potential misunderstanding: A delaying action is not simply a retreat. It requires units able to disengage in good order, which the poorly equipped and inexperienced forces of July often could not do.

On July 6 the 34th Infantry Regiment, under Colonel Robert Martin, took up positions at Pyeongtaek and Ansong, north of Chonan, and was quickly outflanked by KPA tanks and infantry. It fell back to Chonan, where, on July 7 and 8, it fought another action in which Colonel Martin was killed while firing a rocket launcher at a tank. The regiment, badly mauled, withdrew toward Chochiwon, where the 21st Infantry had positioned itself. On July 10 and 11 the KPA attacked at Chochiwon, again using armor along the road and infantry through the hills, and the 21st Infantry's 3rd Battalion suffered heavy losses before the position was abandoned. In each fight the pattern repeated: the KPA found the defenders' flanks, used tanks to isolate roadside positions, and forced withdrawals that frequently turned into disorderly retreats.

The next position was the Kum River, a broad river about twenty kilometers north of Taejon. Here, from July 13 to 16, the 19th and 34th Infantry Regiments tried to prevent the KPA 3rd and 4th Divisions from crossing. The river line was longer than the forces available could hold continuously, and the KPA used assault boats and wading crossings, infiltrating between defensive positions and then bringing tanks across after engineers established crossing points. American units, short of ammunition and communications, held in places and were outflanked in others; on July 16 they withdrew toward Taejon. The loss of the line cost the 24th Division heavily and left it in no condition to do more than defend the city for a short time.

American soldiers in these battles faced conditions that compounded their tactical difficulties. July 1950 brought heat and the first monsoon rains. The soldiers, many of them young and unaccustomed to the peninsula's terrain, were often exhausted; heat casualties and illness were frequent; and the hills and paddies made it difficult to establish coherent lines. KPA infiltration, in which small units moved around defenders at night and appeared in their rear, produced a persistent fear that the enemy was everywhere, and it encouraged both disorderly withdrawals and suspicion of Korean civilians who might be KPA soldiers in disguise. The effects of that suspicion are examined in §68.12.

The ROK Army did not fight only in the western corridor. While the Americans delayed along the Seoul–Taejon road, ROK divisions were reorganizing and fighting on adjacent fronts, in the hills north and east of the road around Cheongju and Chungju and in the central mountains. These formations, reduced in strength and often short of heavy weapons, held key passes and roads and in some places slowed KPA units that would otherwise have been free to move against the flank of the American positions. Their operations are examined in §68.11. Treating the delaying campaign as a purely American affair misreads the front: it was a combined retreat in which Korean and American units fought on separate roads and often without much coordination.

Section 68.10

The Battle and Fall of Taejon, July 19–20

Taejon was more than another stop on the road south. It was the central road and rail junction between Seoul and Pusan, and since July 1 it had served as the ROK's temporary capital. Its defense was meant to hold the KPA for long enough that the 1st Cavalry Division, which was landing at Pohang, could move into position. General Dean, who had personally led the 24th Division since its arrival, stayed in the city with his headquarters and decided to remain until the 1st Cavalry Division arrived, which he expected to be about July 20. He had his regiments, the 19th, 21st, and 34th, all reduced by losses to a fraction of their normal strength, and a few artillery units.

The KPA 3rd and 4th Divisions closed on Taejon from the north and west on July 19. Attacking in coordinated columns, with tanks in the lead, they sent units around both flanks of the city and cut the roads to the south and east, the routes by which the Americans would have to withdraw. Fighting inside the city on July 20 was desperate and confused. American infantry and engineers, equipped now with the 3.5-inch rocket launchers that had arrived by air, destroyed a number of tanks in the streets; the destruction of KPA armor at Taejon was larger than in any previous battle. General Dean himself joined the hunt for tanks, firing a rocket launcher at one, an action for which he would later receive the Medal of Honor. But the fighting was fragmented, with units isolated from one another and communications breaking down, and the KPA's encirclement meant that the Americans could leave only by running a gauntlet of ambushes along the roads to the southeast.

The withdrawal from Taejon was another costly retreat. Columns of trucks and vehicles were fired on at roadblocks and in narrow valleys, men abandoned their vehicles and escaped on foot into the hills, and units lost cohesion. The 24th Division's casualties at Taejon were more than a thousand killed, wounded, and missing, a large fraction, perhaps a third, of the men engaged, and the division's losses since July 5 totaled several thousand. The division, which had been the first to fight, was effectively destroyed as a fighting force and had to be withdrawn and rebuilt. The city fell on July 20.

General Dean became separated from his men during the withdrawal on the night of July 20 while searching for water for a wounded soldier, and for weeks nothing was known of his fate. He wandered in the hills for thirty-six days, and was captured on August 25 near Chinan, after Korean civilians reportedly betrayed him to the KPA. His battle and his capture are distinct events five weeks apart and must not be conflated. He was held as a prisoner of war until the armistice in 1953, and his Medal of Honor was announced in 1951 while his captivity was still continuing. It was only in December 1950 that the Americans learned that he was alive.

Taejon was a defeat, but it was not without effect. Dean's delay at the city gave the 1st Cavalry Division time to arrive and take up positions to the southeast, and the 25th Division, which landed at Pusan in the middle of July, was moving forward. The KPA's 3rd and 4th Divisions had also suffered heavily: they had lost numbers of tanks and men at Chonan, the Kum River, and Taejon, and they paused to reorganize before pressing south. The fight at Taejon, in short, bought days and cost a division, a trade that makes sense only against what the days would be used for. Taejon is therefore not a minor engagement between Osan and Pusan. It was a substantial battle that inflicted real losses on the KPA, that destroyed a major American formation, and that gave the defenders time to prepare the position that followed.

In the days after Taejon, the KPA changed the axis of its main effort. Instead of continuing directly toward Taegu and Pusan along the main road, which was now defended by the 1st Cavalry Division and by ROK units, it sent its 6th Division on a wide westward sweep through the southwest, through Gunsan, Gwangju, and Suncheon, with the aim of approaching Pusan from the west through Jinju and Masan. This flanking maneuver, discussed in §68.15, carried the KPA across the poorly defended southwestern provinces and presented a new threat to the southern flank of the defenders.

Cause and Effect

Why the ROK and American Forces Retreated, June–July 1950

Long-term causes

An ROK Army built rapidly and equipped without armor or effective antitank weapons; an American army reduced to occupation duties after 1945; a KPA prepared for two years with Soviet equipment, advisers, and Chinese Civil War veterans.

Short-term causes

A coordinated offensive on multiple axes; ROK units on leave or dispersed; failures of communication and command; a hasty commitment of small American forces without adequate antitank weapons.

Immediate trigger

The KPA's decision to advance on June 25 with a plan that concentrated armor along the most direct road to Seoul and that kept advancing despite delays on the flanks.

Immediate effects

Loss of Seoul in three days; loss of most ROK heavy equipment; defeat of the first American units at Osan, Chonan, the Kum River, and Taejon; loss of most of the peninsula.

Long-term consequences

A war that extended beyond a quick conquest; the commitment of large American forces; the creation of the UN Command; the shaping of an American military reform program and of the ROK's subordination to UN command.

Historical complexity

The retreat was not caused by a single failure. Material weakness, organizational flaws, KPA tactical skill, and mistaken early assumptions interacted. At the same time, the retreat purchased time, cost the KPA casualties, and set the stage for the perimeter.
Section 68.11

South Korean Resistance in the Central Mountains and Along the East Coast

While American attention, then and since, has focused on the western road, a substantial part of the fighting in July took place on the central and eastern fronts, where ROK divisions bore the main burden. The Taebaek mountains rise along the east of the peninsula, and from Wonju and Chungju to the east coast there are only a few usable roads, many of them narrow, crossing high passes. This terrain constrained the KPA's armor, which could not easily operate off the roads, and it gave defenders numerous opportunities to block and ambush.

After the fall of Seoul, ROK units that had retreated from the Chuncheon and Hongcheon areas, notably the 6th Division and elements of the 8th, took up positions along the road from Wonju through Chungju and Mungyeong to Andong, with orders to protect the central routes into the southeast. On the east coast, the 8th Division and the Capital Division, later reinforced by the 3rd Division, resisted the KPA 5th Division as it came south along the coastal road from Gangneung through Samcheok and Uljin toward Yeongdeok and Pohang. In July the ROK Army was reorganized into corps headquarters that allowed commanders to coordinate several divisions at once, and by the time the perimeter formed, the ROK I Corps held the east and the ROK II Corps held the north-central sector.

The record of these ROK operations is mixed, and it should not be romanticized. The defending divisions were frequently outnumbered, short of ammunition, and dependent on units that had been reconstituted from stragglers and new recruits. Several withdrew faster than the army's leaders wished, and some positions fell to infiltration. But the central and eastern fronts also saw prolonged and effective resistance, notably at the passes north of Mungyeong and Andong, where the ROK 6th Division and other units delayed the KPA 1st and 2nd Divisions, and on the east coast, where ROK units, with the support of American naval gunfire and air attack, slowed the KPA's advance. The KPA's progress outside the western corridor was uneven and slower than its leaders had expected. Its supply lines through the mountains were limited and vulnerable, and its divisions took significant casualties in the repeated clashes.

Local leadership and unit cohesion were decisive in many of these actions. Division commanders such as Paik Sun-yup, Kim Chong-oh, and Yu Jae-heung had commanded units on the first day of the war and by July had accumulated weeks of experience; they knew which of their regimental and battalion commanders could be relied upon, and they could improvise with what they had. Soldiers who had retreated from Seoul and who were now fighting for the territory of their own country, with families behind them, often fought differently from the way they had on June 25. The ROK Army's improvement was gradual and uneven, but it was real, and it is an important part of the explanation for why the north and east of the perimeter held.

By the end of July the ROK forces, along with the American 1st Cavalry Division and the arriving 25th Division, had been pushed back to a line that ran roughly along the Naktong River in the west and through the mountains of the north to the east coast. The terrain explains why the perimeter, when it formed, followed the shape it did. In the north and east, the Taebaek ridges offered a natural barrier through which only a few roads passed; in the west the Naktong, the peninsula's longest river in the southeast, served as a moat, with a limited number of crossing points. The two sectors had different characters. The Naktong front was a river line with defined crossing sites, where the KPA would have to attack across water; the northern and eastern front was a mountain barrier where fighting would be for passes, ridges, and roads. Both depended on the fact that the area they enclosed was small enough for the defenders to shift reserves from one threatened point to another. Walker's use of these interior lines is discussed in §68.13.

On the central and eastern fronts the timetable of war was different from that along the main road to Taejon. Mountain passes compressed attacking formations, and changes in elevation made artillery observation, resupply, and medical evacuation more difficult. ROK units could sometimes hold narrow approaches with modest numbers, but an outflanking movement along an unexpected track could make a defended village untenable. Their operations also depended on local port and rail facilities: the east coast was not merely a picturesque flank but a supply and withdrawal corridor. Korean commanders and naval forces had to weigh whether to keep positions close to the coast, retreat into mountains where transport was scarce, or shift troops by sea when roads were cut. These choices preserved portions of the southern force that were later indispensable around Pohang and the northeast perimeter.

The record deserves a comparison of military perspective. U.S. official operational histories often follow the movements of American divisions because those records are most complete in English. Korean military histories describe a broader set of engagements in which Korean units delayed KPA formations or protected lines of communication without an American regiment immediately beside them. Both perspectives are selective: accounts composed after an eventual victory may turn a difficult withdrawal into an orderly success, while accounts centered on the retreat may overlook a Korean unit that fought effectively in a separate sector. A scholarly account therefore reconstructs the geography of the whole front and identifies where the available evidence is too thin for a confident judgment about an individual commander.

Section 68.12

Refugees, Displacement, and the Civilian Experience of the Retreat

The retreat took place through a populated country of about twenty million people, most of whom lived in villages and towns along the same roads the armies used. From the end of June, civilians moved south in numbers that no one could count: people from Seoul and Incheon, from the towns along the road to the south, and from the villages in the path of the fighting. They traveled on foot with bundles on their backs or on carts, and on trains so crowded that people rode on the roofs and the couplings. Families were separated at river crossings and in the confusion of departure; children were lost; and the elderly, the sick, and the poor often could not leave. The government and the army, preoccupied with military emergencies, had made no plan for mass evacuation, and what relief existed was organized ad hoc by local officials, police, voluntary groups, and, increasingly, the American military.

The refugees' conditions deteriorated as they went south. Food, water, and shelter were scarce; roads and rail lines were needed for military traffic; and the summer weather brought heat and monsoon rain. Disease spread in crowded places. The war arrived at the height of the agricultural year, when rice seedlings had recently been transplanted, and the movement of the farmers, the fighting in the fields, and the requisition of labor and animals threatened the autumn harvest. By August, towns such as Taegu and Pusan held many thousands of displaced people in schools, warehouses, and improvised camps, and the strain on a society that was already poor was severe. Historians have not established precise figures for the number displaced in this period, and estimates run from hundreds of thousands in the early weeks to well over a million by autumn.

Refugees were also a military problem. Both armies feared infiltration. In July KPA soldiers did operate behind the lines, and some wore civilian clothing; U.S. and ROK officers, frightened of ambush in a country where civilians and soldiers could not easily be told apart, came to regard the crowds on the roads as a security threat. Roads needed for military supply were blocked by refugee columns, and commanders imposed restrictions on movement. In late July, as the front neared the Naktong, American commanders and diplomats expressed concern that refugees might be used as cover for infiltration, and orders and instructions were passed down restricting the passage of civilians across the lines. The effect of these measures was that in several places civilians were fired upon.

Violence against civilians came from several directions in this period and is examined in full in Chapter 71. KPA forces and local political committees detained and killed officials, police, and others they regarded as enemies in the areas they occupied. South Korean police and military units executed large numbers of detainees held as leftists or suspected sympathizers, including members of the National Guidance League, in the first weeks of the war, as the front approached; these executions occurred in Daejeon and other places. American forces killed civilians in air attacks on villages and roads and, in at least some documented cases, by ground fire. Students should understand that these categories of violence were not equivalent in number, intent, or command responsibility, and that each requires separate treatment on its own evidence.

The most thoroughly investigated case of the killing of refugees by American forces in this period is that at No Gun Ri. In late July 1950, near the village of Nogeun-ri in Yeongdong County, South Korea, a group of refugees, villagers from the area who had been ordered or pushed out of their homes by American troops, were stopped by soldiers of the 7th Cavalry Regiment of the 1st Cavalry Division. According to survivors, they were attacked from the air on July 26 and then sheltered in a railway underpass, where they were fired upon over several days by American troops. The underpass became a site of killing. The case was brought to wide public attention in 1999 by an Associated Press investigation, and it was subsequently investigated by the U.S. Army and the ROK government, which produced a joint statement in January 2001.

Refugee-control policy emerged from a genuine operational problem, but its consequences cannot be understood only from a commander's viewpoint. Soldiers feared that enemy agents might enter their lines disguised among civilians, while civilians feared approaching troops, bombardment, forcible recruitment, and retaliation if they remained. People leaving a village often lacked reliable information about which roads were open; an order to stop, turn back, or wait could leave them between advancing forces. Children, elderly people, and the wounded traveled at different speeds from military convoys. Food and drinking water became scarce along the routes, while clinics and local administrations struggled to function after their personnel fled. Under these conditions, even a seemingly neutral prohibition on movement might expose a family to danger when the front changed during the night.

No Gun Ri illustrates why chronological source criticism matters. The July 24 entry in the 8th Cavalry Regiment's message log predates the killings and shows that language authorizing gunfire against refugee movement appeared in a military record. It is not proof that soldiers of the 7th Cavalry at the railway underpass received that instruction. Muccio's July 26 letter separately reports a refugee-control arrangement known to senior American representatives, but it does not by itself identify the soldiers or establish their orders at No Gun Ri. Moreover, this letter was brought to broad public attention after the 2001 U.S.–ROK investigation; it should not be presented as a document that investigation had fully weighed. The historical task is to read the documents together without either dismissing the survivors' account or claiming a chain of command that the record does not securely establish.

Evidence Comparison

How Do Historians Know What Happened at No Gun Ri?

No single type of source is adequate. The comparison below considers four kinds of evidence and what each can and cannot establish.

Contemporary military and diplomatic records. Documents from late July 1950, preserved in American archives, show that commanders were concerned about refugees and infiltration. an entry in the 8th Cavalry Regiment's message log dated July 24 reported a directive against refugees crossing the front and included instructions to fire, with discretion for women and children. The U.S. Army review did not find evidence that this regimental entry had been transmitted to the 7th Cavalry soldiers at No Gun Ri. Separately, Ambassador John J. Muccio's July 26 letter to Assistant Secretary of State Dean Rusk described a refugee-control arrangement involving warnings and possible gunfire against people who continued toward American lines. These documents establish that restrictive and potentially lethal policies were in circulation. They do not describe what happened at the underpass.
Survivor testimony. Korean survivors, interviewed from the 1990s onward, described being driven from their village, attacked by aircraft, and then fired upon for days in the underpass. Their accounts agree on the main sequence but differ on numbers, timing, and details, as expected of memories recorded decades later by people who were frightened, often young at the time, and who lost family members. These accounts are the principal evidence for the experience of the victims, and they are consistent with each other on the central events.
American veterans' testimony. Some veterans of the regiment, interviewed in the 1990s, said they had fired at refugees and that orders to do so had been given or understood; others denied that any such order was given or said they had not been present. One account quoted in the original AP story was later questioned on the question of whether the veteran had been at the site. The veterans' testimony confirms that firing occurred, but it conflicts on orders and on responsibility.
Later investigations. The AP investigation (1999), the U.S. Army Inspector General's review and report (January 2001), the ROK government's investigation, and the joint statement of the two governments agree that American soldiers killed and wounded Korean refugees near No Gun Ri in late July 1950. The U.S. review concluded that there was no evidence of an order to fire on civilians and described the killing as an unfortunate tragedy inherent to war; President Clinton expressed regret. Korean investigators and survivors rejected the description of the event as an accident. Through the later statutory victim-recognition process, the ROK authorities recognized 218 victims—150 dead, 13 missing, and 55 people with lasting injuries or disabilities—not 218 fatalities. Recognition concerned documented claims, not a definitive total for all people affected; survivors' organizations have given larger numbers. The fact of the killing is established. Numbers, orders, and intent remain disputed.

What the comparison shows: Records produced at the time show institutional attitudes but are silent about the event; testimony documents the event but depends on memory; later investigations add analysis but rest on the same limited evidence. Historians therefore state what is established, and they explain what is not, and they avoid collapsing a disputed question about orders into either a denial that the killings occurred or an assertion of a deliberate policy of massacre that the documents do not prove. The fuller treatment of this and other incidents is in Chapter 71.

Multiple Perspectives

The Retreat as Experienced by Different Groups, July 1950

Refugee families: Most lived in villages and towns that lay in the path of the war and had to decide whether to stay or go, usually with little information. Leaving meant abandoning homes, crops, and sometimes elderly relatives; staying meant exposure to the fighting and to the occupying army. Their movements were constrained by roads closed to civilians and by military suspicion.
ROK soldiers: They fought and retreated through their own country, past their own families and villages. Many had lost units, weapons, or commanders. They were sometimes treated with suspicion by American troops who could not distinguish them from KPA infiltrators, and they were responsible for enforcing wartime controls on their own people.
American soldiers: Young soldiers, many of them conscripts or recent enlistees from occupation duty, who found themselves in an unfamiliar country, in heat and rain, facing an enemy that appeared to be everywhere. Their fear of infiltration shaped how they regarded Korean civilians, and some units fired on groups they could not identify.
KPA troops: Soldiers advancing through occupied territory under orders to move quickly, many of them veterans of earlier fighting. They were accompanied by political officers and local committees, and they operated in conditions of increasing shortages of supply. In many areas they were welcomed by some residents and feared by others.
Local officials and police: They had to decide whether to flee with the government or remain, what records to destroy, and whom to arrest. In some places they carried out the executions of detained political prisoners; in others they helped organize relief or tried to evacuate people. They were the most exposed to retaliation by the KPA.
Families fleeing the fighting: Households that tried to keep together, often sending an able-bodied son ahead or hiding men of military age from conscription by either side. Women, children, and the elderly bore much of the burden of the journey, and many were separated from relatives for years, an experience that the later divided-family problem examined in Chapter 71 would make permanent for some.
Everyday Life and Social History

A Displaced Household in the Summer of 1950

The household described here is a composite constructed from patterns documented in refugee testimony, relief records, and later oral-history projects. It is not a record of any particular family.

A farming household in the Seoul–Suwon area in early July would typically have included several generations: grandparents, a married couple, and children, with perhaps an unmarried relative. The household had recently transplanted its rice, and its most valuable property was the land, a few tools and animals, stored grain, and household goods. The decision to leave was made as the sound of artillery drew nearer and as neighbors left. The household would take food, bedding, and cooking utensils; an ox or cart if there was one; and whatever money or valuables could be carried. Young men of military age faced the danger of conscription by whichever army reached them.

On the road the family joined streams of people moving south, often at night to avoid air attack. Food ran out within days, and families depended on villagers along the route, on the generosity of strangers, and, later, on camps. Women cooked and cared for children and the old; children carried small bundles; grandparents sometimes could not keep pace and were left with relatives or acquaintances. Many households were split: some members continued south while others turned back to see about the farm, and some never reunited. By August the family might have reached a refugee camp in a school or warehouse near Taegu or Pusan, living on rations of rice or barley provided by the ROK government and by American military and voluntary sources. Because most of the premodern and wartime record is created by governments and armies, ordinary households like this one appear mainly in testimony and in relief statistics, which is why oral history is essential to this period.

Establishment and Defense of the Pusan Perimeter

Section 68.13

Walton Walker, the Eighth Army, and the Decision to Hold Pusan

By late July the question facing the defenders was where, if anywhere, they could stop. Lieutenant General Walton H. Walker, commander of the Eighth Army, had been giving ground for a month; the KPA had destroyed the 24th Division and pushed the ROK Army back to the southeastern corner of the peninsula. Walker was a tough, aggressive tank commander from the Second World War, known for his bluntness, and he had a reputation as a leader who demanded a stand. From the Eighth Army's headquarters, first at Daejeon and then at Taegu, he faced a choice between continued withdrawal toward Pusan, which would have placed the defenders against the sea with no room for maneuver, and a decision to hold the best line available. Contingency planning for evacuation existed, and fear of a "Dunkirk" was present in Tokyo and in Washington, but evacuation would have meant the loss of the ROK.

A perimeter defense concentrated power but created new vulnerabilities. The Naktong was an obstacle to tanks and trucks where bridges had been destroyed, yet crossings by infantry at night could establish bridgeheads before defenders understood their size. The northeastern line lacked a continuous river and relied more heavily on mountainous terrain, road junctions, and ROK units fighting in succession. Pusan's harbor offered secure supply from the sea, but that advantage could be lost if attackers seized Miryang, the railway junctions, or the roads through the southeastern corridor. The command therefore had to consider the perimeter as a system rather than as a single riverbank: the success of one division depended upon transport, artillery and reserves available elsewhere. Walker's instruction to stand did not eliminate retreat within individual sectors; it expressed the requirement that local withdrawals must not become abandonment of the whole defensive area.

Historical Vocabulary
Defensive perimeter and interior lines

Definition: A defensive perimeter is a continuous or semi-continuous line of defense enclosing a territory or base from all sides. Interior lines are the shorter routes by which a defender inside the perimeter can move forces and supplies from one sector to another faster than an attacker outside can concentrate against any of them.

Historical context: The Pusan Perimeter enclosed the southeastern corner of the peninsula, with the sea behind it and a single major port. Its defenders, led by Walker, used roads and railways inside the perimeter to move reserves from one threatened sector to another.

Why it matters: The perimeter's advantage was geometric. The KPA had to attack around a long arc, while the defenders could shift forces along a shorter chord.

Potential misunderstanding: The perimeter was not a continuous fortified line. In the mountains of the north and east, there were gaps and the defenders held key roads and ridges rather than every kilometer.

On or about July 29, Walker met with senior commanders and made clear that the retreat was to end. The accounts of this meeting, preserved in the memoirs of officers present and recounted in later histories, describe him as telling them that there would be no more withdrawals, that the army must stand and fight where it was, that a retreat to Pusan would be disastrous, and that the units must hold as a team. The episode has become known as the "stand or die" order. Students should note what is and is not established about it: that a determination to halt the retreat was communicated to commanders at about this time is clear from the army's subsequent behavior, but the precise words and setting vary among accounts and no verified verbatim transcript is reproduced in this chapter.

The actual withdrawal that preceded the stand continued in the first days of August. Between August 1 and 4, the 1st Cavalry, 24th, and 25th Divisions and the ROK units on the western front withdrew across the Naktong River, blowing up the bridges behind them. The area they enclosed, the Pusan Perimeter, was roughly rectangular, extending roughly 100 miles (160 kilometers) from north to south and about 50 miles (80 kilometers) from east to west at its broadest, while the irregular defended front ran approximately 140 miles (225 kilometers); these are approximate geographic measures rather than the circumference of a closed rectangle. On the west, it followed the Naktong from near Waegwan south to the sea near Masan; on the north, it ran through the mountains from Waegwan through the hills above Taegu to Andong; on the east, it extended through the hills to the coast at Yeongdeok and Pohang. The U.S. Army's chronology dates the establishment of the perimeter to about August 4.

The perimeter's essential asset was the port of Pusan, the peninsula's largest, through which American troops, supplies, and aircraft reached the front. Rail lines and roads ran from Pusan to Taegu and the interior; airfields at Pusan, Taegu, Pohang, and elsewhere supported air operations. Inside the perimeter, the Eighth Army, which by early August included the remnants of the 24th Division, the 1st Cavalry Division, the 25th Division, and the first arrivals of the 2nd Division, the 5th Regimental Combat Team, and the 1st Provisional Marine Brigade, held positions alongside the ROK I and II Corps. The ROK forces held roughly the northern and eastern half of the line and the Americans the western and southwestern part, though units were intermixed at times, and ROK soldiers also served alongside American units from August.

Walker's method of defense was mobile. He lacked the strength to hold every point in force and instead kept reserves, notably the Marine brigade and the 27th Infantry (the "Wolfhounds") of the 25th Division, ready to counterattack at the point of the KPA's most dangerous penetration. This "fire brigade" approach depended on short interior lines and on the American logistical system. It also meant that the same units were repeatedly shifted from one crisis to another in the August fighting. Historians have disagreed about Walker: his supporters credit him with the decisive determination to stand and with skillful use of reserves, while critics point to his abrasive manner, to tactical mistakes, and to the fact that his decisions benefited from conditions he did not control. Students should consider these views in light of the debate in §68.19.

Section 68.14

The First Battle of the Naktong Bulge, August 1950

The first serious attempt to break the new line came on the Naktong River in the sector held by the 24th Division, at a loop in the river near Yongsan known as the Naktong Bulge. The KPA 4th Division, the formation that had overrun Task Force Smith, crossed the river in the night of August 5–6 near Ohang and Pugong-ni, using rafts, boats, and in places underwater bridges, causeways built just below the surface that were invisible from the air. Its infantry waded the river in other places, carrying weapons and ammunition overhead. Within hours several thousand KPA soldiers had established a bridgehead on the east bank and were moving toward the ridges overlooking the river.

The danger was serious. From the bridgehead, the KPA was within reach of Yongsan and of Miryang, the junction on the main road and railway between Pusan and Taegu. Cutting that connection would have severed Taegu and the northern part of the perimeter from the port and divided the defenders. The 24th Division, rebuilt after Taejon and now commanded by Major General John H. Church, did not have enough strength to eject the KPA alone, and Walker moved reserves to the area. The 9th Infantry Regiment of the 2nd Division, the first elements of that division to reach the front, and the 27th Infantry were committed, and on August 8 and in the following days, American counterattacks attempted to dislodge the bridgehead. The KPA held its positions on the ridges, notably Obong-ni Ridge and the Cloverleaf Hill complex, and the fighting remained inconclusive for more than a week.

The turning point was the arrival of the 1st Provisional Marine Brigade, which had been in action at Masan and was shifted north on August 16. The brigade, with its own attached air squadrons flying Corsair fighter-bombers, attacked Obong-ni Ridge on August 17 and 18, supported by artillery, armor (including new Pershing tanks), and close air support, and drove the KPA from the heights with heavy fighting. The KPA 4th Division, which had also lost heavily to the American and ROK defenders and to air attack and was cut off from supplies on the eastern side of the river, withdrew across the Naktong or broke up. By August 19, the bridgehead had been eliminated. The division abandoned much of its heavy equipment and lost most of its effective strength, and it ceased for the time being to be a serious threat.

The battle illustrates the working of the perimeter. The defenders did not hold every point; the KPA crossed and established a position inside the line. But the Americans possessed interior lines, which allowed them to shift reserves rapidly by road and rail; artillery and close air support; and the ability to bring a fresh, highly skilled formation to the threatened sector. The KPA, which had gained a bridgehead, was unable to reinforce it quickly across the river because UN air power attacked the crossing sites and supply routes and the river itself separated the attackers from their supplies. The first battle thus established a pattern that would repeat in September: a KPA penetration, a local crisis, and a counterattack that restored the line at a cost.

The first crossing demonstrated the difference between defeating an initial assault and eliminating a bridgehead. KPA infantry could cross the Naktong in darkness with light weapons, secure hills east of the river, and await heavier supplies before the defenders fully appreciated the penetration. American counterattacks then had to operate through narrow valleys and cultivated ground under observation from ridges held by the enemy. Units that had suffered in July were expected to assault positions rather than merely delay an advance. Marine infantry and artillery, American Army formations, engineers, and tactical aircraft contributed in different ways: infantry closed with entrenched positions, engineers restored mobility, guns suppressed ridge lines, and aircraft attacked movement and supply. Their efforts were not perfectly synchronized, and strongpoints sometimes had to be assaulted more than once.

The battle also had a Korean dimension easily obscured by a description limited to American formations. Villages near the river became observation points, defensive positions, or routes for moving wounded men. The sudden creation of a bridgehead placed residents under the control of whichever army held the nearby hills, while both sides sought guides and transport. Korean personnel supported road movement, carried supplies, and in some areas fought as attached soldiers. For students, the essential operational lesson is not that the Naktong was an impassable obstacle—it was crossed repeatedly—but that an attacking force needed to push far enough beyond the river to disrupt the defenders' rear roads before concentrated reserves could arrive. In August the KPA could establish footholds but could not convert them into decisive exploitation toward Miryang.

Section 68.15

Masan, the Southern Flank, and Task Force Kean

While the Naktong Bulge battle was developing in the center, the KPA's western hook threatened the southern end of the line. The KPA 6th Division, which had marched through the southwest after Taejon, had taken Gwangju and Suncheon, passed through the Hadong area in late July (where it ambushed an American battalion of the 29th Infantry on July 27), and taken Jinju on July 31. From Jinju it was less than 100 kilometers by road to Pusan, and the road through Masan offered a direct approach. The 6th Division's advance had been remarkably rapid and had met little organized resistance until it reached the American positions around Masan. The danger was that a KPA force in the southern corridor could threaten Pusan itself or force Walker to shift his forces from the Naktong.

Walker's response was a limited counteroffensive. Task Force Kean, named for Major General William B. Kean, commander of the 25th Division, was formed from the 25th Division's 24th and 35th Infantry Regiments, the 5th Regimental Combat Team, which had recently arrived from Hawaii, and the 1st Provisional Marine Brigade, with artillery and armor in support. The force began operations on August 7, with the aim of attacking westward from the Masan area toward Jinju to destroy the KPA 6th Division or at least to force it back and secure the southern flank. In the first days the task force advanced against stubborn resistance along the road and across the hills; the KPA fought with its usual combination of infiltration and defense in depth, and the heat and terrain slowed the Americans.

The task force did not reach Jinju. On August 12 and 13, with the KPA's own counterattacks and ambushes in the hills of the Pongam and Chindong-ni areas, and with the Naktong Bulge crisis demanding the Marines, Walker called off the operation and the Marines were withdrawn to the north. Task Force Kean ended on about August 14 with the American units back near their starting positions. In purely territorial terms it had achieved little. But it had fulfilled its purpose of disrupting the KPA 6th Division's advance, had inflicted significant losses on it, had shown that American units working together with air, artillery, and armor could take offensive action, and had kept the southern gateway to Pusan closed. The KPA's attacks in the south did not stop; they continued through August and into the September offensive.

The fighting around Masan was also a civilian catastrophe. The area was densely populated, and refugees who had fled from Jinju and the surrounding villages crowded into the zone between the lines, where they were exposed to artillery, air attack, and the movements of both armies. Villages in the corridor were damaged or destroyed, and many civilians were killed. The military significance of preventing a breakthrough along the southern coastal road was very great, since a KPA force that reached the outskirts of Pusan would have disrupted the port on which everything depended, but its price was paid by the people who lived in its path.

The southern flank posed a distinctive problem because a breakthrough near Masan would threaten the approaches to Pusan without requiring a successful crossing of the main Naktong line farther north. Hilly country, coastal roads, and valleys funneled movement toward a limited number of junctions. Task Force Kean's early-August counterattack therefore sought more than a local gain: its purpose was to disrupt the KPA formations working toward that corridor and to restore room for a mobile defense. An attacker that advanced along the road could find its flank exposed from nearby heights; a defender that captured a hill could discover that the opposing force remained on the next ridge. The campaign involved repeated attacks and counterattacks, not a single clean march forward.

The fighting revealed limitations on both sides. American infantry, armor, Marines, and artillery could inflict heavy losses, but units unfamiliar with the terrain sometimes moved on diverging routes and failed to coordinate at critical junctions. KPA troops, especially those with combat experience from China, used camouflage and infiltration to attack flanks and supply traffic; their depleted transport system nevertheless constrained how long they could maintain pressure. Korean civilians in the sector experienced shelling, searches, interrupted farming, and repeated changes of control over adjacent villages. The perimeter held in the southwest through persistent local actions and the ability to reinforce threatened units; Task Force Kean did not by itself end the danger at Masan.

Section 68.16

The Defense of Taegu, the Bowling Alley, Pohang, and the Eastern Front

The northern sector of the perimeter was held mainly by ROK forces, and its center was Taegu, a city of several hundred thousand people that served as the Eighth Army's headquarters, the ROK Army's headquarters, and the seat of the ROK government for much of the summer, until the capital moved to Pusan on August 18. Taegu lay less than 20 kilometers from the Naktong and was the hub of the railways and roads of the northern perimeter. If it fell, the perimeter's northern half would be separated from the south and the interior lines disrupted. The KPA therefore sent several divisions, the 1st, 3rd, 13th, and 15th among them, against the approaches to the city from the west and north.

West of the city, along the river near Waegwan, the 1st Cavalry Division held the Naktong line against the KPA 3rd and 1st Divisions, which crossed the river in several places in August. In the fighting here, from mid-August, Americans and Koreans fought for hills such as Hill 303, near which, on about August 17, KPA troops shot a number of captured American soldiers, an atrocity discovered by U.S. forces afterward. Chapter 71 treats such episodes on both sides in detail. North and northwest of Taegu, the road from Tabu-dong to the city, nicknamed the "Bowling Alley" by American soldiers because artillery shells fell along it like balls, was the main route by which the KPA's 13th Division, with tanks, tried to reach Taegu. The ROK 1st Division, commanded by Paik Sun-yup, held the hills around Tabu-dong, supported by American artillery and by the U.S. 27th Infantry and, later, the 23rd Infantry of the 2nd Division. In fighting through the second half of August the defenders destroyed a number of KPA tanks and inflicted heavy casualties on the attacking division, and in the following weeks the ROK 1st Division, together with American units, continued to hold the approach, though not without losses and local withdrawals.

The eastern front was held almost entirely by ROK forces. The KPA 5th Division, with the 12th Division, advanced down the coast and through the mountains toward Pohang, an east-coast port and the site of an important airfield, with the aim of breaking through to Gyeongju and the railway that led to Pusan. The ROK 3rd Division and the Capital Division fought a series of actions in the hills and along the coast. In mid-August, KPA units infiltrated into the area of Pohang, and the town was threatened. On August 11 a ROK force that included a number of student volunteers, boys in their mid-teens who had been hastily armed, defended the town against KPA attack; dozens of them died. The air force evacuated the Pohang airfield. The ROK 3rd Division, cut off by the KPA north of Pohang at Changsa-dong, was evacuated by U.S. Navy landing ships in mid-August, and the Capital Division and other units counterattacked, with the assistance of an American task force, naval gunfire, and air attack, and restored the position. The battle for the east coast continued into September.

The record of these sectors requires attention to Korean military contributions that are often understated. The ROK Army in the north and east of the perimeter had been broken in June, was rebuilt in July, and in August fought some of the war's most important defensive battles, often with fewer heavy weapons, less ammunition, and less air support than their American counterparts. Their casualties were heavy. They were also supported by a social mobilization: ROK police, youth organizations, and civilian laborers carried ammunition and supplies up steep hills on their backs, using the A-frame carriers that became a familiar image of the war. From August, some South Korean recruits were assigned to American units under the arrangement later called KATUSA (Korean Augmentation to the United States Army). The Pusan Perimeter was a combined defense, not an American line with Korean units attached.

Korean command decisions were central north of Taegu. The ROK 1st Division under Paik Sun-yup fought through August around Tabu-dong and the approaches from Waegwan, using successive hill positions to slow attacks aimed at the communications center. The fighting cannot be represented as one permanent line on a map. Positions were gained or lost at night; villages between hills might be occupied by one side while neighboring heights were held by the other. Local counterattacks often depended on Korean infantry and American guns or tanks working in support, sometimes effectively and sometimes with serious misunderstandings. When the pressure shifted toward the northeastern approaches, other ROK formations had to prevent an enemy breakthrough that could threaten the road and railway linking Taegu and Pusan.

Pohang was a different operational problem. The city and its airfield lay near the coast, where attackers could exploit narrow routes between mountains and sea. The August fighting involving ROK defenders and student volunteers entered Korean public memory as an example of improvised resistance. It is important not to let the commemorative narrative substitute for an account of military conditions: the defenders had limited training and support, and the battle took place amid a series of withdrawals and renewed attacks. In early September the eastern front again became dangerously unstable, with pressure around Yeongcheon and the road toward Gyeongju. The actions of ROK commanders, assisted by American fire support and sea-borne logistics where possible, helped prevent a local retreat from becoming a breakthrough into the rear of the whole perimeter.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The ROK Army's Performance, June to September 1950

What Changed

  • Command: new Chief of Staff, corps headquarters, and, from July 14, ROK forces under the UN commander
  • Cohesion: units reconstituted from stragglers and recruits acquired weeks of combat experience, and commanders learned which subordinates could be relied upon
  • Support: access to American artillery, air support, and supply; improvement in communications; attachment of advisers and, later, Koreans to American units
  • Defensive position: from a long frontier line that could be outflanked to a compact perimeter with mountains and rivers on its flanks

What Stayed the Same

  • A shortage of heavy weapons, antitank capability, and ammunition compared with the KPA and American units
  • The ROK Army's dependence on large numbers of infantry, frequently poorly trained
  • The determination of many officers and soldiers to resist, evident from the first day at the Imjin and Chuncheon
  • Heavy losses and, at times, the loss of positions to KPA infiltration

Pattern: The change was gradual and uneven rather than abrupt. It was most pronounced for units that had time to reorganize, such as the 1st Division, and least for those that were committed repeatedly. Those who experienced change most directly were soldiers and commanders who had been through June; greater continuity was felt by civilians and by the poorly armed recruits who filled the ranks. The pattern matters because it undermines a narrative in which the ROK Army collapsed and was replaced by American forces, and it shows how the ROK's improvement and the American reinforcement together produced the perimeter.

Division V · Sections 68.17–68.20 · August–September 14, 1950

The September Offensive and the Survival of the Perimeter

Section 68.17

Air Power, Naval Operations, Reinforcements, and the Logistics of Defense

The perimeter's survival depended on capabilities that the KPA could not match. The first was control of the air and sea. In the opening days, American fighters shot down several KPA aircraft, and within weeks the KPA air force had been largely eliminated as an effective force, leaving UN aircraft free to operate over the battlefield. The U.S. Fifth Air Force flew from bases in Japan and, increasingly, from fields inside the perimeter, using F-80 jets, F-51 Mustangs, and B-26 bombers; Marine Corsairs operated with the Marine brigade; and carrier aircraft of the U.S. Navy's Task Force 77, later joined by British carrier aircraft, struck targets from the sea. An Australian fighter squadron operated from Japan. Air power performed three tasks: close air support of troops in contact, interdiction of KPA supply lines far behind the front, and strategic attacks on rail and industrial targets in the north.

Close air support was often the difference in local battles. Forward air controllers attached to ground units called in strikes on KPA tanks, vehicles, and troop concentrations, and the effect on the KPA's ability to move in daylight was profound: by August its columns traveled mainly at night and tried to hide during the day. Interdiction attacked the roads, bridges, and rail lines along which the KPA's supplies traveled from the north, stretching a supply line that already ran several hundred kilometers from the border. The effects of interdiction were substantial but not absolute; the KPA repaired bridges, used porters and oxcarts, and moved at night. Air power was less effective in some roles than hoped. A large raid by B-29 bombers on August 16 against an area west of the Naktong, intended to destroy KPA concentrations before an expected attack, dropped a very large tonnage on a broad area; subsequent assessments found little evidence that it had hit major KPA units. Tactical air support, not area bombing, was what mattered most to the defenders.

The effect on civilians was severe. Air attack against roads, villages, and towns believed to shelter KPA troops killed and injured Korean civilians, destroyed homes, and drove people from their villages. Weapons including napalm were used in tactical strikes, and refugee columns were sometimes attacked from the air in the belief that they included enemy soldiers. These costs were part of the same military system that was saving the perimeter, and they are part of why the survival of the South was accompanied by the destruction of much of the country's built environment and a loss of civilian life that the full account in Chapter 71 explores.

Naval operations were less visible but important. The KPA navy was negligible, and early naval engagements, including the sinking of a KPA vessel carrying troops toward the southern coast by ROK and U.S. ships, ensured that the defenders could not be flanked by sea. American, British, Australian, Canadian, and other warships bombarded coastal targets and supported the east-coast fighting at Pohang and Yeongdeok, and landing ships evacuated the ROK 3rd Division from Changsa-dong. Maritime control was also the precondition for logistics: the sea lanes from Japan to Pusan were never seriously threatened.

Logistics, in fact, was a decisive asymmetry. Pusan, whose port facilities and rail connections were expanded under American direction, received a growing stream of troops, vehicles, ammunition, fuel, and food by sea from Japan and the United States. Japan served as a rear base: its ports, depots, repair shops, and workers supported the Eighth Army, and its industries repaired equipment and produced supplies. The system was not smooth. Early in the fighting there were severe shortages of ammunition, especially for artillery and antitank weapons, and of fuel and spare parts, and replacement of lost equipment was slow. Medical evacuation improved as helicopters and ambulances were organized and field hospitals established, though many wounded men waited long hours. But by late August the Americans could supply the perimeter with an adequacy that would have been impossible a month earlier.

Reinforcements accumulated through August. The 2nd Infantry Division, the 5th Regimental Combat Team, and the 1st Provisional Marine Brigade arrived in late July and August; the 1st Cavalry and 25th Divisions were brought up to strength with replacements; and tank battalions, artillery, and engineers arrived. The British 27th Infantry Brigade, drawn from Hong Kong, arrived at Pusan on August 29 and soon went into the line. Naval forces of Britain, Australia, Canada, and New Zealand were operating in Korean waters. Meanwhile ROK forces were being replenished with recruits and, increasingly, with American weapons, and a portion of them were integrated into American units. The KPA, in contrast, relied on long and vulnerable lines of communication and on replacements who were increasingly poorly trained: many of the troops sent forward in August were South Korean men conscripted in the occupied areas, who received little training and in some cases fled or deserted.

The flow of supplies was also an exercise in prioritization. A truck arriving in Pusan with ammunition was not yet a battery of guns firing at the Naktong; that ammunition had to be counted, unloaded, placed on rail or road transport, moved through depots, delivered to an artillery position, and protected from interruption. Vehicles required fuel and repairs, and combat units needed replacements with enough training to integrate into depleted companies. Engineers repaired bridges and communications, while medical units established routes for evacuation back toward the port and onward to hospitals. The narrower perimeter reduced the distances over which these services operated, which meant a reserve or a load of supplies could be moved between threatened sectors more rapidly than during the July retreat.

This organizational advantage was unevenly distributed. American and other UN units increasingly benefited from the industrial, transport, and naval resources available through Japan and the Pacific; Korean units often continued to face shortages of vehicles, radios, ammunition, and trained replacements. Shared command did not make the units interchangeable. Air attacks and naval firepower complicated KPA supply, but pilots did not always distinguish combatants from civilians, and interdiction could damage railways and villages needed for postwar recovery. The proper balance sheet of logistics includes both the growing effectiveness of the defense and the destruction that accompanied the attempt to cut enemy movement across populated country.

Section 68.18

The Great Naktong Offensive, August 31–September 1950

The KPA's leaders had hoped to complete the conquest of the South in August, and the deadline slipped repeatedly. By late August they were determined to make a final effort before the UN buildup became overwhelming. Estimates of the KPA force surrounding the perimeter at the end of August run from about 70,000 to nearly 100,000 troops, with weaker divisions than in June and many replacements, but still organized and heavily armed. The plan was a coordinated, simultaneous attack on five sectors, intended to overload the defenders' ability to shift reserves: across the Naktong Bulge toward Yongsan and Miryang; in the south toward Haman and Masan; north of Taegu from Waegwan and Tabu-dong; in the mountains toward Yeongcheon; and on the east coast toward Pohang and Gyeongju. Walker's reserves, which had saved the line in earlier crises, could not be in all places at once.

The offensive began on the night of August 31 and September 1. In the Naktong Bulge, the KPA 9th Division and the reconstituted 4th Division, along with the 2nd and 10th, crossed the river in force and broke through the line of the 2nd Division. By September 2 the KPA had penetrated several kilometers and Yongsan was threatened, with Miryang behind it. Walker committed his reserves: the Marine brigade, which had just returned from the north, and the 9th and 23rd Infantry of the 2nd Division, along with the 27th and other units. In several days of confused fighting from September 3 to 5 and after, the Marines counterattacked from the north against the KPA in the Obong-ni area, and American infantry and artillery, with heavy air support, reduced the pockets of penetration. By about September 8 the main danger was over, although isolated groups of KPA soldiers remained inside the line and fighting continued in the area.

In the south, the KPA 6th and 7th Divisions attacked toward Masan and Haman across the Nam River and through the hills, and the 25th Division, which now held the sector, was pushed back in places; Haman was lost. In the fighting around Battle Mountain and Sobuk-san, hilltop positions changed hands repeatedly, with heavy casualties on both sides. The line bent but did not break, and the KPA did not reach Masan in strength. North of Taegu, the KPA 1st, 3rd, 13th, and 15th Divisions attacked in the Waegwan and Tabu-dong sectors and in the mountains west of Gunwi. The ROK 1st Division was forced back from some of its positions but held the main approach, and a series of hilltop engagements, notably on Kasan, northwest of the city, where the KPA reached the fortress heights, kept the defenders occupied. The 1st Cavalry Division and the arriving British brigade took part in the defense; Taegu came under artillery fire but was not taken.

The crisis in the east was acute. In the first week of September, KPA forces drove the ROK units back at Yeongcheon and Gigye, the ROK line east of the mountains was penetrated, and Pohang was overrun in the first week, threatening the railway to Gyeongju, the coastal airfields, and the route to Pusan from the northeast. Walker moved a composite force from the American 24th Division and other units to the east, and the ROK Capital and 3rd Divisions rallied and counterattacked, supported by naval gunfire and air strikes. The threat to Gyeongju subsided in the second week, though the front remained unstable through September 14.

The September offensive failed for reasons the defenders and the KPA each contributed to. It failed because the defenders had reserves, interior lines, and strong artillery and air support, and used them to counterattack at every major penetration. It failed because ROK and American units in the sectors that bent did not break. And it failed because the KPA's divisions had been drained: some had been reduced to a fraction of their original strength, their tanks and heavy weapons had been destroyed, and their supplies and replacements were inadequate. By September 14 the KPA still held the initiative in parts of the perimeter and was still pressing, but it had not achieved a breakthrough, and its offensive power was visibly waning. The offensive did not end on that date; the fighting continued beyond the chapter's endpoint, and Chapter 69 resumes with the landing at Incheon the next day.

The severity of the September attacks tested whether Walker's mobile-reserve system could function under simultaneous strain. When the 2nd Infantry Division's front was pierced near the Naktong Bulge, the danger was not confined to the first hill positions: KPA movement toward Yongsan threatened the roads beyond which a penetration could split the perimeter. A reserve committed too early might be consumed before the main attack was located; one committed too late could find the supply road already lost. The Marines and other reserves were therefore shifted repeatedly under tight time pressure, and battalions fought actions whose purpose was to reopen routes or protect artillery as much as to recover every ridge immediately. The KPA's own simultaneous assaults prevented it from reinforcing every successful penetration with sufficient weight.

The northern and eastern sectors impose an important corrective to an American-centered narrative. ROK divisions and their commanders faced attacks through hills and road junctions where tanks were less useful and close infantry combat determined whether the routes toward Taegu, Yeongcheon, and Gyeongju remained open. At such points a battalion could be forced back without the division ceasing to resist. Some lost positions were recovered through improvised counterattacks and newly arrived support; others remained contested. The outcome is therefore best described by functional geography: which road remained open, whether a KPA force could reach Miryang or Gyeongju in strength, and whether the defenders retained the means to move supplies and reserves. By September 14, none of the attacks had cut the essential communications that sustained the perimeter, despite serious penetrations and immense casualties.

Section 68.19

Why the Pusan Perimeter Held: Command, Combat Power, and the Cost of Survival

By September 14 the military balance had changed in several ways. In June the KPA had had a decisive advantage in armor, artillery, and operational preparation. By September, many of its best divisions had suffered heavy casualties, most of its tanks had been destroyed or abandoned, its supply lines ran several hundred kilometers through territory exposed to air attack, and its replacements were of declining quality. The ROK Army had survived, reorganized, and acquired experience, and by early September held a large share of the perimeter. American and other UN forces had grown from a few hundred men on July 5 to many thousands; artillery, armor, and air support were abundant; and the logistical system through Pusan could sustain them. The defenders, as of early September, numbered well over 100,000 men, about half of them Korean, concentrated in an area small enough that reserves could reach any threatened point in hours.

The cost of survival was high. Casualty figures for this period are uncertain and vary by source and by category (killed, wounded, missing, captured), and record keeping was incomplete, especially for ROK and KPA forces. U.S. Army records tally several thousand killed and many more wounded and missing for the Eighth Army; ROK losses were considerably larger; and KPA losses were also very large, with several of the divisions engaged in the Naktong fighting reduced to a fraction of their original strength. Civilian deaths in these months cannot be reliably counted. Towns and villages along the Naktong, around Masan, Taegu, and Pohang, and in the corridors of the earlier fighting were damaged or destroyed. Refugees were crowded into the perimeter, and the harvest was in doubt. The perimeter's survival, in short, was bought with enormous suffering by soldiers and civilians, and it left the southeastern corner of the peninsula exhausted and dependent on outside supply.

Five related conditions explain that outcome. First, the KPA's initial advantage was real but could not be renewed at its June strength: experienced infantry and tanks were lost while the front lengthened far from the northern supply base. Second, ROK formations retained enough cohesion to defend the eastern and northern sectors that a purely American account might neglect. Third, the widening flow of allied matériel and replacements made local setbacks recoverable. Fourth, the shortened perimeter permitted Walker to concentrate artillery and transport reserves from one sector to another, although simultaneous September attacks repeatedly strained this system. Fifth, North Korean infantry could capture hills and bridgeheads but struggled to maintain an attack far beyond the river against concentrated firepower and a contested road network. None of these conditions was sufficient alone; their interaction explains the survival of the defense.

This interpretation also requires separating an army's capacity to continue fighting from the protection of the population it claimed to defend. Roads that supplied a rifle company were clogged by people fleeing the front; air interdiction that slowed an advancing division could destroy civilian transport; searches intended to prevent infiltration could treat families as threats. Civilian losses do not appear in the same returns as infantry casualties, and some survivors were not able to testify publicly until decades later. A campaign history should not present the containment of an invasion as a purely technical success detached from those human consequences. Nor should it equate all forms of violence simply because civilians suffered from several armies: responsibility must be examined through identifiable actions, orders, and evidence.

The difference between July's retreat and September's defensive survival was a change in the balance of resources and military organization, not a sudden disappearance of KPA tactical ability. The enemy could still mount damaging attacks; Walker still had to make choices under uncertainty; and ROK regiments had to hold vulnerable ground with heavy losses. What the perimeter provided was time. Time enabled the assembly of a combined army, the restoration of Korean units, and an increasingly dependable supply system. That gain must be distinguished from the offensive decision taken next. As of September 14 the defenders had prevented military collapse in the southeast, but had not yet reversed North Korea's conquest of most of the peninsula. The change in the war's operational direction begins with the events assigned to Chapter 69.

Analysis: As a contemporary institutional source produced by neutral observers rather than by a belligerent, the report was an important element in the international assessment of responsibility for the invasion. It documents the early view of UN representatives who had been in Korea and records what they had seen along the border. It complements the Soviet and Korean archival evidence that became available later, which supports its general conclusion.

Limitations: Its institutional perspective and limited access must be disclosed. The Commission had no access to North Korea, and its observers could inspect only some sectors of the border for limited periods. It was composed of representatives of governments with their own positions, and the report was prepared for a General Assembly in which the Cold War divided members. It is evidence of what UN observers believed and reported, but it cannot by itself establish decision-making in Pyongyang, Moscow, or Beijing, nor the course of the later fighting, which it covers only partly.

Perspective: The report represents the perspective of the UN's observers and member governments. It does not represent the perspective of the DPRK, which rejected the Commission's legitimacy, nor of ordinary Koreans whose experience of the early weeks is largely absent from it.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Pusan Perimeter Hold?

The question: Historians agree that the perimeter held, that it was attacked repeatedly and held in September, and that the KPA did not achieve the conquest it sought. They disagree about how much weight to give each of several contributing factors.

Korean resistance: Korean-centered accounts emphasize that ROK divisions defended roughly half the perimeter, delayed the KPA from the first day, and fought with fewer weapons than their allies. On this view, accounts that foreground American command and firepower underrepresent the army that held the north and east.
American command decisions: Some accounts credit Walker's decision to stand and his skillful use of mobile reserves, together with MacArthur's strategic direction and the commitment of the Marine brigade, as decisive. Critics argue that his reputation has been inflated by the outcome and that he benefited from circumstances that he did not create.
Logistics and air power: Other historians emphasize the asymmetry of supply, in which Pusan, Japan, and the sea lanes sustained the defenders, while UN air power attrited the KPA's supply lines and its tanks. On this view, material superiority became decisive once the perimeter shortened the line.
KPA operational limits: A further explanation stresses the KPA's own weaknesses: casualties among its best troops, long supply lines, the pause after Seoul, the dispersion of forces in the southwest, the use of untrained replacements, and the plan's dependence on a rapid victory.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, including U.S. Army operational histories, ROK military histories, memoirs, captured KPA documents, and Soviet archival material. They differ partly because the sources are unevenly distributed: American records are the most detailed and Korean and KPA records the least accessible to English-language readers.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is chiefly one of interpretation and weighting, not of basic chronology. It also involves counterfactual reasoning: whether a more rapid KPA advance in early July could have reached Pusan, and whether the perimeter could have held without the Marines or without air power.

Why it matters: How historians weight these factors shapes who receives credit for the survival of the ROK and what lessons are drawn: a story about American resolve and airpower implies one set of lessons, while a story emphasizing Korean resilience and KPA overextension implies another. This textbook's own view is that none of these explanations is sufficient alone and that they reinforced one another.

Student task: Select the factor you find most important and identify the evidence that supports it and the evidence that complicates it. Then explain whether the perimeter would have held if that factor had been absent, and what additional sources you would need to be confident.

The survival of the perimeter did not mean that the war was resolved. On September 14 the KPA still held most of South Korea, still pressed the defenders in several sectors, and still held the initiative in parts of the line. The defenders were exhausted; casualties had been heavy; and Walker's forces, though stronger, were not in a position to break out of the perimeter by themselves. What had been achieved was a precarious equilibrium: the KPA had won a series of rapid victories but had failed to achieve its central objective before the defenders could reorganize and reinforce. Between June and September the war had been transformed from a rapid conquest into a stalemate at the southeastern corner of the peninsula. The next operation, the amphibious landing at Incheon on September 15, examined in Chapter 69, would break that equilibrium and begin a new phase of the war.

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This section provides the summary, review apparatus, and source register for the chapter. It does not introduce new narrative material.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the KPA's offensive of June 25, 1950, brought the Republic of Korea close to destruction, and why the combined ROK and UN defense of the Pusan Perimeter prevented that outcome. Its argument is that the opening campaign was shaped by four interacting forces: North Korean operational preparation and initial superiority; South Korean defensive weakness; the unexpected difficulties of American intervention; and the enormous effort required to organize a defense in the southeastern corner of the peninsula.

The invasion was a coordinated offensive on several axes, planned with Soviet assistance and led by armored columns down the Uijeongbu corridor. It differed in scale and kind from the border clashes of the previous year, and responsibility for launching it rests with the DPRK, whatever the historiographical debate about the longer origins of the conflict. The KPA's advantages in tanks, artillery, and experienced troops met an ROK Army that had no tanks, no effective antitank weapons, and a command structure that was caught partly unprepared.

Seoul fell on June 28 after the failure of ROK counterattacks at Uijeongbu and after the demolition of the Han River bridges, which cut off soldiers and trapped civilians. Estimates of those killed by the demolition range from several hundred to more than a thousand, and responsibility for the order remains disputed. Yet the ROK Army survived: units such as the 1st Division withdrew in order, the 6th Division delayed the KPA at Chuncheon, and the army reorganized south of the river. The idea that Americans arrived to defend a country with no army is a misreading of the record.

The international response proceeded in distinct steps. Resolution 82 condemned the attack, Resolution 83 recommended assistance, and Resolution 84 recommended a unified command under the United States. Truman ordered air and naval support on June 27 and authorized ground forces on June 30. The resulting UN Command was an American-led coalition, and the ROK Army was placed under MacArthur's operational command on July 14.

The first American ground forces were defeated at Osan on July 5 and in the delaying actions at Chonan, Chochiwon, and the Kum River. Task Force Smith's failure reflected equipment, training, force structure, and the effectiveness of KPA combined-arms tactics, not just the performance of individuals. At Taejon, on July 19–20, the 24th Division suffered heavy losses but bought time for reinforcements. General Dean's capture, five weeks after the battle, must be distinguished from the battle itself.

Meanwhile ROK divisions fought in the central mountains and on the east coast, and the terrain, which constrained armor and offered few routes, shaped the line on which the defenders finally stood. The retreat displaced hundreds of thousands of civilians, exposed them to violence from all armies, and, at No Gun Ri, led to the documented killing of refugees by American soldiers, a case whose existence is established though its numbers and the question of orders remain disputed.

The decision to hold, expressed by Walker at the end of July, and the withdrawal behind the Naktong in early August created the Pusan Perimeter. The defenders used interior lines, mobile reserves, and the port of Pusan to meet each KPA crossing or breakthrough: the First Battle of the Naktong Bulge, Task Force Kean at Masan, the Bowling Alley and Tabu-dong near Taegu, and the defense of Pohang. ROK forces held about half of the line and played a central role in the north and east.

Air power, naval control, and logistics gave the defenders growing advantages that the KPA, with long, exposed supply lines and depleted divisions, could not match. The KPA's five-sector offensive of August 31 and September 1 created crises at the Naktong Bulge, Haman, the Taegu approaches, and Pohang, but each was contained by reserves and counterattacks. The perimeter held at heavy cost to soldiers and civilians.

Historians weigh the reasons differently. Korean resistance, American command decisions, logistics and air power, and KPA operational limits all contributed, and no single factor is sufficient. This textbook's view is that they reinforced one another. The significance of the outcome is also bounded: by September 14 the perimeter had survived but the war had not been decided, and the Incheon landing of the following day, examined in Chapter 69, would transform the situation.

Chapter Reflection

The campaign of June to September 1950 matters because it determined that the Korean War would be a long war and not a short conquest. Had the KPA reached Pusan in July, the Republic of Korea might have ceased to exist as a state and the history of the peninsula would have taken a different course. That it did not is the result of a combination of contingent circumstances, Korean resistance, and the capacity of an outside coalition to reinforce, and each deserves to be remembered in its own right.

The chapter also reveals how a civil conflict between two Korean states became an international war within a week, and how the resulting structure, an American-led command with a subordinate ROK Army, shaped the war and the postwar alliance. It shows, too, that armies and governments make decisions under uncertainty and with incomplete information, from the Han River bridges to the refugee policies of July, and that their consequences fall most heavily on civilians.

Later developments built directly on these months. The perimeter became the base for the Incheon landing and the breakout examined in Chapter 69; the pattern of refugee flight, suspicion, and violence anticipated the humanitarian catastrophe examined in Chapter 71; and the memory of the summer, including the capital's loss, the bridge, and the siege of the southeast, became part of the political culture of both Korean states.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter exemplifies the commitment to treat Korea as the organizing historical subject. The story of 1950 is often told as an episode in American or Cold War history. It is also, and first, the story of Koreans, soldiers and civilians, in the north and the south, whose decisions, sacrifices, and suffering shaped the outcome.
