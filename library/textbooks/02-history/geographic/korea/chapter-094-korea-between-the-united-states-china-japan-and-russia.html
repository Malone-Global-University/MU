Korea between the United States, China, Japan, and Russia

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Three treaties, signed across seven decades, frame the strategic condition of the divided peninsula: a 1953 defense pact between Washington and Seoul, a pair of 1961 treaties binding Pyongyang to Moscow and Beijing, and a 2024 treaty that revived and extended the Russian–North Korean relationship in the midst of a European war. Each document reflects the interests of great powers. Each also reflects the calculations of a Korean government that sought something for itself.

When the Korean armistice was signed in July 1953, neither Korean state controlled the terms on which the peace would be kept. The agreement was negotiated by military commanders of the United Nations Command, the Korean People's Army, and the Chinese People's Volunteers, and the Republic of Korea refused to sign it. What followed was a security order in which the two Korean governments sought guarantees from outside patrons, and in which those patrons, in turn, sought influence through the Koreas. The first Korean decades are often summarized as a Cold War in which a southern client of the United States faced a northern client of the Soviet Union and China. That summary captures something real but also hides a great deal, including the repeated efforts of Syngman Rhee, Park Chung-hee, and Kim Il Sung to bargain with, resist, and exploit the powers that protected them.

This chapter examines how both Korean states operate within the competing security, economic, and diplomatic interests of the United States, China, Japan, and Russia, and how each Korean government has tried to shape those relationships for its own purposes. It is a chapter about relations rather than about the foreign countries themselves. Korea remains the historical subject throughout. The United States, China, Japan, and Russia enter the narrative where their interests intersect with those of Seoul and Pyongyang, and the chapter asks in each case what the Korean government wanted, what it could realistically obtain, and what it paid for it.

The chapter's central argument is that the peninsula's geopolitical position in 2026 is shaped by two distinct relationships rather than one bipolar confrontation. South Korea combines a military alliance with the United States, which retains roughly 28,500 personnel in the country, with deep economic integration across East Asia; China has long been its largest trading partner. North Korea, whose economic options are narrower, has relied on a changing dependence on China and, since 2022, an increasingly substantial military partnership with Russia, formalized in the June 2024 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty. These are not mirror images. They differ in legal form, in institutional depth, in public transparency, and in the degree of agency each Korean government exercises within them.

A second argument concerns Korean agency. It is tempting to describe the peninsula as an object of great-power competition, and in some respects it has been exactly that. But South Korea's semiconductor industry, shipbuilding capacity, and defense-export sector give Seoul bargaining power that earlier generations of South Korean leaders lacked. North Korea's nuclear arsenal, its stockpiles of artillery ammunition, and its willingness to send soldiers abroad have altered the calculations of much larger states. The historian's task is to avoid two opposite errors: treating Korean governments as puppets of patrons, and treating them as free of structural constraint.

The evidence for this chapter is uneven, and students should read it with that unevenness in mind. For the earlier decades, treaties, communiqués, diplomatic papers, and memoirs permit detailed reconstruction. For the 2020s, historians rely on official statements, congressional and parliamentary testimony, government research reports, trade statistics, satellite imagery, the findings of international monitoring bodies, and the claims of intelligence services, some of which cannot be independently verified. Where this chapter relies on assessments rather than documented facts, it says so. The chapter's fixed endpoint is the documented record available by November 14, 2026; subsequent developments belong to a future revised edition.

The chapter has five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the military and institutional foundation of the U.S.–South Korean alliance. Division II asks how South Korea balances that alliance against economic integration with China. Division III explains why Japan and South Korea, despite unresolved disputes over colonial memory, have cooperated on security. Division IV restores North Korea's foreign policy to the center of the narrative through its relations with China and Russia. Division V analyzes the geopolitical reordering of the 2020s. Chapter 93 treats inter-Korean negotiations and the collapse of détente, Chapter 95 treats South Korea's domestic political crisis, and Chapter 96 offers the series' final synthesis; this chapter concentrates on the external alliances and strategic relationships that frame all three.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Korean peninsula has mattered strategically to the United States, China, Japan, and Russia, while distinguishing strategic location from geographic determinism.
  2. Trace the development of the U.S.–South Korean alliance from the 1953 Mutual Defense Treaty to the alliance "modernization" debates of 2025–2026.
  3. Describe the main institutions of allied defense, including U.S. Forces Korea, the Combined Forces Command, the United Nations Command, and the unresolved question of wartime operational control.
  4. Distinguish extended deterrence, conventional deterrence, and missile defense, and explain how the 2023 Washington Declaration altered allied consultation.
  5. Analyze the tension between South Korea's security dependence on the United States and its economic interdependence with China, using the THAAD controversy as a case study.
  6. Assess how colonial memory and security cooperation coexist in South Korean–Japanese relations from 1965 to the Camp David framework.
  7. Compare the legal and political character of the China–North Korea and Russia–North Korea relationships, from the 1961 treaties to 2026.
  8. Evaluate why economic dependence on China has not translated into Chinese control of North Korean policy.
  9. Distinguish independently corroborated facts about North Korean arms and troop transfers from intelligence assessments and unverified claims.
  10. Evaluate the "two triangles" model of Northeast Asian security and explain what it clarifies and obscures.
  11. Interpret primary and government documents, identifying purpose, audience, and limitations.
  12. Connect the external relationships examined here to the Korean developments traced in Chapters 85–93.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what sense has the Korean peninsula been a "strategic" place, and why does that description not by itself explain what Korean governments have chosen to do?
  2. How did South Korea's relationship with the United States change as South Korea grew from an aid recipient into an industrial and technological power?
  3. Why is wartime operational control a question of sovereignty as well as military organization?
  4. Does economic interdependence with China constrain South Korean strategic choices, enlarge them, or do both at different moments?
  5. Why have South Korea and Japan been able to cooperate on missile warning while disputes over forced labor, sexual slavery, and territory remain unresolved?
  6. Why has China, despite being North Korea's largest trading partner, been unable to dictate North Korean nuclear and missile policy?
  7. What explains North Korea's decision to move closer to Russia after 2022, and what did each government expect to gain?
  8. How should historians treat claims about secret military transfers that governments assert but cannot always prove?
  9. Is it accurate to describe Northeast Asia as divided into two opposing blocs, and what evidence supports or complicates that picture?

Historical Context

The chapters preceding this one have established the main contours that this chapter assumes. Chapters 64 through 72 explained how liberation in 1945 produced Soviet and American occupation zones, how two rival governments were founded in 1948, and how the Korean War of 1950–1953 drew in American-led United Nations forces on one side and Chinese forces, with Soviet air and material support, on the other. Chapters 73 through 78 traced the parallel construction of rival Korean states, including the South Korean developmental state under Park Chung-hee and the North Korean order built around Kim Il Sung and Juche. Chapters 85 and 86 examined the Sunshine Policy and the emergence of nuclear North Korea, and Chapter 93 followed the summit diplomacy of 2018–2019 and the collapse of inter-Korean détente. Chapter 88 treated South Korean–Japanese cultural exchange and memory disputes at greater length than this chapter will.

The Cold War settlement on the peninsula rested on two asymmetric alliance structures. In the south, the United States signed a bilateral defense treaty in 1953 and kept a large military presence, with Seoul's dependence on American forces, finance, and markets shaping South Korean politics for decades. In the north, Pyongyang signed treaties of friendship and mutual assistance with the Soviet Union and China in July 1961, during the Sino-Soviet split, and worked skillfully to extract support from both rivals while preserving political autonomy. These structures were never frozen. Each changed with the diplomatic opening of the 1970s, with South Korea's industrialization, with the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, and with the end of the Cold War in Europe, which allowed Seoul to establish diplomatic relations with Moscow in 1990 and Beijing in 1992 while Pyongyang faced the loss of its Soviet patron.

Geography frames but does not dictate this history. The peninsula's northern frontier follows the Amnok (Yalu) and Duman (Tumen) rivers; the border with China runs roughly 1,400 kilometers, while a very short stretch of the lower Tumen separates North Korea from Russia. To the west lies the Yellow Sea and the Chinese coast, to the south and east the Korea Strait and the East Sea (Sea of Japan), beyond which lie Japan and its American-hosted installations. The position gives each neighbor reasons for interest. It does not tell any Korean government whom to ally with. South Korea after 1992 combined an alliance with the United States and trade with China; North Korea in the 1960s balanced between Moscow and Beijing and in the 2020s has reconstructed a bilateral military relationship with Russia while repairing ties with China. Students should read each alignment as a political choice made under constraint, not a geographic inevitability.

Division I

The United States and the Security of South Korea

The first four sections establish the military and institutional foundation of the U.S.–South Korean relationship: why the peninsula matters, how the alliance was built, how American forces are organized, and how deterrence is understood.

Section 94.1

The Korean Peninsula in the Strategic Order of Northeast Asia

The peninsula matters to its neighbors for different reasons, and understanding those reasons separately is the starting point for understanding why no outside power has been indifferent to Korea. For China, the peninsula is a buffer and a border: a shared frontier of roughly 1,400 kilometers along the Amnok and Duman rivers, a coastline on the Yellow Sea facing China's most densely populated and industrial regions, and a place where American military power, if unconstrained, might come close to Chinese territory. In the twentieth century, Beijing intervened in the Korean War in part because it feared hostile forces on the Yalu. China's modern interest in North Korea's stability, and its wariness of unification under an American-allied government, trace to this history.

For Japan, the peninsula has historically been regarded as a potential avenue of threat toward the archipelago, a view embedded in the strategic thinking of the late nineteenth century and in the Japanese empire's decision to colonize Korea, examined in Chapters 53 and 54. In the twenty-first century the concern is narrower and more concrete: North Korean ballistic missiles that fly over or toward Japanese waters, and the vulnerability of the U.S. installations in Japan that would support any Korean contingency. For the United States, the peninsula is a place where a treaty commitment, a forward military presence, and a major trading partner converge, and where credibility toward allies elsewhere in Asia is tested. For Russia, the Korean interest is both older and more limited: a short frontier on the lower Tumen near Vladivostok, a historic Soviet role in North Korea's founding, and, since 2022, an opportunity to obtain munitions and manpower.

The two Korean states weigh these concerns very differently. South Korea is a maritime trading economy whose prosperity depends on open sea lanes, global supply chains, and access to Chinese, American, Japanese, and other markets. Its leaders have tended to treat security and commerce as separate ledgers that nonetheless intersect, as the THAAD case in Section 94.7 shows. North Korea, with a far smaller economy and a political system built around regime survival, has treated its own military capability as the primary instrument for obtaining security, status, and leverage. Its leaders have repeatedly sought patrons who would provide material support without demanding political subordination.

It is important to distinguish strategic location from determinism. The idea that Korea's position "condemned" it to be a victim of larger neighbors has long been popular, but it mistakes a condition for a cause. The same geography produced very different outcomes in different periods: the independent Goguryeo and Silla, examined in earlier volumes, played their neighbors against one another with considerable success; the Joseon dynasty maintained a long and stable tributary relationship with Ming and Qing China; the late nineteenth century saw collapse under combined pressure; and the late twentieth century saw South Korea turn a vulnerable position into an industrial and technological one. What changed was not the map but the balance of capabilities, institutions, and political choices. This chapter therefore treats geography as a set of constraints and opportunities, not as a script.

Historians reconstruct the strategic order of the peninsula from several kinds of evidence: treaties and diplomatic communiqués, force-posture data released by governments, military doctrine documents, trade statistics, official testimony, and the memoirs of participants. Each has limits. Treaties describe commitments but not the way they are interpreted; force-posture numbers change with each administration; trade data can be incomplete, especially for North Korea; and intelligence assessments, though influential, are not always verifiable. Students should keep these distinctions in mind as the chapter proceeds from the alliance system to the more opaque relationships of North Korea.

Section 94.2

The U.S.–South Korea Alliance: From the Korean War to the Twenty-First Century

The U.S.–Republic of Korea Mutual Defense Treaty was signed on October 1, 1953, a little more than two months after the armistice, and entered into force in November 1954. It was not an act of simple American generosity. Syngman Rhee, the South Korean president, had opposed the armistice because it left the peninsula divided, and Washington, worried that he might resume fighting unilaterally, used the promise of a treaty as part of a broader effort to secure South Korean acquiescence. The treaty can therefore be read as both a guarantee to Seoul and a restraint on Seoul, a duality that has recurred in the alliance ever since.

The treaty is notably brief. Its operative provisions commit each party to consult when either believes its political independence or security is threatened by external armed attack, and each to act, in the event of an armed attack on territories under the other's administrative control, to meet the common danger "in accordance with its constitutional processes." That language is weaker than the automatic commitments of some other alliances; it leaves space for each government's legislature and political process. Another article grants the United States the right to station land, air, and sea forces in and about South Korean territory. The legal basis for the American military presence in the country therefore rests on this treaty and on supplementary status-of-forces arrangements negotiated later.

Over the following decades, the alliance's meaning changed as South Korea changed. In the 1950s and 1960s, Seoul depended on American aid, American arms, and American markets, and Washington exercised substantial influence over South Korean politics, as Chapters 74 and 75 showed. South Korean leaders feared two opposite dangers: abandonment, if American commitment weakened, as many believed it did after President Nixon's announcement of troop reductions in 1969–1971 and the withdrawal of an American infantry division; and entrapment, if American policy toward North Korea or China drew South Korea into conflicts it did not want. The same two anxieties, in different forms, shaped the debates of 2025–2026. Park Chung-hee's response to the 1970s reductions, which included a secret nuclear-weapons research effort that American pressure ultimately halted, illustrates how a South Korean government could act on its own assessment of risk.

By the late twentieth century South Korea was a middle-income, then high-income, economy, a democracy after 1987, and a contributor to American-led operations abroad, including deployments to Vietnam in the 1960s and 1970s and later to Iraq and Afghanistan. The alliance moved from patron–client toward a more reciprocal relationship, though never an equal one in military capability. The relationship also widened beyond defense to include trade, technology, and, by the 2020s, shipbuilding and semiconductors. The Congressional Research Service's September 2026 assessment describes the alliance as being "modernized" by the Trump and Lee governments in several respects, including a broader regional mission, higher South Korean defense spending, and changed command arrangements, topics examined in Sections 94.3 and 94.5.

The alliance has always been a political construction, not a mechanical device. American presidents have supported it for varying reasons: containment of communism, protection of regional credibility, or access to a major industrial partner. South Korean presidents have also treated it differently, with progressive governments tending to emphasize autonomy and engagement with Pyongyang and conservative governments emphasizing alignment with Washington, though this generalization has many exceptions. Students should avoid assuming that the alliance has been unchanging, or that any single South Korean political camp has owned it.

Section 94.3

American Forces in Korea: Bases, Command Structures, and Military Readiness

United States Forces Korea (USFK) comprises approximately 28,500 personnel, a figure that has been broadly stable for years and that is fixed in practice by American law and political commitments as much as by operational need. The command's principal base is Camp Humphreys, in Pyeongtaek, south of Seoul, to which USFK headquarters relocated from the Yongsan garrison in central Seoul in 2018 as part of a consolidation agreed decades earlier. The move placed the main American presence farther from the capital and from the DMZ, reduced the footprint of foreign forces in a densely populated city, and responded to long-standing South Korean public concerns about land use, crime, and sovereignty. Other American installations, including air bases at Osan and Gunsan, complement the ground presence.

Three overlapping command structures organize the allied defense. The United Nations Command, established in 1950, is a legacy of the war; it is responsible for the armistice, the military demarcation line, and the Military Armistice Commission, and several nations have maintained liaison officers in it. The Combined Forces Command (CFC), created in 1978, integrates American and South Korean forces under a binational headquarters and is the operative wartime command. And USFK itself is the American sub-unified command. The same American four-star general ordinarily holds all three positions, which is both an administrative convenience and a political sensitivity in Seoul.

Routine allied readiness depends on regular exercises. The best-known are combined command-post and field exercises held in spring and late summer, supplemented by air and naval drills. North Korea has long denounced them as rehearsals for invasion; the allies describe them as defensive. Exercises have also been political instruments. They were scaled back or reshaped during the 2018–2019 diplomacy described in Chapter 93, restored in subsequent years under Yoon Suk Yeol, and, according to the Congressional Research Service, curtailed again by President Trump in 2026 in an apparent overture to North Korea, without prior discussion with Seoul. That episode, if the CRS description is accurate, illustrates how readiness decisions that appear technical may carry diplomatic meaning and may produce friction between allies.

Behind these arrangements lies the issue of wartime operational control, abbreviated OPCON. In 1994, peacetime operational control of South Korean forces returned to Seoul, but wartime control remained with the CFC, led by an American general. Washington and Seoul have been preparing since 2006 to transfer wartime control to a binational command led by a South Korean general with an American deputy; the date has been repeatedly postponed and the process is now tied to agreed conditions rather than a fixed calendar. According to the CRS, South Korea reportedly considers targeting 2028, while USFK has said the allies' roadmap points to the first quarter of 2029. The Lee government has sought an expedited transfer.

OPCON is both a technical military matter and a question of sovereignty. Technically, it concerns command relationships, intelligence capabilities, communications, and the capacity of South Korean forces to lead complex combined operations. Politically, it concerns whether a country's armed forces would, in war, answer to a foreign commander, a question that historically resonated with South Koreans who remembered the period after 1950 when command authority was transferred to the United Nations Command. Supporters of early transfer argue that South Korea is capable and that sovereignty demands it. Critics, often more cautious, argue that the existing combined structure provides deterrence and that premature change could weaken it. A related question, strategic flexibility, concerns whether American forces in Korea may be used for contingencies beyond the peninsula; a 2006 joint statement acknowledged South Korea's understanding of the American need for it, and the Trump administration's emphasis on China has renewed the debate.

Historical Vocabulary
Operational Control (OPCON) and the Combined Forces Command

Definition: Operational control is the authority to direct forces in order to accomplish assigned missions; it is narrower than full command. The Combined Forces Command is the binational headquarters, established in 1978, that exercises wartime operational control over designated American and South Korean forces.

Historical context: Command authority over South Korean forces passed to a United Nations commander in 1950. Peacetime operational control returned to South Korea in 1994, but wartime control remained with the combined structure. Transfer has been negotiated repeatedly since 2006.

Why it matters: OPCON links military effectiveness with national sovereignty, and its timing has served as a measure of alliance maturity.

Potential misunderstanding: OPCON transfer would not end the alliance or require American forces to leave. It would change which country's general leads combined forces in wartime.

Section 94.4

Extended Deterrence, the Nuclear Umbrella, and South Korean Defense Policy

Chapter 86 narrated North Korea's nuclear development. This section examines how the alliance responded: not the weapons, but the institutions of deterrence. Deterrence means discouraging an adversary from acting by threatening unacceptable consequences. Extended deterrence means a nuclear-armed state's commitment to deter attacks on its allies, often called the "nuclear umbrella." South Korea has been under such an umbrella since the 1950s. American tactical nuclear weapons were stationed in South Korea until 1991, when President George H. W. Bush announced their global withdrawal; since then the commitment has rested on strategic forces and declaratory assurances.

As North Korea's arsenal expanded and its missiles became more capable, South Korean confidence in these assurances weakened, particularly during periods when American politics seemed unsettled. Opinion polling in the 2020s showed substantial South Korean public support for an independent nuclear capability, though support varied with how questions were worded and with the costs presented. Government leaders have formally maintained South Korea's commitment to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, but the debate itself became part of alliance diplomacy: the American concern is that doubts in Seoul could translate into proliferation; the South Korean concern is that American assurances need to be credible, visible, and consultative.

The April 2023 Washington Declaration was the alliance's response. Issued by the American and South Korean presidents, it created a Nuclear Consultative Group for planning and information-sharing, pledged enhanced visibility of American strategic assets in the region, and reaffirmed that a North Korean nuclear attack on South Korea would be met with a swift, overwhelming response. In exchange, South Korea reaffirmed its non-proliferation commitments. A 2024 set of joint guidelines for nuclear operations and planning gave the arrangement more detail. For South Korea, the value was less the American pledge, which was not new, than the institutional mechanism: a standing process for consultation and integration of conventional and nuclear planning.

Deterrence also includes conventional and defensive components. South Korea has built a large conventional force and a layered missile-defense and strike architecture, including its own long-range ballistic missiles, a doctrine commonly summarized as preemptive and retaliatory capabilities, and Patriot-class and domestically developed air-defense systems, alongside the American-operated THAAD battery discussed in Section 94.7. South Korea's defense industry, which exports artillery, aircraft, and armored vehicles, has become an economic and diplomatic asset as well as a security one. The Lee government has pledged to raise defense spending from 2.3 percent of gross domestic product in 2025 toward 3.5 percent by 2035, according to the CRS. Disputes continue about whether higher spending, or particular capabilities, would better serve deterrence.

Two recent developments show how the nuclear question has widened. First, according to the CRS, the Lee government has sought American support for South Korean acquisition of nuclear-powered submarines, which the Senate Armed Services Committee has supported, and for peaceful uranium enrichment and spent-fuel reprocessing; negotiations on the details have reportedly been delayed by other tensions in 2026. Second, South Korean analysts continue to debate whether a more capable allied conventional force, a stronger consultative framework, or a national nuclear option best answers a nuclear-armed North Korea. Students should note that a debate about an option is not a decision to pursue it, and that official South Korean policy has remained non-nuclear.

Historical Vocabulary
Extended Deterrence and the "Nuclear Umbrella"

Definition: Extended deterrence is a state's pledge to deter attacks against an allied state by threatening retaliation, including, in the case of nuclear powers, nuclear retaliation. "Nuclear umbrella" is a popular synonym.

Historical context: South Korea has been protected by American extended deterrence since the Cold War. After 1991, American nuclear weapons were no longer stationed on the peninsula, and the pledge rested on strategic forces. The 2023 Washington Declaration added consultative machinery.

Why it matters: The credibility of extended deterrence depends partly on allied confidence, so consultation and visible commitment are themselves instruments of deterrence.

Potential misunderstanding: The Washington Declaration did not give South Korea control over American nuclear weapons or station them in South Korea.

Division II

South Korea between Washington and Beijing

The next four sections examine how South Korea has combined military dependence on the United States with economic integration with China, and how successive governments have tried to protect national autonomy within both relationships.

Section 94.5

Alliance Costs, Strategic Autonomy, and U.S.–South Korean Economic Negotiations

Alliances have costs, and the question of who bears them has been a recurring theme. South Korea contributes to the upkeep of American forces through periodic Special Measures Agreements, which cover a share of local labor, construction, and logistics costs. Negotiations became unusually contentious during President Trump's first term, when Washington demanded a very large increase, and a delayed agreement in 2021 raised the contribution by a smaller margin. Trump has since continued to describe South Korean payments as inadequate. South Korean officials respond that Seoul already provides land, infrastructure, and the financing of most of Camp Humphreys' construction, and that it buys large quantities of American weapons. The numbers are contested because each side counts different categories; students should be wary of any single figure presented without definition.

Economic bargaining has become at least as consequential as defense bargaining. The Korea–U.S. Free Trade Agreement (KORUS FTA), in force since 2012, eliminated virtually all tariffs between the two economies and is, by value, the second-largest U.S. trade agreement. In 2025 South Korea was the eighth-largest U.S. goods trading partner, according to the CRS, and the United States was South Korea's second-largest trading partner. After 2025 the Trump administration imposed tariffs on South Korean goods under several legal authorities. In the fall of 2025 the two governments concluded a trade and investment deal under which the United States lowered certain tariffs, including on automobiles, to 15 percent, and South Korea pledged $350 billion in investment: $150 billion directed toward the American shipbuilding industry and the rest toward industries approved by the American president, with annual investment capped at $20 billion.

The agreement did not end the friction. The CRS reports that in February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled that the president lacked authority to impose certain country-specific tariffs; the administration then used other authorities, including a temporary global tariff that expired in July 2026 and, in July, a 12.5 percent tariff on most South Korean goods justified by a finding about forced-labor imports, while an additional investigation remains open. Steel and aluminum tariffs remained at 50 percent. In January 2026 the president threatened to raise tariffs to 25 percent because of the pace of South Korean investment. Another strain arose from the September 2025 immigration raid on a Hyundai plant in Georgia, after which the Lee government negotiated improved visa conditions for Korean workers. Disputes about South Korean digital regulation, including an investigation of the American-based company Coupang, also troubled the relationship. These details are likely to change, and students should treat the 2026 summary as a snapshot.

Lee Jae-myung, elected in June 2025, has framed alliance modernization in terms of sovereignty. According to the CRS, he appears to have used American demands as opportunities to obtain support for an expedited OPCON transfer, nuclear-powered submarines, and enrichment and reprocessing rights for peaceful purposes. Washington, for its part, has sought to widen the alliance's mission toward regional challenges, including China; the 2026 National Defense Strategy, the CRS notes, calls for South Korea to take "primary responsibility" for its own defense with more limited American support. Tensions also arose in 2026 over Washington's request that South Korea help reopen the Strait of Hormuz during the U.S.–Iran conflict; South Korea, which relies on the Middle East for well over half its oil, said it was reviewing the matter while introducing emergency economic measures.

This episode illustrates South Korean agency. A government that in the 1960s could do little but accept American terms now bargains over shipbuilding, semiconductors, and submarines, bringing industrial capacity to the table. It also illustrates constraints: the security dependence that remains, the exposure of an export economy to American tariffs, and the domestic politics of any concession. Whether the outcome will strengthen the alliance by making it more reciprocal, or weaken it by making it more transactional, is an open question that this chapter returns to in Section 94.20.

Section 94.6

South Korea and China: Diplomatic Normalization and Economic Integration

South Korea and the People's Republic of China fought on opposite sides in the Korean War and had no formal relations for four decades. Their normalization on August 24, 1992 was the product of several converging developments: Seoul's "Nordpolitik," a policy begun under Roh Tae-woo of opening relations with communist states; Beijing's economic reform and need for investment, trade, and technology; the Soviet collapse; and the 1988 Seoul Olympics, which demonstrated South Korean capability to socialist governments. In the joint communiqué, South Korea recognized the People's Republic as the sole legal government of China and took note of its position on Taiwan, and ended official relations with the Republic of China on Taiwan. Pyongyang, which had expected its allies to withhold recognition, regarded the step as a betrayal.

Economic integration followed quickly. Bilateral trade grew from a few billion dollars in 1992 to hundreds of billions annually by the 2010s and 2020s, making China South Korea's largest trading partner. South Korean firms invested heavily in Chinese manufacturing, including electronics, automobiles, and semiconductors; Chinese students and tourists became prominent in Korean universities and shopping districts; and Chinese consumers became the audience for Korean cultural exports, discussed in Chapters 89 and 90. The relationship was asymmetrical in some ways, since South Korea's dependence on the Chinese market was proportionally greater than Chinese dependence on South Korea. But it was also complementary: South Korean intermediate goods fed Chinese assembly industries that served global markets.

Normalization transformed Seoul's international position. South Korea could now pursue diplomacy with the continental power adjacent to North Korea, could seek Chinese influence over Pyongyang, and could broaden its trade beyond Japan and the United States. Presidents Kim Dae-jung, Roh Moo-hyun, and Moon Jae-in each emphasized the importance of Chinese cooperation for peninsular peace. Yet normalization did not alter the American alliance, and South Korean leaders found themselves repeatedly explaining to each partner why they were dealing with the other. The expression sometimes used in Seoul, that South Korea relied on the United States for security and on China for the economy, captures a real pattern but oversimplifies it, since South Korea also depended on the American market and China also affected South Korean security.

The relationship in the 2020s is more complicated. South Korean public opinion toward China has been strongly negative for nearly a decade, in part because of Chinese economic retaliation, disputes about Yellow Sea maritime claims, and cultural controversies, according to the CRS and to polling. The Lee government has attempted to stabilize relations after a period of deterioration. Xi Jinping visited Gyeongju for the 2025 APEC summit, the first visit by a Chinese president to South Korea in about eleven years, and held his first summit with Lee there on November 1, 2025. Lee made a state visit to Beijing in January 2026, the first by a South Korean president to the Chinese capital in six years, and described 2026 as the first year of a full restoration of relations. Xi urged South Korea to make the "right strategic choices," while the leaders emphasized economic and technological cooperation and the shared interest in peninsular peace. Observers cautioned that the summit sidestepped contested issues.

Section 94.7

The THAAD Crisis: Missile Defense, Chinese Opposition, and Economic Retaliation

The THAAD controversy is the chapter's clearest case of an alliance decision generating economic costs in relations with China. In July 2016, after a North Korean nuclear test and satellite launch earlier that year, the American and South Korean governments announced the decision to deploy a Terminal High Altitude Area Defense battery in South Korea, to defend against North Korean ballistic missiles. The system was eventually installed at a former golf course in Seongju County in North Gyeongsang Province, and became operational in 2017 amid local protests, which were joined by environmental and sovereignty concerns.

China objected, though not to North Korean missile defense as such. Its principal concern was that THAAD's powerful radar, even in its terminal mode, could in a different configuration monitor Chinese missile activity deep inside China and thus reduce Chinese strategic deterrence. American and South Korean officials said the radar was configured for the Korean threat and could not be used against China; Beijing was not persuaded. China also saw the decision as part of a broader American alignment of regional missile defenses involving Japan. Its objections were thus strategic rather than commercial.

Chinese retaliation, however, was largely commercial and informal, and it was never officially declared as sanctions. Chinese authorities restricted group tours to South Korea in 2017, causing a steep decline in Chinese visitors; inspected and closed numerous stores of the Lotte conglomerate, which had supplied the land for the battery; delayed approvals for some South Korean products, including cosmetics and automobile batteries; and discouraged Korean cultural content from appearing in Chinese media. The Lotte group later sold many of its Chinese operations. Estimates of the economic damage vary and depend on definitions; the tourism and retail sectors were hit most visibly, while the larger industrial relationship proved resilient.

South Korean domestic politics intensified the crisis. The deployment decision was made during the final months of Park Geun-hye's presidency and carried out after her impeachment, discussed in Chapter 95. Moon Jae-in, who had been ambivalent during his campaign, inherited the dispute. In October 2017 his government announced that Seoul would not join an American-led regional missile-defense network, would not pursue additional THAAD batteries, and would not enter a trilateral military alliance with Washington and Tokyo, statements sometimes called the "three no's." Seoul described them as statements of existing policy, not commitments to Beijing; Beijing treated them as meaningful. China eased some measures afterward but not all, and the effects lingered.

The episode left several legacies. It taught South Korean businesses and officials that Chinese economic leverage could be exercised informally and quickly. It strengthened South Korean public skepticism of China, which polling later confirmed. It also showed that Beijing's pressure did not reverse the deployment: the battery remained. Whether that outcome shows South Korean resilience or merely the limits of Chinese coercion is debated. Students should notice the interplay of four factors: a security decision made with an ally, a domestic political context, a neighbor's strategic fears, and an economic relationship that offered the neighbor tools of pressure.

Section 94.8

South Korea and U.S.–China Strategic Competition

Since the mid-2010s, rivalry between the United States and China has become the primary organizing fact of East Asian international relations, and South Korea's economic structure places it in an unusually exposed position. Its leading industries, particularly semiconductors, batteries, displays, and shipbuilding, are woven into supply chains running through both the United States and China. South Korean memory chips and display panels are manufactured in plants in China; South Korean firms depend on American technology, equipment, and market access; and both Washington and Beijing have used export controls, investment screening, and subsidies as instruments of competition.

American export controls introduced from 2022 sought to limit China's access to advanced semiconductors and manufacturing equipment, and they affected South Korean companies operating Chinese fabrication plants. Waivers and licensing arrangements for those plants were revised during 2025, and the details have changed repeatedly, so students should consult current sources. The central point is that South Korean companies have had to negotiate with both governments. Seoul's strategy has generally been to seek exemptions, invest in American manufacturing, such as in semiconductors and batteries, and keep Chinese operations running where permitted. The $350 billion investment commitment discussed in Section 94.5 is part of this strategy.

Security questions are even more delicate. The Trump administration has sought to widen the alliance's geographic focus beyond the peninsula, and any role for American forces in Korea in a Taiwan contingency would require South Korean decisions. It is important to distinguish actual obligations from hypothetical scenarios. The 1953 treaty covers armed attack on the territories of the two parties, not on third parties. The 2006 joint statement refers to American "strategic flexibility" but does not oblige South Korea to join American operations. South Korean leaders have therefore described Taiwan as a matter of caution, not commitment; the CRS notes that Lee has said South Korea should avoid becoming too deeply involved in the China–Taiwan conflict, while also not being unnecessarily antagonistic toward China. Critics in the United States argue that this ambiguity weakens deterrence, and critics in South Korea argue that any American request for involvement could entrap their country.

Maritime security adds further complexity. Chinese structures in contested waters of the Yellow Sea, naval and air activity near South Korean zones, and fishing disputes have been irritants. China and Russia have conducted joint air patrols near the air defense identification zones of Korea and Japan in several recent years, which South Korea has protested while keeping communication channels open. Seoul also sees Chinese cooperation as relevant to the North Korean problem, even when Beijing's policy frustrates South Korean hopes.

The prevailing South Korean approach has been described as hedging or, in more favorable terms, strategic autonomy: preserving the American alliance as the core of security, maintaining the largest possible economic ties with China, and avoiding explicit alignment in disputes that do not directly concern Korea. Critics from different directions call this either insufficiently loyal to the alliance or insufficiently independent of it. Defenders reply that a middle-sized power with a nuclear-armed neighbor and exposed trade has few alternatives. Section 94.20 returns to this question.

Historical Vocabulary
Hedging and Strategic Autonomy

Definition: Hedging is a foreign-policy strategy of maintaining relationships with competing powers to avoid commitment to either. Strategic autonomy is the related aim of retaining the capacity to make independent security and economic choices.

Historical context: South Korean governments since 1992 have maintained both the American alliance and Chinese economic relations, and progressive and conservative governments have described this in different vocabularies.

Why it matters: The terms help explain Seoul's reluctance to commit publicly on Taiwan and its efforts to reduce vulnerability to tariffs and export controls.

Potential misunderstanding: Hedging is not neutrality. South Korea remains a treaty ally of the United States with American forces on its soil.

Historical Debate

Does Economic Interdependence Limit Strategic Autonomy?

The question: South Korea's large trade with China and its security dependence on the United States raise the question whether economic interdependence constrains its strategic choices or expands its options.

Interpretation A — Interdependence constrains: The THAAD retaliation showed that China could impose costs for security decisions, encouraging caution on issues such as Taiwan, missile defense, and trilateral cooperation.
Interpretation B — Interdependence is mutual and bounded: China also depends on South Korean components and technology, the THAAD battery remained, and Chinese pressure strengthened South Korean skepticism and diversification, suggesting that coercion has limits.
Interpretation C — Dependence on the American market and security is also a constraint: The 2025–2026 tariffs and investment demands suggest that American leverage over South Korea may be at least as substantial as Chinese leverage.

Evidence: Trade data, company records, tourism statistics, government statements, and polling; estimates of economic damage differ by method.

Points of disagreement: Chiefly interpretation and scale: how much damage was caused by Chinese measures as opposed to other factors, and whether similar pressure would succeed again.

Why it matters: The answer shapes assessments of how much room South Korea has to maneuver between Washington and Beijing.

Student task: Identify what evidence would best distinguish Interpretation A from B, and explain how Interpretation C complicates a simple Washington-versus-Beijing framing.

Division III

Japan and the Growth of Trilateral Security Cooperation

The following four sections explain why historical antagonism and security cooperation coexist between South Korea and Japan, and how both became linked through their relationships with the United States.

Section 94.9

South Korea and Japan: Normalization, Colonial Memory, and Political Friction

South Korea and Japan established diplomatic relations in 1965 through a Treaty on Basic Relations and accompanying agreements, including a settlement under which Japan provided grants and loans to South Korea. The agreement was negotiated under Park Chung-hee, who wanted capital for development and wished to align American allies, and it was bitterly opposed by many South Koreans who considered it insufficiently apologetic. Chapter 75 treated the normalization; the point here is that it left the central questions of colonial responsibility, individual claims, and historical acknowledgment contested.

Four disputes recur. The first concerns forced labor during the 1930s and 1940s, discussed in Chapter 62. Japan maintains that the 1965 agreements settled all claims between the two governments and their nationals; South Korean courts, in rulings in 2018, held that individuals retained claims against Japanese companies. The second concerns wartime sexual slavery, the "comfort women" system, for which a 2015 agreement between the Park Geun-hye and Abe governments was reached and then criticized in South Korea for insufficient consultation with survivors. The third concerns territory: the islets known as Dokdo in Korea and Takeshima in Japan, which South Korea administers and Japan claims. The fourth concerns historical responsibility and education: textbook content, official statements, and memorials.

Public memory affects diplomacy through domestic politics, not only through foreign ministries. South Korean governments face a public that views colonial rule as a defining national wound; Japanese governments face a public and political currents that are tired of repeated apologies or that contest certain interpretations. Each government is therefore constrained by domestic audiences. The result is a pattern in which agreements are reached and then challenged by subsequent governments, producing cycles of cooperation and friction. Chapter 88 examined South Korean–Japanese cultural exchange and memory politics more fully; here the question is how those disputes affect security relations.

Historians should avoid two simplifications. One is that South Koreans uniformly oppose cooperation with Japan; opinion varies by generation, political affiliation, and issue, and cultural exchange has often been strongly welcomed. The other is that Japan has uniformly refused acknowledgment; Japanese statements, including the 1995 Murayama and 1998 Kim–Obuchi declarations, expressed apology and remorse, though subsequent statements and actions have been judged by Koreans as inconsistent. Both governments have thus produced mixed records, and the relationship has repeatedly shown that historical grievance and pragmatic cooperation can coexist.

Section 94.10

Japan and Korean Security: Intelligence, Missile Defense, and Military Geography

Despite these disputes, the security interests of Japan and South Korea overlap. North Korean ballistic missiles have flown over or fallen near Japanese territory and into waters within Japan's exclusive economic zone, and Japanese officials treat the North Korean missile and nuclear programs as direct threats. Japan has also long been concerned about the abductions of Japanese citizens by North Korea in the 1970s and 1980s, a humanitarian and political issue that complicates Japanese–North Korean relations. South Korea's concerns are more immediate in the artillery and short-range missile threat to Seoul, but both governments share a stake in missile warning and tracking.

Military geography links the two countries. American installations in Japan, including air and naval bases and the headquarters of U.S. Forces Japan, would support any Korean contingency, and the United Nations Command maintains arrangements with Japanese bases for rear-area support. A conflict on the peninsula would therefore involve Japan whether or not Japan chose to enter it. Likewise, American forces in Korea are part of a regional posture that reaches Japan. This interdependence is sometimes summarized by saying that the defense of Japan and the defense of Korea are linked, though the legal frameworks remain separate: the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty and the U.S.–South Korea Mutual Defense Treaty are two bilateral instruments, not one alliance.

Intelligence sharing is a practical expression of this linkage. In November 2016, South Korea and Japan signed a General Security of Military Information Agreement (GSOMIA), a framework for sharing classified military information, after years of American encouragement; the agreement was politically contentious in Seoul. Its value lies in the different capabilities of the two countries: Japan's maritime surveillance and signals capabilities, and South Korea's human intelligence and geographic proximity to North Korea. The agreement allows rapid exchange about missile launches, which can be important because South Korean radars detect launches early while Japanese sensors may track trajectories over the Sea of Japan.

The relationship is also shaped by the American hub-and-spoke system of alliances in Asia, which for decades linked the United States separately to Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, Australia, and Thailand without forming a collective alliance like NATO. Washington often pressed Seoul and Tokyo to cooperate; both resisted when domestic politics made cooperation costly. The 2020s changed the pattern, not because the underlying disputes were resolved, but because the security environment, especially North Korean missile testing and the Russia–North Korea partnership, increased the incentives for coordination, as the next two sections show.

Section 94.11

From Diplomatic Dispute to Renewed Cooperation, 2019–2023

The 2018 South Korean Supreme Court rulings on forced labor provoked a rapid deterioration. Japan protested that the rulings violated the 1965 settlement. In July 2019 Japan tightened export controls on three chemicals important to South Korean semiconductor and display manufacturers, and it later removed South Korea from a list of preferred trading partners. Japan described the measures as a matter of export-control integrity; South Korean officials and many observers saw them as retaliation for the court decisions. South Koreans organized boycotts of Japanese goods, and cultural and tourism exchanges fell.

In August 2019, Seoul announced that it would terminate GSOMIA, which was due to be renewed. The decision drew strong American criticism, because Washington regarded the agreement as valuable to its own planning. In November 2019, hours before the termination would have taken effect, South Korea suspended the termination notice, in effect preserving the agreement, while the dispute over export controls continued. The episode revealed that the alliance system could be disrupted by memory politics and that Washington would exert pressure to protect cooperation.

The turn came with Yoon Suk Yeol, inaugurated in 2022, who made improved relations with Japan a priority. In March 2023 his government announced a plan under which a South Korean public foundation would compensate the forced-labor plaintiffs using funds from South Korean companies, without requiring direct payment by Japanese firms, a solution that critics called inadequate and supporters called pragmatic. Japan's prime minister, Kishida Fumio, expressed personal sympathy and indicated that Japan stood by previous statements. Export-control measures were relaxed, GSOMIA was fully normalized, and leaders resumed shuttle diplomacy. Many South Korean opposition politicians and some victims rejected the arrangement, and public opinion was divided.

What changed and what remained unresolved are both important. The immediate dispute was de-escalated, trade measures reversed, and security cooperation resumed. The deeper issues, including individual claims, historical acknowledgment, and Dokdo, remained. Moreover, the rapprochement was a political choice by one South Korean government and depended on its continued authority. The rapprochement coincided with a shift in the security environment: the rise in North Korean missile launches, the Russian invasion of Ukraine, and anxiety about Chinese military pressure around Taiwan all encouraged the United States to press its allies toward cooperation. The result was a convergence of pragmatic interests, not a reconciliation on history.

Section 94.12

The Camp David Framework and U.S.–South Korea–Japan Coordination, 2023–2026

On August 18, 2023, President Joe Biden hosted Yoon Suk Yeol and Kishida Fumio at Camp David, the first stand-alone summit of the three leaders. Their joint statement, titled the "Spirit of Camp David," and an accompanying commitment to consult, committed the three governments to consult one another promptly on regional challenges, provocations, and threats affecting their collective interests and security. The leaders also announced real-time sharing of missile-warning data, annual multi-domain trilateral exercises, regular summits and ministerial meetings, and expanded economic and technological cooperation.

Implementation was rapid. Real-time missile-warning information sharing began in December 2023. Trilateral exercises, including the Freedom Edge series, began in 2024. In 2024 the three governments established a trilateral secretariat in Seoul to support coordination. The cooperation extended to coordinated responses to North Korean sanctions evasion and cyber activity, discussed in Section 94.19. The statement was explicit that the framework was political and consultative. It is therefore inaccurate to describe it as a separate three-country mutual-defense treaty: the United States remains bound by its separate bilateral treaties with Japan and South Korea, and Japan and South Korea have no defense treaty with each other.

The framework has proved more durable than its critics expected. It survived the political crisis in South Korea in late 2024 and 2025, examined in Chapter 95, as well as leadership changes in Japan and the United States. According to the CRS, Lee Jae-myung, who had criticized the Yoon government's approach to Japan while in opposition, has embraced the trilateral arrangements as president, said he would honor past South Korean agreements with Japan, and said he would treat history disagreements separately from other issues. Japan's leadership also changed during this period. The durability suggests that the cooperation rests on shared security interests as well as on the personal commitments of particular leaders.

Limits remain. The framework depends on political will, and the American commitment has been affected by Washington's own policy shifts, including tariff conflicts with both allies and the curtailment of exercises in 2026 reported by the CRS. Public opinion in South Korea about Japan remains divided, historical disputes can resurface, and China and Russia have criticized the framework as a bloc-forming exercise. Whether the arrangement can survive a serious test, such as a new forced-labor ruling, a Dokdo incident, or a sharp American shift, has not been demonstrated. Students should therefore treat Camp David as a significant institutional development whose long-term strength is still unknown at the 2026 endpoint.

Division IV

North Korea, China, and Russia

The next four sections restore North Korea's foreign policy to the center of the narrative. They examine the relationships with the two continental neighbors that have, in different ways, sustained the northern state.

Section 94.13

China and North Korea: Revolutionary Alliance, Treaty Obligations, and Strategic Interests

The relationship between China and North Korea was forged in war. Chinese forces entered the Korean War in October 1950, when United Nations forces approached the Yalu, and fought alongside the Korean People's Army until the armistice. Chinese casualties were large, though estimates differ, and the shared sacrifice became a founding element of the official narrative of friendship "sealed in blood." Chinese troops remained in North Korea until 1958. The relationship was never one of equals: Beijing was larger, wealthier, and more powerful, and it had its own interests. But Kim Il Sung worked to prevent dependence from becoming subordination, a pattern explored in Chapter 77.

On July 11, 1961, five days after the Soviet–North Korean treaty, Beijing and Pyongyang signed the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation, and Mutual Assistance. Its central provision commits each side to immediate assistance if the other is attacked. The treaty has been renewed at twenty-year intervals and is widely described as the only mutual-defense treaty China currently maintains with another state. Its significance is both legal and symbolic; it has also allowed each government to describe the relationship as exceptional. The 65th anniversary in 2026 was used for the Xi–Kim summit in Pyongyang, according to the Center for Strategic and International Studies.

Chinese interests in North Korea have been consistent over decades but not uniform. Beijing has wanted stability, to avoid refugee flows, state collapse, or a conflict that would involve American forces near the border; it has wanted influence over Pyongyang, to prevent the North from drifting into the orbit of an adversary or acting in ways that endanger China; it has wanted a non-nuclear peninsula, at least rhetorically, and has preferred diplomatic solutions; and it has opposed a unified Korea allied with the United States. Different Chinese leaders and agencies have weighed these goals differently, and analysts disagree about which is most important.

North Korea's interests have been equally persistent: security guarantees, economic aid, diplomatic cover in the United Nations, and the preservation of political autonomy. Pyongyang has frequently resisted Chinese advice, from the 1960s, when it leaned toward Moscow or Beijing depending on the moment, to the nuclear program of the 2000s and 2010s. The 1992 Chinese normalization with South Korea strained trust, and the relationship cooled for periods, notably after Kim Jong Un's early nuclear tests and the 2013 execution of Jang Song Thaek, his uncle and a known interlocutor with Beijing. Yet when Pyongyang needed to diversify from the United States and break out of isolation in 2018, it turned to Xi; four meetings were held in 2018–2019 as Kim prepared for his summits with Trump, discussed in Chapter 93.

The relationship thus combines an alliance with mutual wariness. For China, North Korea is both a buffer and a liability; for North Korea, China is both a protector and a potential threat to autonomy. This ambivalence explains patterns that might otherwise seem puzzling: why China enforces sanctions imperfectly, why it supports some Security Council actions and blocks others, and why North Korea can resist advice from its largest economic partner.

Section 94.14

Economic Dependence: The China–North Korea Border, Trade, and Sanctions

China has long been North Korea's largest trading partner by a wide margin, and the border towns along the Yalu and Tumen rivers are the principal channels. Dandong, opposite Sinuiju, handles much of the formal trade by road, rail, and the aging Friendship Bridge; other crossings, including those near Namyang and in the Tumen valley, serve smaller flows. China supplies food, fertilizer, consumer goods, machinery, and, in recorded and unrecorded forms, most of North Korea's petroleum products; North Korea has exported coal, minerals, textiles, seafood, and labor. After the severe famine of the 1990s examined in Chapter 83, informal trade across the border became a lifeline for many households and helped create the markets (jangmadang) that changed everyday life.

International sanctions reshaped these flows. Between 2006 and 2017, the UN Security Council adopted ten resolutions responding to North Korean nuclear and missile programs, with Chinese and Russian support. The 2016 and 2017 resolutions banned or capped major exports, including coal, iron, seafood, and textiles, and capped imports of refined petroleum. Recorded Chinese–North Korean trade fell sharply in 2018, according to Chinese customs data. Enforcement was uneven: ship-to-ship oil transfers, smuggling, overseas laborers, cyber theft, and false documentation allowed North Korea to earn and import more than formal statistics show. Distinguishing verified trade from estimates matters: customs data capture only official trade; reports of illicit trade rely on monitoring bodies, satellite imagery, and intelligence sources, each with limitations.

The COVID-19 pandemic imposed a second shock. North Korea closed its borders in January 2020, halted most trade and travel, and restricted movement internally. Official Chinese trade fell to a fraction of earlier levels. Reopening was gradual and uneven. Freight rail and road links resumed in stages from 2022 and 2023, trade recovered substantially, and passenger service, including flights and trains between Pyongyang and Beijing, resumed later. At the June 2026 summit, according to the Associated Press, Xi said the two sides should use the reopening of flight and train services to expand people-to-people exchanges. Both governments emphasized expanded cooperation in economy, trade, and infrastructure.

Dependence is a feature of the relationship but not a mechanism of control. China's leverage includes the ability to slow customs processing, limit fuel supplies, or tighten enforcement. In 2017–2018 it did apply stronger pressure, contributing to Pyongyang's decision to seek diplomacy. But the reverse is also true: North Korea's tolerance for hardship, its emphasis on self-reliance, and its ability to obtain alternative suppliers, including Russia since 2022, limit Chinese influence. The 2020s have also revealed that Russian support, discussed in Section 94.18, reduces North Korea's dependence on China precisely when Chinese pressure might otherwise be most effective.

Everyday Life and Social History

Border Lives, Workers, and Households in a Geopolitical Age

Great-power relations reach ordinary lives through concrete channels. Along the Chinese–North Korean border, traders, truck drivers, customs officials, fishermen, and smugglers have made livelihoods from the movement of goods, and closures during the pandemic years devastated both formal and informal commerce. North Korean workers abroad, including in Russia, whose presence was reported to have grown after 2022, have earned wages that are largely controlled by the state; reports of student visas used to employ laborers, as recorded in the CRS, show how formal categories can disguise labor flows that may violate sanctions. For North Korean households, the consequences of sanctions, border closure, and foreign support are mediated through the markets and the state distribution system, and evidence about their effects is limited and often indirect.

In South Korea, the alliance and the Chinese relationship are visible in different ways: the neighborhoods around bases such as Pyeongtaek, whose population and economy were reshaped by Camp Humphreys; the villages of Seongju, where residents protested THAAD; the tourism and retail workers who lost business in 2017; the factory workers whose employment depends on export markets; and the Korean laborers at the Georgia plant detained in 2025. Soldiers, conscripts, and families live under the constant influence of alliance decisions. Historians draw on oral testimony, local newspapers, labor records, and survey data to reconstruct these experiences, while recognizing that official documents emphasize the perspective of governments.

Section 94.15

Cooperation and Friction between Beijing and Pyongyang

Chinese displeasure with North Korean nuclear testing has been genuine and has taken concrete forms. China voted for the Security Council sanctions resolutions of 2006 through 2017, including those after the 2016 and 2017 nuclear tests, and imposed tighter border controls after the 2017 test. Beijing also hosted the Six-Party Talks from 2003, examined in Chapter 86. Its objections reflected fears of regional proliferation, of a crisis near its territory, and of provoking American and Japanese military buildup. Yet China consistently opposed measures it feared would destabilize North Korea, resisted secondary sanctions on Chinese entities, and favored dialogue over pressure.

Beijing's relationship with Pyongyang deteriorated markedly in the early Kim Jong Un years and recovered with his 2018 diplomacy. After the collapse of the Trump–Kim talks in 2019, the pandemic closed the border and kept the two leaders from meeting; they did not meet between 2020 and 2025. During this time, Pyongyang moved toward Moscow. Beijing, according to the CRS, may have been prompted by the Russia–North Korea upgrade to refocus on its own relationship. Kim attended the September 2025 military parade in Beijing marking the anniversary of the end of the Second World War and met Xi on September 4, a significant public sign of renewed ties.

The June 8–9, 2026 summit in Pyongyang, Xi's first visit in seven years and held ahead of the 65th anniversary of the 1961 treaty, consolidated the reset. Xi made Pyongyang his first foreign stop of 2026. Kim described relations with China as the state's foremost strategic undertaking and pledged support for China's One China principle, according to North Korean media. Xi presented a four-point framework for deepening political, economic, and cultural ties. Notably, Chinese and North Korean reports of the meeting did not mention North Korea's banned nuclear weapons program, in contrast with the 2019 Chinese readout, which referred to a constructive role in denuclearization. Analysts differ on what this silence means: some see Chinese acquiescence in North Korea's nuclear status, others see tactical omission to improve relations, and others see an effort to dilute Russian influence in Pyongyang.

Why does strong economic dependence not translate into Chinese control? Several reasons emerge. First, North Korea's leadership regards autonomy as essential to regime survival, and its nuclear weapons are its main guarantee. Second, China fears collapse more than defiance, which lessens its willingness to apply maximum pressure. Third, Russian support gives Pyongyang alternatives. Fourth, North Korea can resist because it is willing to endure economic hardship. Fifth, China's own priorities, including relations with the United States, Taiwan, and regional stability, sometimes outweigh North Korean policy. The relationship should therefore be understood as a negotiated asymmetry in which the weaker party retains meaningful agency, a theme that recurs through Chapter 92.

Section 94.16

Russia and North Korea: From Soviet Patronage to a Renewed Strategic Partnership

Soviet forces occupied northern Korea in 1945, and Soviet advisers helped establish the political, military, and economic institutions of the new state, as Chapters 65 and 66 described. Moscow supplied weapons and planning support for the 1950 invasion, discussed in Chapter 67, and provided air support in the war. The Soviet–North Korean Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation, and Mutual Assistance of July 6, 1961 committed each side to help the other if attacked. In the following decades, Pyongyang received Soviet aid, technology, and trade, while balancing between Moscow and Beijing during the Sino-Soviet dispute.

The end of the Cold War disrupted this patronage. The Soviet Union established diplomatic relations with South Korea in 1990, causing deep North Korean resentment, and demanded hard-currency payment for trade. Soviet support collapsed in 1991, contributing to the economic crisis that culminated in the famine examined in Chapter 83. The Russian Federation allowed the mutual-assistance provisions of the 1961 treaty to lapse in 1996; a new friendship treaty signed in 2000 contained no military clause. Russian–North Korean relations improved under Vladimir Putin, who visited Pyongyang in 2000, and Kim Jong Il visited Russia several times, but the relationship remained secondary to Russia's ties with South Korea and the West.

From 2006 to 2017, Russia supported the Security Council sanctions resolutions against North Korea, as the CRS notes. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, however, its approach reversed. It blocked, together with China, American-led efforts at the Security Council in 2022 and 2023 to adopt stricter sanctions after missile tests, and it began to treat North Korea as a source of munitions and diplomatic support. North Korea, for its part, supported Russia politically and, according to Western governments, began transferring munitions in 2022.

Why did Pyongyang move closer to Moscow? Its leadership saw several opportunities. Russia offered a market for artillery ammunition and missiles that North Korea could produce, financial benefits, food and oil, diplomatic protection in the Security Council, and potential access to military technology. The collapse of diplomacy with Washington after 2019 removed incentives to restrain itself. The Ukraine war gave Russia an urgent need for materiel and removed its interest in cooperating with Western sanctions. And a Russian partnership reduced North Korea's dependence on China. The convergence was therefore based on mutual interests, not on ideological affinity, though both governments invoked Soviet-era solidarity. Section 94.17 examines the summits and treaty that followed.

Section 94.17

The Putin–Kim Summits and the Russia–North Korea Strategic Partnership Treaty

In September 2023, Kim Jong Un traveled by train to Russia's Far East and met Vladimir Putin at the Vostochny Cosmodrome. It was Kim's first summit with any foreign leader since 2019. The setting was symbolic: Putin spoke publicly of possible Russian help in satellite development, a subject on which North Korea had struggled and which is prohibited by Security Council resolutions as ballistic-missile assistance. Kim toured Russian military and aviation facilities. The visit signaled that Moscow was willing to defy the sanctions it had earlier supported.

On June 19, 2024, Putin visited Pyongyang, his first visit since 2000, and the two leaders signed the Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty. Article 4—not Article 3, as misnumbered in the September 2026 CRS summary—commits each side to provide military and other assistance without delay, with all means in its possession, if the other is placed in a state of war by armed attack. Article 3 instead calls for immediate consultation when a party faces an imminent threat of armed aggression. The treaty also pledges expanded cooperation in science, nuclear energy, and space technology. Both parties later ratified it. Kim and Putin have met three times since 2022, by the CRS count, including an encounter in Beijing in 2025.

The treaty differs from ordinary diplomatic cooperation in three ways. First, it contains a binding mutual-assistance commitment that revives, in modified form, the military-alliance structure that had lapsed after the Soviet collapse. Second, it was concluded while Russia was fighting a major war, so the provision has immediate rather than theoretical significance. Third, it formalizes cooperation that is incompatible with Security Council resolutions that Russia helped to adopt. Its wording leaves ambiguities, such as the definition of "armed invasion" and the conditions of assistance, which each government can interpret to suit its needs.

What did each side seek? Russia sought access to munitions, ballistic missiles, and manpower; a partner to complicate Western strategy in Northeast Asia; and a way to regain influence over a peninsula where China had dominated. North Korea sought revenue, food, oil, technology, diplomatic cover, a security guarantee not dependent on China, and recognition of its status. Neither state is a passive instrument. Pyongyang negotiated payments and technical cooperation; Moscow has pursued its own purposes, such as the use of North Korean laborers. The relationship also carries risks for both: dependence on a single war, the possibility that Russia will not deliver advanced technology, and the effect of reputational and sanctions costs.

Section 94.18

North Korean Arms, Troops, and the War in Ukraine

Evidence about North Korean involvement in Ukraine varies in quality, and students should distinguish categories. Corroborated elements include large-scale shipments of artillery ammunition, documented by satellite imagery, ship and rail tracking, and the work of monitoring bodies; the use of North Korean ballistic missiles in Ukraine, supported by recovered debris and official Western and Ukrainian statements; and the deployment of North Korean troops to Russia, which Moscow and Pyongyang publicly acknowledged in April 2025 after months of denial. The May 2025 report of the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team documented unlawful military cooperation between the two states. Assessed elements, reported by intelligence services, include the scale of munitions deliveries, the extent of North Korean casualties, and possible Russian transfers of technology.

According to the CRS, North Korea provided thousands of shipping containers of munitions and supplied ballistic missiles, and in 2024 committed more than 10,000 troops. North Korean forces took part in combat in 2025 to help Russia expel Ukrainian forces from the Kursk region, and Putin praised them publicly. A North Korean memorial built in 2026 appears to list over 2,000 names of those killed, according to the CRS, a figure that offers some evidence of losses though not a complete count. Ukrainian officials have reportedly estimated that several thousand North Korean engineers and specialists support Russian forces, including drone operators. In August 2026, President Zelensky warned that North Korea would send far larger forces, and Ukrainian intelligence assessed that a missile unit with KN-23 and KN-24 missiles would be deployed. These August 2026 statements are assessments and warnings, not confirmed deployments, and students should treat them accordingly.

What does North Korea receive? One South Korean estimate cited by the CRS suggests North Korea earned between $7.7 and $14.4 billion through the end of 2025, primarily from artillery deliveries; the range is wide because the underlying data are uncertain. Russia is also accused of supplying oil above Security Council limits, of employing North Korean laborers, and of facilitating financial access. The U.S. 2025 Annual Threat Assessment identified Russian financial benefits, diplomatic support, and defense cooperation. The 2026 assessment states that North Korean forces have gained valuable combat experience in modern warfare and equipment. The May 2025 MSMT report said that Russia is believed to have provided air-defense and electronic-warfare equipment.

The most consequential and least verified question is technology transfer. American commanders, including the commander of U.S. Forces Korea and the Indo-Pacific commander, testified in 2026 that North Korea has made significant shifts in capability and is seeking Russian help on space launch, satellites, drones, missile technology, and submarine propulsion. These are official assessments of intent and, in some cases, effect; they are not public proof of specific transfers. Historians should record them as assessments, noting that governments have incentives to emphasize threats, and that secrecy makes independent confirmation difficult.

For South Korea, the consequences are direct. North Korean soldiers learning modern drone and artillery warfare, and North Korean missiles tested in combat, change the military balance on the peninsula. They also complicate Seoul's own policy toward Ukraine: South Korea has provided no lethal weapons to Ukraine, according to its stated policy, and debates continue about whether to change that. For China, the Russia–North Korea partnership competes for influence in Pyongyang. For the United States, it joins European and Asian theaters. The war thus functions as a bridge between regional systems that had previously been largely separate.

Section 94.19

Sanctions, International Institutions, and the Emergence of Competing Security Alignments

The United Nations sanctions regime on North Korea was among the most extensive in the world. It began with Resolution 1718 in 2006 and was strengthened through a series of resolutions culminating in 2017, restricting arms trade, financial transactions, exports of coal, minerals, seafood, and textiles, imports of refined petroleum, and the employment of North Korean workers abroad. A Panel of Experts, established under Resolution 1874 in 2009, monitored implementation and published reports documenting evasion, including ship-to-ship transfers, overseas workers, and cyber theft.

The regime began to fracture after 2022. China and Russia blocked new measures after missile tests in 2022 and 2023. On March 28, 2024, Russia vetoed a resolution to renew the Panel's mandate; thirteen members voted in favor and China abstained. The Panel's mandate expired at the end of April 2024. The veto removed the main independent mechanism for documenting violations, and many analysts concluded that Moscow wished to avoid scrutiny of its own trade with Pyongyang. Existing sanctions remained legally in force, but Russia has declared that it considers certain restrictions obsolete, and the mechanism for enforcement weakened.

The United States, South Korea, Japan, and their partners responded by creating alternatives. In 2024 eleven countries formed the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team, which issues independent reports; its first, in May 2025, examined Russia–North Korea arms cooperation. The United States and South Korea established a task force to disrupt North Korean sanctions evasion, and the three allies expanded cooperation on cyber theft and financial networks. These arrangements lack the legitimacy of the Security Council and cannot compel compliance, but they preserve a body of evidence and a coalition for national sanctions.

Analysts and journalists often describe the result as two competing triangles: the United States–South Korea–Japan grouping and a North Korea–China–Russia grouping. The image captures real developments but also misleads. The first triangle rests on formal bilateral alliances, regular exercises, and an institutional secretariat, though not on a single treaty. The second lacks such institutions: China and Russia are partners but not formal allies; China has treaty obligations to North Korea but has not joined the Russia–North Korea treaty; and the three have not held trilateral exercises on the allied model. The relationships also compete: China may seek to dilute Russian influence in Pyongyang, as noted in Section 94.15. Neither grouping consists of three identical bilateral alliances.

Section 94.20

The Korean Peninsula in 2026: Alliances, Dependencies, and Strategic Choice

By the November 14, 2026 editorial cutoff, the two Korean states occupy structurally different positions. South Korea retains a treaty alliance with the United States and about 28,500 American personnel, while being engaged in a contentious renegotiation of that alliance's terms: tariffs, investment pledges, burden-sharing, OPCON transfer, nuclear-powered submarines, and the alliance's regional mission. It remains deeply integrated economically with China, its largest trading partner, and has sought since late 2025 to repair relations through two summits between Lee and Xi. It cooperates with Japan and the United States in the trilateral framework, and the Lee government has kept that framework despite earlier criticism.

North Korea occupies a different position. It has no formal mutual-defense relationship with a Western power and has rejected nuclear diplomacy, with Kim stating that he will never denuclearize, according to the CRS. It is a nuclear-armed state that is simultaneously linked to Russia by a mutual-assistance treaty and to China by the 1961 treaty and a reset in relations. Its dependence on China is substantial but diluted by Russian support. The partnership with Russia gives North Korea revenue, combat experience, and potential technology, while the Chinese reset gives it economic and diplomatic backing. Pyongyang is therefore less isolated than at any time since the early 1990s, though its economic base remains narrow.

Korean agency is visible on both sides. Seoul used its shipbuilding and industrial strengths to bargain with Washington and, with limits, with Beijing; it has pursued a hedging approach that seeks to preserve both security and trade. Pyongyang used its arsenal and its willingness to provide munitions and troops to attract Russian commitment and Chinese attention, converting a narrow position into leverage. Both governments have also been constrained: South Korea by its dependence on American security and export markets; North Korea by the risks of reliance on one war and by Chinese wariness.

Several uncertainties stand out. For South Korea, the alliance's direction is uncertain: Washington has asked for greater South Korean responsibility and a broader mission, curtailed exercises without prior consultation according to the CRS, and imposed tariffs, while Seoul seeks autonomy and new capabilities. Whether South Korea will obtain nuclear-powered submarines and enrichment rights, accelerate OPCON transfer, and sustain defense spending increases is unresolved. For North Korea, the end of the Ukraine war, if it came, could reduce Russian demand; a strengthened China could compete for influence. The trilateral framework has survived leadership changes but has not faced a serious test. And the American commitment to diplomacy with Pyongyang, which Lee has welcomed, has not produced engagement, as Kim Yo Jong's dismissal in June 2026 of denuclearization as an "anachronistic dream" illustrates.

This chapter has argued that the peninsula's alignments are shaped by two different relationships and by the choices of Korean governments. A historian writing in 2026 should avoid prediction. The record shows that alignments have changed repeatedly: South Korea's relations with China were nonexistent in 1991; North Korea's alliance with Russia lapsed in 1996 and revived in 2024. Such changes arose from choices made under constraint, not from inevitability. The task for readers is to understand the interests, institutions, and evidence that make each choice intelligible. Chapter 96 will place these relationships within the final synthesis of the divided peninsula in 2026.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how North and South Korea operate within the competing interests of the United States, China, Japan, and Russia, treating Korea as the historical subject and the foreign powers as partners, patrons, and rivals whose interests intersect with Korean choices. Its central claim is that the peninsula's geopolitical position in 2026 is shaped by two different relationships: South Korea's military alliance with the United States combined with economic integration across East Asia, and North Korea's changing dependence on China together with a substantial military partnership with Russia.

Division I traced the foundation of the southern alliance. The 1953 Mutual Defense Treaty, concluded to reassure and restrain Syngman Rhee, established an American commitment and a legal basis for forces that today number roughly 28,500. The combined command structure, the unresolved question of wartime operational control, and the institutions of extended deterrence, including the 2023 Washington Declaration, show how the alliance adapted as South Korea became wealthier and as North Korea acquired nuclear weapons.

Division II examined how South Korea has balanced security dependence on Washington against economic interdependence with Beijing. Normalization in 1992 transformed Seoul's position. The THAAD crisis of 2016–2017 showed that alliance decisions could invite informal economic retaliation, though it did not reverse the deployment. In 2025–2026, tariffs, investment pledges, and demands for a wider alliance mission showed that American leverage could be as constraining as Chinese leverage, while Lee Jae-myung sought both a repaired relationship with China and gains in autonomy from Washington.

Division III explained why security cooperation with Japan coexists with unresolved colonial memory. Disputes over forced labor, sexual slavery, territory, and historical responsibility have repeatedly disrupted relations, as in 2018–2019, yet shared concern about North Korean missiles and the geographic linkage of American bases produced renewed cooperation after 2023. The Camp David framework created consultative and institutional mechanisms without becoming a trilateral defense treaty, and it has so far survived leadership changes.

Division IV restored North Korea's foreign relations to the center. The 1961 treaty with China and a pair of mutual-assistance treaties in July 1961 created the Cold War structure; Soviet collapse disrupted it. China remains North Korea's principal economic partner, but economic dependence has not meant control, because Pyongyang prizes autonomy, Beijing fears collapse more than defiance, and Russia now provides alternatives. The Xi–Kim summits of September 2025 and June 2026 completed a reset in Chinese–North Korean relations.

Division V analyzed the reordering of the 2020s. After Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, North Korea and Russia exchanged munitions, summits, and ultimately the June 2024 treaty with its mutual-assistance clause. North Korea reportedly supplied artillery ammunition and ballistic missiles and deployed troops, who fought in the Kursk region. Evidence for some elements is strong, while claims about technology transfer and future deployments remain assessments. The Russian veto of the UN Panel of Experts weakened international monitoring, and allied states created alternatives such as the MSMT.

The chapter evaluated the popular image of two competing triangles. The southern grouping rests on formal bilateral alliances and institutionalized consultation; the northern grouping rests on separate and asymmetrical relationships, with Beijing and Moscow competing for influence in Pyongyang. The model describes a real alignment trend but should not be mistaken for two matching alliance systems.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized Korean agency. South Korea used its industrial and technological capacities to bargain with allies and partners, and North Korea used its arsenal and its willingness to serve Russia's war to transform a narrow position into leverage. Both operate under constraints: Seoul's dependence on American security and export markets, and Pyongyang's reliance on a single war and a wary Chinese neighbor.

The evidence base is uneven. Treaties, communiqués, and trade data support firm conclusions for many topics, while claims about intelligence, secret transfers, and private intentions remain provisional. The chapter's account of 2025–2026 relies substantially on government research reports and contemporary reporting and should be treated as a snapshot. The question of how the alignments will evolve is left open, as it must be, and is carried into Chapter 96.

Key Terms

Mutual Defense Treaty (1953)
The U.S.–Republic of Korea treaty signed in October 1953 and in force from November 1954, providing for consultation and for action against armed attack according to each party's constitutional processes, and authorizing American forces in South Korea.
U.S. Forces Korea (USFK)
The American military command in South Korea, with about 28,500 personnel and its main base at Camp Humphreys.
Combined Forces Command
The binational American–South Korean headquarters created in 1978 that exercises wartime operational control over designated forces.
United Nations Command
The Korean War–era command that oversees the armistice and military demarcation line.
Operational control (OPCON)
Authority to direct forces for assigned missions; peacetime control returned to South Korea in 1994, while wartime control transfer remains under negotiation.
Strategic flexibility
The American view that forces based in Korea may be used for regional contingencies, acknowledged in a 2006 joint statement.
Extended deterrence
A state's commitment to deter attacks on allies, including through nuclear retaliation.
Washington Declaration
The April 2023 U.S.–South Korean declaration creating a Nuclear Consultative Group and reaffirming extended deterrence and South Korean non-proliferation commitments.
Special Measures Agreement
A periodic accord on South Korean financial contributions to the costs of the American presence.
KORUS FTA
The Korea–U.S. Free Trade Agreement, in force since 2012.
Nordpolitik
South Korea's policy, begun in the 1980s, of opening relations with communist states, culminating in ties with the Soviet Union and China.
THAAD
A U.S.-developed missile-defense system deployed in South Korea after a 2016 decision, which provoked Chinese objections and retaliation.
Informal economic coercion
Pressure applied through unannounced administrative measures, tourism limits, or consumer campaigns rather than declared sanctions, as in China's response to THAAD.
Hedging
Maintaining relations with competing powers to avoid exclusive commitment to either.
Strategic autonomy
The capacity to make independent security and economic choices despite dependence on partners.
Abandonment and entrapment
The two alliance fears: being left unsupported by an ally, or being dragged into an ally's conflicts.
GSOMIA
The 2016 South Korea–Japan General Security of Military Information Agreement for sharing classified military information.
Spirit of Camp David
The August 2023 trilateral statement committing the U.S., Japan, and South Korea to consultation, missile-warning sharing, and exercises.
Hub-and-spoke alliance system
The American arrangement of separate bilateral alliances in Asia rather than a collective alliance.
Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation, and Mutual Assistance (1961)
The China–North Korea treaty of July 11, 1961, with an assistance commitment, renewed at twenty-year intervals.
Jangmadang
North Korean informal markets that expanded after the famine of the 1990s.
Panel of Experts
The UN body created in 2009 to monitor North Korean sanctions, whose mandate ended in 2024 after a Russian veto.
Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team (MSMT)
A 2024 coalition of eleven states reporting on sanctions violations outside the UN framework.
Sanctions evasion
Methods such as ship-to-ship transfers, false documentation, overseas labor, and cyber theft used to circumvent restrictions.
Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty
The June 2024 Russia–North Korea treaty containing a mutual military-assistance clause.
Kursk operation
The 2024–2025 effort in which North Korean troops supported Russian forces against Ukrainian forces in Russia's Kursk region.
Two-triangles model
A shorthand image of competing U.S.–South Korea–Japan and North Korea–China–Russia groupings, which simplifies asymmetrical and informal relationships.
Assessment (intelligence)
A government judgment based on evidence that may not be public, distinct from independently verified fact.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the divided peninsula is not a passive object in the world's great-power politics. Its two states have repeatedly used the interests of others to pursue their own, whether through alliance bargaining, economic diplomacy, nuclear development, or military assistance to a foreign war. Understanding this requires attending both to structure and to choice.

The chapter also reveals how institutions outlast individual leaders. The 1953 treaty has persisted for more than seven decades, the 1961 treaties shaped northern alignment for generations, and the Camp David and Russia–North Korea frameworks of the 2020s were deliberately designed to endure. At the same time, alignments have shifted dramatically: ties with China began only in 1992, and a military partnership with Russia lapsed and revived. Continuity and change coexist.

These relationships shape later developments examined in the remaining chapter. South Korea's democratic crisis, treated in Chapter 95, occurred while its alliances were being renegotiated, and the final synthesis in Chapter 96 must consider how external alignments, domestic politics, and national identity interact. The chapter also reminds readers that the history of the present rests on incomplete evidence, and that historical judgment includes recognizing what cannot yet be known.
