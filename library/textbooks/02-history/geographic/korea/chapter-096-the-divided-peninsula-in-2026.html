The Divided Peninsula in 2026

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the Military Demarcation Line, the border that has divided the peninsula since 1953, the markers that once showed where one army's territory ended and the other's began have largely disappeared. In October 2026, South Korea's unification minister told reporters that only about 180 of the line's original 1,292 markers could still be found. A land mine had exploded inside the Demilitarized Zone a few weeks earlier. The most heavily fortified frontier on earth, it turned out, was in places not clearly marked on the ground.

The mine explosion of September 21, 2026, took place on the southern side of the line, and South Korea's military attributed it to mines planted by the North. In its aftermath the two arguments that most divide South Korean opinion about the North were repeated in miniature. Some South Koreans demanded that loudspeaker broadcasts toward the North be resumed. The unification minister, Chung Dong-young, called that idea irresponsible and dangerous and proposed instead that officials of both sides meet to re-survey the line. The Defense Ministry separately pursued military talks over the line and over communication channels. Meanwhile the government in Pyongyang, which had taken the position since late 2023 that the South was no longer a partner in any national project but a separate and hostile state, offered no public sign that it would talk. The episode was small, but it contained the condition of the peninsula in 2026: a frontier whose physical markers had faded while the political distance across it had grown.

This chapter is the last of ninety-six, and its task is different from that of most of its predecessors. It does not narrate one episode in depth. Chapters 91 through 95 have already examined, in turn, South Korea's social and demographic crisis, Kim Jong Un's North Korea, the collapse of inter-Korean détente, Korea's place among the great powers, and the stress placed on South Korean democracy between 2016 and 2025. Chapter 96 instead draws those strands together and carries them to the fixed endpoint of this textbook: late 2026, with evidence available through November 15, 2026. It asks what the two Koreas had become, how they differed, what they still shared, and which questions about the peninsula's future remained genuinely open.

The year 2026 supplies unusually dense evidence for such a synthesis. In February, the Workers' Party of Korea held its Ninth Congress, its first in five years, re-elected Kim Jong Un as general secretary, and adopted a five-year plan for 2026 to 2030. In the same month, a Seoul court sentenced South Korea's former president, Yoon Suk Yeol, to life imprisonment for leading an insurrection through his December 2024 declaration of martial law. In March, North Korea's newly elected Supreme People's Assembly revised the state's constitution, and Kim told it that the country would treat the South as its most hostile state. In June, South Korea held local elections, and China's president Xi Jinping visited Pyongyang for the first time in seven years. In the course of the year, South Korea's semiconductor exports reached levels without precedent in the country's history, the seven members of BTS returned to the stage with an album named after a Korean folk song, and official figures showed that the country's fertility rate had risen for a second year while still being the lowest of any member of the OECD.

Writing about the immediate present requires a particular discipline. Most of the evidence for 2026 is recent. It consists of official statistics, opinion surveys, court rulings, intelligence assessments released to legislators, state media from Pyongyang, journalism, and satellite imagery, and almost none of it has yet been tested by the slow work of archival history. Some of it comes from governments with strong interests in how events are described. Where this chapter gives figures it identifies who produced them, and where interpretations differ it says so. It also avoids a temptation common in accounts of the contemporary Koreas: writing as if the present arrangement were the settled outcome of seven decades. The division, the nuclear arsenal, and the contrasting political systems all have causes that earlier chapters trace, but none was fixed in advance, and several of the questions raised in 2026 were still unanswered when this chapter was written.

The chapter has five divisions of four sections each. Division I establishes the historical homeland, the sovereignty conflict, the armistice frontier, and competing memories. Division II synthesizes South Korean democracy, economy, culture, and demography. Division III examines North Korea’s party-state, markets, information controls, and armed forces. Division IV compares external relationships and restores divided families and everyday experiences to the center of the historical narrative. Division V evaluates the changing meaning of reunification, public opinion, coexistence, and the continuity of Korean history across all four volumes.

The chapter also asks students to consider what an endpoint means. The title of this textbook begins with ancient kingdoms and ends with a divided peninsula, and Chapter 1 warned against reading Korean history as a march toward division. The warning applies with equal force at the other end of the book. A peninsula that is divided in 2026 may or may not be divided in 2056, and nothing in this chapter predicts either outcome. What the chapter can do is describe, as precisely and as even-handedly as the evidence allows, what two societies with a shared inheritance had become by 2026, and how historians, citizens, and governments were then arguing about what that meant.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the armistice system, the Demilitarized Zone, and the Military Demarcation Line, and explain why the Korean War has never been formally ended by a peace treaty.
  2. Trace the principal developments in South Korean democracy from the martial-law crisis of December 2024 through the court verdicts and local elections of 2026, distinguishing established legal outcomes from pending proceedings.
  3. Analyze the strengths and vulnerabilities of South Korea's technology-led economy in 2026, including its dependence on semiconductors and on external markets.
  4. Explain how South Korea's cultural influence changed from importation to adaptation and global export, and distinguish foreign influence from cultural dependency.
  5. Interpret South Korea's demographic statistics, including the 2023 low, the 2024 and 2025 rebound, and the uneven regional pattern, without overstating what a two-year rise can establish.
  6. Describe the main institutions of North Korea's party-state in 2026, including the Ninth Party Congress, the revised constitution, and the status of the Kim family's hereditary leadership.
  7. Assess the evidence for the size, character, and political purposes of North Korea's nuclear and conventional forces, noting what outside observers can and cannot verify.
  8. Compare the changing roles of Russia, China, the United States, and Japan in the foreign relations of both Korean states in 2026.
  9. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether the peninsula is best understood as one nation divided, two separate nations in formation, or a more complicated case.
  10. Distinguish divergent political, linguistic, and social identities from shared historical memory and inheritance in North and South Korea.
  11. Apply the textbook's historical method, including attention to evidence, contingency, and the avoidance of teleology, to the contemporary peninsula.
  12. Synthesize the political, cultural, and institutional development of Korea across all four volumes without projecting a modern nation-state onto the ancient past.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why has the Korean War, which ended in combat in 1953, never ended in law, and what does the condition of the Demilitarized Zone in 2026 reveal about that unfinished settlement?
  2. How do historians assess a democracy that has just survived an attempted suspension of constitutional order, and what distinguishes the repair of institutions from the resolution of political conflict?
  3. What are the sources of South Korea's economic and technological strength in 2026, and what do they imply about vulnerability as well as achievement?
  4. What is the difference between a society that receives foreign culture and one that exports its own, and how should historians describe the transition without turning it into a story of national triumph?
  5. Why might a modest recovery in South Korean births not settle the question of demographic decline, and what evidence would be needed to decide?
  6. How should historians evaluate claims about North Korea when much of the evidence comes from the North's own state media, from intelligence services with their own interests, and from a small number of defectors?
  7. Why did North Korea's leadership abandon the language of national reunification, and how does this decision compare with earlier phases of its policy toward the South?
  8. How have the changing relationships among Pyongyang, Moscow, Beijing, Washington, and Seoul affected the options available to each Korean state?
  9. Is the idea that all Koreans form a single nation still historically sustainable, and what would count as evidence for or against it?
  10. Why must a history ending in 2026 be careful not to treat the present division as the fixed outcome of Korean history?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter should carry forward from earlier chapters a limited set of facts. The peninsula was divided at the 38th parallel in 1945 by two occupying armies, and in 1948 two states were proclaimed, the Republic of Korea in the South and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in the North (Chapter 66). Between 1950 and 1953 the two fought a war that drew in the United States, a coalition of United Nations members, and China, and that ended in an armistice signed on July 27, 1953, not in a peace treaty (Chapters 67 to 72). The armistice created the Demilitarized Zone and a system of military supervision that remains in place. Nothing in later history replaced it.

The two states then developed in parallel, each claiming to represent the whole nation. South Korea passed through the Rhee, Park, and Chun governments and a developmental state built on export industry and the chaebol (Chapters 74 to 76 and 79), democratized after the June struggle of 1987 (Chapter 82), absorbed the shock of the 1997 financial crisis (Chapter 84), and became a technological and cultural power (Chapters 87, 89, and 90). North Korea built a party-state around Kim Il Sung, the ideology of Juche, and a hereditary succession (Chapter 77), endured economic stagnation and a famine in the 1990s (Chapters 80 and 83), developed a nuclear arsenal (Chapter 86), and under Kim Jong Un consolidated power, expanded its missile and nuclear programs, and adopted a harder line toward the South (Chapter 92). The summitry of 2018 and 2019 raised hopes of a different future and collapsed (Chapter 93). From 2022 onward, Russia's war in Ukraine transformed North Korea's external position (Chapter 94). Democracy in the South came under severe stress between the candlelight protests of 2016 and 2017 and the removal of President Yoon Suk Yeol in 2025, after which Lee Jae-myung was elected president (Chapter 95).

Three further background points matter for 2026. The first concerns the peninsula's geography, introduced in Chapter 2. The border between the states runs for about 248 kilometers across the waist of the peninsula, and the capital of the South, Seoul, lies only a short distance south of it, so that roughly half of South Korea's population lives within the Seoul capital region. The North's capital, Pyongyang, is less than two hundred kilometers from Seoul, as Chapter 1 observed. The second is legal. Under South Korea's constitution the Republic claims, as a matter of law, sovereignty over the whole peninsula and calls for peaceful unification, a position that has coexisted since 1991 with the two states' separate membership in the United Nations. The third is political. In December 2023, Kim Jong Un declared that the two Koreas were no longer related as one people but as two hostile states, and in January 2024 he called for the removal from the North's constitution of references to cooperation and unification. This chapter examines what those decisions became in 2026.

Students should also keep in mind a methodological point. The historian writing about 2026 cannot know how any of the developments described here will be judged later. The Yoon appeal is not decided. The question of whether Washington and Pyongyang will meet again is open. The recovery in births may continue or reverse. This chapter therefore uses careful language, marks what is established, and reserves strong claims for what the evidence supports.

Section 96.1

The Korean Peninsula in 2026: One Historical Homeland, Two Political Systems

A textbook that ends in a particular year must explain what that year can and cannot show. The year 2026 is the endpoint of this book by editorial decision, as the Authoritative Outline states, not because any historical process concluded in it. Several of the developments described in this chapter had begun before 2026 and were still in motion when it ended. Others, such as the Ninth Party Congress in the North or the verdict against Yoon Suk Yeol in the South, were discrete events whose long-term meaning remained unclear. The chapter therefore does two things at once. It reports what had happened by the late autumn of 2026, and it identifies, as carefully as it can, what historians would need to learn before saying more.

The evidence available for the present differs in kind from the evidence for earlier chapters. For South Korea, it is abundant and, in most respects, open to scrutiny: official statistics published by the Ministry of Data and Statistics and the Bank of Korea, annual opinion surveys, parliamentary hearings, court judgments delivered in public and often broadcast live, a free and competitive press, and the testimony of participants. For North Korea it is much thinner and of a different character. The state publishes its own account of events through the Korean Central News Agency and the newspaper Rodong Sinmun, but it does not publish internationally comparable economic statistics, and foreign journalists have very limited access. Outside observers rely on state media read closely, satellite imagery, intelligence assessments released to the legislatures of South Korea and the United States, trade statistics from partner countries, the statements of foreign officials, and interviews with people who have left the country. Each kind of source has limits. State media shows what a government wishes to say. Intelligence assessments reflect the interests and methods of the agencies that produce them, and are sometimes revised. Defector testimony is valuable but comes from a self-selected group and cannot easily be generalized. Trade data capture only registered exchange.

The chapter's second discipline is symmetry of treatment. The outline asks that North and South Korea be treated as parallel Korean histories, and that neither become merely a foreign-policy problem for the other. This does not mean pretending that the two states are alike. They differ profoundly in their political systems, in their freedoms, in their economies, and in the evidence available about them. It means giving each state's internal history its own sections, asking of each the same analytical questions about politics, economy, society, culture, and international position, and avoiding the habit of describing one only in terms of what it means for the other. For this reason the chapter first treats the shared line between the states, then each state on its own terms, then the relations among them.

Third, the chapter resists teleology. Earlier chapters have shown that the peninsula's political plurality has deep roots and that the present division is nonetheless a twentieth-century creation with specific causes (Chapter 1; Chapters 64 to 66). Describing 2026 as the destination of those causes would make the event of that year seem more determined than it was. Historians can observe that, by 2026, North Korea's constitution no longer mentioned unification, that a majority of South Korean survey respondents regarded the North as a separate state, and that both governments planned for a long period of coexistence. They cannot responsibly conclude from this that Korean unification is impossible, or that it is inevitable. The chapter's closing section returns to this point.

Section 96.2

Competing Sovereignties: How North and South Korea Define the Nation

The two governments do not merely operate different political systems; they define the territory and people to whom their authority extends in different ways. The Republic of Korea's constitution, revised in 1987, defines its territory in Article 3 as the Korean Peninsula and its adjacent islands. Article 4 obliges it to seek unification peacefully under a free and democratic basic order. These clauses coexist with practical diplomatic reality: Seoul exercises effective authority only in the South, maintains its own citizenship laws, and has participated in the United Nations alongside North Korea since 1991. A constitutional claim is therefore neither a map of effective administration nor proof that all Koreans agree on the future state they desire.

North Korea's official position changed sharply under Kim Jong Un. The December 2023 party deliberations and January 2024 speeches rejected a political relationship organized around the idea of one ethnic nation awaiting reunification. At the first session of the Fifteenth Supreme People's Assembly in March 2026, legislators amended the socialist constitution. The version made accessible through the state-run Naenara portal in November 2026 removed earlier unification references and introduced a territorial clause describing the North as bordering China and Russia to the north and the Republic of Korea to the south, together with its corresponding waters and airspace. The document did not establish a precisely agreed land or maritime border, nor did it literally reproduce every hostile-state characterization used in Kim's separate speeches.

The distinction between public declaration and constitutional wording is historically important. A leader's characterization of an adversary can influence party and military policy; a territorial clause has a more specific legal function. Neither should be mistaken for a negotiated settlement between Seoul and Pyongyang. Both states still maintain very different views of sovereignty, even as the North's explicit treatment of the South as a separate neighboring country marks a fundamental departure from the vocabulary of the 1991 Basic Agreement. The historian must also distinguish North Korean official statements, translations by outside agencies, and interpretations by South Korean analysts. Because the November publication of the amended text is so recent, later textual collation may reveal details not visible in early translations.

National identity further complicates legal claims. The Korean word minjok can express a sense of shared ancestry and historical peoplehood, but its political uses are modern and changeable. South Koreans may identify as members of one Korean people while opposing near-term political unification; others may prioritize civic membership in the Republic of Korea. North Koreans inhabit a state whose educational system has long treated its own revolutionary history as the central narrative of national legitimacy. Without independent public-opinion research inside North Korea, no responsible historian can assume that official constitutional language measures the private beliefs of its citizens.

The two states' competing definitions are not merely theoretical. They shape family law, the treatment of arrivals from the North, military planning, the description of maritime areas, schoolbooks, and diplomatic negotiations. They also complicate the meaning of peaceful coexistence: an arrangement for living without violence need not answer every question about ultimate sovereignty. In 2026 the legal and ideological disagreement remained unresolved, even though both governments had strong practical reasons to prevent military escalation.

Historical Vocabulary
Two Hostile States and Peaceful Coexistence

The "two hostile states" doctrine (in Korean, jeokdaejeok du gukga-ron) is the position, announced by Kim Jong Un in December 2023, that the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea are no longer related as members of one nation who share a common aim of unification, but as two states that are hostile to each other and in a relationship that Kim described as one between belligerents. In January 2024 he called for amending the constitution to remove references to unification and cooperation, to define the North's territory, and to designate the South as the principal enemy.

Peaceful coexistence is the term used by the Lee Jae-myung government in South Korea for its policy toward the North. The Unification Ministry describes the aim as redefining inter-Korean relations as one of peaceful coexistence while continuing to pursue unification as a long-term goal. The ministry's minister has said that the two Koreas have in practice already been two states, and the goal is to turn the hostile relationship into a peaceful one, a position that officials in other parts of his government have not always shared.

Why they matter: The two terms, though opposed in tone, share a practical premise: that, for the foreseeable future, the relationship between the states will be one of coexistence of two sovereign entities, not of imminent reunification. The disagreement between them concerns whether that coexistence will be hostile or peaceful and whether unification remains an objective at all.

Potential misunderstanding: South Korea does not recognize North Korea as a foreign state in law. Under the 1991 Basic Agreement, and under the South's own constitution, the relationship is treated as a special one, and Seoul still refers to the North by its geographic name, not its official title. The two states are not "two nations" in the language of South Korean law, and the South Korean government's own use of "peaceful coexistence" does not abandon that legal position.

Section 96.3

The Demilitarized Zone: Geography, Militarization, and the Persistence of Division

Any account of the peninsula in 2026 must begin with the line that separates its two states, because that line expresses in physical form the unfinished settlement of the Korean War. The Korean Armistice Agreement of July 27, 1953, was a military document. It was signed by the commander of the United Nations Command on one side and by the commander of the Korean People's Army and the commander of the Chinese People's Volunteers on the other. South Korea's government, under President Syngman Rhee, had opposed ending the war without unifying the peninsula and did not sign it. The agreement established a ceasefire, set up a Military Demarcation Line, created a Demilitarized Zone extending about two kilometers on each side of that line, arranged for the repatriation of prisoners, and set up a Military Armistice Commission and a Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission to supervise compliance (Chapter 72). A political conference intended to settle the larger issues, held in Geneva in 1954, produced no agreement. The armistice was therefore never superseded by a peace treaty, and in a strict legal sense the war has been suspended, not concluded.

For seven decades the armistice system has proved remarkably durable, though far from peaceful. The frontier has seen infiltrations, assassination attempts, exchanges of fire, a hatchet killing at Panmunjom in 1976, the sinking of the South Korean corvette Cheonan in 2010, and artillery duels on Yeonpyeong Island in the same year, as earlier chapters have recounted. The Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission lost its former permanent Czechoslovak and Polish delegations at Panmunjom, but it remains operational: Swedish and Swiss officers still serve there, observing exercises, inspecting agreed activities and providing armistice training, while Poland participates periodically. Its access to and direct contact with the northern side have greatly diminished. North Korea has repeatedly challenged its own commitment to the armistice. Yet the broad lines of the 1953 settlement have held, and no general war has resumed. Both sides keep very large forces near the frontier, and the United States maintains a military presence in South Korea under the mutual defense treaty signed in 1953, with about 28,500 American personnel in the country according to the figure U.S. officials have long cited.

The September 2026 mine explosion highlighted a problem that rarely attracts attention in political debate: the line itself is not clearly defined. The Military Demarcation Line was fixed in 1953 by a set of 1,292 markers along some 248 kilometers. According to Unification Minister Chung Dong-young, only about 180 of them survive. South Korea's Defense Ministry acknowledged that the baseline used by the United Nations Command and the one used by the South Korean military differ in roughly sixty percent of the border coordinates. Decades of erosion, vegetation, floods, and neglect have blurred the physical line, and in 2024 and 2025 North Korean soldiers working on border construction projects were reported to have crossed it on several occasions, prompting South Korean warning shots. Where a boundary is uncertain and both sides are heavily armed, accidents can have political consequences. South Korea's military attributed the 2026 explosion to mines laid by the North. The Lee government, while seeking accountability, held to its policy of peaceful coexistence and proposed talks on re-surveying the line, while critics urged a firmer response, including the resumption of loudspeaker broadcasts that had been suspended earlier in Lee's presidency. North Korea gave no public indication of accepting talks.

The unresolved armistice also shapes the diplomatic structure of the peninsula. South Korea and the United States conduct combined military exercises each year, which North Korea denounces as preparation for invasion and which the allies describe as defensive. In August 2026, President Donald Trump ordered the Pentagon to substantially reduce that year's summer exercises, and the exercises proceeded in reduced form. Pyongyang did not publicly alter its stance. The episode illustrated how the armistice system's most visible rituals have become bargaining counters in a larger set of relationships, and how the South Korean government could find itself informed of changes in alliance posture by public announcement from Washington. Historical Vocabulary
Armistice, Demilitarized Zone, and Military Demarcation Line

Armistice is a formal agreement by combatants to stop fighting. It is a military arrangement and differs from a peace treaty, which settles political questions and ends a state of war in law. The Korean Armistice Agreement of 1953 was signed by military commanders, not heads of state, and its own text anticipated a later political settlement that never came.

The Military Demarcation Line (MDL) is the line fixed in 1953 where the two sides' forces stood at the ceasefire, roughly 248 kilometers long. The Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) is the buffer, about four kilometers wide, that extends two kilometers on either side of the MDL, in which armed forces and military installations are in principle prohibited. The line does not follow the 38th parallel; it runs south of the parallel on the western side and north of it on the eastern side, reflecting the battlefield positions of 1953 and the outcome of the war rather than the earlier occupation boundary.

Why it matters: The DMZ is simultaneously one of the world's most militarized borders and, because human activity has been restricted for decades, an unusual wildlife refuge. The term "demilitarized" is therefore misleading in both directions. The surrounding area is heavily armed, and the zone itself, while subject to armistice restrictions, has not been free of weapons or incidents.

Potential misunderstanding: The DMZ is often described as the "border" between North and South Korea. Legally it is a ceasefire line, not an internationally recognized boundary, and neither Korean government accepts it as a permanent frontier. The South Korean constitution still claims the whole peninsula, and North Korea's revised constitution of 2026, according to South Korean analysts, defines the North's territory by reference to the area north of the line and its adjoining waters.

Section 96.4

Shared History, Different Historical Memories

Seven decades of separate political life left each Korea with its own institutions, public culture, and language practices, while a great deal of inheritance remained in common. This section considers both, because the textbook's purpose is neither to exaggerate the separation nor to deny it. The history in Volume I establishes the depth of what is shared: the writing system devised in the fifteenth century, the Goguryeo tombs, the Goryeo capital of Kaesong, the Buddhist and Confucian heritage, folk songs, food, and a language tradition that Chapter 1 described. All of these lie in a pre-1945 past that both states claim.

Language provides an instructive case. Spoken Korean in the North and South remains mutually intelligible, as the outline of this textbook assumes, but the standard languages have drifted apart in vocabulary, spelling conventions, pronunciation of some sounds, and the treatment of foreign loanwords. The South readily adopts English-derived words, whereas the North has promoted native or coined replacements, and the North has enforced a standard based on the Pyongyang speech it calls "cultured language." The two states also keep different calendars of commemoration, even of shared cultural symbols. South Korea marks Hangeul Day in October, and North Korea in January. The two states use different official names for the same peninsula, as Chapter 1 explained, and different romanization systems for their languages. People who have left the North and settled in the South often describe difficulties in understanding everyday speech, especially vocabulary borrowed from English, in addition to the social and economic difficulties of adjustment.

Political memory has diverged more sharply than language. The two states tell the colonial period, the founding of their governments in 1948, and the Korean War in incompatible ways. South Korea's official memory treats the war as a North Korean invasion in June 1950 and emphasizes the sacrifice of its own and allied soldiers and the civilian suffering that Chapter 71 recounted. North Korea's official account describes a "Fatherland Liberation War" against American and South Korean aggression and emphasizes American bombing. Each state's historiography of anti-colonial resistance draws on different movements, as Chapters 59 and 63 showed: the Provisional Government and nationalist networks in the South's narrative, and Kim Il Sung's guerrilla activity in Manchuria in the North's. These contrasting narratives mean that, even when citizens of the two states refer to the same events, they often attach different meanings to them. Students should recognize here that the nationalist narratives that both states promote are historical phenomena in their own right, as Chapter 1 stated, and not neutral descriptions of the past.

Culture offers several examples of continued sharing. The folk song Arirang, the title of BTS's 2026 album, has been recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage in separate nominations by South Korea in 2012 and North Korea in 2014. In 2018 the two states made a joint nomination for traditional Korean wrestling, the first such joint submission. Both countries' scholars study the same classical texts and the same archaeological sites. Many families, though dispersed, preserve memories of a shared hometown, and the diminishing number of separated families remains a link of personal memory. These continuities do not mean that the two societies are the same, and they do not guarantee political convergence. They show that a shared past remains available to both, and that the two states' divergence is partial.

The claims made in schoolbooks and public ceremonies about Gojoseon, Goguryeo, Silla, Goryeo, and Joseon illustrate a shared archive used for different projects. Ancient polities are often presented retrospectively as precursors of the modern Korean nation, yet Volume I showed that their political reach, loyalties, and cultural affiliations did not match present state boundaries. The same sites and figures can be celebrated in both Koreas without implying agreement over what their history means. Goguryeo, for instance, belongs to a wider northeast Asian past and cannot be reduced to a modern competition over exclusive national ownership.

The experience of colonization deepens this complexity. Korean-language movements, independence activism, exile networks, and the provisional government formed part of a common struggle against Japanese empire, but later North and South Korean narratives privileged different actors and legitimating lineages. The North's founding mythology assigned special centrality to the guerrilla struggle associated with Kim Il Sung. The South's historical traditions have emphasized the March First Movement, the Republic's provisional institutions, anti-colonial campaigns and democratic state formation. These emphases have shifted with successive governments, and neither country's official memory is a direct substitute for the wider archival record.

The Korean War is more divisive still. Political narratives of its outbreak, causes, foreign intervention, sacrifice, and aftermath differ sharply. South Korean scholarship and public debate have expanded significantly since democratization, including reconsideration of civilian killings and state violence. North Korean public accounts remain closely tied to the party's legitimacy and portray the war through the state's prescribed anti-imperialist frame. Families who lost relatives or homes may preserve memories that fit neither official story. A history attentive to evidence does not equate incompatible factual claims merely because both governments make them.

Religious practice, literature, Hangeul, food traditions, folk performance, and monuments provide other forms of continuity. Yet continuities do not abolish profound differences in how society is governed or how people encounter their past. This chapter's comparative method therefore treats common inheritance as a matter for investigation, not proof that a particular constitutional outcome must follow. The resulting tension—shared historical materials and diverging institutional memories—is one of the defining conditions of the peninsula in 2026.

Section 96.5

South Korean Democracy after the Constitutional Crises

South Korean democracy in 2026 is best described by a combination of two observations that are both supported by evidence. The first is that its institutions responded to an attempt to suspend constitutional order, and that the response, including a unanimous Constitutional Court decision, a presidential election, and criminal prosecutions, took place largely within legal channels (Chapter 95). The second is that the underlying political conflict that produced the crisis was not resolved by the response. It continued, in different forms, in the courts, in the electoral system, and in the relationship between the two largest parties.

The legal consequences for the former president and his associates unfolded through 2026. On February 19, a panel of the Seoul Central District Court, presided over by Judge Ji Gwi-yeon, found Yoon Suk Yeol guilty of leading an insurrection by deploying military forces to the National Assembly on December 3, 2024, with the intention, the court found, of obstructing and paralyzing its functions. Prosecutors had sought the death penalty. The court imposed life imprisonment, the only other penalty available under the statute for leading an insurrection. South Korea has not carried out an execution since 1997. The proceedings were broadcast live. The court also sentenced Yoon's former defense minister, Kim Yong-hyun, to thirty years. Yoon denied the charge, argued that martial law was a constitutional warning against an obstructive opposition, and appealed. In a separate case he had been given a five-year sentence in January 2026 for obstructing authorities' attempts to arrest him, and on appeal in April the Seoul High Court raised that sentence to seven years. In July, the Supreme Court upheld the seven-year term. In a further case, a court sentenced him to thirty years in prison over the sending of drones into North Korea to create a crisis before the martial-law declaration; press reports did not describe that judgment as final. In November 2026 the Seoul High Court upheld an eighteen-month suspended sentence for election-law violations committed in the 2022 campaign, a ruling that, if it stands, could have consequences for his party, and Yoon's lawyers said they would appeal to the Supreme Court. The appeal in the insurrection case itself was scheduled for decision in January 2027, after the endpoint of this textbook, and he was reported to face several further trials. Former prime minister Han Duck-soo, in May 2026, had his sentence reduced on appeal to fifteen years while the court upheld his conviction for playing a role in the insurrection.

Students should note carefully what these facts do and do not establish. A first-instance verdict is a finding by one court, subject to appeal, and the insurrection judgment was not final in November 2026. South Korea's judicial process has multiple stages, and the outcome of the appeals will determine what the legal record finally says. What can be said is that, as of late 2026, the judiciary had convicted the former head of state of serious offenses connected with martial law in more than one proceeding, and had in one case reached final judgment. Historians will also eventually weigh the proceedings' fairness and legitimacy, an issue on which Yoon's supporters and defenders dispute the verdicts. Yoon's lawyers described the insurrection trial as a formality leading to a predetermined conclusion. Supporters gathered outside the court in February, while critics gathered nearby, and the sharply opposed public reaction illustrated the continued polarization of South Korean politics. Other presidents of South Korea had been prosecuted after leaving office, as press accounts noted, so the pattern is not new, but the gravity of the charges in this case is without precedent in the republic's democratic period.

The political landscape under President Lee Jae-myung, who took office on June 4, 2025, was shaped by these proceedings and by the Democratic Party's control of the National Assembly. Lee's government brought a notable administrative change by returning the presidential office to the Cheong Wa Dae complex, the traditional presidential residence and office, after Yoon had moved it to Yongsan. The government pursued domestic priorities that included economic recovery, social policy, and, as the Democratic Party had long promised, reform of the prosecution service. Disagreement inside the governing camp also continued, and in November 2026 the press described a feud within the ruling party that had erupted into an open challenge to the party's own leader. The Democratic Party's dominance in the legislature gave Lee considerable power to legislate, but the president is limited to a single five-year term under the 1987 constitution, so the political calendar includes a presidential election in 2030.

The nationwide local elections of June 3, 2026, supplied the first broad electoral test of the new political order. The results do not support a simple narrative. The Democratic Party won twelve of the sixteen provincial-level offices, including Gyeonggi, the Chungcheong and Jeolla provinces, Busan, and Incheon. The People Power Party retained four, Seoul, Daegu, and the North and South Gyeongsang provinces. In the capital, the incumbent Seoul mayor Oh Se-hoon of the People Power Party won a fifth term with 49.22 percent of the vote against the Democratic Party's Chong Won-o by a margin of 1.15 percentage points, about 60,000 votes, even as the Democratic Party won seventeen of Seoul's twenty-five district mayoralties. Analysts concluded that neither major party could claim an unequivocal victory. The election also showed lingering distrust of the voting process among some citizens. A shortage of ballot papers disrupted voting at more than a dozen polling stations in Seoul, and protesters alleging election fraud blocked the counting of two ballot boxes for a time. Election officials completed the final tally two days later, and it did not significantly alter the results.

Three features of South Korean democracy in 2026 deserve emphasis in a historical account. First, the constitutional system that emerged from the June 1987 struggle (Chapter 82) proved capable of constraining an executive who attempted to bypass it. Troops sent to the National Assembly on December 3, 2024, did not prevent legislators from convening, the Assembly voted to end martial law within hours, and the Constitutional Court later upheld the president's impeachment unanimously. These outcomes depended on the actions of many individuals, including legislators, soldiers, civil servants, judges, and citizens who gathered outside the Assembly, and on institutional rules established in earlier decades. Second, the crisis exposed a conflict over the meaning of democratic legitimacy. Yoon claimed to be defending the country against "anti-state" forces, a phrase the courts rejected and that reflected the long influence of anti-communist rhetoric in South Korean political language (Chapters 74 and 78). Third, the experience had not ended polarization. Elections and the public reaction to the verdicts show an electorate sharply divided between the two camps, and mutual distrust among their partisans remained high.

The relevance of this history for the question of the two Koreas is substantial. South Korean politics has long been organized in part by competing ideas about the North, and in 2026 those competing ideas remained important. The Lee government's approach of peaceful coexistence and willingness to talk, and the conservative opposition's emphasis on deterrence and reciprocity, are not merely foreign-policy preferences. They are part of the domestic political identities of the two sides. Section 96.18 returns to how this domestic divide shapes South Korean attitudes toward unification.

Section 96.6

The Korean Economic Transformation: From Postwar Poverty to Technological Power

South Korea's economy in 2026 presents a paradox that earlier chapters help explain. By the standards of per capita income and technological sophistication, it is among the world's advanced economies, with a gross domestic product usually ranked in the low teens of national economies and a GDP per capita estimated by the International Monetary Fund at roughly $43,500 for 2026. Yet its growth in recent years had been modest, domestic consumption had been subdued, and the construction sector had been declining. Then, in 2026, a surge in global demand for the memory chips used in artificial intelligence computing drove an export boom that transformed the year's statistics.

The scale of the boom is documented in the Bank of Korea's data. The economy grew 1.8 percent in the first quarter of 2026 relative to the previous quarter, its strongest quarterly growth in several years, and 1.0 percent in the third quarter, bringing cumulative growth over the first three quarters to 3.6 percent. By comparison, annual growth in 2025 had been about 1.1 percent, according to figures cited in the press. South Korean exports reached a record $120.9 billion in September 2026, up more than 83 percent from a year earlier, with semiconductor exports alone exceeding $60 billion in that month. The two companies at the center of this development, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, are the principal producers of high-bandwidth memory, the stacked memory architecture that advanced AI processors require, and together supply the large majority of the world's market for it. The Bank of Korea's governor was reported in mid-2026 to have estimated that the chip boom alone would add roughly 0.7 percentage points to the year's growth. These developments grew from the long-term strategy of the developmental state and chaebol described in Chapters 75 and 76 and the later digital transformation in Chapter 87. In the 1980s and 1990s, state support for the electronics industry and chaebol investment in memory fabrication created the base that the AI era exploited.

Historians should be careful not to treat this boom simply as a triumph. The same data reveal a high degree of concentration. According to press reports, semiconductors accounted for close to half of South Korean goods exports in some months of 2026, and the stock market's composition was heavily weighted toward the two leading chip companies, by some counts for half or more of the main index. A national economy that relies so heavily on one product category for growth and export revenue is exposed to a downturn in that category, and the boom itself rests on the investment plans of a small number of foreign technology companies. Domestic indicators were more mixed than the headline numbers. Private consumption was subdued and construction investment was weak even during the export surge, as reports on the second-quarter data noted. The gains from the boom were also unevenly distributed. The extent to which they would reach households, small firms, and regional economies, and how far they would address the social problems examined in Chapter 91, was not yet clear in 2026.

The boom took place against a backdrop of trade and security negotiations with the United States. In October 2025, at the APEC summit in Gyeongju, President Trump and President Lee concluded a trade arrangement later set out in a Joint Fact Sheet of November 14, 2025. Under it, the United States lowered its tariffs on South Korean goods, including automobiles, from 25 percent to 15 percent, in exchange for a South Korean commitment to invest $350 billion in the United States, including $150 billion in shipbuilding and $200 billion in strategic sectors. The fact sheet also recorded American approval for South Korea to build nuclear-powered submarines and support for a process that could allow South Korea to enrich uranium and reprocess spent fuel for civilian purposes. By late 2026 the implementation of these commitments was uncertain. Negotiations on the nuclear cooperation provisions had stalled since an initial round in June, South Korea's foreign minister said that Trump's order to shorten the joint exercises was intended in part to press Seoul to speed up its investment, and the results of the November 3 U.S. midterm elections introduced new uncertainty about congressional support. President Lee instructed his government to prepare for several scenarios and to broaden South Korean contacts across the American political system. The first project under the investment pledge, a gas-fired power plant in Texas intended to supply data centers, had already come under political scrutiny in the United States.

These developments show why historians of the contemporary Korean economy distinguish between dependence and interdependence. South Korea in 2026 was neither a dependent client state nor an economically autonomous one. Its chips supplied a global industry on which major American technology firms relied, which gave Seoul real bargaining weight, and its large investment pledge to the United States also gave Washington leverage over its policy. The same duality marked its relationship with China, the country's largest trading partner, and with Japan. This is a form of economic power shaped by integration into global production networks, and it differs from the autarkic model of North Korea's official Juche ideology (Chapter 77), though North Korea's economic position has also become more dependent on foreign partners than the ideology suggests, as Section 96.10 shows.

Finally, the chaebol themselves continued to occupy the central position they have held since the 1960s, though with changes. Family-controlled conglomerates remained dominant in industry and finance, and the political economy of their relationship with the government had changed from the state-directed credit allocation of the Park era. President Lee Jae-myung, in November 2026, described the facilitation of hostile takeovers as the most important tool for normalizing the Korean stock market, a reference to the persistent problem of minority-shareholder protection and corporate governance in family-controlled groups. Students should recognize here a long-term theme of this textbook: the strengths and distortions of chaebol-led capitalism examined in Chapter 76 were still being debated in 2026, now in the context of an AI-driven export boom.

Section 96.7

The Korean Wave: Culture, Digital Society, and International Influence

The outline for this textbook asks that South Korea's cultural history be told as the movement from importing American, Japanese, and other cultural forms, to adapting them, to exporting Korean cultural products globally, and that the narrative distinguish foreign influence from cultural dependency. By 2026 that movement was largely complete in its main outlines, and the year supplied several vivid markers of it. Chapters 88 through 90 have traced how it happened: the staged opening to Japanese popular culture from the late 1990s, the construction of the idol training system by SM, YG, and JYP, the first regional wave of television dramas and music in Asia, the global breakthroughs of Psy, BTS, BLACKPINK, Parasite, and Squid Game, and the spread of webtoons, games, food, and cosmetics. This section does not retell that history. It asks what 2026 shows about the result.

Three events are especially instructive. The first was the return of BTS. After all seven members completed the compulsory military service required of South Korean men, the group released an album called Arirang on March 20, 2026, its first full-group release in nearly four years, and held a free comeback concert at Gwanghwamun Square in central Seoul the next day, an event reported as the largest public concert held in South Korea. A world tour covering thirty-four regions was scheduled from April 2026 to March 2027. The album's title is the name of a folk song known across the peninsula, a song sung by Koreans in both states and by Korean communities abroad. The choice illustrates a feature of the cultural export economy that students should note: the global product was marketed, in part, through an explicit appeal to a shared Korean heritage, and in that sense the commercial culture of the South drew on a tradition the two Koreas hold in common.

The second event was the success of KPop Demon Hunters. The animated film, released on Netflix in June 2025, became the most-watched film in the platform's history, and in March 2026 it won an Academy Award for Best Animated Feature at the ninety-eighth Academy Awards. It also won recognition for its songs. The film is a useful case for the analytical distinction the outline requires, because its production was transnational. It was made by an American animation studio for a global streaming company, and it was directed by a Korean-Canadian filmmaker and an American co-director, but its setting, its story of a girl group, its use of Korean folklore, food, and the idol system, and much of its music drew directly on Korean popular culture and on the industry that South Korea had built. The film therefore shows neither a purely Korean product nor one made by outsiders about Korea. It shows a form of cultural exchange in which Korean cultural forms had become a global resource that others drew on, alongside Korean-produced works such as the musical Maybe Happy Ending, which won the Tony Award for Best Musical in 2025. A third development was less visible than the first two: South Korean governments continued to treat cultural export as an object of public policy, as they had since the 1990s, so that the commercial success of the industry coexisted with state promotion of it.

Historians should distinguish carefully between cultural influence and cultural dependency. A society is culturally dependent when its own production is weak, its audiences consume mostly imported products, and it has limited ability to reshape what it imports. South Korea's own history up to the 1990s had elements of this condition, particularly with respect to American popular music and film and, under restrictions, Japanese culture. By 2026 the relationship was reversed in at least one important respect: Korean products were consumed in volume across Asia, North America, Europe, Latin America, and the Middle East, and the industries that produced them were exporting methods as well as content, including training systems, production formats, and fan-engagement platforms. At the same time, the transition did not remove foreign influence from South Korean culture. American and Japanese genres, global platforms, and international capital remained deeply embedded in South Korean entertainment, and the dominance of a few global streaming and music platforms meant that Korean creators remained dependent on foreign distribution infrastructure even as they supplied content to it. The more accurate description is a position of mutual influence in which Korea occupied a more powerful and creative place than it had a generation earlier.

Cultural exports also raise questions for the history of the divided peninsula. South Korean popular culture has circulated, in violation of North Korean law, among some North Koreans, as defectors have reported, and the North's leadership has responded with laws and enforcement campaigns against foreign, and especially South Korean, media, as Section 96.11 notes. The cultural attractiveness of the South thus has a political dimension that South Korean governments have sometimes tried to use and that the North treats as a threat. Evidence about how many North Koreans consume such material, and with what effects, is limited and rests heavily on the testimony of those who left. Historians therefore should treat claims about the transformative influence of South Korean culture on the North with caution, while recognizing that Pyongyang's own repressive response is a sign that it regards the influence as significant.

The social and demographic problems of South Korea examined in Chapter 91 remained severe in 2026, but the statistics for births changed in an unexpected direction. It rose to 0.75 in 2024 and to 0.80 in 2025, according to the Ministry of Data and Statistics. The number of births rose from about 238,300 in 2024 to about 254,300 in 2025 in the ministry's final figures, an increase of 6.7 percent and the highest total since 2021. The rise was driven largely by an increase in marriages, which grew by 14.8 percent in 2024 and by about 8 percent in 2025, since in South Korea almost all births occur within marriage. A large cohort of people born in the early 1990s was also passing through the ages at which marriage and first births are most likely, and the rate of births among women in their late thirties reached the highest level since records began. Local governments and the national government had expanded financial support for housing, childbirth, and child care, and officials attributed part of the change to these policies, though how much they contributed is difficult to isolate.

The consequences of low fertility continued to accumulate despite the rebound. Deaths in 2025, about 363,400, exceeded births by about 109,000 in preliminary figures, and the population declined naturally for the sixth consecutive year since 2020. The population over sixty-five reached one-fifth of the total at the end of 2024, making South Korea what demographers call a "super-aged" society, and the share continued to rise. The regional pattern was sharply uneven. South Jeolla Province had the highest regional fertility rate, 1.09, followed by Sejong, the planned administrative city, at 1.06, while Seoul's rate, although up from 0.55 in 2023 to 0.63 in 2025, remained the lowest in the country. The contrast is consistent with explanations that point to housing costs, local family-support programs, and the intensity of educational and occupational competition, all of which are most acute in the dense capital region (Chapter 91). No single factor explains the regional pattern, and the statistics alone cannot show which causes matter most.

Other social changes in South Korea in 2026 belong to the same picture. The country had become more ethnically and nationally diverse as migrants, students, and workers arrived in larger numbers. Roughly 2.6 million foreign residents lived in South Korea in recent years, about five percent of the population, and the government in 2026 was debating questions of citizenship, labor migration, and the integration of multicultural families. Educational competition remained intense and centered on the university entrance examination, the Suneung, and the private tutoring academies, the hagwon. Gender conflict, youth employment, and housing costs remained active political issues. The population's attitude toward the North, discussed in Section 96.18, was itself shaped by generation, with younger South Koreans, who are fewer in number than their elders and face greater economic competition, considerably less likely to describe unification as necessary.

The demographic story is also a story about the North, though much less is known about it. North Korea's population is estimated at roughly 26 million, although the state does not release reliable recent census data, and outside estimates rely on partial surveys, United Nations projections, and indirect indicators. What is known is that North Korea's fertility rate is reported by the United Nations to be well above South Korea's, though still below replacement by most estimates, and that the country's population is younger than the South's. These differences have implications for any future relationship between the two, but the uncertainty of the North Korean data makes comparison difficult, and the chapter makes no firm claims beyond the evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Total Fertility Rate

The total fertility rate (TFR) is a statistical estimate of the average number of children a woman would bear over her lifetime if she experienced, at each age, the birth rates observed in a given year. It is calculated from the age-specific birth rates of that year and is a snapshot of current behavior, not a measurement of the number of children that any real group of women has actually had or will have.

Demographers generally regard a TFR of about 2.1 as the "replacement" level in societies with low mortality, the level at which each generation would approximately replace itself in the absence of migration. South Korea's rate has been far below that level since the early 2000s. Its 2023 figure of 0.72 was the lowest recorded for any country with comparable statistics, and its 2025 figure of 0.80 remained the lowest among the thirty-eight members of the OECD, whose average for 2024 was about 1.4.

Why it matters: A TFR below replacement, sustained for decades, leads to a shrinking and aging population. Because the number of potential parents in the next generation depends on the number of births in this one, the effect compounds.

Potential misunderstanding: A rise from 0.72 to 0.80 is a real change but a small one in absolute terms, and the TFR can move for reasons unrelated to women's lifetime intentions, such as delayed births being made up in later years. Two years of increase cannot establish a lasting reversal.

Section 96.9

The Kim Dynasty and the Political Order of North Korea

Turning to North Korea requires, first, a statement about perspective. The outline requires that North Korea's history be treated as a Korean history in its own right, with Soviet origins, Chinese interaction, Korean nationalism, Juche, hereditary leadership, a military-first phase, marketization, information control, nuclearization, and a changing set of external relationships, and not as a generic communism or as a problem for the South. This section takes up the most politically significant events of 2026 in the North, the Ninth Party Congress and the constitutional revision, and asks what they reveal about its political system.

The Ninth Congress of the Workers' Party of Korea met in Pyongyang from February 19 to 25, 2026. Party congresses are the party's highest decision-making body, and the Ninth was the first since January 2021. About 5,000 delegates attended, according to analysts who followed the reports. On the fourth day, the congress re-elected Kim Jong Un as general secretary, and the decision was announced as unanimous. The congress revised the party rules, and more than half of the members of the Central Committee were replaced, according to South Korean reports of the state media announcement. Kim Yo Jong, the leader's sister, was promoted from deputy department director to director of the party's General Affairs Department, a sign of her continued importance. The congress adopted a plan for strengthening the party and advancing what it called socialist construction over the five years from 2026 through 2030, and it unveiled a new five-year program for weapons development.

Analysts differed in how they interpreted the congress. Some, including authors writing for the research site 38 North, argued that it formalized the leader's personal predominance, and that it effectively abandoned what had been called the Kim Il Sung–Kim Jong Il line, the party's earlier framing of its program as the continuation of the first two leaders' work, in favor of a program defined by Kim Jong Un's own leadership. Others noted that the state media's published summaries did not include full texts, that the military section was shorter on specifics than at the previous congress, and that the congress gave no figures on the achievement of the previous five-year plan's targets. The Seoul-based Asan Institute argued that the failure to report outcomes suggested that the second five-year economic plan had effectively failed. The Unification Ministry in Seoul assessed that the congress focused mainly on reinforcing Kim's leadership and on domestic messaging. All of these interpretations rest on partial evidence, and students should treat them as informed readings of limited material, not as established facts about the inner workings of the party.

The congress was followed by elections to the 15th Supreme People's Assembly on March 15, 2026, and by its first session on March 22 and 23. North Korean elections offer voters a single candidate per constituency, and state media reported the results as overwhelmingly favorable to candidates of the ruling party. In a departure that reference sources noted as the first since 1957, state media reported that some votes were cast against candidates, though the reports did not change the character of the process. At the first session, Kim Jong Un was re-elected president of the State Affairs Commission, Jo Yong-won became the commission's first vice president and chair of the Assembly's standing committee, and Pak Thae-song continued as premier and vice president. Kim delivered a speech in which, in the words reported by South Korean press, he said North Korea would officially treat South Korea as its most hostile state. He also described the country's nuclear-armed status as irreversible.

The session adopted a revised constitution. The text was not initially disclosed, but South Korean analysts obtained a version and reported changes in May. In November 2026 North Korea itself posted the amended constitution through its Naenara portal, allowing comparisons with the earlier disclosure. The revision removed references to unification and to the unity of the Korean nation. It added a territorial clause that, according to the analysts, defines only the North's own region as its territory. It formally identified the president of the State Affairs Commission as the head of state and, for the first time, placed that office above the Supreme People's Assembly in the constitutional ranking, and it removed the Assembly's power to dismiss the president. The constitution also continued to provide that the president may command the nuclear forces and delegate authority to use nuclear weapons. In November 2026, North Korea posted the full revised text on a state propaganda website, which South Korean observers read as a deliberate public confirmation of the two-state approach. Because analysts' accounts of the text came through South Korean officials and press before the North's own publication, students should note the sequence of the evidence.

The question of hereditary succession also drew attention. North Korea has not officially identified a successor to Kim Jong Un, who was born in the early 1980s, and state media has referred to his daughter, whose name is generally given by outside observers as Kim Ju Ae, as his beloved or noble child instead of naming her. She has appeared at military ceremonies, missile launches, and official visits since November 2022, and South Korea's National Intelligence Service has progressively raised its assessment. In April 2026, its director said it would be appropriate to regard her as a successor on the basis of what he described as credible intelligence, and in September the agency told legislators that she appeared to be effectively in the stage of being designated as successor, with her media exposure intended to establish her as a leader in the minds of North Koreans. Other analysts urged caution. The Asan Institute noted that she was not officially designated at the Ninth Congress and that Kim Yo Jong's promotion suggested that hereditary succession was not at its final stage, and that for the time being she might be more a symbolic figure projecting the regime's long-term durability than a substantive successor. These are inferences from public appearances and agency statements, and neither can be verified from independent sources. What can be said is that the principle of hereditary leadership, established in the 1970s and 1980s (Chapter 77), had not been abandoned, and that the revised constitution further concentrated authority in the office of the leader.

Section 96.10

North Korea’s Economy: State Planning, Markets, Inequality, and Survival

North Korea's economy in 2026 resists easy summary because the state does not publish internationally comparable statistics. The best available systematic estimates come from the Bank of Korea in Seoul, which constructs annual figures from trade data, intelligence, and other indicators. By its estimates, North Korea's real GDP grew by 3.1 percent in 2023, by 3.7 percent in 2024, and by 3.5 percent in 2025, the third consecutive year of growth above three percent. Bank officials attributed the growth to expanded cooperation with Russia, increased trade with China, and state-directed construction and industrial projects, and they reported particularly strong growth in heavy and chemical industries and in construction. These estimates followed a period of sharp contraction after the tightening of international sanctions in 2017 and during the COVID-19 border closures, so the recent growth partly represents recovery from a low base. An American analyst noted in October 2026 that North Korean growth may have been understated because of income from Russia, trade with China, and cybercrime, and also emphasized that a higher percentage growth rate from a small economy does not mean that its size or living standards are approaching those of the South.

Several components of this growth were politically driven. In January 2024 the leadership announced the 20×10 regional development policy, a ten-year effort to build modern light-industry factories in twenty cities and counties each year, which was incorporated into the 2026–2030 plan. Housing construction in Pyongyang and in rural and mining regions was a second priority. Both programs aim to produce visible results with relatively limited fiscal outlay, and analysts have noted that they respond to a long-standing problem of urban-rural inequality in a system where the capital has been privileged. At the same time, the Ninth Congress did not provide targets or productivity figures for the previous plan, and analysts at the Asan Institute concluded that the 2021–2025 plan had probably failed to meet its goals. Kim told the Supreme People's Assembly in March 2026 that industrial output should rise by about one and a half times during the new plan. That target would, if achieved, require sustained inputs and favorable external conditions that cannot be assumed.

The external sources of North Korea's recent economic improvement are historically significant because they contradict the self-reliant image of Juche (Chapter 77). Sales of artillery shells and missiles to Russia in exchange for food, energy, hard currency, and military technology produced revenue that South Korean research institutes estimated, in a report published in March 2026, at between roughly $7.7 billion and $14.4 billion for the period from August 2023 to the end of 2025, an estimate with a wide range reflecting the difficulty of measurement. Trade with China, the country's largest trading partner, expanded after the post-pandemic reopening, and flights and train services between the two countries resumed. The effect of such flows has been to reduce the practical impact of the international sanctions regime on the North, whose strengthening Russia and China have declined to support at the United Nations Security Council. Yet dependence on arms revenue is also a source of vulnerability, because it ties the economy to Russia's war and to Russia's continuing need for North Korean shells.

The role of markets, which Chapter 83 traced from the jangmadang of the 1990s, remains important but is a matter on which evidence is limited. The Kim Jong Un government has tried to manage rather than abolish markets, to channel trade into state-controlled outlets, and to maintain political control over the border and over information. The state has adopted laws since 2020 that criminalize the consumption and distribution of foreign, and especially South Korean, films, television, and publications, and that punish the use of South Korean speech patterns, measures that reflect the leadership's concern about cultural influence and that Chapter 92 discusses. The state's information control also extends to restricting domestic travel, monitoring communications, and, since the pandemic, tightening border security. The effectiveness of these controls is difficult to measure from outside. Defectors report that foreign media continue to circulate, while the severity of penalties reported by those who have left suggests the authorities regard the stakes as high.

For students, the main analytical point is that North Korea in 2026 was neither economically static nor on the verge of collapse, nor was it the self-sufficient economy its ideology describes. It was a state with a sharply stratified, partly marketized economy sustained by new external partners, by the state's ability to mobilize resources for chosen projects, and by a repressive system of political and informational control, whose growth depended heavily on conditions, in particular the continuation of the Ukraine war and the tolerance of its patrons, that it could not itself guarantee. Whether the growth of 2023 to 2025 would translate into improved welfare for ordinary North Koreans, as opposed to resources for the military and the leadership's priority projects, cannot be established from the evidence now available.

Section 96.11

Information Control, Surveillance, Human Rights, and Everyday Life

A discussion of North Korean markets is incomplete without a separate account of information and social control. The ruling party, internal-security agencies, neighborhood surveillance networks, and political education institutions regulate many of the ordinary channels through which people elsewhere learn about public affairs. State television, newspapers, and approved intranet services emphasize leadership messages and achievements, while unauthorized contact with outside media can carry severe penalties. Restriction of information does not necessarily mean that every citizen believes official accounts. It means that obtaining, exchanging, and acting on alternatives is dangerous and that outside scholars cannot readily measure private opinion.

Classifications associated with songbun—family background and presumed political reliability—have historically affected access to education, employment, residence, and other opportunities. Their application has changed, and money, patronage, military service, local relationships, and market activities may alter a household's prospects. The existence of these informal differences should caution against presenting the country as a uniform society governed exclusively through central plans. Pyongyang's privileged housing projects and consumer amenities have not erased disparities between the capital, provincial centers, and rural districts. Nor can a photograph of a newly completed street establish average living standards across the country.

The evidence is difficult. The United Nations Commission of Inquiry's 2014 investigation documented a system of serious abuses, including political prison camps and severe restrictions on movement and expression. Later reports, survivor testimonies, imagery, and state documents help establish continuities and changes, but researchers must account for the time and place of testimony, translation, memory, and the inability to conduct open household surveys. Not every defector has lived in the same social circumstances, and a testimony about one locality cannot automatically describe the whole peninsula north of the DMZ. At the same time, independent verification challenges do not justify dismissing convergent evidence of repression.

Markets and informal communications produce adaptation as well as vulnerability. Traders may rely on trusted intermediaries, kinship, limited cross-border networks, and tacit arrangements with officials. Some North Koreans have encountered South Korean dramas and music through clandestine storage devices or personal networks, but the scale and timing of this circulation are uncertain. The authorities have periodically tightened punishment for unapproved cultural materials. Such restrictions reveal state anxiety about competing influences; they do not by themselves demonstrate how ordinary viewers interpreted a foreign program or how many watched it.

Daily life also involves activities that cannot be reduced to politics: children attending school, relatives organizing household work, farmers confronting weather, commuters finding transport, and people caring for older family members. State-provided housing, ration systems, market purchases, and access to health care differ widely across communities and periods. This chapter should not create imaginary diary entries to make these lives visible. Instead, a historically responsible comparison identifies the kinds of sources available—official regulations, satellite photographs, carefully contextualized interviews and aid-agency reports—and marks what those sources cannot tell us about 2026.

The contrast with South Korea is not only one of wealth. The South has competitive elections, private media, travel rights, judicial challenges to government action, and a highly connected digital economy; it also faces problems of privacy, inequality, political polarization, and concentrated corporate power. Comparing the two societies requires recognizing these distinct political conditions and resisting a simple ranking that substitutes for history. The most illuminating question is how institutions shape what people can choose, what they can know, and which risks attend ordinary decisions.

Section 96.12

The Nuclear-Armed State: Military Power, Deterrence, and National Strategy

North Korea's nuclear weapons and conventional forces were central to its political position in 2026. Understanding them requires attention to what is known, what is estimated, and what is merely asserted. Pyongyang's own statements assert that its nuclear status is permanent. Kim Jong Un said in February 2026 that the country's status as a nuclear state is completely and absolutely irreversible, and he told the Party Congress that the arsenal would never be negotiable, while leaving open the possibility of talks with the United States only if Washington accepted the North's nuclear status. Outside governments disagree with the premise. The United States has said that its goal remains the complete denuclearization of North Korea, and the White House said in May 2026 that Presidents Trump and Xi had confirmed their shared goal of denuclearizing North Korea, a statement that Kim Yo Jong dismissed in June.

The size of the arsenal is uncertain. The North has conducted six nuclear tests, the most recent on September 3, 2017, and has said nothing about its holdings. Independent analysts in early 2026 estimated that it had about fifty nuclear warheads and enough fissile material to build roughly forty more. In August 2026 President Trump put the number at fifty-seven, the first time he had cited a specific figure; the basis for the number was not explained. Satellite imagery from March 2026 showed the continued expansion of a uranium-enrichment complex, consistent with Kim's call for more and larger nuclear weapons, and the International Atomic Energy Agency warned of very serious advances in the North's nuclear efforts. Since the United Nations Panel of Experts that monitored sanctions was disbanded in 2024 after a Russian veto, the international community has had a weaker institutional basis for verifying the North's activity and its sanctions evasion. The United States' 2026 annual threat assessment concluded that North Korea's forces could strike the American homeland and that Pyongyang was committed to expanding its strategic weapons, though it also judged that North Korea was likely to remain deterred from using the weapons offensively by American and allied capabilities. Intelligence services in Washington and Seoul had also assessed that the Punggye-ri test site was ready for a seventh test, awaiting a political decision. No seventh test had been reported by the time this chapter was written.

The Ninth Congress approved a new five-year weapons development plan that emphasized, according to analysts, unmanned attack systems enabled by artificial intelligence, anti-satellite weapons, and electronic-warfare systems intended to disrupt an adversary's command and control, in addition to the continued expansion of nuclear forces. Missile testing continued throughout 2026. In March, North Korea reported a hypersonic missile test, and it fired twelve 600-millimeter short-range ballistic missiles on March 14 in what was reported as a massed salvo, and conducted a ground test of an intercontinental-class solid-fuel rocket motor, following the earlier test associated with the Hwasong-20 solid-fuel ICBM in September 2025. A particularly visible development was the expansion of the navy. The 5,000-ton destroyer Choe Hyon, launched in April 2025, conducted cruise-missile and anti-ship missile tests in March and April 2026 and was formally commissioned at Nampo on June 23, 2026, with Kim describing the progress of the navy's nuclear armament. A second ship of the class, the Kang Kon, was commissioned at Wonsan on September 6. Kim reportedly ordered that two destroyers or larger cruisers be built in each year of the new plan, and North Korea has also displayed a nuclear-powered submarine under construction. The submarine program's actual status is uncertain, and independent observers have questioned how mature North Korea's underwater capabilities are.

Historians should distinguish the military's role in North Korea's political system from the weapons themselves. The "military-first" (Songun) politics of the Kim Jong Il era (Chapter 83) gave the army a privileged claim on resources and a central place in ideology. Under Kim Jong Un, the party has reasserted its primacy, and the congresses and constitutional revisions of 2026 emphasize party and leader authority, with the nuclear command vested directly in the leader. Weapons development remains the state's highest priority. The Ninth Congress treated nuclear deterrence as the foundation of national security, and the economic plan was designed in part to support the defense industry. Yet it would be wrong to treat the arsenal solely as a military instrument. It also has political functions: legitimating the leadership domestically, giving the state bargaining weight, raising the cost of any attack, and creating a national project through which a state under sanctions could claim success. Students should consider that these functions may reinforce one another, and that the arsenal's role in North Korea's political self-understanding is part of why outside observers believe the North will not trade it away easily.

North Korea's conventional forces also changed because of the war in Ukraine. The country maintains more than a million active-duty personnel, according to press accounts of defense estimates, a very large proportion of its population, and its soldiers sent to Russia gained experience with drone warfare, modern artillery coordination, and large-scale combined operations that its army had not previously had. The effect on the North's military capability is one of the most consequential outcomes of the Russia partnership, examined in Section 96.14, and U.S. and South Korean commanders testified in 2026 that the shift was significant.

Historical Debate

Does Nuclear Deterrence Preserve Peace or Increase the Risk of War?

The question: North Korea’s nuclear forces and the United States’ extended deterrence commitment may discourage invasion, but they also make mistakes and crises more dangerous. How should historians weigh seven decades without a renewed general war against the repeated possibility of escalation?

Interpretation A — Deterrence restrains attack: Analysts emphasizing the costs of war argue that nuclear weapons and strong conventional alliances raise the expected cost of aggression, narrowing leaders’ incentives to launch major operations. They distinguish the existence of weapons from a decision to use them.
Interpretation B — Armed rivalry amplifies danger: Other analysts emphasize misperception, rapid missile flight times, conventional clashes, cyber interference and nuclear doctrines. A confrontation that both sides initially consider limited might escalate through fear of losing strategic forces.
Interpretation C — Institutions and diplomacy matter as much as arsenals: A third perspective treats deterrence and communication as complementary. Military readiness may prevent coercion, while hotlines, clear signaling, mutual restraint and humanitarian contact reduce opportunities for accidents.

Evidence: The record includes decades without a second full Korean War, repeated border crises, competing military statements, the 1953 armistice, alliance doctrine, and SIPRI estimates of the North’s arsenal. None is a controlled experiment, and any historical explanation must consider other causes of restraint.

Limitations and disagreement: Government statements may serve deterrent purposes rather than reveal private plans. The actual North Korean arsenal is not publicly inspected, and estimates vary. The debate is about causal weight and risk, not about whether the Korean War and later crises occurred.

Student task: Build a three-column evidence matrix explaining what each interpretation predicts in a border incident. Then identify which facts would alter your conclusion and why no single indicator establishes that a future war is inevitable or impossible.

The Two Koreas in a Changing World

External relationships and the human consequences of living in two Korean political systems.

Section 96.13

South Korea’s International Position: Alliance, Diplomacy, and Global Influence

Chapter 94 treated the relationships among the two Koreas and the four neighboring powers in detail. This section summarizes the situation as it stood in the autumn of 2026 and asks how it affected the options available to each Korean state. The central observation is that the external setting changed in different directions for the two Koreas. North Korea's international position, which had been weakened by sanctions and isolation after 2017, was strengthened by new partnerships. South Korea's position as an ally of the United States, a major trading partner of China, and a neighbor of Japan became more complicated, and in some respects less predictable.

The United States' relationship with the two Koreas was marked in 2026 by shifts of emphasis that left both allies and adversaries uncertain. President Trump repeatedly expressed his wish to meet Kim Jong Un, citing a good personal relationship, and in August he said that he expected to do so later in the year and that North Korea had fifty-seven nuclear weapons. Pyongyang did not accept. By late October, Reuters reported that U.S. and South Korean officials saw the chances of a meeting in 2026 as fading, and in mid-November a U.S. official said there were no plans for Trump to engage with North Korea during an Asian trip that was to include meetings with Xi and Putin at the APEC summit in Shenzhen. The official U.S. position stated that the president remained open to talks without preconditions and that the goal of complete denuclearization had not changed, and North Korea, for its part, said that talks were conceivable only if the United States accepted its nuclear status. The result was a stalemate in which both parties asserted openness to dialogue on mutually incompatible terms.

In the alliance with South Korea, Trump's August order to reduce the summer exercises, his complaint that Seoul had declined to join the U.S. military effort in the 2026 conflict with Iran, and the unresolved implementation of the investment and nuclear-submarine agreements of November 2025 left Seoul uncertain about the direction of the relationship. South Korea's foreign minister, Cho Hyun, said the order was intended partly to pressure Seoul on its investment pledge. President Lee said Korea should be prepared for a worst-case scenario and called for stronger independent defense capabilities, describing a strong alliance and the strengthening of Korea's own capabilities as mutually reinforcing. The government also moved, in November, to expand the operating area of its anti-piracy naval unit toward the Strait of Hormuz, a decision awaiting National Assembly consent that was criticized by two smaller left-leaning parties and by Iran, and that the Defense Ministry said would not involve the unit in the war. These decisions show a South Korean government trying to maintain its alliance, defend its economic interests, and avoid military entanglement at once. Public opinion supported the alliance strongly in the Korea Institute for National Unification's 2026 survey, with 87.3 percent saying it was necessary and 82 percent saying U.S. forces in Korea were necessary, while support for South Korea developing its own nuclear weapons stood at 68 percent in the same survey. These figures should be read together: they suggest a public that supports the alliance while also being anxious about its reliability.

Japan's role in 2026, treated in Chapters 88 and 94, was largely that of a partner in security and economic cooperation with the United States and South Korea and of a neighbor whose relationship with Seoul retained the burdens of colonial-era memory. North Korea's leadership has also been critical of Japan's military buildup and of its security ties with the United States and South Korea. Japanese popular culture, as Chapter 88 explained, had become a normal part of South Korean consumer life, while disputes over history continued to produce periodic tension. These conflicting tendencies remained part of the contemporary relationship.

South Korea's international influence in 2026 rested on a combination of state security commitments and commercial capabilities accumulated over generations. Its alliance with the United States provided extended deterrence and access to integrated military planning, but alliance management also required negotiations over defense spending, force posture, and how far commitments should extend beyond the peninsula. The details of these arrangements belong to Chapter 94. At the concluding level, the point is that the alliance was a continuing institutional choice made by elected South Korean governments, not merely an inheritance from the Korean War.

Seoul's ability to bargain has increased with its material capabilities. Semiconductor manufacturing, advanced shipyards, batteries, automobiles, communications infrastructure, and defense exports make South Korea economically consequential to partners in North America, Europe, and the Indo-Pacific. Those links can create leverage, but they also expose firms and workers to export controls, tariffs, geopolitical risks, and technological change. A factory that supplies essential components to foreign customers is influential precisely because it participates in a cross-border system on which it cannot exercise complete control.

Relations with China demonstrate the limits of dividing foreign policy into security and economics. Beijing has been a major destination for South Korean trade and investment; at the same time, many South Koreans regard Chinese military policies and pressure over missile defense as causes for concern. Seoul's governments have pursued different emphases toward Beijing and Washington, yet no administration has been able to treat the relationships as fully independent. Japan presents a different combination of historical memory, trade competition, and practical security cooperation, especially over missile-warning data.

South Korea's diplomacy has not been confined to its largest neighbors. Its development assistance, participation in international institutions, cultural industries, and relationships with Southeast Asian and European governments give it multiple venues in which to pursue interests. Democratic contestation affects these policies: elections and parliamentary scrutiny can change priorities, and public attitudes may constrain concessions. The contrast with the South's earlier position as a heavily aid-dependent postwar state shows the transformation in agency across Volume IV, without implying that prosperity has removed the country's strategic vulnerabilities.

A 2026 assessment should therefore ask not simply which great power South Korea follows but what it tries to preserve: territorial security, open trade, technological competitiveness, domestic political legitimacy, and a measure of freedom to pursue diplomacy toward the North. These objectives sometimes reinforce one another and sometimes conflict. Because they involve concrete negotiations rather than abstract geopolitical destiny, their relative priority can change with governments and crises.

Section 96.14

North Korea’s External Relationships: China, Russia, and Strategic Dependence

The most consequential change for the North was its partnership with Russia. In June 2024, Vladimir Putin and Kim Jong Un signed a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty in Pyongyang containing a provision for mutual military assistance in the event of an attack on either party. North Korea supplied artillery shells and missiles for Russia's war in Ukraine, and from late 2024 it sent troops, estimated by South Korean and Ukrainian sources at between 14,000 and 15,000, most of whom fought in Russia's Kursk region, where Ukrainian forces had seized territory in 2024. Casualty estimates differ widely. South Korean intelligence assessments cited in the press ranged from roughly 2,000 North Korean troops killed to about 6,000 killed or wounded by early 2026, and a memorial built in Pyongyang in 2026 appeared to list more than 2,000 names. About 3,000 soldiers had reportedly returned home by early 2026, and many were assigned as instructors to pass on battlefield lessons. In April 2026, Russia's defense minister Andrei Belousov visited Pyongyang, presented awards to North Korean soldiers, and said that the two countries had agreed to place military cooperation on a long-term footing, and press reports described a plan for cooperation to run from 2027 to 2031. In August, President Volodymyr Zelensky warned that North Korea might send many more troops, a claim that was not independently verified. South Korean researchers estimated that North Korea earned between about $7.7 billion and $14.4 billion from the military cooperation between August 2023 and the end of 2025, and the United States' military commander in Korea testified that the partnership had produced significant shifts in North Korean military capabilities. The effects in the other direction, including what Russia has provided in technology and what role the partnership will have if the Ukraine war ends, remain subjects of dispute among analysts.

Relations with China, which had appeared to cool as North Korea turned toward Russia, were restored to a closer footing. Kim Jong Un attended China's Victory Day parade in Beijing on September 3, 2025, standing near Xi Jinping, and in June 2026 Xi made a state visit to Pyongyang on June 8 and 9, his first since 2019. Xi described relations as being at a new historical starting point and said that China's support for Kim Jong Un's leadership of what he called the socialist cause would not change. State media reported that the two sides agreed to expand cooperation in political, economic, and cultural fields, and Xi proposed that the resumption of flights and train services be used to expand exchanges. The visit came shortly after a summit in Beijing at which the U.S. government said Trump and Xi had confirmed their shared goal of denuclearizing North Korea, a statement Pyongyang rejected. Analysts differed on what China sought. Some saw an effort to regain influence over Pyongyang and to prevent Russia from displacing it, others a continuing view of North Korea as a strategic buffer and asset. In July, Premier Pak Thae-song traveled to China for the sixty-fifth anniversary of the 1961 treaty of friendship between the two countries. In September, both Xi and Putin sent messages promising closer ties to Kim on the anniversary of the state's founding.

Section 96.15

Divided Families, Migration, Defectors, and the Human Consequences of Partition

The line should not be understood only as a military boundary. For millions of Koreans, it separates families. The war and the division of 1945 to 1953 left an enormous number of separated family members, and the organized reunions of the early twenty-first century (Chapter 85) reached only a small fraction of them before the program stopped in 2018. Most surviving separated family members are now of very advanced age, and the possibility of reunion has become a closing window. The line also separates two states that, since 2024, have physically hardened parts of the border. North Korean forces have laid mines and built barriers along stretches of the frontier, and the North has dismantled road and rail links across the line that had been symbolic of the era of engagement. The frontier of 2026 is therefore less porous in some respects than it was a decade earlier, even as its physical markers have faded.

The most immediate human consequence of division is not visible on military maps. It is the rupture of households during liberation, the 1945 division, the war, and the consolidation of separate states. People crossed frontiers to flee violence, pursue work, or follow political convictions; others were separated by retreating armies and sudden changes of control. Their descendants inherited family histories with missing people, letters that could not be sent, and graves beyond reach. Family separation did not end when the armistice was signed. It became a durable condition of everyday life and personal identity.

The urgency was measurable in 2026. South Korea's Ministry of Unification reported that at the end of 2025, 134,516 people had registered in its separated-family system; 101,148 were already deceased and 34,368 remained alive. By late summer 2026 the number of living registrants had fallen further, to roughly 33,000. These figures count the government's registrants, not all people touched by division in both states. They nevertheless show that surviving members of the generation with direct recollections of relatives across the line have little time remaining. The last official large-scale inter-Korean reunion occurred in August 2018.

Reunions brought extraordinary emotion but also raised difficult questions. They usually involved selected participants meeting under tightly controlled conditions for limited periods; they were not equivalent to restored freedom of travel or permanent family contact. Political relations and security disputes repeatedly interrupted them. Some families sought confirmation of life or death, photographs, telephone access, or exchanges of letters rather than a short ceremonial meeting. The contrast between the small number reached by official programs and the vast population affected by partition is one reason that humanitarian cooperation appears repeatedly in South Korean coexistence proposals.

A related but distinct population consists of people who have left North Korea, often through China, and resettled in South Korea or elsewhere. In South Korea, constitutional and statutory arrangements generally treat people from the North as Korean citizens, but the practical process of arrival, security screening, and resettlement is complex. Newcomers may confront differences in dialect and vocabulary, educational credentials, workplace practices, housing markets, social prejudice, and the trauma of separation. Their experiences vary by age, gender, family resources, and route of migration. They should not be reduced to a single story of rescue or successful adjustment.

The wider Korean diaspora also complicates ideas of national belonging. Korean communities in northeast China, Japan, Russia, the United States, and other countries were formed through colonial migration, wartime displacement, labor recruitment, and later voluntary movement. Some maintain cultural associations with both Koreas; others identify primarily with their country of residence. North Korean overseas labor and travel restrictions have created a different relationship between the state and many of its citizens abroad. A complete history of Korean belonging must account for people whose lives cross or lie outside the peninsula's political division.

The inability to contact family across the DMZ is therefore simultaneously a humanitarian problem, an artifact of an unfinished war, and a measure of what sovereignty means in practice. Governments may describe division in terms of security and ideology, but for an aging registrant it can mean not knowing whether a sister or brother is alive. A final chapter should preserve that scale of experience without turning unverifiable personal anecdotes into historical evidence.

Section 96.16

Two Korean Modernities: Different Economies, Cultures, and Everyday Experiences

The two Koreas are not simply different political systems imposed on an otherwise identical social world. Decades of separate institutions have shaped the experience of school, work, household budgeting, media, movement, and expectations about authority. In a South Korean city, a student may prepare for competitive university entrance exams, use global platforms, encounter private tutoring, and debate public policy on a networked phone. In a North Korean city, schools combine education with ideological instruction, access to global digital media is severely restricted, and relationships with party and local authorities may affect educational and occupational opportunities. The comparison must remain a comparison of documented institutions; neither example should be represented as the biography of an invented individual.

The labor systems offer a second contrast. South Korea's corporations, smaller firms, public employers, and service industries hire within a market economy regulated by law and influenced by trade unions and professional qualifications. Income and working conditions vary sharply, and young people may struggle to obtain secure employment despite national prosperity. North Korean workplaces operate within state and party structures, while market activity provides supplementary income for some households. The relation between formal assignments and practical survival has changed repeatedly since the famine years. Estimates of informal earnings and market scale are imprecise, so a textbook should not turn individual testimonies into national averages.

Housing and consumption likewise reveal differences within each society as well as between them. South Korean households may face large mortgages or rental deposits despite living in a high-income country, and prosperity is concentrated unequally among regions and generations. North Korean residents can encounter shortages of power, water, or goods, while favored Pyongyang districts display modern housing and consumer amenities. Photographs of apartment blocks in either capital cannot establish the conditions of elderly people, migrants, farmers, or provincial families. Geographic and social variation belongs inside any comparison.

Communications make the divergence especially visible. The South's highly connected media environment enables commerce, entertainment, organization, and criticism of government, but also political disinformation and intrusive data collection. The North's controlled telecommunications and restrictions on outside information narrow possibilities of independent communication. Some individuals circumvent prohibitions, but doing so carries danger. These different information systems influence how citizens learn about the other Korea; they also affect how historians obtain and verify evidence about public opinion and social change.

Family life provides a final point of comparison without reducing Koreans to state ideology. In both societies, parents care for children, younger generations negotiate expectations, and households adapt to economic pressures. The South's falling marriage rate and exceptionally low fertility have generated debate over work, housing, gender norms, and caregiving. The North also faces demographic and health challenges, but reliable comparable statistics are scarce. Shared vocabulary for kinship does not guarantee common experience of schooling, employment, or the future.

The phrase “two Korean modernities” is useful only if it is analytical rather than celebratory or dismissive. Each society is the result of human decisions, inherited institutions, external pressures, and adaptation; each has changed since 1953. The phrase does not imply equal freedoms or outcomes, nor does it imply that either state represents the authentic completion of Korean history. It asks students to compare the mechanisms through which modern life is organized and the evidence on which the comparison rests.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life on Two Sides of the Line, 2026

For a young adult in Seoul in 2026, daily life was shaped by a dense urban environment, long working hours in many sectors, very high housing costs, and educational competition that began in childhood. Many such households faced decisions about marriage and children in a setting where housing in the capital region was expensive, where private tutoring added to the cost of raising children, and where employment in the best-paid firms was highly competitive. Smartphone, broadband, and digital payment use were nearly universal, and entertainment, including Korean dramas, music, webtoons, and games, was available on global platforms. At the same time, the country's elderly population faced relatively high rates of poverty by the standards of international comparison. For households in smaller cities and rural regions, the experience differed from that of the capital region, with population decline, school closures, and a more limited range of services, even where fertility was higher.

For ordinary North Koreans, daily life in 2026 was shaped by a state that controlled housing, employment assignment, and access to travel, and by a mixed economy in which official rations coexisted with market purchase, as Chapters 83 and 92 explained. The government's emphasis in recent years on housing construction in Pyongyang and on the "20×10" regional development program, which was announced in January 2024 and aims to build modern factories for local industries in twenty counties each year for ten years, affected the lives of those in the capital and of rural populations who received new housing or employment. The experience of different groups varied greatly with songbun, the political-class classification, with region, and with access to border trade or remittances. Outside observers have limited ability to measure these differences directly, and most of what is known comes from state media, which presents an idealized picture, from the reports of those who have left, and from a small number of surveys that raise methodological issues.

Social historians should also note groups whose experiences cross the line. The Korean communities in China, Japan, Russia, and Central Asia, and the Korean diaspora in North America and Europe, maintain ties to one or both states, as earlier chapters have described. More than thirty thousand people who left North Korea had settled in South Korea by the mid-2020s, and their experience of discrimination, adaptation, and, in some cases, success is a distinct part of the social history of the peninsula. The remaining members of families separated by the war, now very elderly, also form a small and rapidly diminishing group whose personal memories link the two societies.

Section 96.17

The Changing Meaning of Reunification: From National Destiny to Political Uncertainty

For most of the history of the two Korean states, both governments asserted that the division was temporary and that the peninsula's people formed one nation. This claim underlay the constitutions and slogans of both. The South's constitution declares the entire peninsula to be Korean territory and calls for peaceful unification. The North's leaders, for decades, promoted proposals for confederation and declared reunification the highest national task. This section examines how far that shared assumption had changed by 2026, in the policies of both governments and in the attitudes of ordinary South Koreans, where measurement is possible.

In the North, the change was explicit. The two hostile states doctrine, the March 2026 constitutional revisions documented by South Korean researchers and later confirmed by the publicly posted text, the removal of references to unification, and the physical dismantling of symbols of reunification such as the Arch of Reunification in Pyongyang in 2024, together amounted to an official abandonment of the framework that had guided the state's declared policy since its founding. The Ninth Party Congress, according to a South Korean research foundation and press analysis, defined inter-Korean relations as those between hostile states and dismissed Seoul's reconciliation policies, and the specialist outlet NK News summarized its outcome as permanently rejecting ties with the South. Students should be cautious about two points. First, this is the leadership's declared position, and the evidence on what ordinary North Koreans think is almost nonexistent. Retrospective surveys of people who have left the North, conducted by South Korean institutions, found that large majorities said they had believed unification to be necessary while living there, but these samples were small, self-selected, and drawn from a period before the state's current position, and cannot be treated as measures of present North Korean opinion. Second, the North's decision does not in itself establish a lasting end to the idea of reunification, since earlier shifts in the North's line, such as the engagement of 2018, were also reversed.

Legal questions complicate the matter. Under the South Korean constitution, Articles 3 and 4 respectively define the territory of the Republic as the whole peninsula and its islands and commit the state to pursue peaceful unification. The Basic Agreement of 1991 described the relationship between the two sides as a special relationship in the process of unification, not a relationship between states, a framework the South has not formally abandoned. South Korea's government therefore faces the paradox of conducting a policy that treats the North in practice as a separate entity with which a stable coexistence is sought, while maintaining a legal and constitutional position that does not recognize it as a foreign state. North Korea's revision of its own constitution resolves its side of that tension by abandoning the unification framework outright. Section 96.18

Do Koreans Still Want Reunification? Generations, Identity, and Public Opinion

In the South, the change was more gradual and can be traced in survey data. The Korea Institute for National Unification's 2026 survey, based on interviews with 1,003 adults in April and published in late September, found that 48.2 percent said unification was necessary, down slightly from 49 percent in 2025, the first year in which the share had fallen below half. It found that 66.9 percent agreed that unification would not be necessary if the two Koreas could coexist peacefully without war, up from 63.2 percent in 2025 and 43.1 percent in 2016. About 71.6 percent agreed that North Korea is a separate state, and 70.3 percent said they were not interested in North Korea, the highest figure since the survey began in 2014. The survey also showed a large generation gap: 61.6 percent of the war generation and 61.3 percent of the industrialization generation said unification was necessary, compared with 33.4 percent of millennials and 36.3 percent of Generation Z. A separate poll by the Institute for Peace and Unification Studies at Seoul National University, published in September, found that 39 percent considered unification necessary, down from 41.1 percent the previous year, with only 25.2 percent of people in their twenties and 27 percent of those in their thirties agreeing, and that the share regarding unification as impossible had reached 39.1 percent, the highest since the institute began its survey in 2007. The two surveys use different question formats and response options, so their absolute levels are not directly comparable, but both show the same direction of change.

These findings require careful interpretation. They do not show that South Koreans hold hostile feelings toward North Koreans as people. Surveys that ask about North Korean residents, as distinct from the North Korean state, generally find more sympathetic attitudes, and responses depend heavily on how a question is worded, as the contrast between the "necessary" item and the "unnecessary if peaceful coexistence is possible" item shows. The data also show that large majorities of respondents in the Korea Institute for National Unification's survey supported the alliance with the United States, and that majorities favored South Korea acquiring nuclear weapons or the return of American nuclear weapons to its territory, indications of a public concerned primarily with security. Researchers also noted that low support among younger South Koreans might encourage the major parties to emphasize stability and practical management of inter-Korean relations over a unification-centered approach, an observation that applies to both parties, though they differ on how to manage the relationship.

Longer-term interpretations remain debated. The declining personal memory of a united peninsula, generational economic anxieties, differences in national identity, North Korean nuclear development, and the collapse of inter-Korean cooperation may all influence answers. Causation cannot be established from one survey item. Nor do survey findings prove indifference toward the people of North Korea: respondents can oppose political integration while supporting human rights, family contact, or humanitarian assistance. The degree of support for such actions depends on the exact wording of the question and the circumstances under which it is asked.

Reliable equivalent polling within North Korea is unavailable. State rhetoric, officially organized gatherings, or carefully selected interviews cannot reproduce the conditions of anonymous, nationally representative surveys. Analysts can document constitutional changes and official campaigns, but cannot responsibly translate them into precise percentages of personal approval. The asymmetry in available evidence is itself an important feature of two Korean political systems and should be disclosed wherever claims about “what Koreans want” are made.

Historical Debate

Are the Koreas One Nation Divided, or Two Societies Becoming Two Nations?

The question: By 2026, North Korea's constitution no longer referred to unification, a majority of South Korean survey respondents regarded North Korea as a separate state, and a majority of younger South Koreans did not consider unification necessary. Historians and social scientists disagree about what this shows. Does it indicate that the idea of one Korean nation has weakened to the point of being replaced by two national identities, or is it a response to a particular political situation that does not settle the long-term question?

Interpretation A — One nation divided by external events: Some scholars emphasize that the peninsula's shared language, long common history, family ties, and the division's origin in the Soviet and American occupations and the Cold War show that the two states are parts of one nation whose division is political and external. On this view, the surveys show a response to the North's hostility and the weight of seven decades of division, not a change in the underlying national community, and the older generation's stronger support for unification reflects direct memory of a unified society.
Interpretation B — Two societies forming distinct identities: Others argue that seven decades of separate institutions, economic systems, education, language usage, and historical memory have created two societies whose members increasingly see themselves as belonging to different communities. They point to the surveys, to the North's formal declaration, to differences in vocabulary and speech, and to the fact that people born after 1990 on both sides have no experience of a common society. On this view, the Chapter 1 concept of minjok, a single ethnic nation, is a modern idea that is losing its political force.
Interpretation C — The framing itself is the problem: A third group notes that the ethnic concept of the nation was itself a historical construction of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, as Chapter 1 argued, and that identities are shaped by political choices and not fixed by ancestry. They observe that South Korea has become more ethnically diverse, that attitudes could shift with changes in the political situation, and that questions about the North's population cannot be answered because reliable evidence is missing.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same evidence: survey data from the South, the texts and statements of the North's government, linguistic comparison, defector testimony, and the history of the division. They differ in how much weight to give to attitudes expressed in particular political conditions, how to treat the absence of evidence from inside the North, and whether the concept of a single nation should be treated as a social fact or as a political claim.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology more than basic facts. No participant disputes the survey figures or the content of the North's constitution as reported. The disagreement is about what these facts imply for a period of decades, and, in the case of Interpretation C, about whether the question is framed correctly.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians describe the relationship between North and South Korean history, whether they treat the two as variants of a single national history or as increasingly separate histories with a common prehistory, and how they interpret policy choices in Seoul, Pyongyang, and the capitals of neighboring states. It also affects how this textbook's own organizing principle, the treatment of the two states as parallel Korean histories, is understood.

Section 96.19

Peace Without Unification? The Possibilities and Limits of Coexistence

The South Korean government's own policy was itself a subject of debate. President Lee's administration called its approach a policy of peaceful coexistence on the Korean Peninsula, and the Ministry of Unification prepared a Fifth Master Plan on the development of inter-Korean relations, which was reviewed in March 2026 and aimed to redefine the relationship as one of peaceful coexistence while pursuing unification. Within the government, officials disagreed about the details. The unification minister, Chung Dong-young, argued that the two Koreas have in reality been two states and that policy should aim to turn a hostile two-state relationship into a peaceful one. Other officials, including the foreign minister and the national security adviser, placed greater emphasis on coordination with the United States and on the alliance, and a senior government official said that the unification ministry's proposal for four-way talks involving the two Koreas, the United States, and China had not been coordinated within the government. In August 2026, Lee himself warned that well-intentioned policies can be exploited for political gain and told the ministry to remain patient. Critics in the opposition and elsewhere said the government was leaning too far toward conciliation toward a North that showed no sign of responding.

Peaceful coexistence is an attempt to govern a dangerous relationship without requiring agreement about its ultimate constitutional destination. Its first problem is practical: how can two heavily armed states prevent accidents, limit retaliation, and preserve channels for emergency communication? The September 2026 controversy over the precise location of military demarcation markers shows why a seemingly narrow technical question can have national significance. Even if neither government agrees about sovereignty, predictable procedures for identifying personnel and communicating after incidents can reduce the danger of escalation.

In February 2026 South Korea's Ministry of Unification published the Lee government's Policy for Peaceful Coexistence on the Korean Peninsula; an August work plan elaborated crisis-management measures, mutual respect, possible restoration of military arrangements and gradual cooperation. These texts are authoritative evidence of Seoul's policy intentions, not evidence of agreement with Pyongyang. A proposal to replace hostile terminology does not by itself change the language used by the other government. The credibility of peaceful coexistence depends on implementation, reciprocity, and the difficult relationship between sanctions and humanitarian or economic exchange.

Supporters argue that security and dignity should not depend on immediate political reunification. They emphasize freedom from war, the possibility of identifying separated relatives, restraint in inflammatory messaging, resumption of direct communication, and incremental cooperation over health, disasters, or the environment. In this view a stable peace could leave questions of ultimate statehood to later generations. Others worry that accepting permanent separation as the practical starting point may normalize coercion and information restrictions in the North or weaken South Korea's constitutional commitment to unification. Still others object that dialogue without verifiable security measures may reward military threats.

The options are not simply war, permanent division, or immediate absorption of one system by the other. Negotiated military confidence-building, formal peace arrangements, limited economic exchange under applicable law, humanitarian channels and long-term constitutional dialogue are analytically distinct. Each requires different participants and levels of verification. A political settlement would also involve questions about United States forces, Chinese security interests, Japan's position, sanctions imposed through the United Nations, and North Korea's nuclear weapons. Chapter 94 explains these external constraints in detail; the question here is what room they leave for Korean initiatives.

A 2026 textbook cannot assign probabilities to reunification, regime collapse, military conflict, or stable peace on the basis of the sources available. Past diplomatic openings have collapsed; periods of hostility have not always produced war. A historically useful conclusion must hold uncertainty openly: policies can reduce danger without promising harmony, and humanitarian benefits may matter even where the political relationship remains adversarial. The future depends on choices by governments and people who have not yet made them.

Cause and Effect

Why the Unification Framework Weakened by 2026

Long-term causes

Seven decades of separate political, economic, and educational systems; the memory of the Korean War and of repeated provocations; the end of the Cold War and the Soviet collapse, which removed the shared global framework in which unification had been discussed; South Korea's economic success and the North's relative poverty, which raised the perceived cost of unification; and generational turnover in the South.

Short-term causes

The collapse of the 2018–2019 summit diplomacy; the North's expansion of its nuclear and missile programs; the pandemic border closures; the North's decision to treat the South as a primary enemy; the war in Ukraine, which opened new partnerships for Pyongyang; and South Korea's domestic crisis of 2024–2025.

Immediate trigger and event

Kim Jong Un's declaration of December 2023 and the constitutional changes that followed, culminating in the March 2026 revision, together with the Party Congress's endorsement of the policy.

Immediate effects

Removal of unification from the North's constitutional language; dismantling of symbolic and physical links across the border; an increase in South Korean skepticism about unification; a shift in the South Korean government's policy vocabulary from engagement toward coexistence.

Long-term consequences

These remain open. They may include a prolonged hostile or peaceful coexistence, a new set of regional alignments, a continued decline in public attachment to unification as a goal, or, if circumstances change, a revival of engagement.

Historical complexity

The weakening of the framework cannot be attributed to any single cause or actor. It involved decisions in Pyongyang, Seoul, Moscow, Beijing, and Washington, demographic and generational change, and events that no one planned, and earlier shifts in the North's policy had been reversed.

Historical complexity: Students should note that each cause listed here is contested in its weight. Some analysts emphasize the North's strategic calculation, others emphasize South Korean policy, and others emphasize the structure of great-power relations. The evidence does not allow a confident ranking.

Section 96.20

The Divided Peninsula in 2026: Korea’s Historical Continuity and Unresolved Future

A history that ends in a particular year is bound to tempt its readers to treat that year as a conclusion. This section reminds students why that temptation should be resisted and identifies the questions that were unresolved in late 2026. The point is not to predict. It is to show what evidence a future historian will need in order to judge the period this chapter describes.

Several outcomes were unresolved when this chapter was written. In South Korea, the appeal in the insurrection case against Yoon Suk Yeol was scheduled for decision in January 2027, and the Supreme Court had yet to rule on the election-law case, so the final legal record of the martial-law crisis was incomplete. The question of whether the rebound in births would continue depended on whether marriages would keep increasing and whether the cohort effects that helped the recovery would fade. The semiconductor-driven economic boom depended on the continuation of global investment in artificial intelligence and on the tariff and investment agreements with the United States, which faced political uncertainty after the American midterm elections. The governing party's internal disagreements and the course of Lee's presidency were still unfolding.

In North Korea, the questions were different and harder to see. It was unclear whether the country would conduct a seventh nuclear test, whether it would enter talks with the United States, and on what terms. It was uncertain what would happen to its partnership with Russia if the Ukraine war ended, and what Beijing's renewed engagement would mean in practice. It was unknown how the succession would proceed, whether the 20×10 program and the new five-year plan would improve living conditions, and how ordinary citizens understood the abandonment of unification. Some of these questions may be answered by events in 2027 and beyond, and others will become answerable only when archives and eyewitness accounts become available.

Finally, students should consider what the textbook's method requires at its end. Throughout this book the narrative has tried to treat Korea as the organizing subject, to avoid projecting modern states onto the past, to distinguish the evidence from interpretation, and to avoid reading outcomes as inevitable. The divided peninsula of 2026 is the product of identifiable decisions and conditions, from the Japanese colonial rule that ended in 1945 through the occupations of 1945 and the war of 1950 to 1953 to the choices of leaders and citizens in the decades since. These facts mean that the peninsula's present condition has a history that can be explained, and that history does not dictate the future. The final reflection below returns to that point.

The first volume began with geography and the earliest communities, then followed Gojoseon traditions, the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, Silla and Balhae, and Goryeo. These societies were not unfinished versions of the modern Republic of Korea or the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. Their rulers, communities, trade networks, legal systems, and religious institutions operated within changing Northeast Asian worlds. Yet they also left written traditions, places, religious art, historical memories, and political vocabularies that later generations inherited and reinterpreted. The present division does not erase this earlier plurality, and the earlier past does not dictate a modern national settlement.

The second volume traced Joseon, whose officials used Neo-Confucian institutions, examination and family systems, Hangeul, technologies, agricultural communities, and diplomatic relations to govern a long-lived monarchy. Joseon changed repeatedly; it was neither permanently isolated nor culturally static. The experience of invasion, reconstruction, commercial growth, religious exchange, and nineteenth-century reform provides a history of adaptation under pressure. Modern North and South Koreans draw differently on this legacy, but neither can claim that its present government is the uninterrupted political expression of the dynasty.

The third volume followed the opening of the peninsula, imperial rivalry, Japanese colonial rule, resistance, migration, liberation, and the Korean War. Those developments transformed the meaning of political sovereignty and national belonging. Colonialism and the partition after 1945 did not occur because Koreans lacked history or agency. Korean reformers, activists, workers, officials and soldiers made choices inside unequal regional and global power structures. The war's destruction and armistice created the immediate political geography from which the rival postwar states developed, but neither their institutions nor the form of their eventual relationship was predetermined in 1953.

The fourth volume examined two parallel trajectories. South Korea passed through authoritarian development and struggles for democracy into a technologically advanced and culturally influential society whose constitutional order again faced a serious test in 2024–2025. North Korea formed a durable hereditary party-state, endured economic disruption and famine, permitted and managed market practices, developed nuclear weapons, and adjusted its relationships with China and Russia. These are both Korean histories. The similarities of language, ancestry and inheritance do not make their political outcomes interchangeable, and the distance between their political systems does not make their shared history imaginary.

Across all four volumes, several recurring problems emerge. Political authority has been repeatedly created and contested; foreign relations have brought both danger and possibility; religious, literary, technological and commercial practices have crossed boundaries; and ordinary people have made lives within systems they did not wholly choose. Geography matters without making history inevitable. Historical evidence is abundant for some questions and radically incomplete for others. A responsible historian must be able to say what is firmly documented, what rests on an estimate or a government claim, and what cannot yet be known.

The year 2026 shows two Korean states whose governments differed sharply over national purpose, military security, rights, markets, and the meaning of reunification. It also shows continued family attachments, shared cultural inheritances, linguistic connections, cross-border risks, and the efforts of people to imagine safety and dignity. Nothing in the preceding ninety-five chapters requires either a single future state or perpetual separation. The historian's task ends with the evidence available in November 2026, not with a prophecy. Korea's history continues beyond this book, and the decisions that will shape it remain open.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Division of the Peninsula, 1953–2026

What Changed

  • South Korea moved from an impoverished, aid-dependent, authoritarian state to a democratic, technologically advanced economy with global cultural reach and a very low birth rate
  • North Korea moved from a Soviet-supported postwar reconstruction to a self-described nuclear state with a hereditary leadership and, since 2022, a new military partnership with Russia and a renewed one with China
  • Both governments' declared aim moved from competing claims to represent the whole nation to, in the North's case, abandoning unification altogether, and in the South's case, prioritizing peaceful coexistence
  • South Korean public opinion moved from broad support for unification as a national duty to a divided and, among the young, largely skeptical attitude
  • The DMZ's physical markers faded, even as both sides' military capabilities grew

What Stayed the Same

  • The armistice, signed in 1953, remains the only formal arrangement governing the frontier, and no peace treaty has replaced it
  • The U.S.–South Korea alliance, based on the 1953 treaty, and North Korea's treaty relationship with China, dating from 1961, remain central to each state's security
  • A shared language, a shared deep history, and family connections persist across the line
  • Each government continues to define its legitimacy partly in contrast to the other
  • The leadership of the North remains hereditary and party-centered, as it has been since the 1970s

When and how change occurred: Some changes were gradual, such as the shift in South Korean attitudes, the growth of the economy, and the decline in births. Others were sudden, such as the North's declaration of 2023, the Russia treaty of 2024, and South Korea's martial-law crisis. Change was experienced very differently by different groups: young South Koreans experienced an altered relationship to the idea of unification, residents of border regions experienced increased militarization, elderly separated family members experienced the closing of channels, and North Korean citizens experienced changes whose full extent cannot be measured from outside. Why the pattern matters: The combination of deep continuity in structures and rapid change in policy shows why a history of this period must avoid both claiming that nothing has changed since 1953 and treating the latest turn as permanent.

Chapter Summary

This final chapter has described the divided peninsula as it stood in the autumn of 2026 and has tried to do so with the same method applied to earlier periods: attention to evidence and its limits, treatment of the two Korean states as parallel histories, and resistance to the idea that the present arrangement was the inevitable outcome of the past. Its starting point was the line that separates the states. The armistice of July 27, 1953, a military agreement signed by the United Nations Command, the Korean People's Army, and the Chinese People's Volunteers, created a Military Demarcation Line and a Demilitarized Zone and was never replaced by a peace treaty. By 2026 the physical markers of that line had largely disappeared, and a land-mine explosion in September exposed the ambiguity of a frontier that is simultaneously among the most militarized and among the least clearly marked in the world.

South Korea in 2026 was a democracy that had survived an attempted suspension of constitutional order but had not resolved the conflict that produced it. The former president Yoon Suk Yeol was sentenced to life imprisonment at first instance on February 19, 2026, for leading an insurrection, and was serving a final seven-year sentence in a separate case, with further proceedings pending, including an appeal in the insurrection case that was not due to be decided until January 2027. The local elections of June 3 produced results that neither major party could claim as a clear victory. The legal and electoral record shows institutions working within constitutional channels and also an electorate and political class that remained deeply polarized.

The South's economy in 2026 was powered by a surge in demand for the memory chips used in artificial intelligence. Exports reached a record in September and growth over the first three quarters was 3.6 percent, following a weak 2025. The boom drew on the developmental state, the chaebol, and the digital transformation of earlier chapters, but it also showed the concentration of the economy in a few firms and one product category and its exposure to American trade policy, to the implementation of the 2025 Joint Fact Sheet, and to the AI investment cycle. The relationship with the United States was both a source of security and a source of uncertainty, as the reduction of the summer exercises and the unfinished nuclear-cooperation negotiations showed.

South Korean culture in 2026 had completed the transition from importer to adapter and exporter described in earlier chapters, with the return of BTS, the Academy Award for KPop Demon Hunters, and the continuing global presence of Korean music, film, television, and other products. The chapter distinguished influence from dependency and noted that this export power coexisted with continued reliance on foreign distribution platforms and with the persistence of foreign influences within South Korean culture. It also noted that Korean cultural products circulate illicitly in the North and that Pyongyang treats them as a political threat.

Demographically, South Korea remained the OECD's lowest-fertility country, but its total fertility rate rose from 0.72 in 2023 to 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025, and births rose to about 254,000, driven by an increase in marriages and by births to women in their thirties. Deaths still exceeded births, the population was shrinking naturally and aging rapidly, and the recovery was concentrated in particular regions while Seoul's rate remained the lowest. The chapter cautioned that two years of increase cannot establish a lasting reversal. The evidence for the North's demography is far weaker.

North Korea in 2026 was a hereditary party-state that had reorganized its constitutional and ideological position. The Ninth Party Congress of February 19 to 25 re-elected Kim Jong Un as general secretary, replaced more than half of the Central Committee, and adopted a five-year plan. The Supreme People's Assembly elected in March revised the constitution, which according to South Korean analysts removed references to unification, defined the North's territory as its own region, and made the president of the State Affairs Commission head of state above the Assembly. Kim's daughter was increasingly prominent, and South Korea's intelligence service judged that she was being positioned as a successor, though other analysts urged caution and the North had not confirmed it.

The North's nuclear and conventional forces were central to its political position. Estimates of the arsenal ranged from about fifty warheads in early 2026 analysis to the fifty-seven cited by President Trump, no seventh nuclear test had been conducted since 2017 though the test site was reported ready, and the navy expanded with the commissioning of two destroyers. The economy, by the Bank of Korea's estimates, grew by more than three percent for the third consecutive year in 2025, helped by arms sales to Russia, trade with China, and state construction programs such as the 20×10 regional development policy, while information controls remained strict and the effects on ordinary living standards could not be measured.

The external environment of the two states changed in different ways. North Korea's treaty partnership with Russia, its troop deployment to Kursk, the restoration of high-level ties with China marked by Xi Jinping's June 2026 visit, and Washington's unrealized attempts to restart diplomacy gave it a stronger international position than it had held at the start of the decade. South Korea's position as an American ally, a major economic partner of China, and a neighbor of Japan became more complicated, with the new demands of a transactional alliance, the Hormuz-related deployment debate, and anxieties about the alliance's reliability. Both governments maintained claims and policies that left the peninsula without a functioning channel of dialogue.

The chapter's central question was whether the peninsula was still understood as one nation awaiting reunification. In the North, the answer from the leadership was explicitly no, expressed in the two hostile states doctrine and its constitutional codification. In the South, government policy emphasized peaceful coexistence while retaining unification as a long-term aim and a constitutional commitment, and surveys showed that fewer than half of respondents considered unification necessary, that about two-thirds agreed it was unnecessary if peaceful coexistence were possible, and that support was much lower among younger people. Historians disagree about whether this shows two national identities forming, one nation divided by external forces, or a framing problem, and the evidence available cannot decide among them.

The chapter closed by stressing contingency. The condition of the peninsula in 2026 was the product of identifiable decisions and circumstances, from colonial rule and occupation to the war and the choices of leaders and citizens in the decades since. Several questions were unresolved: the final legal outcome of the martial-law cases, the durability of the birth rebound, the economic effects of the AI cycle, the possibility of a North Korean nuclear test or a Trump–Kim meeting, the future of the Russia partnership after the Ukraine war, the succession, and the attitudes of ordinary North Koreans. A history that ends in 2026 can explain how the present came to be, but it cannot say what will follow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Armistice
A military agreement to suspend fighting, signed July 27, 1953, that ended large-scale combat in Korea without a political settlement or peace treaty.
Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)
The roughly four-kilometer-wide buffer created by the 1953 armistice, extending two kilometers on each side of the Military Demarcation Line.
Military Demarcation Line (MDL)
The roughly 248-kilometer ceasefire line fixed in 1953 along the battlefield positions of the opposing forces, of which only a fraction of the original markers survive.
United Nations Command
The American-led multinational command that fought in the Korean War and signed the armistice on behalf of the United Nations forces.
Insurrection
In South Korean criminal law, an act of violence intended to subvert the constitutional order; leading one carries a sentence of death or life imprisonment.
Impeachment
The constitutional procedure by which the National Assembly charges a president, subject to a Constitutional Court ruling on removal from office.
Peaceful coexistence
The term used by the Lee Jae-myung government for a policy of avoiding war and building stable relations with North Korea while retaining unification as a long-term goal.
Two hostile states
Kim Jong Un's doctrine, announced in December 2023, that the two Korean governments are separate and hostile states, not parts of one nation seeking reunification.
Basic Agreement (1991)
The inter-Korean agreement on reconciliation, non-aggression, exchanges, and cooperation that defined the relationship as a special provisional one aimed at unification.
Developmental state
A state that directs economic growth through planning, credit allocation, and support for chosen industries, a model associated with South Korea from the 1960s.
Chaebol
Large, family-controlled South Korean business conglomerates, such as Samsung and Hyundai, central to the country's industrial and export economy.
High-bandwidth memory (HBM)
A stacked memory architecture used with advanced artificial intelligence processors, produced mainly by Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.
Joint Fact Sheet (2025)
The November 2025 U.S.–South Korea statement linking tariff reductions, a $350 billion Korean investment pledge, and approval for South Korea to build nuclear-powered submarines.
Hallyu
The Korean Wave: the international popularity of South Korean music, television, film, and other cultural products.
Total fertility rate
A statistical estimate of the average number of children a woman would bear over her lifetime at a given year's age-specific birth rates.
Natural population decline
A decrease in population caused by deaths exceeding births, excluding migration.
Super-aged society
A demographic category, conventionally defined by a share of people over sixty-five of at least one-fifth, that South Korea reached at the end of 2024.
Party congress
The formally highest decision-making meeting of the Workers' Party of Korea, held irregularly, at which leaders and plans are approved.
State Affairs Commission
The North Korean state organ whose president, Kim Jong Un, was formally designated head of state in the 2026 constitutional revision, according to South Korean analysts.
Supreme People's Assembly
North Korea's formal legislature, elected in single-candidate contests; the 15th Assembly was elected in March 2026.
Juche
North Korea's official ideology of self-reliance, associated with Kim Il Sung, whose practical meaning has changed with the state's economic and external circumstances.
Songbun
North Korea's hereditary political-class classification, which has affected residence, occupation, and opportunity.
20×10 regional development policy
A ten-year program announced in 2024 to build modern local factories in twenty North Korean cities and counties each year.
Nuclear deterrence
The strategy of preventing attack by threatening a nuclear response; North Korea declares its arsenal irreversible and central to its security.
Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty
The June 2024 treaty between Russia and North Korea that includes a provision for mutual military assistance.
Minjok
A Korean term for the nation conceived as a people sharing ancestry, language, and history, central to modern Korean nationalism.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not exist or apply in the same way.
Teleology
The practice of explaining events as if they were directed toward a particular outcome, such as treating division or reunification as the inevitable end of Korean history.
Contingency
The condition of depending on particular choices and circumstances that could have produced different results.

Building a Source Dossier on the Divided Peninsula in 2026

This project asks you to construct a small evidence dossier on one question about the peninsula in 2026, using the kinds of sources discussed in Section 96.1 and testing how far each can support a historical claim.

  1. Choose a question. Examples include: Has South Korean support for unification declined, and among whom? What did the Ninth Party Congress change? How did the Russia partnership affect North Korea's economy and military? How did South Korea's political institutions respond to the martial-law cases?
  2. Collect sources of at least four different kinds. Use, for example, official statistics, opinion surveys, court rulings, parliamentary testimony, state media, intelligence assessments, press reports, and scholarly analysis.
  3. Evaluate each source. Identify who produced it, for what purpose, using what method, and what it cannot show. Note whether a source depends on another source or on a single interested party.
  4. Compare and corroborate. Where sources agree, explain why that strengthens a claim. Where they disagree, identify the reason, such as differences in question wording, definitions, or political aims.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, state a conclusion that is as strong as the evidence permits and no stronger, identify what remains uncertain, and explain what additional evidence would help.

Because the events are recent, check the publication date of every source and note whether later developments may have changed the picture.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has been the last in a book that began with the question of what Korea is. The answer offered in Chapter 1 was that "Korea" names a peninsula-centered historical civilization whose political form has varied, from several competing states to a single dynasty to the two states of the present, and that its history must be told without assuming either permanent unity or permanent division. The divided peninsula of 2026 is one more form in that long sequence, and it needs the same careful explanation as the earlier ones.

What the chapter shows most clearly is how different the two histories had become and how much they still shared. One society had built a democracy that survived an attempted subversion, an economy at the center of the world's most advanced computing supply chains, a culture heard and watched around the world, and a population that was shrinking and aging with exceptional speed. The other had built a hereditary party-state with a nuclear arsenal, a new military partnership with Russia, restored ties with China, and a constitution that no longer mentioned the unification of the nation. Between them lay a frontier whose markers were disappearing and whose armistice had never become a peace. Both societies drew on a language, a deep history, and a body of memory that neither could entirely discard.

The chapter also shows how the later history of the peninsula connects to its earlier periods. The questions of this book's first chapters, who counts as Korean, what unity means, and how a peninsula positioned between continental and maritime powers manages its relations with neighbors, returned in new forms in 2026. The Goguryeo and Silla periods saw political plurality and competition over the peninsula, and the Joseon dynasty's long order gave way to the crisis of sovereignty described in Volume III. The twentieth-century division was a different thing from the earlier pluralities, as Chapter 1 insisted, and yet the habit of asking what the whole peninsula is, and who speaks for it, is a very old one in Korean history.

For readers, the larger lesson is methodological. Historians who write about the very recent past must keep distinguishing established evidence from interpretation, must remain alert to the interests of those who produce their sources, and must resist the pull of a story that makes the present seem inevitable. A history that ends in 2026 should leave its readers better able to understand why the peninsula looks as it does, and better able to recognize that the next chapters, which this book does not and cannot write, will depend on choices not yet made by people not yet born as well as by those now alive in Pyongyang, Seoul, and beyond.
