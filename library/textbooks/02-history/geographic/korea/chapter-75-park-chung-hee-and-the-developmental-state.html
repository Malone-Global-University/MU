Park Chung-hee and the Developmental State

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On July 7, 1970, a highway of roughly 428 kilometers between Seoul and Busan opened to traffic, built in under thirty months. Four months later, in the garment district of the same capital, a young tailor's assistant set himself on fire to demand that employers obey the country's labor law. Both events belong to the same history, and a chapter on Park Chung-hee must explain how one government produced both.

In 1961, South Korea was among the poorest countries in East Asia. Its economy, still recovering from the war examined in Part XII, depended heavily on American aid, its exports consisted largely of raw materials and a narrow range of primary goods, and its government had just been overthrown by a student-led uprising that removed Syngman Rhee (Chapter 74). By 1979, the country exported manufactured goods to markets on several continents, produced steel, ships, machinery, and electronics, had built an expressway network linking its principal cities, and counted a rapidly growing urban industrial workforce. Whatever indicator historians prefer, whether exports, output per person, electrification, school enrollment, or the share of workers employed in agriculture, the change over eighteen years was extraordinary by the standards of the twentieth-century world.

The same eighteen years also saw the steady narrowing of political freedom. A military coup in May 1961 ended the parliamentary Second Republic. A civilian constitution and elections returned in 1963, but under the leadership of the officer who had led the coup. A constitutional amendment in 1969 allowed that leader to seek a third term; a close election in 1971 was followed by a declaration of national emergency; and in October 1972 President Park Chung-hee suspended the constitution, dissolved the National Assembly, and imposed the Yushin ("revitalizing reform") system, under which presidents were chosen by an indirectly elected conference and could rule by emergency decree. Dissidents were imprisoned, newspapers were censored, labor organizing was restricted, and some opponents were tortured or executed. Park's presidency ended only with his assassination in October 1979.

This chapter treats these two developments as a single historical problem, without collapsing one into the other. Its central argument is that South Korea's industrial transformation resulted from deliberate state intervention, institutional planning, export incentives, international financing, private enterprise, and the labor of millions of Koreans, and that these achievements occurred alongside the concentration of presidential power, restrictions on civil liberties, labor repression, and increasingly coercive political institutions. Economic transformation and authoritarian consolidation were closely intertwined, but neither was an inevitable consequence of the other. Historians still debate how the coercive institutions affected particular policies, and that debate is one of the chapter's recurring subjects.

Two simple interpretations must be avoided. The first holds that Park single-handedly created South Korean prosperity, an image cultivated by his own government and preserved in later nostalgia. The second holds that industrial development under his presidency can be understood only through its authoritarian political system, as though every factory, road, and export contract were merely an instrument of repression. The evidence supports neither. The developmental state of the 1960s and 1970s drew on schools, administrative institutions, private businesses, American-sponsored reconstruction programs, and a land reform inherited from the 1950s. It was also shaped by Japanese capital, American security guarantees, the Vietnam War, global trade expansion, and the daily work of farmers, engineers, soldiers, construction laborers, and above all young factory workers, many of them women.

The chapter's evidence is unusually varied. Government plans, budgets, and statistical series from the Bank of Korea and international agencies document the economy, though their figures have been revised and must be read carefully. Treaties, constitutional texts, and National Assembly records document institutions. American diplomatic reporting provides an outside view, as do Japanese records of the 1965 negotiations. Park's own writings explain the regime's self-image but were produced for political purposes. Labor history, memoirs, opposition manifestos, and decades of later truth-commission and court investigations recover the experience of those who left few contemporary records, and revise some official accounts of political cases. Throughout the chapter, students are asked whose evidence a statement rests on, and whose voices that evidence leaves out.

The chapter is organized in twenty numbered sections across five divisions. The first four sections trace the coup and the construction of a new administrative order (1960–1963). Sections 75.5 through 75.8 follow the institutions of planning and export-led growth. Sections 75.9 through 75.12 examine how relations with the United States, Japan, and Vietnam supported and constrained that strategy. Sections 75.13 through 75.16 turn to farmers, workers, the heavy and chemical industry drive, and the uneven distribution of benefits. Sections 75.17 through 75.20 explain the authoritarian consolidation of the 1970s and the crisis that ended it. Detailed histories of the great conglomerates belong to Chapter 76, the North Korean state to Chapter 77, inter-Korean military confrontation to Chapter 78, the aftermath of Park's assassination to Chapter 79, and the comprehensive social history of urban life to Chapter 81.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Park Chung-hee and the military officers around him seized power in 1961 and how military and intelligence influence persisted inside the formally civilian Third Republic.
  2. Describe the principal institutions of the developmental state, including the Economic Planning Board, the five-year plans, state control of banking, and the Korean Central Intelligence Agency, and distinguish economic coordination from political surveillance.
  3. Trace the shift from import-substituting priorities in the first five-year plan to the export-oriented strategy of the mid-1960s, identifying the incentives, exchange-rate decisions, and coordination mechanisms involved.
  4. Analyze how preferential credit, foreign borrowing, and state guarantees shaped industrial investment, and assess their risks, including concentration, corruption, and excessive debt.
  5. Evaluate the roles of the United States, Japan, and the Vietnam War in providing security, capital, and markets, while avoiding the assumption that foreign assistance alone produced growth.
  6. Distinguish the categories of the 1965 Japan–South Korea economic package and explain why the settlement remained controversial.
  7. Assess the Saemaul Undong as both a rural development program and a vehicle of political mobilization, accounting for the agency of rural Koreans.
  8. Analyze the working conditions of industrial laborers, especially young women, and explain the significance of Jeon Tae-il's protest in 1970.
  9. Explain the purposes, instruments, and risks of the Heavy and Chemical Industry drive of 1973–1979.
  10. Compare the constitutional mechanisms of the Third Republic and the Yushin system, and explain how presidential power was extended.
  11. Interpret primary sources, including retrospective memoirs, treaty texts, diplomatic memoranda, constitutions, and emergency decrees, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.
  12. Evaluate competing historical explanations of South Korean economic transformation and of the relationship between development and dictatorship.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What capacities of the South Korean state and society already existed in 1961, and what did Park's government add, redirect, or destroy?
  2. Why did a coup in 1961 succeed when the Second Republic had been freely elected, and what justifications did the military later offer?
  3. How did the shift from planning for domestic self-sufficiency to promoting exports occur, and how much of it reflected design rather than improvisation under pressure?
  4. In what ways did control of banking and foreign borrowing give the state leverage over private firms, and what problems did that leverage create?
  5. How did external relationships with the United States, Japan, and Vietnam supply resources while also constraining or shaping South Korean choices?
  6. Who bore the costs of rapid industrialization, and how did the distribution of those costs differ among workers, farmers, women, entrepreneurs, and regions?
  7. Was the Yushin system a necessary instrument of economic development, a response to security fears, a reaction to political competition, or some combination, and what evidence bears on each explanation?
  8. How did opposition and dissent survive under emergency decrees, and why should later democratic success not be treated as inevitable?
  9. What are the strengths and limits of aggregate growth rates as a measure of national development?
  10. Why did the accomplishments of the developmental state help create the conditions of its own political crisis?

Historical Context

Students should arrive at Chapter 75 with the main outlines of Chapters 73 and 74 in mind, which this section summarizes only as far as needed. The economy Park inherited had been shaped by Japanese colonial industrialization (Chapters 58 and 61), much of whose heavy industry had been concentrated in the north, by the division of 1945, and by the destruction of the Korean War. During the 1950s, American economic assistance financed a large share of imports and of the government's budget, and American surplus agricultural commodities supplied much of the food supply. Reconstruction restored roads, rail lines, power capacity, and light industries, particularly food processing, textiles, and the manufacture of consumer goods from imported raw materials. Private enterprises that obtained import licenses, foreign exchange at favorable rates, and the sale of formerly Japanese-owned properties often prospered through their relationships with the Rhee government.

Several foundations of later growth were laid in this period, even though the economy as a whole grew slowly and unemployment and underemployment remained severe. The land reform carried out between 1949 and 1950 had redistributed much agricultural land and weakened the old landlord class. Primary school enrollment expanded rapidly, and secondary and university education grew faster than the economy could employ graduates, creating an educated, underemployed urban population. The army, expanded to roughly 600,000 men during the war and sustained afterward with American equipment and training, became one of the largest organized bodies of technically trained manpower in the country, with officers who had studied in American military institutions. Government planning agencies, including a reconstruction ministry and an Economic Development Council, had produced draft development plans under Rhee, though they were seldom carried out.

Rhee's government fell in April 1960 after massive protests against fraudulent elections, examined in Chapter 74. An interim government under Heo Jeong prepared for new elections, and in July 1960 the Democratic Party won a commanding victory. Under a revised constitution establishing a parliamentary system, Yun Bo-seon became the largely ceremonial president and Chang Myon (also romanized Jang Myeon) became prime minister. The new government faced unemployment, rising demands from students and workers for the full freedoms promised by the revolution, bitter conflict within the governing party, and an army whose senior officers had mostly served under Rhee. Section 75.1 examines this brief experiment more closely.

Park Chung-hee's own background shaped how he and many in his circle understood these conditions. Born in 1917 in North Gyeongsang Province to a poor farming family, he trained as a teacher, then entered the Manchukuo military academy and later the Imperial Japanese Army Academy, and served as an officer in the Manchukuo army before 1945. After liberation he joined the South Korean constabulary, was arrested in the 1948 military purge following the Yeosu-Suncheon rebellion on suspicion of communist associations, and was spared from severe punishment, and then returned to service during the Korean War. Scholars differ on how much his colonial-era training and his earlier leftist connections shaped his later outlook, and these topics were politically sensitive both under his government and afterward. The chapter treats them as context rather than explanation.

The geopolitical setting was equally important. The Cold War division of the peninsula meant that the South Korean state considered itself to be in constant competition with North Korea, whose reconstruction in the late 1950s was widely perceived, in the South and abroad, as proceeding faster than the South's (Chapters 73 and 77). The United States, which retained command of South Korean forces under the United Nations Command and maintained tens of thousands of troops in the country, was beginning to reduce the share of its aid given as grants and to look for ways to make South Korea more self-reliant. Japan, an essential regional trading partner, remained without diplomatic relations with Seoul. These conditions framed the choices of 1961 and after.

Division I — Military Revolution and the Construction of a New State, 1960–1963 · Section 75.1

75.1 — The Second Republic, Political Instability, and the Road to the 1961 Coup

The Second Republic lasted less than ten months, and its brevity has shaped the way it is remembered. The government that took office in August 1960 was the freely elected product of a revolution, and it presided over the freest political environment South Korea had yet experienced. Newspapers multiplied, labor unions and teachers' associations organized, and student groups, who regarded themselves as the guardians of the April Revolution, demanded punishment of Rhee-era officials and of police who had fired on demonstrators. Within the governing Democratic Party, however, a long-standing rivalry between an "old" faction around Yun Bo-seon and a "new" faction around Chang Myon had already divided the party, and the parliamentary system gave little room for a compromise. Chang's cabinet was reshuffled repeatedly, and by early 1961 the old faction had broken away to form a separate party.

The government's difficulties were real, though they were not simply those of incompetence. The economy was weak: unemployment, particularly among educated urban youth, was high; agricultural production was uncertain; and the budget depended on American assistance whose character was shifting. The Chang government drafted a five-year economic plan, devalued the currency in early 1961, and spoke of an "economy first" policy, but it had little time to carry these out. Historians have noted that the plan later associated with Park's government built on earlier planning work, including drafts prepared under Rhee and under Chang, an important reminder that the idea of national planning predated the coup. Public order was also contested. Large and sometimes disorderly demonstrations, conservative alarm at student calls for contact with North Korean students, and proposed security legislation all contributed to a climate in which many conservatives, and some Americans, doubted the government's authority.

Inside the military, resentment and ambition had been gathering for several years. The army had grown large and rapidly promoted its officers, but a blocked career ladder, widespread belief in corruption among senior generals, and anger at the treatment of younger officers who had called for the resignation of senior commanders in 1960 produced a group of dissatisfied officers, many from the Korean Military Academy's eighth graduating class (which included Kim Jong-pil). Park, then a major general in a senior but marginal position, had previously been involved in plots against Rhee and had a reputation as a disciplined, austere officer who was skeptical of both politicians and senior colleagues. By the spring of 1961 he and the younger officers around him were planning to seize power.

Historians distinguish carefully between the Second Republic's actual weaknesses and the military's later justifications. The junta's proclamations described a government paralyzed by factionalism, corrupt, and unable to resist communism, and portrayed the coup as a rescue of the nation. Some of these accusations had substance, particularly regarding party conflict and economic difficulty. Others were exaggerated: the government had not collapsed, its political opponents had not seized power, and the army's justifications about communist infiltration were not matched by evidence of an imminent crisis. Many scholars also stress that the Second Republic's problems were those of a new democracy emerging from authoritarian rule, and that the officers' decision to intervene reflected their own institutional interests, their view of themselves as the nation's most capable organization, and the knowledge that no effective force existed to stop them. The chapter therefore treats the coup not as an inevitable result of democratic failure but as a decision made by particular actors in a particular situation.

Section 75.2

75.2 — The May 16 Military Coup and the Supreme Council for National Reconstruction

In the early hours of May 16, 1961, units loyal to Park, including marines and army troops numbering a few thousand, crossed the Han River bridge into Seoul, which was reportedly lightly defended at that hour, and took control of the key government buildings, the broadcasting station, and the army headquarters. Their numbers were small, and the coup might have been reversed had senior commanders acted decisively. Instead, the army chief of staff and several generals initially wavered or acquiesced, the First Army commander did not mobilize against the plotters, and the senior American officers, including the commander of the United Nations Command, publicly stated that they backed the legally constituted government. Without a clear indication that they would use force to restore it, the American statement did not prevent the coup from succeeding. Prime Minister Chang Myon went into hiding and, after two days, led his cabinet to resign. President Yun Bo-seon, who remained in office, did not resist and for a time lent the junta a form of constitutional cover, resigning only in March 1962.

The coup leaders proclaimed a Military Revolutionary Committee, which was soon renamed the Supreme Council for National Reconstruction (SCNR). Its first announcements declared martial law, dissolved the National Assembly, suspended political parties and activities, closed borders and airports temporarily, and imposed press censorship. A set of six "revolutionary pledges" read over the radio committed the junta to anti-communism, adherence to alliances with the United States and the free world, the eradication of corruption, the relief of poverty through the building of a self-sufficient economy, preparation for confrontation with communism and eventual unification, and the transfer of power to "conscientious" civilians once these tasks had been accomplished and the officers could return to military duties. The last promise would prove significant, as it provided the basis on which Park would later transform a military regime into a civilian-led government.

Park's role in the early weeks was somewhat concealed. The council's first chairman was the army chief of staff, General Chang Do-yong, a senior officer who had accepted the coup. In July 1961, however, he was arrested on charges of plotting against the council and Park took over as chairman, thereby making himself the formal head of the regime. A sweeping set of political measures followed. The council arrested thousands of persons, including politicians, labor and student leaders, and suspected leftists, as well as alleged gang members who were paraded through the streets; set up revolutionary courts; passed a law enabling retroactive punishment of those regarded as having amassed illicit wealth under Rhee; and in 1962 passed a political purification law that barred many politicians from public life for several years. Several people were executed after trials by revolutionary courts, including the newspaper publisher Cho Yong-su, whose case later became a symbol of the junta's treatment of the left.

It is important to describe the coup as a rupture in constitutional government rather than as a mere change in administrators. A legally elected government was replaced by force, the legislature was dissolved, civil liberties were suspended, and for more than two years the country was ruled by decree. Defenders of the regime later argued that this rupture was necessary to achieve economic transformation, a claim examined in the historical debate later in this chapter. Whatever one's view of that claim, it should not obscure the fact that the officers who seized power did not at first have an economic program, were divided about priorities, and made many of their initial decisions, including a disastrous currency reform in 1962, under severe political and fiscal uncertainty. The economic strategy for which the era is remembered emerged gradually, through trial, error, and changes of direction, over the following years.

The United States' response illustrates the limits of external influence. After initial public opposition, Washington accepted the new reality, partly because the officers repeated their anti-communist commitments and partly because it feared that further instability would help North Korea. The Kennedy administration pressed the junta to return to civilian government and to hold elections, and used the aid relationship to do so. In November 1961 Park, then chairman of the SCNR, visited Washington and met President Kennedy, a meeting that gave him legitimacy and underscored the dependence of the new government on American support. American pressure, however, did not determine what kind of civilian government would emerge, as section 75.4 shows.

Section 75.3

75.3 — The Economic Planning Board, State Administration, and Military-Led Reform

The new government's most durable institutional innovation was administrative. In July 1961 the SCNR established the Economic Planning Board (EPB), headed by a deputy prime minister, and gave it a combination of functions that had previously been dispersed: preparing and coordinating long-term economic plans, drawing up the national budget, which was taken from the Ministry of Finance, and overseeing the management of foreign capital and technical cooperation. Combining planning and budgetary authority in one agency with a minister of high rank gave it the means to enforce its plans, and its leaders were often among the best-trained economists and civil servants of the government. Over time the EPB became the central coordinating ministry of the development strategy, working alongside the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, the Bank of Korea, and public enterprises, and sometimes in rivalry with them.

Strengthening state capacity was a broader aim of the military government. It reorganized revenue administration, with the establishment of a national tax service in 1966 following earlier improvements, and expanded efforts to collect taxes more effectively; reduced the autonomy of the commercial banks by bringing them under tighter government control, so that decisions about credit allocation increasingly reflected official priorities; created public enterprises in sectors such as fertilizer, electric power, and later steel; and introduced a system of examinations and training for officials. Many of these measures built on the existing civil service, which retained much of the personnel and procedures of the Rhee era and of the Japanese colonial administration. Military officers also took many positions in government and in public enterprises, a practice that brought new energy and discipline to some agencies and cronyism and inexperience to others. The administrative state of the 1960s was thus the product of both continuity and reorganization.

The regime also moved to discipline the business community, whose leaders had flourished under Rhee. Within weeks of the coup, a number of leading entrepreneurs were arrested under the law against illicit wealth accumulation. They were soon released after negotiating an understanding, in which they agreed to invest in industrial enterprises that the government regarded as priorities, in exchange for leniency. This episode, often cited as an origin of the later partnership between the government and large private firms, illustrated the government's power to threaten and to reward, and its recognition that it needed private enterprise rather than public ownership as the principal engine of industrial expansion. A business association, which became the Federation of Korean Industries, was organized in this period to provide a channel for consultation. Private firms retained their own interests and sometimes resisted official preferences, but their position was now plainly dependent on state favor.

The Korean Central Intelligence Agency, created in June 1961 under Kim Jong-pil, was a different kind of institution. Modeled in part on the American Central Intelligence Agency and given a mandate to coordinate the collection of domestic and foreign intelligence, it quickly became an instrument for investigating opponents, supervising political activity, intimidating the press and the business community, and organizing political parties and operations. Its agents had powers of arrest and interrogation outside normal police channels, and the agency's role in the political system continued long after the military government had formally ended. It is important to distinguish the economic and the security functions of the new state while recognizing that they operated together. The EPB planned budgets and credit; the KCIA monitored dissent. Yet the KCIA also intervened in economic matters, as with its involvement in a series of financial scandals of 1963–1964 involving stock manipulation, imported automobiles, and entertainment projects, which showed how political and economic power could be intertwined.

The new government's early economic measures were not uniformly successful. The currency reform of June 1962, which replaced the hwan with the won at a rate of ten to one, was intended to draw out hidden private savings and channel them to productive investment. Instead it disrupted markets, provoked a loss of confidence, and was substantially reversed within weeks, partly under American pressure. A poor harvest in 1962–1963 and shortage of foreign exchange added to the difficulties. These setbacks, discussed further in 75.5, are a useful corrective to accounts that treat the early military government as an efficient technocracy. Its administrative capacities were growing, but its early decisions were improvisations made under pressure.

Section 75.4

75.4 — The Return to Civilian Government and Park's Presidency, 1963

Park and his colleagues had promised to return to barracks, and under American pressure and domestic expectation they felt compelled to appear to do so. The transition, however, was managed with a view to preserving their position. A constitutional revision, approved in a national referendum in December 1962 by a large official majority, replaced the parliamentary system with a presidential one, established a unicameral National Assembly, and contained provisions, such as requirements that presidential candidates belong to a political party and restrictions on party switching by legislators, that favored a strong organized governing party. The new constitution also included a clear limit of two terms for the president, a provision that would be amended in 1969.

Park's own plans were at first uncertain. In early 1963 he announced that he would not run, then reversed himself amid disputes among the military factions, some of whom wished to continue military rule and others of whom had come to prefer a civilian transition, and amid tension with American officials. Meanwhile the KCIA, with Kim Jong-pil as its principal organizer, secretly built the Democratic Republican Party as a vehicle for the junta's civilian transformation; funds for the party came from irregular sources, including the stock market scandals and import manipulations noted in 75.3, which later became a subject of political scandal. In August 1963 Park retired from the army and became the party's candidate.

The presidential election of October 15, 1963 was closer than the government anticipated. Park's principal opponent was the former president Yun Bo-seon, who campaigned on a platform that criticized the coup and military rule, and other candidates divided the opposition vote. Park won by fewer than 160,000 votes out of more than ten million cast, with strong support in both the southeastern provinces and parts of the southwest, as well as Jeju, but markedly weaker support in Seoul, Gyeonggi, Gangwon, and much of Chungcheong. The 1963 map should not be confused with the sharply polarized Yeongnam–Honam pattern visible in the 1971 contest. In the National Assembly elections in November, the Democratic Republican Party won a decisive majority of seats, though the opposition parties together won a substantial share of the popular vote. Park was inaugurated in December 1963, and the Third Republic began.

The Third Republic was a genuinely different institutional order from the junta, with an elected president, a legislature in which opposition parties held seats, newspapers that, within limits, criticized the government, and a judiciary that retained some independence. Opposition politicians, students, and journalists continued to contest the government's policies, and the 1964 and 1965 protests over normalization with Japan (75.10) showed how seriously they could challenge it. It would be wrong to describe the period as simple dictatorship. But it would also be wrong to suppose that the return of constitutional forms ended the military's influence. Many senior positions in the government, the party, and public enterprises were held by former officers; the KCIA and the military security organizations retained broad powers; and the governing party's finances, sometimes drawn from official or semi-official sources, gave it advantages over its opponents. The government repeatedly turned to emergency measures, martial law, and garrison decrees when challenged, and the ruling party's willingness to alter rules to suit its interests, evident in 1969, foreshadowed more drastic changes after 1971. The limits of the civilian transition were thus built into it from the beginning, though they did not make later events inevitable.

Historical Vocabulary
Developmental State, Five-Year Plan, and Export Promotion

Definition. A developmental state is a scholarly term for a government that treats rapid economic growth and industrial transformation as its central purpose and uses planning agencies, credit allocation, trade policy, and cooperation with private firms to pursue it. A five-year plan is a set of national economic targets and priorities for a five-year period. Export promotion refers to policies, including exchange-rate adjustments, tax relief, preferential credit, and targets, that encourage firms to sell abroad, in contrast to import substitution, which seeks to produce at home goods previously imported.

Historical context. The term "developmental state" is an analytical label applied by later scholars, and it was not used by Park's government to describe itself. South Korean plans differed from the comprehensive plans of Soviet-style economies: they gave binding commands mainly to public enterprises and relied on incentives and pressure to guide private firms.

Why it matters. These terms organize the debate over what the state did and how far it was responsible for growth.

Potential misunderstanding. A "plan" in this context was neither a complete schedule that was fulfilled nor a socialist command system. It was a statement of priorities that was revised repeatedly. "Export promotion" did not mean that import substitution ended; the two coexisted, especially in the 1970s.

Division II — Economic Planning and Export-Led Development, 1962–1971 · Section 75.5

75.5 — The First Five-Year Economic Development Plan, 1962–1966

The First Five-Year Economic Development Plan, announced in January 1962 and covering 1962–1966, reflected a mixture of nationalist ambition, inherited planning, and the pragmatic priorities of a government with few foreign exchange reserves. It set a target of sustained growth, an average of around seven percent per year in its original version, to be achieved by expanding the basic capacities of the economy: electric power and coal as energy sources; fertilizer, cement, and oil refining as basic inputs; and the improvement of agriculture and transportation. It also envisioned the creation of an industrial complex at Ulsan on the southeastern coast, where a groundbreaking ceremony was held in 1962. The language of the plan stressed the building of a "self-sufficient" or "self-reliant" economy, reducing dependence on imports and on American aid, and creating employment for a population in which unemployment and underemployment were widespread. In this early form the strategy leaned toward import substitution, or producing domestically what had previously been imported, though with an increasing awareness of the need to earn foreign exchange.

The plan was a statement of intent rather than a blueprint that was executed as designed, and its early years brought serious difficulty. The financial reform of 1962 failed to mobilize the hoped-for savings. A poor harvest in 1962 and further problems in 1963 required large imports of grain, drawing down foreign exchange, which was also depleted by imports of machinery and materials for the new projects. Inflation accelerated, and the government was forced into emergency measures, including negotiations with American officials over aid and surplus commodity shipments. The plan's original assumption that large amounts of foreign capital could be obtained on favorable terms proved unrealistic, since the government's creditworthiness was low and many lenders were hesitant. Some of the planned investments, such as an integrated steel mill that international lenders declined to finance, could not be carried out in the form first intended, and the integrated steel project was postponed for several years.

By 1963 and 1964 the government and its advisers were revising their approach. Stabilization measures, strongly encouraged by the United States and international financial institutions, tightened credit and reduced inflation, and the plan itself was revised in 1964, with lower targets for the more ambitious industrial projects and a greater emphasis on exports as the means of earning foreign exchange. The government had also begun to see that the labor-intensive manufacture of goods such as textiles, plywood, and wigs could be competitive in foreign markets, since South Korea had an abundance of workers with at least primary education and wages that were low compared with those in wealthy countries. These changes were not simply the execution of a design formed in 1961. They emerged from a combination of observation, advice, pressure, and the fortunes of particular industries, and historians continue to debate how deliberate the export turn was, as discussed in the Historical Debate.

The results of the first plan were nevertheless substantial by any measure. According to commonly cited Bank of Korea series, which have been revised over time, real output grew at an average annual rate of roughly eight percent during 1962–1966, with considerable year-to-year variation, from slow growth in 1962 and 1965 to very rapid growth in 1963–1964 and 1966. Exports, which had been a very small share of the economy at the beginning of the decade, grew several times over in dollar terms, and the economy's structure shifted toward manufacturing. The electric power, cement, fertilizer, and refining capacities envisioned in the plan were largely created, with the help of foreign loans and technology. These figures must be interpreted carefully, since they are measured in constant or nominal prices, in won or in dollars, and from low starting points, a distinction students should observe when reading the statistical series cited in this chapter.

The plan was also political. By setting national targets, publicizing progress, and presenting economic growth as the principal national goal, the government offered a purpose for the coup and a standard against which its performance could be judged. The plan's authors came from the military, the civil service, universities, and research institutes, and the government's frequent public statements and ceremonies gave planning a symbolic importance beyond its technical content. The following source window examines Park's own later account of this effort.

75.6 — The Export Strategy: Incentives, Exchange Rates, and Government Coordination

The export strategy that took shape in the mid-1960s was a package of measures rather than a single policy. Its foundation was a sharp change in the exchange rate. After years in which the won had been overvalued, making imports cheap and exports unprofitable, the government devalued the currency substantially in 1964, from roughly 130 to roughly 255 won per American dollar, and in 1965 replaced the system of multiple exchange rates with a unified, managed floating rate. A realistic exchange rate made it profitable for firms to produce for foreign markets, and a series of later adjustments helped maintain exporters' competitiveness. In 1965 the government also raised interest rates on bank deposits sharply, which increased the volume of domestic savings available to the banking system, a measure that complemented rather than replaced foreign borrowing.

On this base the government built an extensive system of incentives. Exporters could obtain bank credit at preferential interest rates, receive reductions or exemptions from income and corporate taxes on export earnings, and import raw materials and intermediate goods duty-free for use in export production, with a system of rebates and "wastage allowances." Import licenses, which were scarce and valuable, were linked to export performance, so that a firm that exported successfully could also obtain the right to import or to produce for the domestic market at protected prices. Industrial estates, such as the export complex at Guro in Seoul created in the mid-1960s and the Masan Free Export Zone established in 1970, provided infrastructure, tax advantages, and ready access to ports. Government agencies, including the Korea Trade Promotion Corporation (KOTRA), established in 1962, helped firms find foreign buyers and collect market information.

Coordination between government and business was frequent and direct. From 1965 the president personally chaired regular, often monthly, export promotion meetings attended by ministers, bankers, trade association leaders, and executives, at which targets for particular industries and firms were set and results reviewed. Export achievements were publicly celebrated: November 30, 1964, when annual exports first reached one hundred million dollars, was later commemorated as Export Day, and medals and tax benefits rewarded successful firms. The system combined incentives and pressure. Firms that met targets received favors, including easier credit and licenses, and those that failed risked being penalized. Because the principal business partners were firms that proved their ability to meet targets, the system also favored the growth of larger enterprises, a tendency examined further in 75.7 and in Chapter 76.

The strategy depended on international conditions. In the 1960s world trade was expanding rapidly, tariffs on manufactured goods were falling, and the United States, in particular, offered a large and open market for labor-intensive goods such as clothing, plywood, and electronics components. The United States also permitted South Korea to participate in markets under conditions that were beneficial to it, and tolerated its use of export subsidies for some years. Japan became a major source of capital goods and intermediate inputs, and thereafter a trading partner, a dependence that created a persistent bilateral trade deficit, and Vietnam-related demand (75.11) also gave a boost to exports. The strategy was therefore the product of South Korean policy and of a favorable, but changing, external environment. Later in the 1970s, as protectionist pressure rose in the United States and Europe against textiles and other products, the same environment became more difficult.

Firms responded to both the opportunities and the pressures. Many entrepreneurs, including those who would build large conglomerates, entered export manufacturing quickly, taking advantage of the incentives and the access to credit they provided. Others, including small and medium enterprises, produced as subcontractors or in small workshops, and a considerable share of exports were produced by such firms or by small factories that supplied large ones. The commodities that dominated early exports were light manufactured goods: plywood, textiles and garments, wigs, footwear, and simple electronics assembly. By the early 1970s manufactures made up the great majority of exports, and the product composition was moving gradually from the simplest goods to more sophisticated ones.

It is also necessary to distinguish rising exports from improvements in living standards. The early export boom depended on low wages. Because labor was abundant and unions were restricted, employers could keep wages low while productivity increased, and these low labor costs were a central part of South Korean competitiveness. Real wages did rise over time, as employment expanded and the labor market tightened, but for many years wages lagged behind productivity, and the benefits of the export boom were shared unevenly (see 75.14 and 75.16). The export strategy thus created jobs and foreign exchange while also placing a heavy burden on the people who produced the goods.

Section 75.7

75.7 — Banks, Foreign Capital, State-Directed Credit, and Industrial Investment

Control of finance was the government's most powerful instrument. The commercial banks, which had been privatized in the 1950s but dominated by the government and large debtors, were placed under tight official control after 1961, and specialized development banks, such as the Korea Development Bank, and later banks such as the Korea Exchange Bank (1967), extended credit for designated purposes. The central bank lost much of its independence to the Ministry of Finance. Interest rates were set by the government, and credit was allocated by official guidance and through "policy loans" to sectors and firms that the government wished to promote. Because the supply of credit was limited and the demand for it large, access to bank loans at preferential rates was a valuable privilege, equivalent to a subsidy when the rates were lower than the rate of inflation or the market rate.

Foreign capital was a second pillar. Domestic savings were low, and the government's strategy depended on borrowing abroad. The Foreign Capital Inducement Law, enacted in 1960 and revised in 1966, allowed the government to guarantee repayment of foreign loans to private firms, which gave lenders security and gave the government control over which firms could borrow. Because South Korea had no easy access to private international capital markets in the early 1960s, the government obtained funds in a variety of ways: official development assistance and loans from the United States and later from international institutions such as the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank (founded in 1966); funds from Japan under the 1965 agreement (75.10); commercial loans from West Germany, whose arrangement was supported by the earnings of thousands of Korean miners and nurses sent to work there under government contracts from 1963 onward, a program that Park publicized during a visit to West Germany in 1964; and, as the decade progressed, loans from foreign commercial banks. External debt grew steadily as a result, and the total rose from a very low level in the early 1960s to several billion dollars by the early 1970s and roughly twenty billion by the end of the decade, by commonly cited estimates.

Public policy used these instruments to favor particular investments and to impose performance conditions. Firms that wished to borrow abroad or to receive preferential credit were expected to establish factories in priority sectors, to meet export targets, and to follow official guidance about the scale and timing of investments. The government could and did withdraw credit from firms that failed to meet its expectations, and some firms went bankrupt or were taken over by others. In this sense the system was not merely a subsidy but a form of conditional support, an arrangement in which the government acted as a kind of lender of the first resort. Many scholars regard this performance discipline as an important distinction between South Korea and other developing countries where subsidies were distributed without comparable conditions, though they disagree about how consistently it was applied.

The system also created risks, which became evident over time. Preferential credit, concentrated in the hands of firms with political connections or proven records, strengthened the position of large enterprises relative to small ones and encouraged them to expand rapidly through borrowing, so that corporate debt-to-equity ratios were very high. Because the government guaranteed many foreign loans, it bore the risk of default, and a downturn would have to be addressed by the state. Political favoritism and corruption were persistent concerns: firms contributed to the governing party, and officials with control over credit and licenses were susceptible to bribery. The financial scandals of 1963–1964 and later cases demonstrated this tendency, and opposition politicians and journalists criticized it, though under conditions of restricted press freedom.

The informal credit market, or "curb market," in which small and medium firms borrowed at high interest rates from private lenders because they were excluded from bank credit, expanded alongside the formal system. By 1972 many firms were heavily dependent on curb loans at high rates, and a downturn in the economy, combined with high foreign debts, produced a crisis. On August 3, 1972 the government issued an emergency decree, announced as an "emergency decree for economic stability and growth," which declared a moratorium on repayment of curb loans, forced a conversion of those loans into longer-term obligations at lower interest, and provided the firms with new bank credit. The measure rescued many firms, to the detriment of private lenders, and demonstrated again the government's willingness to use extraordinary powers to manage the private economy. It preceded by only a few weeks the political emergency of October 1972 (75.18).

The great private firms are introduced here only as examples of the system. Their ownership, internal organization, and long-term histories, including the origins of Samsung, Hyundai, and others as conglomerates, are the subject of Chapter 76. The point for this chapter is that finance was a political as well as an economic instrument, one that allowed the government to guide the economy without owning most of its productive assets, and that it also tied the fortunes of business to those of the government.

Section 75.8

75.8 — The Second Five-Year Plan and the Expansion of Industrial Infrastructure, 1967–1971

The Second Five-Year Plan, covering 1967–1971, was launched at a time of confidence. The first plan had been succeeded by a period of rapid growth, exports were increasing quickly, and the government, which won re-election in 1967, felt able to set more ambitious goals. The plan's emphasis was on modernizing the structure of industry, increasing exports, expanding employment, developing the chemical, steel, and machinery industries (though still at a modest scale compared with the 1970s), and building the transportation, power, and communications infrastructure on which further industrialization depended. In practice, actual growth during these years was very high, in the range of about ten percent per year in real terms, according to commonly cited estimates, and exceeded the plan's targets. The plan's targets for the export sector, in particular, were exceeded.

The most celebrated project of the period was the Gyeongbu Expressway, linking Seoul and Busan over a distance of roughly 428 kilometers. Construction began in February 1968 and was completed in July 1970, a pace that required the use of the army's engineers alongside private construction firms, including Hyundai Construction, which built a large share of the road. The project was not without controversy. Opposition politicians questioned its cost and priority, arguing that investment should be made in railroads or in agriculture and that the expressway would mainly benefit the southeastern region and the wealthy. Some international advisers, including World Bank officials, had expressed doubts about the economic justification. The government financed the construction largely from domestic sources and went forward. Dozens of workers died during construction, a figure commonly cited as seventy-seven, though the number varies by source and by what is counted. Once completed, the expressway cut travel time between the two largest cities by many hours and, in combination with the growth of motor transport, reshaped the movement of goods and people.

Infrastructure was a practical part of the strategy. Port facilities at Busan and Incheon were expanded and modernized, with new container handling and deeper berths; industrial estates were built in Seoul, Ulsan, Masan, Gumi, and other places, equipped with roads, water, power, and sometimes housing for workers; the national power grid was extended, and the first nuclear power plant, at Kori near Busan, was begun in 1971; telecommunications were expanded, though demand continued to exceed supply for years; and the Seoul-Incheon Expressway, opened in 1968, linked the capital with its principal port. In the technology sphere, the Korea Institute of Science and Technology was founded in 1966 with American financial support, and the government began to build a framework of research institutions and technical schools. These investments supported industrial growth in a direct way, lowering transport costs, ensuring supplies of electricity, and providing trained engineers and technicians.

The geography of investment was uneven. Most of the new infrastructure was concentrated along the Seoul–Busan axis and in the southeastern region, where major ports and the birthplace of Park himself were located, and the northwestern and southwestern regions, including the Honam region in the southwest, received proportionally less investment. Scholars debate how much this unevenness reflected economic logic, such as the advantages of the southeastern ports and the Seoul market, and how much reflected political favoritism, but it is clear that regional differences in infrastructure and industry became a significant feature of South Korean life and politics. The decisions about financing also generated debate. Large capital outlays were funded partly by foreign loans, partly by government borrowing and tax revenue, and partly by monetary expansion that contributed to inflation. Critics argued that the money could have been used differently, while supporters held that the infrastructure was essential. The historian should treat infrastructure not as a collection of celebrated projects but as a set of decisions about priorities and resources, made under constraints and with results that varied by region and by social group.

Division III — Diplomacy, Security, and International Development Finance · Section 75.9

75.9 — The United States, the Security Alliance, and Economic Bargaining

The alliance with the United States, formalized in the Mutual Defense Treaty of 1953, was the framework within which Park's government operated. American forces remained in South Korea, the United States retained operational control of the South Korean military under the United Nations Command, and American military and economic assistance continued to underwrite a substantial portion of the defense budget and of imports. This dependence gave Washington influence but did not give it control. Park and his officials were skilled bargainers, and their strategy repeatedly involved linking South Korea's security value to American interests, in order to win assistance, markets, and commitments.

Relations with the three American administrations of the era followed different rhythms. The Kennedy administration, after initial hesitation, accepted the coup and sought to influence the new government toward civilian rule and economic planning, using the leverage of aid, as in the pressure that helped reverse the 1962 currency reform. President Lyndon Johnson, whose administration was deepening its war in Vietnam, valued South Korean military participation, and Park's visit to Washington in May 1965 produced American pledges of assistance, including support for what became the Korea Institute of Science and Technology. Johnson visited Seoul in 1966, a sign of the closeness of the relationship. The Nixon administration, by contrast, sought to reduce American commitments in Asia. The Nixon Doctrine, announced in Guam in July 1969, held that Asian allies should take primary responsibility for their own ground defense, and in 1970–1971 the United States withdrew one of the two American infantry divisions in South Korea, the Seventh Division, of about twenty thousand men, over Park's objections. In exchange, Washington promised a program of military modernization worth approximately 1.5 billion dollars.

The withdrawal, combined with President Nixon's opening to China in 1971–1972 and the American retreat from Vietnam, provoked deep anxiety in Seoul about the reliability of the United States. Its effect on domestic politics and economic policy was significant. The government's emphasis on self-reliance in defense helped justify the Heavy and Chemical Industry drive (75.15), since steel, machinery, shipbuilding, and chemicals were also the basis of a domestic arms industry. It established the Agency for Defense Development in 1970 and, from the mid-1970s, a long-term program of force modernization and a defense tax. Historians also report that the government explored the possibility of developing nuclear weapons and missile capabilities in the 1970s, a program that was ended or suspended under American pressure, though details remain incompletely documented. These developments illustrate how security anxieties and economic strategy were linked, a connection that officials used to justify both industrial policy and the expansion of presidential power.

Economic assistance was changing in these years. American grant aid, which had been very large in the 1950s, declined sharply, and from the early 1960s a growing portion of American assistance took the form of loans, surplus agricultural commodities under Public Law 480, and support for trade and investment. By the early 1970s direct economic aid was largely over, and South Korea's economy depended on exports, on private and international credit, and on the funds it earned from military operations abroad. This shift was both a result of South Korean growth and a stimulus to it, since the end of easy aid heightened the government's concern with earning foreign exchange.

American attitudes toward Park's domestic politics were not simple. The United States pressed for a return to civilian rule in 1963 and expressed concern during the 1969 amendment and the 1971 election. Following the Yushin declaration of 1972, American officials expressed reservations, mostly in private, while avoiding public condemnation, as the administration's priorities were security and the China opening. American diplomatic intervention was important in saving Kim Dae-jung's life after his abduction from Tokyo in 1973 (75.19), and the Carter administration, which took office in 1977 with a human rights policy and a plan to withdraw ground troops (later suspended), made relations tense. The "Koreagate" scandal of the mid-1970s, involving allegations that South Korean agents had tried to influence members of the United States Congress, further strained them. It would therefore be mistaken to treat the alliance as unconditional American approval of Park's domestic authoritarianism. Washington tolerated it, sometimes criticized it, and occasionally acted to restrain its worst excesses, but its priorities were strategic, and its leverage was limited by the interests it shared with Seoul.

North Korea's actions, examined in Chapter 78, were an important part of this context. The seizure of the American intelligence ship USS Pueblo and the attempted raid on the presidential residence in January 1968 heightened security concerns, and the government responded by creating a Homeland Reserve Force, introducing a national identification system, and tightening control. Those events are treated in detail elsewhere. For Chapter 75 it is enough to note that a real military threat gave security arguments a force that the government drew upon, sometimes legitimately and sometimes as a pretext for political control.

Section 75.10

75.10 — Normalization with Japan, 1965: Capital, Diplomacy, and Colonial Memory

South Korea and Japan had no diplomatic relations after 1945, though they had been negotiating on and off since 1951. The talks stalled over the legacy of colonial rule, claims for property and compensation, the "Rhee Line" in the fishing waters, the status of Koreans resident in Japan, and the question of whether Japan would acknowledge that the colonization had been unjust. Both the United States, which wished to strengthen cooperation among its allies, and Park's government, which needed capital for development, pressed for a settlement. In November 1962 Kim Jong-pil and the Japanese foreign minister Ohira Masayoshi reached a secret understanding on the financial package, which was later formalized. Public knowledge of such an arrangement, and the sense that the government was making concessions on issues of national honor, brought a strong response.

The protests of 1964 were among the largest of the Park era. Students, opposition politicians, intellectuals, and religious leaders attacked the negotiations as "humiliating diplomacy," accused the government of selling out national interests for money, and demanded a formal apology for colonial rule. In June 1964, after weeks of demonstrations, the government declared martial law in Seoul and used troops to clear the streets. In August 1965, after the agreements were signed, the government forced them through the National Assembly in the absence of opposition members, and when students and professors protested, it imposed a garrison decree in Seoul, sending troops to occupy university campuses. The treatment of this opposition revealed the government's readiness to use military force against dissent, a precedent that would be repeated.

The settlement consisted of several agreements, signed in Tokyo on June 22, 1965, and ratified later that year: a Treaty on Basic Relations, which established diplomatic relations; an agreement on claims and economic cooperation; and agreements on fisheries, the legal status of Koreans in Japan, and the return of some cultural property. The Basic Relations Treaty declared that earlier treaties between the two countries, including those of the colonial period, were "already null and void," a formula that both governments interpreted differently, Seoul as meaning that they had been invalid from the start and Tokyo as meaning that they had become void when Korea became independent in 1948. The treaty also recognized the Republic of Korea as the only lawful government in Korea, as defined by a United Nations resolution of 1948, a clause that Japan understood narrowly and that had implications for its later relations with North Korea.

The economic package had three parts, and it is important to distinguish them. Japan agreed to provide three hundred million dollars in grants, in the form of Japanese goods and services, over ten years; two hundred million dollars in government loans, on concessional terms, over the same period; and more than three hundred million dollars in private commercial credits. The grants and government loans were provided under the claims and economic cooperation agreement. Under it, the two governments declared that the problem concerning property and claims of the two countries and their nationals was settled, in the words of the agreement, "completely and finally." The commercial credits were ordinary, though facilitated, loans that had to be repaid and did not depend on a claim of responsibility. The funds were substantial relative to South Korea's economy at the time, representing a significant portion of its annual imports and equal in magnitude to much of the aid it had received in a decade. They were used for projects including agriculture and fishing, railways, and, most famously, the Pohang steel mill, which was built with Japanese funds and technology (75.15).

Why did the settlement remain controversial? Critics in the 1960s argued that Japan had not apologized, that the money was termed "economic cooperation" rather than reparations, and that the Park government had given up claims that should have been pursued. Defenders replied that South Korea needed capital and had secured it at a time when other sources were unavailable, and that the claims were limited by the difficulties of proof and by Japan's own arguments about colonial-era benefits. In the decades that followed, the central issue was what the settlement covered. The government of South Korea itself paid limited compensation in the 1970s, mostly to the families of those who had died as conscripted workers or soldiers. In 2005 the South Korean government released its diplomatic documents on the negotiations, and a joint government–civilian committee concluded that the claims agreement had not settled certain issues, including the claims of survivors of Japanese military sexual slavery, Koreans left on Sakhalin, and victims of the atomic bombings. Later disputes, examined in Chapter 88, centered on whether individuals' claims against Japanese companies and the Japanese state had been extinguished by the 1965 agreements. For this chapter the key point is that the settlement resolved the relationship between governments in the terms that the two governments agreed on, and not every historical claim of individuals affected by colonial rule.

75.11 — South Korea and the Vietnam War: Military Deployment and Economic Consequences

South Korea sent more troops to South Vietnam than any other American ally. The first South Korean deployment was a 140-person medical and Taekwondo-instructor contingent dispatched on September 11, 1964. A separate noncombat force of approximately 2,000 engineering and logistical personnel followed in March 1965. In August 1965 the National Assembly, with opposition members absent, approved the dispatch of combat troops; from October 1965 the Capital Division and a marine brigade were deployed, followed in 1966 by the Ninth Division. Troop strength peaked at roughly fifty thousand, and over the course of the war, until the withdrawal of 1972–1973, more than three hundred thousand South Koreans served on rotation. Roughly five thousand were killed and many more wounded, according to commonly cited figures. The South Korean forces operated mostly in central coastal provinces of South Vietnam under their own command structure, in coordination with American forces.

Park's government gave several reasons for participation. It argued that defending South Vietnam against communism was a form of forward defense of South Korea, that the security of the two were linked, and that the alliance with the United States, to which South Korea owed its survival in the Korean War, required reciprocity. The American government and President Johnson, who wished for allied participation in order to demonstrate that the war was not a purely American effort, requested troops and offered inducements. Opposition politicians, students, and some religious leaders questioned the deployment, though the issue was less central in domestic politics than the Japan treaty.

The terms of participation were set out in negotiations that culminated in the Brown Memorandum, a memorandum dated March 4, 1966 and formally delivered on March 7 by the American ambassador, Winthrop G. Brown, to the South Korean government. The memorandum listed American commitments including financing of the deployments and the allowances for soldiers, equipment and training for South Korean forces, the modernization of the South Korean military, procurement of supplies and services in South Korea for the war effort, opportunities for South Korean firms in Vietnam, and assistance for economic development. (See the Source Window below.) The economic arrangements generated earnings of several kinds: soldiers' pay and allowances, much of which was remitted home; American purchases of South Korean goods and services; and contracts for South Korean construction and transport companies in Vietnam, which gave some firms, including some that would become large conglomerates, experience and capital. Estimates of the total vary and depend on what is counted, and scholars debate how large a share of the growth of these years can be attributed to Vietnam, but most agree that the earnings were significant, especially for foreign exchange in the mid-1960s, and that the war also brought South Korea concrete security and military benefits.

Section 75.12

75.12 — Industrial Corridors, Ports, Power, and the Geography of Modernization

The new industrial economy created a distinctive geography. Early export manufacturing clustered where labor, ports, and markets could be reached most easily: around Seoul and its port of Incheon, where the Guro industrial estate and the factories of Yeongdeungpo and Bupyeong employed tens of thousands of workers in textiles, garments, and electronics; and around Busan, South Korea's principal port, where footwear, plywood, textiles, and later other industries thrived. In both regions a large pool of migrants from the countryside could be recruited, and transportation to the sea was short. Daegu, the center of the textile trade, was a third node, and the Masan Free Export Zone, created in 1970 on a southern port, attracted foreign-invested electronics and textile plants.

The industrial complexes of the 1960s and 1970s were located along the southeastern coast. Ulsan, where an industrial complex was begun in 1962, grew from a small fishing town into a center of oil refining, fertilizer, petrochemicals, and later automobiles and shipbuilding. Pohang, 80 kilometers or so up the coast, became the site of the steel mill built from 1970. Gumi, in North Gyeongsang Province, was designated an electronics complex in the late 1960s and became a center of that industry. Changwon, in the south, was planned from 1974 as a machinery complex. These choices reflected a combination of considerations: deepwater ports for imports of raw materials and exports of finished products; industrial land that could be acquired at low cost from farmers and fishing communities, sometimes with disputes over compensation; the availability of water and energy; the desire to keep strategic industries far from the demilitarized zone and the vulnerable northwest; and political considerations, as the region included Park's home province and its leading political supporters.

Infrastructure bound these places together. The Gyeongbu Expressway and the railway between Seoul and Busan formed a trunk line along which the country's industrial population was concentrated; ports at Busan, Ulsan, Pohang, and Incheon handled imports of crude oil, coal, iron ore, and machinery and exports of manufactured goods; and the electric grid expanded to supply the new factories, though shortages and blackouts continued. The relative cost of transportation, and the access to export outlets, gave established centers advantages that new investment tended to reinforce. The government attempted to encourage dispersal in some cases, for example by promoting industrial estates in smaller cities, but the dominant pattern was concentration.

Concentration brought opportunities and inequalities. Regions along the main corridor gained jobs, public investment, schools, and hospitals, and became the site of rising incomes. Other regions, notably the southwest (Jeolla provinces) and parts of the northeast, received less industrial investment, and their rural populations tended to migrate to the cities. The extent to which this regional imbalance reflected geography and economic logic or deliberate political favoritism toward the southeast is debated; both factors operated, and the resulting regional grievance, particularly in the southwest, became a long-term feature of South Korean politics. Migration from villages to cities transformed the demography of the country. Seoul's population, roughly 2.4 million in 1960, passed five million by 1970 and continued to grow rapidly, producing shortages of housing, informal settlements on the hillsides and riverbanks, and pressure on urban services, a story told more fully in Chapter 81. In the countryside, the departure of young people left aging villages, and agricultural employment, which accounted for nearly two-thirds of workers in the early 1960s, had fallen to roughly a third by 1980. The geography of modernization thus altered the lives of those who stayed as well as those who moved.

This chapter does not narrate the corporate histories of the firms that built the factories and shipyards. That subject belongs to Chapter 76. It is sufficient here to note that the pattern of location was a product of state decisions about industrial estates, ports, and infrastructure, as well as of the choices of firms and workers.

Division IV — Rural Transformation, Labor, and the Industrial Turn of the 1970s · Section 75.13

75.13 — The Saemaul Undong and the Transformation of Rural Korea

By the end of the 1960s the government faced a rural problem. Industrial and urban incomes were rising faster than farm incomes, young people were leaving the villages, and the Park government, whose electoral base had historically included the countryside, was concerned about the political and economic consequences of a growing gap. In April 1970, at a meeting with provincial governors, Park proposed a campaign to improve village life through self-help, which became the Saemaul Undong, or New Village Movement. It drew on earlier community development programs, on the model of village cooperation and mutual aid that had long existed in rural Korea, and on the government's wish to mobilize rural labor in the service of its goals.

The program began with a simple experiment. In the winter of 1970–1971 the government distributed free cement, about 335 bags each, to roughly 33,000 villages and encouraged them to use it for projects of their own choosing, such as widening village roads, building bridges and small dams, constructing wells, and repairing embankments. When the government's officials found that some villages used the materials effectively while others did not, it adopted a policy of selective support, classifying villages into categories such as basic, self-helping, and self-sufficient, and giving additional cement, steel bars, and other support to those that performed well. This approach rewarded villages that demonstrated initiative and community cooperation and turned improvement into competition among villages, with public recognition and prizes for the leaders and communities that did best.

Over the following years, the movement's scope widened. Projects included replacing thatched roofs with tile or slate, which became one of its most visible achievements; building farm roads; extending electricity to villages, which were mostly connected to the grid by the end of the 1970s; improving water supply and irrigation; constructing village halls and cooperative facilities; promoting new crops and high-yield rice varieties, including the Tongil rice developed by agricultural researchers in the early 1970s; and establishing cooperative enterprises and savings associations. Training institutes, such as one near Suwon, taught village leaders methods of organizing, and later brought business executives, officials, and even foreigners to learn from the movement. The government helped finance these activities through subsidies, low-interest loans, and the supply of materials, and supported farm incomes by purchasing rice at prices above market levels, a policy that carried substantial costs for the budget.

The role of village leaders and of women deserves attention. Each village selected a male and a female leader, and women's organizations, which became the Saemaul Women's Associations, led savings campaigns, improvements in kitchens and sanitation, and campaigns against waste and drinking. These activities gave many rural women new public roles and organizational experience, though the movement was usually led by men, and female leaders often worked within a framework set by official priorities. Participation was also uneven across regions and villages: those with strong leadership, accessible markets, and pre-existing cooperation prospered, while remote or poorer villages benefited less, and results varied among the provinces.

Assessment of the movement requires distinguishing its material results from its political uses. Official statistics showed that average rural household income rose sharply in the early 1970s and, by the mid-1970s, matched or exceeded urban household income, a result that the government publicized. These figures have been questioned by scholars who point to differences in the way incomes were defined and measured, to the role of higher rice prices, to the exodus of rural population, which raised the average incomes of those who remained, and to the movement of farm households into non-farm employment. Even so, the changes in village infrastructure were real and visible, and many rural people remember them with appreciation. At the same time, the movement became increasingly a tool of political mobilization. After the Yushin declaration of 1972 it was explicitly linked to national ideology, with a heavy use of slogans, ceremonies, and administrative pressure; participation was sometimes compulsory, and local officials were under pressure to produce results; the removal of traditional thatched roofs and other practices disregarded local preferences; and some villages incurred debts. Rural people were not passive recipients. They made choices, some enthusiastically embracing the program and others resisting or adapting it, and the movement's results depended on their labor and decisions. It is best understood as a combination of genuine rural investment, community effort, and state supervision, with each element entangled with the others.

Section 75.14

75.14 — Industrial Workers, Women, Wages, and the Human Cost of Export Growth

Export manufacturing in the 1960s and 1970s was labor-intensive, and it depended on a large supply of cheap labor. The workforce was drawn from rural families who sent sons and especially daughters to cities to work in textile mills, garment workshops, electronics assembly plants, wig factories, and other light industries. Young women, many in their teens and early twenties and many with only primary or middle-school education, made up a large share of the manufacturing workforce in these industries, often more than half of it, and a substantial share of the industrial workforce as a whole. They were sometimes called "factory girls," a term that conveyed both their prominence and the low esteem in which they were held.

Conditions were often harsh. Working days of twelve hours or more, with a six- or seven-day week, were common, and the weekly hours of Korean manufacturing workers were among the longest in the world. Wages were low relative to productivity and, in many cases, below subsistence for a family. Many workers lived in company dormitories, where they shared rooms, ate cheap meals, and were subject to the discipline of supervisors; others lived in cramped rented rooms in the new industrial districts. Factories were crowded and poorly ventilated, and the cotton dust, chemicals, and machinery caused respiratory diseases, injuries, and accidents. The garment workshops of the Pyeonghwa Market in Seoul, where young "assistants" worked in low-ceilinged attics, were notorious. Supervisors, who were often men, exercised close control, and sexual harassment and arbitrary dismissal were frequent complaints. Although a Labor Standards Act, passed in 1953, set limits on hours and conditions in principle, it was rarely enforced.

Many families depended on these workers. Daughters' remittances paid for brothers' schooling and supported parents, and the willingness of families to send them reflected both necessity and the expectation that daughters contribute to the household. This contribution was an essential subsidy to the export economy, since it allowed employers to pay wages that did not cover the full cost of raising and supporting a family. Women also contested their treatment. Some used their savings to pursue evening schooling, some joined church groups and informal networks that supported workers, and some, as discussed below, organized unions. The workers' experiences were not uniform: skilled men in heavy industry, construction laborers, and women in small workshops all faced different circumstances, and wages rose over time as industry expanded.

The legal framework restricted independent organization. Labor unions were legal, but the dominant national federation was closely aligned with the government, and workers who tried to form independent unions faced dismissal, blacklisting, and the intervention of police and the KCIA. A law of 1971 on special measures for national security limited the right to collective action in the name of security, and the Yushin system tightened controls further. Strikes were rare and, when they occurred, usually suppressed. Under these conditions, the pressure that did build up found its outlet in individual protests and in the support of religious organizations, notably the Catholic Young Christian Workers and Protestant urban-industrial missions, which provided meeting places, education, and advocacy.

The event that brought these conditions into public view was the death of Jeon Tae-il. A young garment worker in the Pyeonghwa Market, he had studied the Labor Standards Act, tried to persuade employers and labor officials to obey it, and organized a group of fellow workers, after he found that petitions to authorities achieved nothing. On November 13, 1970, during a demonstration, he set himself on fire, calling, according to witnesses, for observance of the labor law and for an end to the treatment of workers as machines, and he died that night at the age of twenty-two. His death shocked students, intellectuals, clergy, and the press, many of whom had been little aware of the conditions of workers. It led to the formation of a union among the Pyeonghwa Market workers, led in part by his mother, Lee So-sun, who became a prominent labor activist, and it inspired a generation of students and intellectuals who moved into factories or supported workers' struggles. Jeon Tae-il became a lasting symbol of the human cost of industrial growth. The broader transformation of urban life, including housing, education, and consumer culture, is reserved for Chapter 81.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Households in the Developmental Era

The experience of the 1960s and 1970s differed sharply among households, and aggregate figures hide this variety. Consider three composite situations, assembled from patterns that historians and oral-history projects have documented rather than from any single named family.

A rural family. In a village in the southeast in the early 1960s, a family farms a small plot acquired in the land reform of the late 1940s. The household's food comes mostly from its own land, its cash needs depend on the sale of rice and a few other crops, and it has no electricity or paved road. A daughter leaves at sixteen for a textile mill in Daegu and sends part of her pay home. In the 1970s, the village's participation in the Saemaul Undong brings a widened road, a tile roof, and electric lighting, and the household benefits from higher rice purchase prices, but a son moves to Seoul for work, and the parents' labor on the farm increases as the village ages.

A factory worker's household. A young woman who works in an electronics or garment factory in Seoul lives in a dormitory or in a rented room shared with other workers. She works long hours, sends money home, and may attend a night school or a church group on her scarce free time. Her wages rise slowly, and she expects to leave the factory upon marriage, though many continued working. The household economy of her family, and her brothers' education, depends on her earnings, and she has little ability to bargain with her employer.

An emerging urban household. A civil servant, a schoolteacher, or an employee of one of the large companies, with a secondary or university education, lives in a rented house or one of the new apartment complexes of the 1970s. The household's income grows with the economy, though inflation erodes it in some years, and the children's education becomes a central family project. Such households were the basis of an emerging middle class, whose expectations would shape later political demands.

Because most surviving documents were produced by governments, firms, and the educated, historians recover the experiences of workers and farmers through labor records, newspapers, memoirs, oral history, and later investigations, reading them with attention to who recorded them and why.

Section 75.15

75.15 — The Heavy and Chemical Industry Drive, 1973–1979

In January 1973, in his New Year policy statement, Park declared a new stage of industrial policy. The government would give priority to the development of heavy and chemical industries, specifically steel, nonferrous metals, machinery, shipbuilding, electronics, and petrochemicals, and it announced the goal of reaching exports of ten billion dollars and income per head of one thousand dollars by 1981. The Heavy and Chemical Industry (HCI) drive was announced three months after the October Yushin, and its timing has led some observers to link it to the political change. The relationship is not simple, since planning for these industries had begun earlier, but both reflected a sense of national urgency.

Several motives overlapped. National security was central: a domestic capacity to produce steel, machinery, ships, and weapons would reduce dependence on American arms at a time when the United States was drawing down its commitments (75.9) and North Korea was ahead in some heavy industries. Economic motives were also important. Planners expected that moving into higher-value-added industries would diversify exports before the competitive advantage of cheap labor in textiles faded, would reduce dependence on imported intermediate goods, and would raise productivity. Technological ambition and national pride played a role, as did the belief, associated with Park's closest economic advisers, that the country needed to climb the industrial ladder while conditions allowed. Critics and some of the government's own economists argued, however, that the strategy was premature, since the economy lacked the scale, technology, and skills for such industries, and that it risked overinvestment and a diversion of resources from more competitive industries.

The government implemented the strategy through concentrated, and costly, instruments. Industrial estates were built or expanded at Changwon (machinery), Ulsan and Yeosu (petrochemicals), Onsan (nonferrous metals), Gumi (electronics), and Pohang (steel). A National Investment Fund, established in 1973–1974 from contributions by banks, insurance companies, and pension funds, channeled savings to favored sectors at low interest rates. Banks were directed to lend to HCI firms, tax concessions and tariff protection were granted, and the government guaranteed foreign loans for imports of technology and machinery. Technical colleges, vocational schools, and research institutes were expanded to train engineers and skilled workers. Businesses selected for HCI projects were expected to commit large investments, and because their undertakings were so big and the government's support so important, the policy reinforced the dominance of a small number of large firms, a result examined in Chapter 76.

Specific projects illustrate implementation. The Pohang Iron and Steel Company (POSCO), a state-owned enterprise established in 1968 under the leadership of Park Tae-joon, after the World Bank and other lenders declined to finance an integrated steel mill, built its first mill with funds from the 1965 Japanese agreement and technology from Japanese steelmakers. Construction began in 1970 and the first blast furnace began production in 1973; subsequent expansions made POSCO one of the most efficient steel producers in the world. At Ulsan, a shipyard was built, beginning in the early 1970s, by a private firm that had never built a ship, with foreign technical and financial support, and delivered its first large tankers in the mid-1970s; other shipyards followed. Machinery, petrochemical, and electronics complexes were founded at the other estates, along with a growing defense industry. Not all projects succeeded. Some firms and plants were undercapacity, and other projects were completed later than planned.

The first oil crisis of 1973–1974 raised the cost of imported energy and slowed world demand, and the government's response was to continue the HCI program while relying on overseas construction in the Middle East, where South Korean firms and workers earned large sums, to finance imports. The foreign debt rose rapidly. Domestic inflation was high and, because credit was allocated at rates below the rate of inflation, it created large implicit subsidies to borrowers. By the end of the decade, critics, including some within government, argued that capacity was excessive in some industries, that resources had been diverted from light industry and agriculture, and that the dominance of large firms had grown too great, issues that would reach a crisis in 1979–1980 (75.20). Supporters point out that many of the industries established in the 1970s, including steel and shipbuilding, became internationally competitive, and that the technical and organizational capacities built in these years underlay later achievements. Economists continue to argue about whether the HCI drive accelerated or distorted growth, as the Historical Debate describes. Specific analyses include Joseph J. Stern, Jihong Kim, Dwight H. Perkins, and Jung-Ho Yoo, Industrialization and the State: The Korean Heavy and Chemical Industry Drive (Korea Development Institute, 1995), and Jihong Kim, Korean Industrial Policy in the 1970s: The Heavy and Chemical Industry Drive (KDI Working Paper 9015, 1990). These studies help students distinguish the creation of durable capacity from the question of whether the instruments and timing were economically efficient. The history of the participating firms belongs to Chapter 76.

Section 75.16

75.16 — Growth, Inflation, Inequality, and the Uneven Benefits of Development

The aggregate record of the Park years is remarkable. According to commonly cited figures, which have been revised by the Bank of Korea and differ among sources, real output grew by roughly eight to ten percent per year over most of the 1960s and 1970s, with slower years in 1971–1972, 1974–1975, and 1979. Annual exports rose from tens of millions of dollars in the early 1960s to roughly one billion in 1971 and ten billion in 1977, a target reached ahead of schedule. Income per head, measured in current dollars, rose from less than one hundred dollars in the early 1960s to roughly one thousand dollars in 1977 and over one and a half thousand by 1979. The share of manufacturing in output rose substantially, and the economy's structure shifted from agriculture and light industry toward heavy industry and services. Electricity reached nearly all households, school enrollment expanded at every level, and life expectancy rose. Students should observe that these figures are partly nominal and partly real, that they are measured in won or in dollars, that annual and cumulative growth differ, and that exports and national income are different measures.

The record also includes costs and vulnerabilities. Inflation was a persistent problem: consumer prices rose by more than twenty percent in 1974 and 1975 following the oil crisis, and again in 1979–1980, and by smaller but significant amounts in other years, eroding the wages of workers on fixed incomes. External debt grew rapidly: an International Monetary Fund historical series reports $20.41 billion in total gross external debt at the end of 1979, including short-term liabilities, whereas estimates using different institutional coverage or debt classifications can be higher. This figure is gross debt, not net debt or annual debt-service payments. The economy was vulnerable to changes in the price of oil and the willingness of foreign banks to lend. The oil crises of 1973–1974 and 1979 revealed this exposure, and the second one, combined with overinvestment in heavy industry and a poor harvest, contributed to the contraction of 1980. The dependence on exports to a small number of markets, particularly the United States and Japan, was another source of fragility.

The distribution of benefits was uneven. Wages rose with industrial employment, and the majority of the population saw an improvement in living standards, but the gains were not shared equally. Workers' wages lagged behind productivity, and the working class bore many of the costs described in 75.14. Business owners, especially the owners of large firms with access to preferential credit, accumulated wealth rapidly. Farmers' incomes improved, though the Saemaul figures that showed parity with urban incomes are disputed and rural dwellers faced aging and depopulation. Regional differences persisted, with the southeast benefiting more than the southwest, and urban housing shortages affected the poor, including those removed from squatter areas in Seoul, such as the residents resettled to the Gwangju Daedanji area in Gyeonggi Province, where a riot of urban poor broke out in August 1971. Measures of overall income inequality in this period are less certain than those of output, and scholars differ in their reading of them; it is generally agreed that inequality of household income in South Korea was relatively low in international comparison in the early stages of industrialization, helped by the land reform and the expansion of education, and that disparities in wealth, particularly in land and real estate, widened in the 1970s.

For these reasons, growth rates alone cannot provide a complete measure of development. They do not record the length of working hours, the safety of workplaces, the freedom to organize, the quality of political rights, or the costs borne by particular groups. They also tell little about the sustainability of the strategy, which depended on state-allocated credit and external borrowing. Historians generally agree that the transformation was real and large, and that its costs were substantial and unevenly borne. They disagree about how to weigh these, about whether the costs were necessary conditions of the growth, and about what alternatives were available, questions taken up in the Historical Debate.

Historical Vocabulary
Saemaul Undong, Yushin, and Emergency Measures

Definition. The Saemaul Undong ("New Village Movement") was the state-guided rural development and mobilization program begun in 1970. Yushin ("revitalizing reform"), the name given to the system established in October 1972, uses a word with the same characters as the Japanese Meiji "restoration" (Ishin), a connotation that was not lost on contemporaries. Emergency measures were presidential decrees issued under the 1972 constitution's emergency powers, which could suspend constitutional freedoms and were not subject to judicial review. A garrison decree placed troops in a city or on campuses.

Historical context. The government presented Yushin as a "Korean-style democracy" suited to a nation facing security threats and needing rapid development. Opponents called it a dictatorship.

Why it matters. These terms identify the institutions and instruments by which rural mobilization and presidential authority were organized.

Potential misunderstanding. The word "emergency" suggests a short-lived response to crisis; in the Yushin period emergency measures became a normal form of government, and they were repeatedly issued over a period of years.

Division V — Authoritarian Consolidation, Yushin, and the Limits of the Developmental State · Section 75.17

75.17 — The Third-Term Amendment, the 1971 Election, and the Breakdown of Constitutional Restraint

The constitution of the Third Republic limited the president to two consecutive terms. Park was re-elected in 1967 against Yun Bo-seon, in a campaign marked by accusations of official intervention, and the National Assembly elections of that year, in which the governing party won a large majority, were disputed by the opposition as fraudulent. In 1969, as his second term entered its last years, Park and his party moved to amend the constitution to allow a president to serve three consecutive terms. Opposition within the governing party itself, and student protests, were suppressed, and the proposal was passed by the governing party in September 1969 in an early-morning session held in an annex building to avoid opposition members. A national referendum in October 1969 approved the amendment by a majority, amid opposition claims that the vote had been improperly influenced. The episode demonstrated the readiness of the governing party to change the constitution to extend its leader's tenure.

The presidential election of April 27, 1971 was the closest that the Park regime would face. The opposition New Democratic Party nominated Kim Dae-jung, a politician in his mid-forties from the southwestern Jeolla region, who campaigned on a platform that combined criticism of authoritarian practices, proposals for expanded social welfare and fairer distribution, calls for a more conciliatory policy toward North Korea, and a four-power guarantee of peninsular security. Large crowds attended his rallies, notably in Seoul. Kim warned that if Park won he would establish a permanent dictatorship; Park, for his part, was reported to have appealed to voters in his campaign as if it were the last time he would ask for their votes. The government used its advantages in resources, administrative influence, and control of the media, and it appealed to regional loyalties and security concerns. Park won with about 53 percent of the vote to Kim's roughly 45 percent, a margin of roughly one million votes. The opposition and independent observers alleged vote-buying, official pressure, and manipulation of the electoral rolls, and the extent of the manipulation remains debated, though scholars generally agree that the contest was held on unequal terms and that Kim's strong showing, which included large majorities in his native southwest and in Seoul, revealed the extent of opposition sentiment and also the regional divide in voting.

Parliamentary elections the following month confirmed the opposition's strength. The New Democratic Party won close to ninety seats in the National Assembly, a larger number than it had held before, and the governing party lost the two-thirds majority that it would have needed to amend the constitution again without opposition support. For Park and the governing party, the combination of a narrow win and the opposition's gains was a warning. The government faced other challenges in 1971: student demonstrations against corruption and military training on campuses, which led to the imposition of a garrison decree in Seoul in October; a riot of the urban poor in Gwangju Daedanji in August; a strike by physicians and doctors and other labor unrest; and, from outside, the shock of the American announcement of troop reductions and the Nixon administration's moves toward China.

In December 1971 Park declared a state of national emergency, citing the threat from North Korea and the changing international situation, and soon after the governing party passed, in a hurried session, a Special Measures Law for National Security that gave the president broad powers to control the economy, prices, labor, and speech in an emergency, and restricted the right of collective bargaining and strikes. The declaration was a crucial step: it established the habit of justifying domestic restrictions by reference to external threats, and it gave legal form to powers that would be drawn on after 1972. Its critics argued that the security situation did not justify such measures, and that they were really intended to strengthen the president's hand against domestic opposition. Government officials argued that the threat was real, as the international events of that year had altered the strategic environment.

The international setting also included the first steps toward contact between the two Korean governments. In 1971 the Red Cross organizations of North and South began talks on divided families, and in the spring of 1972 the KCIA director, Lee Hu-rak, secretly visited Pyongyang, and a North Korean official visited Seoul. On July 4, 1972, the two governments issued a Joint Statement announcing agreement on principles of unification: independence without external interference, peaceful means, and a "great national unity" transcending differences in ideology and system. The statement was received with hope by many Koreans, but both governments soon used the new situation to justify tighter domestic control, as shown in 75.18. The detailed story of inter-Korean confrontation and diplomacy belongs to Chapter 78.

Section 75.18

75.18 — The October Yushin, the Constitution of 1972, and Presidential Dictatorship

On the evening of October 17, 1972, Park announced a "special declaration." Martial law was imposed across the country; the National Assembly was dissolved; the activities of political parties were suspended; universities were closed and the press was placed under military censorship; and the provisions of the constitution that conflicted with the declaration were suspended. Tanks and troops took up positions in Seoul. Park justified the measures as necessary to make use of the opportunity presented by the thaw in relations with North Korea, to respond to changes in the international order, and to rebuild national unity and efficiency. He announced that a new constitution would be drafted, to be approved by referendum. The constitutional change was not the result of a legislative process or open debate. It was drafted by a small group of officials and scholars working under the direction of the presidential staff and the KCIA, and was published at the end of October.

The referendum of November 21, 1972, conducted under martial law, with open discussion limited and the opposition unable to campaign freely, produced an official approval of more than nine-tenths of the votes cast on a very high turnout. The new constitution, usually called the Yushin Constitution, took effect in December. Its principal features concentrated authority in the presidency in several ways. First, the president was to be elected not by direct popular vote but by the National Conference for Unification, a body of about two thousand delegates chosen in local elections in which political party affiliation was effectively prohibited and in which government-aligned candidates dominated. Second, the presidential term was extended to six years, and there was no effective limit on the number of terms that a president could serve. Third, the president was empowered to nominate one third of the members of the National Assembly, who were then confirmed en bloc by the National Conference, giving the president a bloc of legislators on whom he could rely; the remaining two thirds were elected from two-member districts that tended to return one governing-party and one opposition member. Fourth, the president could issue emergency measures, suspending constitutional freedoms and overriding laws, and these were not subject to review by the courts. Fifth, the president could dissolve the National Assembly, appoint judges, and initiate constitutional amendments subject to a referendum, and the Assembly's powers were reduced through shortened sessions and the elimination of its power to conduct audits of state affairs. A Constitutional Committee replaced the earlier Constitutional Court, and its practical influence was slight.

The election of the president on December 23, 1972 showed how the system worked. Of the roughly 2,359 delegates of the National Conference who voted, all but two ballots, which were declared invalid, were cast for Park, who was the only candidate. He was reelected in the same way in 1978, again by an almost unanimous vote. The Conference for Unification was thus a mechanism for confirming a decision that had already been made, with a procedure designed to give it the appearance of a representative process. The delegates were also, in effect, subordinates of the government, and the electoral laws and the supervision of the KCIA and the police made independent candidacy unlikely.

The regime justified the system in several ways. It argued that national security required unity and a strong executive in the face of a North Korean threat and the uncertainty of American support; that rapid economic development, then entering its heavy industrial stage, required political stability and continuity of policy that Western-style partisan competition would disrupt; and that South Korea needed a "Korean-style democracy" adapted to its conditions. Its opponents answered that security and development did not require the abolition of elections and the rule of law, that the system was designed to preserve Park's personal power, and that the thaw with the North, so recently announced, was being used as a pretext. Historians offer varied accounts of Park's motives. Some emphasize the 1971 election and the opposition's strength as the immediate cause; some stress international developments and the president's sense of strategic isolation; some emphasize the logic of the HCI drive, which, they argue, required a more insulated state; and many find a combination of these. In each case the evidence indicates that the decision was a political choice made by Park and a small circle, not an inevitable result of economic development, a point that the experience of other rapidly growing societies with different political systems reinforces.

The consequences were profound. The Yushin Constitution eliminated the possibility of a peaceful transfer of power through competitive elections, and it removed the main institutional restraints on the president. It also made the regime dependent on repression, as the opposition, deprived of institutional outlets, moved to the streets, the churches, the universities, and the press, and the government responded with the emergency measures described in 75.19. Section 75.19

75.19 — Emergency Measures, Political Prisoners, and the Democracy Movement

Even after Yushin, opposition persisted, and the government responded with growing repression. The KCIA, directed by Lee Hu-rak until 1976 and subsequently by Kim Jae-gyu, was the central instrument, supported by the police, the military security command, and the prosecutors and courts. The agency's operations included surveillance of politicians, students, clergy, and journalists; arbitrary detention; interrogation that, according to later investigations by official commissions and by courts, frequently involved torture; pressure on newspapers through the presence of agents in newsrooms; and operations abroad. The government also made extensive use of emergency measures. In January 1974, Emergency Measures No. 1 and No. 2 prohibited the denial, criticism, or advocacy of revision of the constitution and created emergency courts to try offenders; later measures extended these controls, and Emergency Measure No. 9, issued in May 1975 and in force until December 1979, banned criticism of the constitution and of the emergency measures themselves, as well as student political activity and reporting of such criticism.

Several cases of the period have become emblematic. In August 1973 the leading opposition politician Kim Dae-jung was abducted from a Tokyo hotel by KCIA agents and brought to Seoul, where he reappeared in his home five days later after intervention by American and Japanese officials; the abduction created a diplomatic crisis between Seoul and Tokyo, and later investigations indicated that the KCIA had been responsible. In 1974 the government charged students from around the country in the case of the National Democratic Youth and Students League, using Emergency Measure No. 4 and military courts; more than two hundred people were indicted, and several leaders, including the poet Kim Chi-ha, received death sentences that were later commuted. In April 1975 the government charged members of an alleged organization, the People's Revolutionary Party Rebuilding Committee, with attempting to overthrow the state in collusion with North Korea. Eight men were executed within about a day of the Supreme Court's confirmation of their death sentences. In 2007, after the opening of records and a reinvestigation, a Seoul court retried the case and acquitted all of the defendants, finding that the confessions had been obtained by torture and that the evidence did not support the charges. The case is widely regarded as a judicial killing and as an instance of the way in which charges of communist subversion could be used against domestic opponents, though the government of the time maintained that the organization was real.

Resistance took many forms. Students organized demonstrations and underground reading groups, and their protests brought arrests, expulsions, and forced conscription into the army. Journalists protested censorship: in late 1974 reporters at the Dong-A Ilbo newspaper issued a declaration of freedom of the press, the government responded by pressuring advertisers to withdraw, and in a notable display of public solidarity, readers purchased small advertisements of support, until the newspaper, under pressure, dismissed many of its journalists in 1975. Religious organizations were crucial: Catholic and Protestant clergy and lay activists provided protection, meeting places, and moral authority, and on March 1, 1976, a group of prominent figures including former president Yun Bo-seon, Kim Dae-jung, and the Protestant leader Ham Seok-heon issued the Declaration for Democracy and National Salvation at Myeongdong Cathedral in Seoul, calling for the restoration of democracy and for an end to emergency rule. Lawyers defended political prisoners at risk to their careers, opposition politicians used the limited space of the Assembly, and workers' and women's organizations continued their struggles. A campaign launched in December 1973 to collect one million signatures for constitutional reform was crushed within weeks. The families of prisoners, particularly mothers, held prayer meetings and demonstrations demanding their release, and their persistence helped to keep the issue before the public.

Park's government sometimes used concessions alongside repression. It released many political prisoners in 1974–1975, only to jail others, tolerated the Assembly's debates within limits, and announced that the emergency measures were temporary, which the experience of later years belied. Dissent was successfully contained for long periods, but at a high cost of legitimacy. The opposition was divided among a politically moderate parliamentary wing, a more radical student movement, and church and labor groups, and the government was able to exploit those divisions. Nonetheless the opposition survived. It built organizations, networks, and a moral language of human rights and democracy that would be mobilized in the crises of 1979–1980 and in 1987. At the same time, it is important not to read this later outcome back into the 1970s. At the time, the future of the democracy movement was uncertain, many activists spent years in prison or in exile, and some died, and few could have predicted how the political system would end.

Section 75.20

75.20 — The Crisis of the Yushin State and the End of Park's Presidency, 1978–1979

By 1978 the Yushin system appeared stable, but its foundations were eroding. Park was reelected by the National Conference for Unification in July 1978 by an almost unanimous vote. Yet in the National Assembly elections of December 1978, in which the government retained the advantage of its appointed one third of seats, the opposition New Democratic Party won a slightly larger share of the popular vote than the governing Democratic Republican Party, about 32.8 percent to 31.7 percent, although it obtained fewer elected seats. It was the first clear popular rebuke of the system in years and showed that a public that could express itself, even in a restricted election, did not endorse it.

The economic situation compounded the political one. The government's heavy industry program, undertaken at high cost, had produced overcapacity in some industries, and inflation reached high levels; a value-added tax introduced in 1977 and stabilization measures announced in April 1979 reflected attempts to cope with the imbalances. The second oil crisis, beginning in 1979, hit an economy that was already strained. Workers' grievances over low wages, long hours, and the denial of union rights continued to grow. Women workers at the Dongil Textile Company in Incheon were attacked in 1978 when they attempted to elect a democratic union leadership, and the incident, in which men threw excrement at women strikers, became a symbol of the repression of workers. Tensions within the ruling elite also increased, as officials disagreed about policy, particularly between hardliners, such as the head of the presidential security service, Cha Ji-chul, and others, including the director of the KCIA, Kim Jae-gyu, whose relationship with Park deteriorated.

The political crisis accelerated in 1979. In May Kim Young-sam, a veteran opposition politician from the Busan region, was elected president of the New Democratic Party on a platform of confrontation with the government. In August, hundreds of women workers from YH Trading, a wig manufacturer facing closure, occupied the party's headquarters in Seoul to protest the shutdown and the dismissal of workers. On August 11 police stormed the building, and one of the workers, Kim Gyeong-suk, died. Her death provoked an outcry and strengthened the connection between the labor and opposition movements. In September a court suspended Kim Young-sam's party leadership, and in an interview with an American newspaper he called on the United States to stop supporting the Park government. In response, the governing party and the appointed members of the Assembly voted on October 4, 1979 to expel him from the National Assembly. Opposition members then resigned en masse.

The expulsion of Kim Young-sam, whose political base was in Busan and the southeastern region that had been the heart of Park's own support, set off a wave of protest. On October 16, 1979, students at Pusan National University took to the streets, and they were joined by thousands of citizens in demonstrations in central Busan against the Yushin system, the economic situation, and the repression. The unrest, often referred to as the Bu-Ma protests (for Busan and Masan, where demonstrations spread a few days later), involved attacks on police stations and government buildings and was widely understood as a sign that popular discontent had reached even Park's own region. The government declared martial law in Busan on October 18 and imposed a garrison decree in Masan and Changwon on October 20, sending in paratroops. Hundreds were arrested.

The legitimacy of the system was therefore increasingly contested, and divisions inside the leadership about how to respond were deepening. Several elements converged: a political opposition revitalized by electoral success and new leadership, labor grievances that had gained public sympathy, the slowing of the economy, popular protest in the regime's heartland, and conflicts within the ruling circle over whether to meet protest with greater force or to seek compromise. Historians differ about how precarious the regime was in October 1979, with some arguing that it still had considerable capacity to suppress dissent and that the end came suddenly because of an event at the top, and others arguing that the structural strains made a crisis likely. The question remains open. What is clear is that the accomplishments of the developmental state, including the growth of an educated urban population, a more complex economy, and a generation of workers and middle-class citizens with their own expectations, helped to produce demands that the Yushin system could not accommodate.

On October 26, 1979, Park Chung-hee was assassinated by Kim Jae-gyu, the director of the KCIA, ending his presidency after eighteen years. The circumstances of the assassination, the political crisis that followed, the military struggle for power, the Gwangju Uprising, and the establishment of the Chun Doo-hwan government belong to Chapter 79, and are not narrated here.

Historical Debate

Who Made the Miracle? State Planning, Firms, Workers, and the World Economy

The question: How much of South Korea's economic transformation between 1961 and 1979 can be attributed to the government's planning and intervention, and what roles did private firms, workers, international conditions, and earlier developments play?

Interpretation A — A state-led transformation: Scholars of the "developmental state," among them Alice Amsden and Robert Wade, who drew on earlier work on Japan by Chalmers Johnson, argued that South Korea's government deliberately "governed the market" through control of credit, protection of infant industries, export targets, and discipline of firms, and that this intervention accounts for the speed of industrial upgrading. In this view the HCI drive, in particular, was a successful strategic use of state power.
Interpretation B — A market-oriented export strategy: Other economists, including those associated with a 1993 World Bank study of East Asian growth, emphasized the role of export orientation, macroeconomic stability, high savings, investment in education, and competitive markets, and argued that most state intervention was either neutral or harmful, with some exceptions. They tended to criticize the HCI drive as an expensive distortion and to regard the liberalizing reforms of the mid-1960s as the heart of the success.
Interpretation C — A broader set of conditions: Many historians stress additional factors: the land reform and educational expansion of the 1940s and 1950s; American aid and security guarantees; Japanese capital and technology after 1965; Vietnam-related earnings; the favorable world trade environment of the 1960s; the entrepreneurial and managerial capacities of Korean firms; and, above all, the labor of workers and farmers whose wages and working conditions made the export strategy profitable. These accounts also ask whether authoritarian rule was a necessary condition, an enabling one, or an obstacle in certain areas.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on economic statistics, which are subject to revision; government and corporate documents; comparisons with other countries; econometric studies of the effects of credit and protection on productivity; and case studies of industries and firms.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation more than the basic facts. Scholars differ about whether intervention was selective and disciplined or broad and costly, how to measure the effects of preferential credit, whether the 1960s and 1970s strategies should be judged together or separately, and what counterfactual to use, since no other South Korea existed to compare.

Why it matters: The debate bears on what other countries might learn from South Korea, and on how South Koreans remember Park, whether as the architect of prosperity or as a dictator whose achievements came at a heavy cost. It also affects how historians treat the connection between development and repression.

Multiple Perspectives

The Developmental Decade: Seven Ways of Seeing

State planners: Officials in the Economic Planning Board and finance ministries saw a country short of capital and foreign exchange, which had to be allocated carefully. They regarded planning, discipline, and export targets as rational responses to scarcity, and many believed that political stability was a precondition of the policies they favored.
Entrepreneurs: Business owners saw opportunities in access to credit, licenses, and foreign markets, and risks in dependence on official favor. Many welcomed the government's support and pressure, but some resented controls and the demand for political contributions.
Industrial workers: Male factory and construction workers saw the opportunity of steady wages and the hardship of long hours, accidents, and the lack of independent unions. Many hoped for upward mobility for their children and were wary of confrontation.
Women workers: Young women in textile, garment, and electronics plants experienced low pay, dormitory discipline, and harassment, while also gaining wages, independence from the village, and in some cases education and organizational experience. Their earnings supported families, and their protests, as at Dongil and YH, brought them into political conflict.
Rural families: Farm households experienced roads, electricity, and higher rice prices, and the departure of children to cities. Some welcomed the Saemaul Undong, others experienced it as official pressure.
Political opponents: Opposition politicians, students, clergy, and journalists saw a regime that had overthrown a legitimate government, manipulated elections and the constitution, and silenced critics, and they argued that development should not require the sacrifice of rights, though they differed over strategies and over economic policy.
International partners: American officials valued a stable anti-communist ally and a partner in Vietnam but worried about Park's methods; Japanese officials and businesses saw a market and an opportunity for regional leadership; international lenders weighed risk and return. None of them controlled South Korean decisions, though all affected them.
Continuity and Change Over Time

State, Economy, and Political Authority: The 1950s Compared with the 1970s

What Changed

  • The economy moved from dependence on American grant aid and agricultural commodities to dependence on exports, foreign borrowing, and private investment, with manufacturing replacing agriculture as the leading sector
  • The state acquired planning and budgeting capacity in the Economic Planning Board, control of the banking system, and a security agency with broad powers, so that it could direct credit and monitor society more effectively than in the 1950s
  • The workforce changed from largely rural to substantially urban and industrial, with large numbers of women in factories
  • Political authority moved from a civilian authoritarian presidency under Rhee, checked by an active legislature and vulnerable to mass protest, to a military-originated presidency that by 1972 had removed electoral and legislative constraints

What Stayed the Same

  • The United States remained the principal security guarantor, and external dependence shifted in form rather than disappeared
  • Anti-communism and the division of the peninsula remained the framework of politics and a justification for restrictions on dissent
  • Close ties between government and private business, including political funding and favoritism, continued, though they were organized more systematically
  • Much of the civil service and many of the administrative practices of earlier regimes remained in place, and the educated population and private firms that carried out the transformation had roots in the earlier period

When and how it changed: Change was uneven: institutional reorganization came quickly after 1961, the export turn in 1964–1965, the political transformation in 1969–1972, and the industrial turn in 1973. Workers, particularly young women, experienced change most directly, while landowners of the old order and many farmers experienced greater continuity in the early years. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the "miracle" was a reorganization of inherited capacities as well as a creation of new ones.

Cause and Effect

How Security, Finance, Infrastructure, Labor, and Exports Interacted

Long-term causes

Land reform and educational expansion, an inherited administrative apparatus, the division of the peninsula and rivalry with North Korea, a large army, a shortage of capital and foreign exchange, and the wartime destruction and postwar reliance on American aid.

Short-term causes

The political instability of the Second Republic, the officers' discontent, the failure of early import-substitution and currency reform, the reduction of grant aid, and the opportunity presented by expanding world trade.

Triggering context

The coup of 1961 and the foreign-exchange crisis of 1963–1964, which pushed the government toward exchange-rate reform and export promotion, followed by the 1965 Japan settlement and the Vietnam deployments, which brought capital and earnings.

Transformation and immediate effects

Control of banks and guarantees of foreign loans directed capital to exporters; infrastructure lowered transport costs; low-wage labor, especially of young women, made exports competitive; rising exports earned the foreign exchange to import machinery, which enabled further investment.

Long-term consequences

A new industrial economy, a large urban working and middle class, large conglomerates, regional inequality, high debt, and a political system whose methods the new society increasingly rejected.

Historical complexity

Each element depended on others and on external conditions, and none alone produced the outcome. Security concerns shaped industrial policy; industrial success strengthened the state's claim to legitimacy and the capacity to repress; and repression in turn shaped labor costs and political risk.

Why a single cause is insufficient: Explanations that point only to Park's leadership, to American aid, to Japanese capital, to cheap labor, or to authoritarian discipline all omit the others and the mechanisms by which they combined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that South Korea's industrial transformation between 1961 and 1979 was the product of deliberate state intervention, institutional planning, export incentives, international financing, private enterprise, and the labor of millions of people, and that it took place alongside the progressive concentration of presidential power and the restriction of civil liberties. The two developments were closely intertwined, but neither followed inevitably from the other, and the chapter has avoided both the claim that Park created prosperity single-handedly and the claim that development can be understood only through repression.

Park came to power through a military coup on May 16, 1961 that ended the brief parliamentary Second Republic. The officers who seized control justified their action by pointing to political instability and economic difficulty, claims that had some basis but were also retrospective rationalizations. The coup was a rupture in constitutional government. The new regime built institutions that would define the era: the Economic Planning Board, which combined planning and budgetary authority; tighter state control of banking; and the Korean Central Intelligence Agency, which monitored and intimidated opponents. After a return to formal civilian government in 1963, the president and his party retained substantial military, administrative, and intelligence resources.

The economic strategy emerged through trial, error, and adjustment. The first five-year plan emphasized basic industries and a degree of import substitution, but failures in 1962–1963, including the currency reform, a poor harvest, and a shortage of foreign exchange, pushed the government toward a policy of export promotion. A devaluation, a unified exchange rate, higher interest rates, tax and credit incentives, import-license linkages, and regular presidential meetings with exporters created a system that rewarded firms for selling abroad. Control of banks and guarantees of foreign loans gave the government the means to direct capital and to enforce performance, but also created risks of favoritism, corruption, concentration, and excessive debt.

Relations with the United States, Japan, and Vietnam supplied security, capital, and markets. The alliance with the United States persisted but was bargained over continuously, and the Nixon Doctrine and troop withdrawals of 1969–1971 intensified Seoul's anxiety and its drive for self-reliance. The normalization with Japan in 1965 brought a package of grants, loans, and credits, used in part to build the Pohang steel mill, but at the cost of major protests and of unresolved issues of colonial responsibility, which the agreement settled between governments but not, in the view of later critics, for every individual claimant. Participation in the Vietnam War brought American commitments, earnings, and experience, and also casualties among South Korean soldiers and the killing of Vietnamese civilians in documented incidents.

The social costs and distribution of the benefits formed a continuing theme. Rural families gained roads, electricity, and higher incomes through the Saemaul Undong, which was also a vehicle of political mobilization. Young women in textile, garment, and electronics factories, whose low wages and long hours underpinned export competitiveness, endured harsh conditions, and the death of Jeon Tae-il in 1970 revealed those conditions to the public. Industrial concentration in the Seoul–Incheon and southeastern corridors created opportunities and regional inequalities, and rapid migration changed the structure of cities and villages.

In January 1973 the government announced the Heavy and Chemical Industry drive, directing credit and technical resources to steel, shipbuilding, machinery, petrochemicals, electronics, and nonferrous metals, motivated by security, export diversification, and technological ambition. It established industries that later became internationally competitive, but also contributed to inflation, debt, overcapacity, and the dominance of large firms, strains that the oil crises exposed. Aggregate growth was extraordinary, but growth rates cannot measure working hours, safety, rights, or the distribution of gains.

Politically, the third-term amendment of 1969, the close election of 1971, and the declaration of national emergency in December 1971 prepared the way for the October 1972 Yushin. Under the new constitution the president was chosen by an indirectly elected conference, could serve without a term limit, nominated one third of the National Assembly, and could govern by emergency decree beyond the reach of the courts. The regime justified the change by security, development, and "Korean-style democracy," although the evidence suggests that it was a political choice rather than a requirement of economic growth.

Under Yushin the government used the KCIA, emergency measures, military courts, and censorship to suppress dissent. Cases such as the abduction of Kim Dae-jung, the student league trials, and the People's Revolutionary Party executions of 1975, later judged by a court to have rested on tortured confessions, illustrate the costs. Yet students, clergy, journalists, lawyers, workers, opposition politicians, and families of prisoners kept resistance alive, building networks and a language of democracy and human rights, though their later success was not predetermined.

In 1978–1979 electoral rebuke, labor protest at YH Trading, the expulsion of Kim Young-sam, popular uprisings in Busan and Masan, economic strain, and divisions within the ruling circle brought the system to crisis. Park was assassinated on October 26, 1979, ending his presidency. The chapter has reserved the assassination's circumstances and the crisis that followed for Chapter 79. Its broader conclusion is that the achievements of the developmental state, including a larger educated and urban population and a more complex economy, helped to generate the demands that its political system could not meet.

Producing at home goods previously imported, in order to save foreign exchange and build industry.
Export promotion
Policies including exchange-rate, tax, and credit incentives that reward firms for selling abroad.
Preferential credit
Bank loans at below-market interest or favorable terms granted to firms in designated sectors.
Payment guarantee
A government promise to repay a foreign loan taken by a private firm if it defaulted.
Curb market
The informal private credit market, where borrowers excluded from banks paid high interest rates.
August 3, 1972 measure
Emergency decree imposing a moratorium on curb-market debts and rescheduling them.
Treaty on Basic Relations (1965)
Agreement establishing diplomatic relations between Japan and the Republic of Korea.
Claims and economic cooperation agreement
The 1965 accord providing Japanese grants and loans and declaring claims between the states settled.
Brown Memorandum
The March 4, 1966 American statement of commitments connected with additional South Korean troops in Vietnam.
Nixon Doctrine
The 1969 American policy that Asian allies should bear primary responsibility for their own ground defense.
Gyeongbu Expressway
The roughly 428-kilometer highway between Seoul and Busan, completed in July 1970.
Saemaul Undong
The New Village Movement, a state-guided rural development and mobilization program begun in 1970.
Labor Standards Act
The 1953 law setting limits on working hours and conditions, rarely enforced in the export boom.
Heavy and Chemical Industry drive
The policy launched in 1973 to promote steel, machinery, shipbuilding, petrochemicals, electronics, and nonferrous metals.
National Investment Fund
A pool of bank, insurance, and pension savings channeled to priority industries in the 1970s.
Yushin
The "revitalizing reform" system established in October 1972, with an indirectly elected president and emergency powers.
National Conference for Unification
The indirectly elected body that chose the president and approved a portion of the National Assembly under Yushin.
Emergency measures
Presidential decrees that could suspend constitutional freedoms and could not be reviewed by courts.
Garrison decree
An order stationing troops in a city or on campuses to suppress protest.
Bu-Ma protests
The October 1979 demonstrations in Busan and Masan against the Yushin regime.
Real and nominal values
Real values are adjusted for price changes; nominal values are not, and the distinction matters when reading economic statistics.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 75 matters because it explains how a poor, aid-dependent society became an industrial one within a generation, and because it shows that this change was bound up with decisions about who held power and who bore costs. The developmental state was neither a neutral technocracy nor merely a dictatorship with economic side effects. It was a set of institutions, policies, alliances, and daily labor that produced both a new economy and a new political problem.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the Cold War world: how security guarantees, regional capital flows, and a war in Southeast Asia could be converted into industrial opportunity; how a postcolonial state negotiated with its former colonizer; and how the memory of those negotiations, and of the Vietnam War, would continue to provoke argument. The experiences of young women workers, farmers, and migrants remind us that national statistics conceal the people who supplied the labor on which growth depended.

The developments of these years shaped much of what followed. The heavy industries established in the 1970s and the firms that built them set the stage for Chapter 76, and the strains of the HCI drive foreshadow the economic restructuring of Chapter 84. The labor movement, the churches, the students, and the opposition politicians who endured Yushin prepared the ground for the events of Chapters 79 and 82. The urban and educational changes introduced here are developed in Chapter 81, and the disputes over the 1965 settlement return in Chapter 88.

Park's government left a double inheritance: a productive economy and a tradition of presidential authority that its society would spend decades contesting. How to hold those two facts together, without erasing either, is the task this chapter has asked of its readers, and it will remain a question for the chapters that follow.
