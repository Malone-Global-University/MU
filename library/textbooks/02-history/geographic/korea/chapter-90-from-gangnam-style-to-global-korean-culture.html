From Gangnam Style to Global Korean Culture

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On December 21, 2012, a music video by a Korean entertainer in his mid-thirties, one that mocked the conspicuous consumption of a single Seoul neighborhood, became the first video in YouTube's history to reach one billion views. The milestone was real and historically significant. It was also the visible tip of an industry, an audience, and a set of institutions that had been under construction in South Korea for more than two decades.

The video was Gangnam Style, released by Psy on July 15, 2012, and YouTube announced the one-billion-view milestone in December of that year. Within five months a comic dance, a satirical premise, and a chorus most viewers could not translate had been imitated in schoolyards, parodied on television programs, and performed by celebrities in countries where almost no one had previously encountered Korean popular music. For many people outside East Asia, this was their first awareness that South Korea produced popular culture at all.

Historians have to resist the story that this moment suggests. Gangnam Style did not begin the Korean Wave. Chapter 89 traced the Korean Wave, or Hallyu, from the television dramas and cinema of the late 1990s and the idol-training system of SM, YG, and JYP, through the first idol groups, to a regional audience across East and Southeast Asia that was already large by 2011. What the video demonstrated was that Korean-language popular culture could now travel through global digital platforms to audiences far outside that region, and that a viral event could briefly focus the world's attention on an industry already in place.

This chapter asks what happened next. Between 2012 and November 2026, South Korean music, film, television, comics, literature, cosmetics, food, and language instruction reached audiences in North America, Europe, Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, and elsewhere, in markets that had no earlier tradition of consuming Korean-language entertainment. A boy band, BTS, debuted in 2013 from a small company and became a participant in the world's largest music markets. A girl group, BLACKPINK, played Coachella in 2019 and headlined it in 2023. A film, Parasite, won the Palme d'Or in 2019 and the Academy Award for Best Picture in 2020. A television series, Squid Game, became a global streaming phenomenon in 2021. In 2024, the novelist Han Kang received the Nobel Prize in Literature. In 2025, an animated film involving Korean-diaspora creators, Sony Pictures Animation, and Netflix, KPop Demon Hunters, became an international hit and won two Academy Awards in 2026.

The chapter's central question is how these achievements became possible, and what they reveal. Its answer has several parts. Korean cultural globalization built on institutions that existed before 2012: entertainment agencies, broadcasters, film studios, and a domestic market already shaped by intense competition and by the broadband and smartphone infrastructure described in Chapter 87. Digital platforms made international circulation easier, but they did not by themselves create songs, films, or audiences. Fans acted as translators, promoters, and interpreters, not merely as consumers. Korean influence reached well beyond music into screen entertainment, literature, animation, fashion, food, tourism, and language learning. And global success brought new opportunities together with commercial concentration, dependence on foreign platforms, labor pressures, and disputes over who gets to represent Korean culture.

The evidence for this history is varied and uneven. Platform announcements, chart tables, award records, corporate filings, and government surveys supply numbers, but each measures something different and each was produced for particular purposes. Interviews, fan-community records, press coverage, and the works themselves supply interpretation. Because this chapter closes in November 2026, much of the evidence for its last years consists of contemporary reporting, announcements, and official records rather than the settled scholarly assessments available for earlier periods, and the chapter says so where it matters. Planned activities extending beyond November 2026 are described as plans, not as completed history.

The chapter is organized in four parts. Part I follows the breakthrough of 2012, the digital platforms that carried it, and the rise of BTS and its fandom. Part II turns to BLACKPINK, later generations of K-pop, the global concert economy, and the international breakthrough of Korean cinema. Part III widens to television, Squid Game, webtoons and literature, KPop Demon Hunters, and consumer culture. Part IV evaluates cultural diplomacy, corporate structure, labor, representation, and the position of Korean culture at the fixed endpoint of 2026.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the 2012 success of Gangnam Style was historically significant without being the origin of Hallyu or of Korean popular music.
  2. Distinguish a viral cultural event from the construction of a sustainable international entertainment industry.
  3. Analyze how digital platforms, recommendation systems, subtitles, and streaming services changed the geography and economics of cultural distribution.
  4. Trace the development of BTS, from the 2013 debut to the 2026 return to group activity, as a case of business strategy, music, and fandom operating together.
  5. Evaluate the role of organized international fandom as an intermediary and participant in cultural globalization.
  6. Compare the strategies, audiences, and management of BTS and BLACKPINK, and of later K-pop acts, without treating them as interchangeable.
  7. Distinguish among chart positions, concert attendance, streaming figures, awards, and revenue as different measures of success.
  8. Assess how Parasite and other Korean films reached international audiences through festivals, critics, distributors, and awards institutions.
  9. Interpret the global reception of Squid Game and Korean drama, using streaming-platform data with attention to its methodology and limits.
  10. Explain why KPop Demon Hunters should be understood as a transnational production rather than simply a South Korean one.
  11. Analyze the roles of government policy, corporate consolidation, and labor conditions in shaping global Hallyu.
  12. Evaluate whether Korean cultural influence in 2026 is durable, drawing on institutions, audiences, and evidence rather than individual successes.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did South Korean cultural products achieve worldwide recognition after 2012, and what does that success reveal about globalization, digital media, and national identity?
  2. In what ways did the global expansion of Korean culture depend on institutions created before 2012, and in what ways did it depart from them?
  3. What can a billion views, a number-one chart debut, or an Academy Award tell historians, and what can they not tell us?
  4. Did digital platforms create global audiences for Korean culture, or did they only reduce the cost of reaching audiences that creators and fans had already built?
  5. How should historians understand fans: as consumers, as unpaid marketers, as translators, as a public, or as all of these?
  6. What distinguishes a Korean cultural product from a product made by Koreans, about Korea, or in the style of Korean pop culture?
  7. Who benefits financially and creatively when Korean culture circulates globally, and who bears its costs?
  8. How have different societies interpreted Korean cultural products, and what do backlash and controversy reveal alongside enthusiasm?
  9. Is cultural influence the same as soft power, and can a state claim credit for achievements that private companies, artists, and audiences produced?
  10. As of 2026, what would it mean to call Korean cultural influence durable?

Historical Context

Three earlier developments, treated in other chapters, form the background to this chapter. The first is the industrial and social transformation described in Chapters 75 through 81: rapid urbanization, mass education, rising incomes, and the dense, competitive, highly literate consumer society of Seoul and other cities. This society produced both a demanding domestic audience and large numbers of young people willing to enter intense training in music, dance, and acting. The second is the financial crisis and restructuring of 1997–98 (Chapter 84). Cultural industries became an object of deliberate government and corporate interest as the country sought new sources of export growth, and the South Korean state under Kim Dae-jung and his successors established funding and agencies for cultural and content industries.

The third is the development of Hallyu itself (Chapter 89). By 2011, Korean television dramas had found large audiences across East and Southeast Asia, Korean films had earned festival attention and regional box-office success, and the agency system developed by SM, YG, and JYP had produced idol groups with substantial followings in Japan, China, Taiwan, Thailand, and elsewhere. Korean companies had learned to train performers over several years, to manage them under exclusive long-term contracts, to build fan communities through variety programs and fan clubs, and to sell music in physical and digital formats across borders. The generation that appeared after 2012 inherited these methods and often criticized or modified them.

The fourth element is global digital infrastructure. YouTube, founded in 2005, and the social-media platforms Facebook and Twitter had created channels through which individuals and companies could distribute content without going through national broadcasters. South Korea's broadband and smartphone networks (Chapter 87) meant that Korean companies were early and sophisticated users of these tools. Korean agencies had begun posting official music videos on YouTube channels in the late 2000s, and the Korean music industry had already experimented with global live events, such as the KCON convention that the entertainment conglomerate CJ ENM first held in the United States in 2012. The infrastructure was therefore in place just as a particularly shareable video appeared.

Finally, students should recognize the limits of the term "Korean culture." The cultural products in this chapter were made by companies, artists, and workers with differing nationalities, languages, and affiliations, in a global economy where capital, creative labor, and audiences cross borders constantly. Some of the most visible works were produced partly or wholly outside South Korea. Treating "Korean" as a fixed label applied to everything that audiences associated with Korea would repeat the error that Chapter 1 warned against in an earlier context, namely treating a complicated historical network as a single unchanging essence.

Section 90.1

Gangnam Style and the Global Breakthrough of 2012

Psy, born Park Jae-sang, was not a newcomer when he released Gangnam Style on July 15, 2012. He had debuted in 2001, built a reputation in South Korea for comic, exuberant performance, and developed a career that included studies in the United States, military service obligations, and several controversies. He was a mature entertainer working within the YG Entertainment system, with a persona quite unlike the carefully trained young idols who dominated Korean pop. The song and video were conceived for a domestic audience that already knew his work and understood the references.

Those references were central to the video's meaning. Gangnam, the district south of the Han River, had been developed intensively from the 1970s (Chapters 75 and 81) and had become a symbol of wealth, status, and aspiration in South Korean society. The song's narrator presents himself as a sophisticated man of the district. The video undermines this claim through absurd settings, including a sauna, a parking lot, a playground, a subway car, and a tour bus, and through comic dance. In other words, the song was a satire of status-seeking consumer culture, though it was received outside Korea mostly as a joyful novelty. Much of the satire required knowing what Gangnam represented, and many international viewers did not.

The video's circulation had several sources. Its visual appeal was strong: bright colors, a simple, imitable dance in which the performer mimics riding a horse, and a series of comic cameos by well-known Korean television personalities. The chorus could be sung without knowing the language. Prominent figures in American entertainment shared or imitated it on social media, and Psy's connections to American music business figures, including the manager Scooter Braun, helped carry the song into American media. Television programs, parodies by schools, businesses, police departments, and military units, and countless reaction videos amplified it. The result was a form of participatory circulation, in which millions of ordinary people made their own versions.

On December 21, 2012, YouTube announced that the video had passed one billion views, the first video on the platform to do so. In the United States, the song reached number two on the Billboard Hot 100, behind a single by an American group, a distinction that Section 90.5 revisits. In many countries the song reached the top of national charts. For Korean companies and government agencies, the video gave cultural industries a prominence they had not previously had in Western media.

The historical significance of the event lies in what it demonstrated, not in what it started. It showed that a Korean-language song could reach a worldwide audience without a conventional Western record-company campaign, that digital platforms could carry such content directly to viewers, and that audiences abroad would respond to Korean performance and humor. It also made Korean pop culture a subject of discussion in Western media in a way that had not occurred before. But it was a viral event, an exceptional peak of attention, rather than a sustained industry presence. Psy released further songs and videos, some of which also drew large audiences, but none matched the original's reach, and he did not become a regular presence in Western charts. Distinguishing the two, a viral moment and a sustainable international industry, is the central analytical task of this section, and the following sections show how the second kind of achievement, much harder than the first, was accomplished by others.

Historical Vocabulary
Viral Event and View Count

Definition. A viral event is a rapid, widespread circulation of a piece of content through sharing among individuals rather than through institutional distribution. A view count is a platform's tally of the number of times a video has been played according to the platform's own counting rules.

Historical context. Gangnam Style was the first YouTube video to reach one billion views. Platforms define what counts as a view and adjust their rules to deter automated or fraudulent counts. In 2014, the number of views reached the limit of the counter YouTube was then using, and the platform changed its system, a technical episode widely noted at the time.

Why it matters. A view count measures plays, not people, and does not distinguish a delighted fan from a viewer who watched once out of curiosity, a user who played the video repeatedly, or an autoplay sequence. It is a useful indicator of attention, but a poor indicator of audience size, income, or lasting influence.

Potential misunderstanding. One billion views does not mean one billion viewers, and it does not mean that one billion people knew or liked Korean culture.

Section 90.2

YouTube, Social Media, Streaming, and the Transformation of Cultural Distribution

Before the 2000s, entertainment reached foreign audiences chiefly through national broadcasters, theatrical distributors, record companies, and licensing agreements. A Korean drama would be sold to a television network in another country, edited or dubbed, and scheduled according to that network's decisions. A Korean record might be released abroad by a local label, with its own promotion budget. The costs and gatekeeping of these channels favored products from the United States, Japan, and a few other large producers, and they limited what audiences in other countries could encounter.

Digital platforms rearranged these relationships over a period of about fifteen years. YouTube, founded in 2005, allowed anyone to upload video, and Korean agencies, broadcasters, and fans increasingly used it from the late 2000s. Facebook and Twitter, followed by Instagram and, from the second half of the 2010s, TikTok, supplied channels for sharing and promotion. Music streaming services, including Spotify and Apple Music, and Korean services such as Melon, shifted listening from purchases to subscriptions and playlists. Subscription video services, notably Netflix, which began operating in South Korea in 2016 as part of a broad international expansion, offered viewers access to catalogs across borders.

Several mechanisms mattered. Recommendation systems suggested related content to viewers, so that an audience member who watched one Korean video might be offered others. Subtitles, whether provided officially or by volunteers, lowered the language barrier. Online sharing allowed content to spread among friends without institutional promotion. Digital advertising and platform payments created revenue streams for creators and rights holders. International charts, such as the Billboard Social 50 and, from 2020, the Billboard Global 200, gave measurable status to cross-border popularity. Mobile consumption made access possible on inexpensive phones in markets where television ownership or cinema access had been limited.

These changes meant that audiences could encounter Korean cultural products without waiting for a domestic broadcaster or a foreign record company to introduce them. Korean companies, which already controlled large parts of their own production chain, could address a worldwide public directly. Performers could communicate with fans in several languages. Fans could discuss, translate, and organize across borders.

Students should nonetheless avoid technological determinism. Platforms did not create music, films, or dramas; they transmitted works that people made, and many works uploaded to the same platforms attracted few viewers. Reach is not engagement: a video may be available in every country and attract no audience in most of them. Engagement is not revenue: platforms paid relatively small amounts per view, and the largest revenues for Korean music often came from concerts, merchandise, physical albums, and fan-club memberships rather than streaming. Platforms also introduced new dependencies, since changes to a recommendation system, payment terms, or content policy could affect creators who had no control over them. Chapter 87 explained how South Korea built the technological infrastructure; this section shows that the infrastructure became culturally and economically powerful only when combined with creative content, organized audiences, and commercial strategy. The next sections show that combination in practice.

Section 90.3

BTS: From a 2013 Debut to International Recognition

BTS, short for Bangtan Sonyeondan, debuted on June 13, 2013, under Big Hit Entertainment, a company founded in 2005 by the producer Bang Si-hyuk. Big Hit was far smaller than SM, YG, or JYP, and its first idol group had been preceded by only a limited track record. The seven members, RM, Jin, Suga, j-hope, Jimin, V, and Jung Kook, had been recruited and trained over several years, and the group had been originally conceived with a strong hip-hop orientation, in which members would write and produce part of their own material.

Early recordings combined hip-hop, pop, and dance, with lyrics addressing the pressures of school, social expectations, and youth, themes already familiar in Korean popular music but handled in a manner that many young listeners found direct. The group's early public identity was shaped by comparison with better-funded groups, and its members spoke publicly about being underestimated. A critical early difference was online communication: from the beginning, members posted regularly on social platforms, and Big Hit released videos, behind-the-scenes content, and later fan-community applications that encouraged daily contact with followers.

The group's commercial and artistic development can be followed through its releases. The The Most Beautiful Moment in Life series, beginning in 2015, introduced a coherent narrative and visual world around adolescence and uncertainty, expressed through connected albums, music videos, and online content. The 2016 album Wings marked a widening of musical range and international attention. The Love Yourself series, from 2017 to 2018, brought the group to American and European audiences in large numbers, and its 2018 installment is generally recognized as the first album by a Korean-language group to reach number one on the American Billboard 200 albums chart.

In May 2017, BTS won the Billboard Music Award for Top Social Artist, an award determined primarily by measures of online engagement. The recognition surprised some American commentators and signaled to American entertainment media that a Korean group had developed a large international audience. In the following years, appearances on American television programs, concert tours in stadiums and arenas, and growing coverage in mainstream outlets made the group familiar to audiences who did not follow K-pop.

Historians should avoid attributing this rise to a single factor. The music itself, its performance, and its visual presentation mattered. The company's strategy mattered, including intensive use of online communication, the creation of connected narrative worlds, and the willingness to release material in an unusual sequence. Distribution through YouTube and streaming services allowed audiences to find the group without relying on radio. International fans, discussed in Section 90.4, translated lyrics and interviews and promoted the group. And the structure of the Korean industry, with its trained performers, producers, choreographers, and video makers, supplied labor and skills that few foreign competitors could match. The result was not an accident, but it was also not inevitable: other equally trained groups did not achieve the same international position, and the reasons remain a subject of discussion among scholars and industry observers.

Section 90.4

ARMY and the Emergence of Global Participatory Fandom

BTS's fan community, which took the name ARMY in 2013, became a defining example of a worldwide participatory fandom. ARMY was not a single organization with a membership roll, but a large, loosely connected network of individuals, local groups, online communities, and volunteer projects in dozens of countries, communicating primarily through social-media platforms, online forums, and the fan-community applications provided by the company. Its significance for this chapter is that it shows how audiences actively shaped the circulation and meaning of cultural products, rather than simply receiving them.

Fans performed several kinds of work. Volunteer translators rendered lyrics, interviews, and social-media posts into English, Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, and other languages, often within hours of release, supplying access that official channels could not match. Online communities explained the group's narrative and references, circulated analyses, and welcomed new fans. Fans organized streaming campaigns to push songs up streaming charts, coordinated purchases, and discussed strategies for appearing in voting systems. They organized charitable fundraising, including a widely reported 2020 effort in which fans matched a donation made by the group to a racial-justice organization. They attended concerts, organized collective choreography, and built local meeting groups that persisted between tours.

Students should distinguish these activities from formal agency marketing. A company could encourage fans by providing content, platforms, and merchandise, and it benefited commercially from fan enthusiasm. But much fan activity was independent: translation groups, analysis communities, and charity campaigns were organized by fans without company direction. At the same time, the line was not sharp. Company platforms structured fan interaction, commercial packaging incentivized purchase, and fans sometimes acted in ways that the company later accepted or rewarded. The proper description is a relationship of mutual dependence in which fans and the company each shaped the other.

Organized fandom could affect media attention, chart performance, and public perception of Korean culture. Coordinated streaming and buying influenced weekly chart results. Fan-run accounts shaped how news about the group was discussed. Fans served as informal cultural explainers, introducing viewers to Korean language, food, and history. Charitable campaigns brought public attention that differed from commercial promotion, and in some cases changed how outsiders thought about K-pop audiences.

The limits of fandom are as important as its achievements. Online fandom is marked by disagreements, including disputes among fan factions, competition between fandoms of different groups, harassment of critics, and conflicts over how to treat the performers' private lives. Participation is unequal: those with the time, money, language skills, and internet access to participate most actively have more influence than others. Fan activity may also be difficult to measure, so claims about its impact on charts or sales can be exaggerated. Fans were active participants in the promotion and interpretation of Korean culture, but they were also a commercial audience whose enthusiasm was channeled and monetized, and historians must hold both facts together.

Historical Vocabulary
Participatory Fandom

Definition. A form of audience engagement in which fans create, translate, organize, and promote content or activities around a cultural product, instead of only consuming it.

Historical context. Fan communities have a long history, from letter-writing and fan clubs to online forums. In the 2010s, social-media platforms and fan-community applications allowed fans in different countries to coordinate in real time. Korean agencies had cultivated fan clubs since the 1990s (Chapter 89); ARMY and similar groups extended this to a transnational scale.

Why it matters. Participatory fans shaped the translation, circulation, and interpretation of Korean culture abroad. Their activities complicate any account in which companies or platforms alone explain global success.

Potential misunderstanding. "Fandom" is not a single organization, and fans do not all act alike. Fans' voluntary labor can also be commercially useful to companies that do not pay for it.

Section 90.5

BTS as a Global Music Institution, 2018–2026

By 2018, BTS was recognized as an international act, and the next years moved it from popularity to a sustained position in the world's largest music markets. In September 2018, its leader, RM, spoke at the United Nations as part of a campaign on youth and self-esteem that the group and its company had undertaken with UNICEF. The appearance indicated public prominence beyond the entertainment industry. In 2019 the group performed in stadiums in Europe and the Americas, and the Map of the Soul releases of 2019 and 2020 consolidated its position on American and international albums charts.

The achievement most often cited is "Dynamite," a song sung in English and released on August 21, 2020. It debuted at number one on the Billboard Hot 100, the American singles chart, making BTS the first Korean act to reach that position. Students should distinguish this from Psy's earlier peak: Gangnam Style had reached number two in 2012. The difference is not only one of rank. "Dynamite" was released by a group with an established international audience, supported by a deliberate release strategy, and its debut at number one reflected, in large part, a first-week surge of purchases and streams driven by organized fans, as well as broad radio and streaming interest. Chart recognition is therefore evidence of a group's measured performance in specific categories, not a direct measure of the size of a worldwide audience.

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted touring in 2020, and BTS responded with online concerts and further English-language releases. Later releases extended its presence on American charts. In 2022, the group announced a shift toward solo activities, a decision shaped by creative exhaustion and by South Korea's military-service obligations. A 2020 amendment to the Military Service Act allowed certain recognized popular-culture artists to postpone enlistment until age 30, but not to avoid it; the members then served in the following years, with the eldest enlisting in December 2022 and others after him. In the interim the members released solo work, and some of it performed strongly internationally.

The group's return to coordinated activity is a major event of 2026. Its fifth studio album, Arirang, was released on March 20, 2026, its first full group album since the 2022 anthology Proof and the first new studio album since 2020. The title refers to a traditional Korean folk song with a long history in Korean and diaspora culture, and its use signaled an explicit engagement with Korean heritage. On March 21, 2026, all seven members performed at Gwanghwamun Square in Seoul in a comeback concert streamed live on Netflix, which subsequently made the performance available to watch; a companion documentary was released March 27. The Arirang world tour opened in Goyang, near Seoul, on April 9, 2026. Further dates were scheduled across Asia, North America, South America, Australia, and Europe, continuing into 2027; the concert record at this chapter’s November 2026 endpoint must be separated from future itineraries. The opening and concerts already held by November 2026 are completed events; announced concerts thereafter remain plans. Projections of total tour revenue or attendance are industry estimates and are not used here as verified outcomes.

BTS's trajectory shows how a Korean-language act became an enduring participant in the international music economy. It did so through a combination of musical output, long-term fan relationships, strategic release planning, English-language material when useful, digital infrastructure, corporate organization, and, importantly, the dedication of its members, who endured demanding schedules. It also depended on contingencies, such as the timing of the pandemic and the military-service law. Its example should not be generalized: very few acts have achieved comparable positions, and the group's long hiatus indicates that even a leading global act is subject to the constraints of its own society.

BLACKPINK and the Global Expansion of Korean Girl Groups

BLACKPINK debuted in August 2016 under YG Entertainment, with four members: Jisoo, Jennie, Rosé, and Lisa. Lisa is Thai and Rosé was raised partly in Australia, so from the beginning the group's membership reflected the multinational recruiting that the Korean industry had practiced since the 2000s. YG, a company associated with hip-hop-influenced production and with a distinct visual style, positioned the group as a confident, high-fashion act with a strong emphasis on performance and branding. The debut singles combined electronic, hip-hop, and pop elements and were released with polished music videos.

International recognition developed through several channels. Online audiences grew rapidly on YouTube, where the group's videos attracted very large view counts. Collaborations with foreign artists, such as a 2018 song with the British singer Dua Lipa and later work with American performers, introduced the group to listeners of Western pop. Partnerships with fashion houses made individual members familiar through luxury advertising and fashion events, so that the group's public presence extended well beyond music. English-language and multilingual releases, and members' individual comfort in English, Thai, and other languages, supported communication with audiences abroad.

In April 2019, BLACKPINK performed at the Coachella Valley Music and Arts Festival in California, where it was widely described as the first K-pop girl group to appear at the festival. In 2023 it returned as a headliner, a position that signaled a change from a novelty appearance to a headline attraction at one of the most prominent festivals in the United States. Albums in 2020 and 2022 and tours that followed extended its audience in arenas and stadiums across several continents.

Its strategy differed from that of BTS in ways that matter historically. BTS built its audience through sustained narrative and emotional connection with a fan base that came to treat the group's messages as personal and communal; its lyrics and public statements emphasized candor about youth and self-worth. BLACKPINK emphasized glamour, fashion, and spectacle, with more selective releases and a stronger emphasis on individual members as brand ambassadors. BTS grew from a smaller company that used online communication intensively, while BLACKPINK was produced by an established agency with an existing network. Their audiences overlapped but were not identical, and their musical identities differed. Treating them as interchangeable obscures the diversity of routes to international prominence.

Individual and group activities both mattered. During the 2020s members pursued individual recordings and branding, including releases through independent arrangements, following a negotiated agreement in which they renewed with YG for group activities while handling individual careers separately. A collaboration released in 2024 by one member with a prominent American singer achieved wide international chart success. For the 2025–2026 period, YG Entertainment documented a tour branded Deadline beginning in 2025 and the group’s third mini album Deadline, released on February 27, 2026, as confirmed by YG Entertainment’s discography. As with BTS, the story of the group in these years includes the economics of touring, individual branding, and negotiation between artists and a company over rights and control of their careers.

Section 90.7

New Generations of K-pop and the Diversification of International Markets

BTS and BLACKPINK were not the only Korean acts that found international audiences. During the 2010s and 2020s a succession of groups debuted, many of them with distinct musical styles and distinct strategies. TWICE, formed through a television competition run by JYP Entertainment, debuted in October 2015 and became prominent in Japan as well as in Korea. SEVENTEEN, which debuted in May 2015 under the smaller agency Pledis, was known for a large membership and for members' involvement in composition and choreography. Stray Kids, which emerged from a JYP survival program and debuted in 2018, drew attention for self-produced material and became notable for reaching the top of the American Billboard 200 albums chart. ATEEZ debuted in 2018 under the small company KQ and built a significant international following largely through touring and online communication.

Later groups included aespa, which debuted in 2020 under SM Entertainment with a concept that blended the members with virtual avatars; IVE, which debuted in late 2021 under Starship Entertainment; LE SSERAFIM, which debuted in 2022 under a label affiliated with HYBE; and NewJeans, which debuted in 2022 under the HYBE label ADOR and drew attention for a more understated visual and musical style than many earlier groups. Each had different circumstances, and the dates and corporate affiliations of these acts changed over time, so students should treat each case individually. The reorganization of corporate groups and several disputes between artists and companies during 2023–2025, including the legal dispute surrounding NewJeans and its label, illustrate how institutional change continued to shape the industry.

Several developments marked the diversification. Multilingual production became common: groups included members from Japan, Thailand, China, Australia, the United States, and elsewhere, and released versions of songs in Japanese and English. Performance-centered acts, whose appeal rested on precise group choreography and live skill, found audiences at concerts and online. International fan communication expanded through platforms that offered translation and direct messaging. Specialized audience communities developed around particular groups, subgenres, and styles, reflecting a market that had become large enough to sustain many different kinds of act. Some Korean companies also collaborated with American and other foreign companies to create groups that combined Korean training methods with foreign membership and markets, which raised the question of what, exactly, makes an act "K-pop."

Industry shorthand classifies groups into "generations": first generation for the 1990s acts, second for those debuting in the late 2000s, third for the 2010s, and fourth for the 2020s, with some commentators also proposing a fifth. These labels are retrospective conventions, and the boundaries are disputed. Fans, journalists, and companies draw the lines in different places, and the categories sometimes obscure continuities, such as the persistence of long-established acts and the continuing influence of older production methods. They are useful shorthand, but students should not mistake them for precise periods.

The broader point is that international recognition was not the exclusive achievement of a small number of leading acts. The industry diversified rather than simply repeating its original idol model, and many acts built substantial audiences of different sizes in different regions. At the same time, the market remained concentrated: a small number of groups and companies accounted for a disproportionate share of attention and revenue, and the vast majority of trainees never debuted or never achieved wide success. Diversification and concentration coexisted.

Section 90.8

World Tours, Festivals, Music Charts, and the Global Concert Economy

By the late 2010s, live performance had become a central part of the economics of Korean music. Leading groups undertook tours of arenas and stadiums in Asia, North and South America, Europe, and Australia. Festival appearances, such as BLACKPINK's at Coachella, provided exposure to audiences who did not seek out K-pop. Fan conventions, including events such as KCON, which began in the United States in 2012, combined concerts, fan meetings, and commercial exhibitions. Merchandise, from light sticks to apparel, and fan-club memberships contributed substantial revenue. Some concerts were broadcast in cinemas or streamed online, extending live events to audiences unable to attend.

Touring depended on a network of intermediaries. Korean companies worked with promoters in each region, booked venues, and negotiated with ticketing firms. International distribution partnerships helped place albums and merchandise in retail chains abroad. Regional promotional strategies varied: a group's approach to Japan, where a large and long-established market for Korean artists existed, differed from its approach to the United States, Brazil, or Europe. Artists depended on these promoters, venues, streaming services, ticketing platforms, and distributors, and their bargaining position varied by their popularity.

Charts became an important language of status. Billboard's charts, especially the Hot 100 for singles and the Billboard 200 for albums, were widely cited. Japan's Oricon charts remained essential for acts selling physical albums in that country, and Korea's own Circle Chart and other national charts mattered in the domestic market. Students should note that each chart measures something different. Some count sales, some streaming, some airplay, and some combine them using weights that have changed. Chart rules also adapted: Billboard, for example, altered its treatment of bundled digital sales in the late 2010s. Fan purchasing behavior, including multiple editions of the same album, could affect results, so rankings reflect organized commercial behavior as well as popularity.

Students must therefore distinguish several measurements. Concert attendance counts people at events; ticket revenue counts money, which depends on prices; album sales count physical or digital units; streaming figures count plays; airplay counts broadcasts; social-media activity counts interactions. A group may be strong in one and weak in another. A Korean group that sells large numbers of physical albums to a committed fan base may have little radio airplay. A song with many streams might not sell concert tickets. Claims of "success" that do not specify the measure should be treated with caution.

The COVID-19 pandemic abruptly interrupted this economy beginning in early 2020. Tours were cancelled or postponed, and companies turned to online concerts, which charged for streaming access and developed interactive features. The shift illustrated both the flexibility of Korean companies' digital infrastructure and the dependence of the industry on live revenue. After restrictions eased in 2021 and 2022, international touring recovered and, for leading acts, reached new scale, with stadium tours in multiple regions. The subsequent recovery also exposed new pressures, including high travel schedules for artists and rising ticket prices that fans complained about.

Historical Debate

What Made Hallyu Global: Creative Innovation, Digital Platforms, or Organized Fandom?

The question: Historians, sociologists, and industry observers disagree about the principal reason Korean cultural products reached worldwide audiences after 2012. Which factor should receive primary weight, and how do the factors interact?

Interpretation A — Artistic and commercial innovation: Korean producers developed distinctive products: highly trained performers, genre-blending music, polished visuals, high-quality television and film storytelling, and detailed attention to narrative and presentation. In this view, audiences responded to the quality and novelty of the work, and other factors were secondary.
Interpretation B — Digital platform distribution: Global platforms removed the gatekeeping of broadcasters and record companies, offered subtitles and recommendation systems, and reduced the cost of reaching foreign audiences. In this view, the decisive change was infrastructural: without YouTube, social media, streaming, and mobile devices, similar products would have remained regional.
Interpretation C — Participatory fans as intermediaries: Fans translated, explained, promoted, and organized, and they turned passive reach into engaged communities. In this view, platforms and products needed fans to become cultural movements.

Evidence: Evidence includes platform data, which can show the timing of audience growth; company strategy documents and interviews; the persistence of earlier, regional Hallyu success before platforms were global; fan-community records, such as translation archives; and comparison with other non-English-language products that had platform access but did not achieve comparable reach.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and weight, and partly terminology, since "innovation" and "fan labor" can be difficult to separate. Evidence that would distinguish the explanations includes comparisons of acts with similar products and platform access but different fan strategies, and the timing of audience growth relative to translation and platform changes.

Why it matters: If innovation was central, policy and business emphasis should fall on creative investment. If platforms were decisive, dependence on foreign intermediaries becomes a major concern. If fans were indispensable, their voluntary labor and their relationships with companies deserve scrutiny. Most scholars see the three as interacting rather than competing.

Section 90.9

Korean Cinema beyond Asia: International Festivals and Global Audiences

Chapter 89 described the growth of the South Korean film industry from the 1990s, supported by increased investment, the arrival of a new generation of directors, and a domestic audience that favored Korean films. By the 2010s, Korean cinema had a significant international reputation among festival audiences and critics. Directors such as Park Chan-wook, Bong Joon-ho, and Lee Chang-dong had built careers that combined artistic distinction with commercial success at home. The questions of this section are how and to whom Korean cinema reached beyond Asia.

Three films illustrate different paths. The Handmaiden (2016), directed by Park Chan-wook, adapted a British novel by shifting its setting to colonial-era Korea and Japan, and it was selected for competition at the Cannes Film Festival; it also received recognition from critics in English-speaking countries. Train to Busan (2016), directed by Yeon Sang-ho, was a zombie thriller that screened at Cannes in a midnight section and became a commercial success in Korea and several other markets, showing the appeal of Korean genre filmmaking. Burning (2018), directed by Lee Chang-dong and loosely based on a short story by the Japanese author Haruki Murakami, competed at Cannes and was widely praised by critics, receiving a critics' prize there. Each reached a different audience: Burning and The Handmaiden primarily art-house viewers and critics, Train to Busan a broader genre audience.

Several institutions mediated their reception. Film festivals gave visibility to films that might otherwise not have been distributed abroad. International critics shaped reputations through reviews. Subtitling made the films accessible, though translation choices affected how dialogue and humor were understood. Distribution agreements with foreign companies determined which films were shown in which countries and how widely. Theatrical releases remained important, but online platforms increasingly made films available to home audiences. Genre storytelling, whether horror, thriller, or crime, helped films travel because genre conventions are recognizable across cultures, even as Korean films frequently added local social detail.

Students should distinguish three kinds of achievement. An international festival success brings prestige and critical attention but may attract a limited audience. A commercially successful export earns revenue in foreign markets. A film that reaches a broader international audience becomes known beyond specialized viewers. The three can overlap but are not identical. A film can win prizes and sell few tickets; a film can earn substantial money without critical acclaim.

Behind these films were many professionals whom audiences rarely noticed: producers who financed and organized production, cinematographers, editors, composers, production designers, actors, and distributors who negotiated foreign releases. The Korean film industry also had financing and distribution firms, including CJ ENM and other companies, that invested in both domestic and international production. This industrial and artistic environment, built over more than two decades, formed the setting for the breakthrough discussed in Section 90.10.

Section 90.10

Parasite and the International Breakthrough of Korean Cinema, 2019–2020

Bong Joon-ho, born in 1969, had directed several widely praised films before Parasite, including the crime drama Memories of Murder (2003), the monster film The Host (2006), and the English-language Snowpiercer (2013). He was already a prominent international filmmaker. Parasite, co-written with Han Jin-won and produced with the Korean company Barunson E&A, with distribution support from CJ ENM, brought together a cast that included Song Kang-ho, a long-time collaborator, and a production team of Korean specialists, including cinematographer Hong Kyung-pyo.

The film tells the story of a poor family that infiltrates the household of a wealthy one through a series of deceptions. It combines comedy, thriller, and social drama, and its structure moves from satire to violence. Its central theme is class, which it conveys visually through vertical space: the wealthy family lives in a modern house on a hill, while the poor family lives in a semi-basement apartment, and rain that is merely an inconvenience to the wealthy floods the poor family's neighborhood. These images resonated with audiences in societies as different as South Korea, the United States, and France, where inequality was a matter of public concern. The film drew on Korean specifics, such as housing, private tutoring, and status, which Chapters 81 and 91 discuss, while using a story that viewers elsewhere could readily interpret in terms of their own societies.

In May 2019, Parasite won the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival, the first Korean film to do so. It was released in the United States by the distributor Neon, which conducted a sustained awards campaign. In February 2020 it won four Academy Awards: Best Picture, Best Director, Best Original Screenplay, and Best International Feature Film. It was the first film not in English to win Best Picture. The film also earned substantial box-office revenue internationally, on the order of a quarter of a billion U.S. dollars, a high figure for a subtitled film, though students should consult box-office sources for exact figures.

The victory mattered for international cinema because it demonstrated that a non-English-language film could win the most prominent American film prize, which had long been dominated by English-language productions. Bong publicly referred, in his remarks at an earlier awards ceremony, to subtitles as a small barrier that audiences could overcome, and the success was widely cited in discussions of how American audiences relate to subtitled film. Some observers also noted that the Academy's membership had been expanded and internationalized in the preceding years, which may have affected the outcome, a point on which commentators disagree.

Most importantly, the film's achievement should be understood as the culmination of decades of Korean cinematic development. It was supported by institutions that had grown since the 1990s: government support for film, a large domestic audience, professional training, domestic financing and distribution firms, and a generation of filmmakers with international experience. It was not the sudden emergence of a previously unknown national industry. The film's success also did not erase the working conditions of film crews or the structural constraints of the industry, themes explored in Section 90.18.

Section 90.11

Korean Television Drama in the Global Streaming Era

Korean television drama had reached audiences in East and Southeast Asia from the late 1990s (Chapter 89), typically through sales of programs to national broadcasters, who selected, scheduled, and sometimes dubbed them. The arrival of global streaming services altered this arrangement. Netflix began operating in South Korea in 2016, and later services, including Disney+ and Apple TV+, entered the market, while Korean services such as Tving and Wavve developed. Dramas that once reached foreign audiences years after broadcast, if at all, could appear in many countries at once or nearly so.

Three series show different stages. Descendants of the Sun (2016), broadcast on KBS2, was a military romance that was produced largely before broadcast, an unusual practice in Korea's then-normal system of filming while airing, and it was sold across Asia and became a major regional success. Crash Landing on You (2019–2020), broadcast on the cable channel tvN, imagined a South Korean heiress who lands in North Korea after a paragliding accident; it became widely available through Netflix and drew international viewers who, in many cases, had little prior exposure to Korean television. Extraordinary Attorney Woo (2022), broadcast on the smaller channel ENA, followed a lawyer on the autism spectrum and became a prominent international streaming success, even though its domestic broadcaster was much less established than the largest networks.

These cases reveal a changing production system. Established broadcasters such as KBS, MBC, and SBS and cable channels like tvN and JTBC continued to commission and air dramas. Independent production companies, including Studio Dragon, an affiliate of CJ ENM, played a growing role and negotiated their rights. Global platforms acquired licensing rights, co-financed productions, or commissioned original series. Subtitles and dubbing in many languages broadened access, and recommendation systems introduced viewers to related titles. The shift was from territorial sales, in which rights were sold country by country, to simultaneous or near-simultaneous cross-border access in which a single platform held rights across many territories.

Students should distinguish productions that aired first on Korean broadcasters and later appeared on platforms from Netflix originals made for the platform. The two models have different financial arrangements: broadcaster productions are funded partly through advertising and domestic licensing, while platform originals may be funded in advance by the platform, which may hold broad or complete intellectual-property rights, a point of controversy among Korean producers and writers. Netflix announced in 2023 a large multi-year investment commitment in Korean content, while Korean producers weighed the benefits of secure financing against the loss of long-term ownership.

Korean-language storytelling reached viewers with limited previous exposure to Korean television, often through subtitles that viewers accepted more readily than earlier generations of television audiences. Such viewers frequently reported being drawn by the combination of genres, such as romance, thriller, and workplace drama, with relatively short season lengths and particular treatments of family, class, and social pressure. These responses were recorded in surveys and press accounts and should be treated as indicative rather than comprehensive. The reach of Korean dramas did not eliminate regional variation: audiences in Asia, with long familiarity, differed from newer audiences elsewhere.

Section 90.12

Squid Game and the Worldwide Transformation of Korean Television, 2021–2025

Hwang Dong-hyuk, the writer and director of Squid Game, had worked in Korean film, including the 2011 film Silenced, which dramatized a real case of abuse at a school for deaf children and prompted public and legislative reaction in Korea. He wrote the script for Squid Game in the late 2000s, and by his own account it was repeatedly judged too unusual to produce. Its eventual realization depended on Netflix's decision to commission Korean originals. The series premiered on September 17, 2021.

The story concerns indebted people recruited to compete in children's games for a large cash prize, with death as the penalty for losing. Its critique addresses household debt, inequality, economic desperation, and the commodification of human lives, themes tied to Korean conditions described in Chapters 84 and 91, but also recognizable in many other societies. Its visual style, with brightly colored sets, uniform costumes, and the contrast between childhood imagery and violence, was highly distinctive and easily circulated as images and costumes, aiding its spread on social media.

Its international success was rapid. Netflix reported that it became the platform's most-watched series at launch and in later reporting ranked it at or near the top of its non-English television lists. The series won major awards, including recognition at the 2022 Emmy Awards for Hwang's direction and for lead actor Lee Jung-jae, and at the Screen Actors Guild Awards. A second season was released on December 26, 2024, and a third and final season in June 2025, bringing the series to a close. A reality competition inspired by the series, and related merchandise, illustrated how the series' intellectual property extended beyond the drama.

The reasons for its reach included Netflix's simultaneous global release, the availability of subtitles and dubbing in many languages, a premise that required little cultural knowledge, social-media circulation of images, memes, and costumes, and word of mouth. Netflix's recommendation system and promotional placement also contributed. Students should be cautious in assigning weight: these factors acted together, and platform promotion cannot be separated from audience response.

The series also generated discussion of who benefited. Hwang commented in interviews that the platform, not he, held the intellectual-property rights and that his earnings did not reflect the series' global scale, a theme that became part of a broader debate about compensation and ownership in Korean creative industries, and Section 90.17 and Section 90.18 return to it. Students must be cautious with the evidence, since a creator's remarks are one source among several and contractual terms are not public.

Korean Webtoons, Literature, and Global Storytelling

Webtoons, digital comics designed for vertical scrolling on phones and computers, originated in South Korea in the early 2000s. Naver launched its webtoon service in 2004, and its international arm began publishing English-language and other versions from 2014. Kakao, another large Korean internet company, developed publishing and entertainment businesses that included webtoons and web novels, with services in Japan and other markets. International serialization, translation, and local-language versions allowed Korean comics to find readers abroad, and in June 2024 the global webtoon business was listed on the Nasdaq stock exchange in the United States, a sign of its commercial scale.

Webtoons became important sources for adaptation. Stories moved between web comics, web novels, films, television series, and animation. Examples include the television series Itaewon Class, adapted from a webtoon, and the zombie series All of Us Are Dead and the horror series Sweet Home, both adapted from webtoons and distributed internationally through Netflix, as well as an animated series based on the story Solo Leveling, which began as a web novel and was then serialized as a webtoon. This circulation illustrates the concept of intellectual property managed across media: a story proven with online readers could be developed with less risk into screen projects. It also created an interdependence in which comics became research and development for screen industries.

Korean literature reached international readers through different institutions. Translated novels circulated through publishers, literary prizes, translators, and book fairs. Han Kang's novel The Vegetarian, translated into English by Deborah Smith, won the International Booker Prize in 2016, which brought wide attention. In October 2024, the Swedish Academy awarded Han Kang the Nobel Prize in Literature, citing, in its own words, her intense poetic prose that confronts historical traumas and exposes the fragility of human life. She was the first Korean author to receive the prize. Her works include Human Acts, which deals with the 1980 Gwangju Uprising described in Chapter 79, and We Do Not Part, which engages with the Jeju violence of 1948–49 treated in Chapter 66. The recognition therefore also brought international attention to episodes in modern Korean history.

Students must distinguish literary recognition from mass-market entertainment. Literary prizes are awarded by committees and juries, driven by critics, translators, and publishers, and they bring a different kind of audience, often smaller but more attentive to a writer's whole body of work. Mass-market entertainment relies on platforms, advertising, and fan communities. Both contributed to Korean cultural influence, but they function through different mechanisms, and the global sales surge that followed the Nobel announcement, widely reported in Korean and international press, was a response to institutional recognition rather than to platform recommendation. The diaspora also contributed: Korean American authors, such as Min Jin Lee, whose English-language novel Pachinko concerns Koreans in Japan and was adapted for television, show that stories about Korean history can be produced outside South Korea.

This section ensures that Korean cultural globalization is not reduced to music and streaming television. It also shows the variety of institutions through which culture crosses borders, from digital platforms to literary translators, and the way in which stories travel between forms, languages, and countries.

Section 90.14

KPop Demon Hunters and Transnational Cultural Production, 2025–2026

KPop Demon Hunters is an animated musical film directed by Maggie Kang, a Korean Canadian filmmaker, and Chris Appelhans, produced by Sony Pictures Animation and distributed by Netflix, which released it on June 20, 2025. It follows a fictional K-pop girl group whose members secretly fight demons, and it draws on Korean folklore and visual motifs, including figures from Korean tradition, alongside the staging conventions of K-pop performance. Creative personnel included Korean diaspora artists and others from several countries, and Korean performers took part in the voice cast and in the songs.

The film's combination of elements is its distinguishing feature. It uses K-pop performance conventions: choreographed group numbers, rival groups, fan culture, and the pressures of idol life. It uses Korean folklore and visual motifs, including traditional imagery. It uses comedy and action in the style of American animated features. And it was accompanied by internationally marketed songs, of which the best known, "Golden," drew a large audience separately from the film.

The film became a major international success following its June 2025 release, drawing exceptionally large viewing numbers on Netflix, a phenomenon discussed widely in the press, and creating fan communities that sang along, wore costumes, and shared clips. At the 2026 Academy Awards it won Animated Feature Film, credited to Maggie Kang, Chris Appelhans, and Michelle L. M. Wong, and Music (Original Song) for “Golden,” credited by the Academy to EJAE, Mark Sonnenblick, Joong Gyu Kwak, Yu Han Lee, Hee Dong Nam, Jeong Hoon Seo, and Teddy Park. The Academy lists named award recipients; Sony Pictures Animation identifies the film’s production and creative leadership, while Netflix handled distribution. Platform viewing records remain corporate measurements subject to the cautions in Section 90.12.

Historians should not classify the film simplistically as a South Korean-produced film. Its production company was American, its directors and many of its artists were from the Korean diaspora and elsewhere, and its commercial ownership rests with companies outside Korea. Its significance lies partly in demonstrating how Korean cultural forms became material for multinational creative collaborations. The film's success also shows that K-pop conventions and Korean folklore had become recognizable enough to an international audience to serve as the basis of a mainstream production.

The development brings both benefits and tensions. It increased visibility of Korean traditions, such as the symbolic figures and material culture the film uses, and gave opportunities to diaspora creators and Korean performers. It also raised questions about representation, since a story about Korea told by a studio based elsewhere might simplify or reshape Korean traditions for international consumption; about ownership, since the rights and revenues belonged to non-Korean companies; and about diaspora participation, since Korean-descended creators worked from perspectives different from those of people in South Korea. These tensions illustrate a broader point of this chapter: global Hallyu includes products that Korea neither owns nor controls.

Historical Vocabulary
Transnational Production

Definition. A creative or commercial work made through cooperation among companies, artists, and funders based in more than one country, so that no single national label describes it accurately.

Historical context. International co-productions in film are old, but the growth of global streaming platforms and of diaspora creative communities increased their frequency. KPop Demon Hunters combined an American studio, a global streaming distributor, Korean-diaspora creators, Korean performers, and Korean cultural material.

Why it matters. Cultural influence can be real even when the products are not owned by the country whose culture they draw upon. Classifying a work by nationality affects how credit, revenue, and influence are assigned.

Potential misunderstanding. A work about Korea or inspired by Korean culture is not automatically a Korean production, and a production by Korean nationals is not automatically about Korea.

Section 90.15

K-beauty, Korean Food, Fashion, Language Learning, and Cultural Tourism

The Korean Wave influenced consumer markets beyond entertainment. Korean cosmetics and skincare, often called K-beauty, developed an international market through brands produced by companies such as Amorepacific and LG Household & Health Care, through retail chains such as Olive Young in Korea, and through online sales abroad. Products such as sheet masks and multi-step skincare routines became known internationally, and Korean companies increased exports during the 2010s and 2020s. Fashion, whether through entertainers' styles or Korean designers and brands, also drew attention.

Korean food spread through restaurants, supermarkets, and packaged products. Companies such as CJ and Nongshim expanded sales of instant noodles, sauces, and prepared foods abroad, and kimchi and Korean barbecue became more familiar in many countries. In 2021, the Oxford English Dictionary added twenty-six Korean-origin words or senses, including hallyu, K-drama, mukbang, and kimbap, a sign of the growing presence of Korean terms in English. The Korea Tourism Organization recorded 17,502,756 international visitor arrivals in 2019, a pre-pandemic high under its published reporting series; the category includes some overseas Korean entrants and air or ship crew and must not be read as an exact count of foreign leisure tourists. Arrivals fell sharply during the pandemic and recovered afterward. Music, television, and food influenced some travel decisions, but no total arrival figure can be attributed to Hallyu alone.

Students must be careful with causation. Entertainment celebrities, product placement, online creators, and retail networks promoted Korean products, and consumers sometimes sought out items they had seen in dramas or heard about from fans. But many commercial increases had other causes, including price, quality, retail expansion, trade agreements, and changes in global beauty and food markets. Korean cosmetics firms were already exporting before 2012, and Korean restaurants and groceries had served diaspora communities for decades. It would therefore be a mistake to attribute every commercial increase to Hallyu, though cultural visibility plausibly lowered the barriers to market entry.

Language learning provides some of the clearest evidence of influence. The King Sejong Institutes, supported by the South Korean government, began opening abroad in 2007 and expanded to dozens of countries, offering classes in Korean language and culture. The Test of Proficiency in Korean (TOPIK) drew increasing numbers of candidates, and language-learning platforms such as Duolingo reported rising numbers of Korean learners in the early 2020s. Universities in several countries expanded Korean programs. These developments reflect growing interest, though motivations vary among learners, from interest in entertainment to study, work, or family connections.

Korean diaspora communities and preexisting Korean businesses abroad, in places such as the United States, Japan, China, Central Asia, and Latin America, were already active before 2012 and often provided the initial distribution networks, restaurants, shops, churches, and cultural centers through which Korean products entered local markets. They also served as interpreters of Korean culture for local audiences.

The geographical diversity of Korean cultural reception deserves emphasis. Audiences in Japan, with its long relationship with Korean culture (Chapter 88), differ from those in Southeast Asia, where Korean dramas and pop had been familiar for years, and from those in Latin America, Europe, the Middle East, and Africa, where exposure is more recent and often concentrated among younger, urban, and online audiences. Each market has its own regulations, retail conditions, tastes, and political context. Treating every foreign market as equivalent would obscure these differences.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Ordinary People Experienced Global Hallyu

For many people the Korean Wave was experienced in daily routines. A fan in Brazil or Mexico might attend a concert, join a local group that met to practice dances, or spend months saving for a ticket and, in some cases, travel to another city. Fans in the United States or Europe might watch streamed concerts at home during the pandemic, join online watching parties, or translate lyrics voluntarily. Students in many countries enrolled in Korean language classes at universities or King Sejong Institutes, or studied alone using apps. Viewers watching a drama on a streaming service might learn phrases, cook Korean dishes, or buy products they saw on screen.

In Korea, the Wave changed daily life as well. Tourists visited filming locations and entertainment districts. Retail and service workers adapted to international visitors. Young people trained for years in the hope of debuting, most without success. Korean audiences debated how their culture was portrayed abroad and how global attention affected their own society. Evidence for these experiences comes from surveys, interviews, press reports, and fan-community records; it is uneven, tends to reflect the most visible and active participants, and says less about people who engaged casually or were indifferent.

Section 90.16

Cultural Diplomacy, Government Policy, and South Korea's Soft Power

South Korean governments took an interest in cultural industries well before 2012. After the 1997 financial crisis, the government of Kim Dae-jung described culture as an important national resource and expanded funding and institutions for content industries. The Korea Foundation for International Cultural Exchange (KOFICE), established in 2003, supported exchange and research on Hallyu; the Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA), created in 2009 by merging several earlier bodies, supported content businesses with financing, training, and overseas promotion. The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism set policy, and Korean cultural centers operated by the government in dozens of countries offered classes, exhibitions, and performances. The King Sejong Institutes, from 2007, taught the Korean language.

After the international successes of the 2010s, governments of different political orientations responded with additional measures: support for overseas events, subsidies and tax arrangements for content producers, promotion of exports, and public recognition of prominent artists. In 2018, President Moon Jae-in's government awarded BTS members state cultural-merit decorations, and in 2022 the group appeared at the White House to discuss anti-Asian hate crimes. The 2020 amendment to the Military Service Act, which allowed certain artists to postpone enlistment, was itself a policy response to the commercial importance of the group, though it did not exempt them. These cases show that governments both promoted the Korean Wave and struggled with how the state's expectations, such as universal military service, applied to cultural celebrities.

The concept of soft power, introduced by the American political scientist Joseph Nye, refers to the ability to generate attraction and influence through culture, political values, institutions, and reputation, rather than through coercion or payment. Korean policymakers and commentators frequently described Hallyu as a source of soft power, and cultural popularity can improve national image, increase tourism, and generate goodwill. But the concept has limits in this case. Cultural popularity is not diplomatic control. Governments could fund promotion but could not determine which songs, films, and programs foreign audiences would embrace. The most successful products were created by private companies, artists, and audiences, often without government direction, and sometimes in tension with official preferences.

Evidence of effects on tourism, visibility, and national image is suggestive but hard to isolate. International surveys, tourism statistics, and brand-reputation studies show rising awareness of South Korea in many countries, and visitors cite cultural products among their motives. But attitudes toward the country also depend on trade, politics, security, and personal experience, and cultural fondness does not necessarily translate into political support. This section does not repeat the broader geopolitical analysis of Chapter 94; its concern is the cultural policy and institutions through which the state sought to benefit from, and in some cases managed, the global spread of Korean culture.

Historical Vocabulary
Soft Power

Definition. The capacity of a state or society to influence others by attraction, through culture, ideals, institutions, and reputation, in contrast to "hard power" based on military force or economic coercion.

Historical context. The term was coined by Joseph Nye in 1990. South Korean officials, scholars, and journalists applied it to Hallyu from the 2000s, and it became a common frame for discussing the country's international image.

Why it matters. The concept helps explain why governments invest in cultural promotion, but it can also encourage overstating how much influence cultural popularity confers and how much the state controls it.

Potential misunderstanding. Soft power is not simply popularity, and it cannot be assumed to translate into diplomatic leverage. Much Korean cultural success arose from private actors.

Section 90.17

Entertainment Conglomerates, Streaming Platforms, and the Global Cultural Economy

The enterprises that produced Korean cultural exports changed substantially after 2012. Big Hit Entertainment grew into HYBE, renamed in 2021 and composed of multiple labels, a fan-community platform called Weverse, and international subsidiaries, including an American operation that acquired the company Ithaca Holdings, the business associated with the manager who had worked with Psy. SM Entertainment, YG Entertainment, and JYP Entertainment, publicly listed companies, continued to manage large rosters, and in 2023 a contest for control of SM between HYBE and the technology company Kakao, which became a major shareholder, drew public attention to the financial scale of the sector. CJ ENM, a diversified conglomerate, operated broadcasting channels, film and drama production through affiliates such as Studio Dragon, music events, and cinema chains.

These firms pursued several strategies: investment in new groups, acquisition of other companies, partnerships with international record labels and streaming platforms, expansion of fan-commerce platforms, and the creation of acts in cooperation with foreign companies, including a group formed through a joint venture between HYBE and an American label that debuted in 2024 with non-Korean members. Intellectual-property ownership was central: companies sought to own and license music, characters, and formats, and platforms commissioning Korean series often retained rights. Distribution partnerships with multinational firms gave Korean companies access to markets but also tied them to the priorities of larger partners. In April 2023 Netflix announced a planned investment of US$2.5 billion in Korean creative content over four years; this was a corporate commitment, not proof that the total had already been spent or that each project would become profitable.

International success changed bargaining relationships. Korean producers gained leverage as the evident popularity of their products increased platforms' interest; multinational platforms, in turn, held distribution power that gave them advantages in negotiating rights and prices. Artists with large followings gained greater bargaining power with their companies, as shown by negotiations over contract renewal and individual activities. Audiences, through organized fan campaigns, could influence company decisions in some cases, such as by publicly protesting management choices. Bargaining power was thus distributed unevenly and shifted with each party's circumstances.

Students should distinguish broad visibility from financial returns. A series can be watched by tens of millions without producing proportionate income for its creators if the platform owns the rights and pays a fixed fee. A group's popularity may generate large revenue for a company while individual members' share depends on contracts that are rarely public. Commercial concentration is a further concern: a handful of large entertainment businesses and a few streaming intermediaries account for a very large share of the visible success, and smaller companies and independent creators often struggle. Disputes between artists and companies, including the legal conflict involving NewJeans and its label, showed how contested control over names, contracts, and management could become.

Historical Debate

Has Global Hallyu Increased South Korea's Cultural Influence or Strengthened Multinational Corporations and Platforms?

The question: The international expansion of Korean content increased visibility and created new audiences. But much of it passes through large companies and foreign platforms. Did the main beneficiary become Korean culture and Korean creators, or the corporate and technological intermediaries that distribute and own it?

Interpretation A — Cultural and national gain: International visibility created new creative employment, expanded audiences for Korean language, food, and tourism, improved national image, and gave Korean artists access to global markets they could not have reached otherwise. Platforms were a means for Korean creators to reach the world.
Interpretation B — Corporate and platform gain: Ownership, distribution control, and a large share of revenue accrued to a small number of corporations, domestic and foreign. Creators and workers received limited shares, and dependence on a few platforms left Korean producers exposed to changes in terms and strategy.

Evidence: Evidence includes corporate financial reports, contract and rights disclosures where available, labor surveys, statements by creators, export statistics, tourism data, and comparative studies of other national industries.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns scale and interpretation, and partly data availability, since contract terms are often confidential. Scholars also disagree about whether cultural visibility can be valued apart from revenue.

Why it matters: The two readings imply different policy responses, such as support for independent producers and rights protections, versus emphasis on promotion. They are not mutually exclusive: cultural visibility and corporate concentration can increase together.

Section 90.18

Performers, Creative Workers, Labor Rights, and the Costs of Global Success

Global success had human costs, which scholars, journalists, and performers themselves described. Idol training typically began in the teenage years and lasted several years, with long hours of practice, restrictions on private life, and uncertain prospects, since only a minority of trainees debuted. Exclusive contracts bound performers to a company for years. The Korean Fair Trade Commission had introduced standard contract guidelines, generally limiting the term of such contracts, in response to disputes described in Chapter 89, but later conflicts continued over revenue sharing, control of creative decisions, and the ownership of names and recordings. International touring added further demands: crowded schedules, travel, and constant promotion in multiple languages.

Online harassment and loss of privacy were serious problems. Between 2017 and 2019 the deaths of several well-known performers prompted public discussion of mental health, malicious online comments, and pressures within the industry. Daum announced the removal of comments on entertainment news in October 2019; Naver moved to restrict such comments in March 2020. These platform-policy responses addressed one vector of harassment without eliminating the wider problems of privacy invasion and social-media abuse. Fan scrutiny, some of it intrusive, and the intensity of global social media, where criticism could come from many countries, increased the pressure. Some artists spoke publicly about exhaustion and depression, and some companies introduced mental-health programs, though their adequacy is debated.

Performers were not the only workers. Backup dancers and choreographers, stylists, video crews, sound engineers, translators and interpreters, and fan-community managers contributed essential labor, often with less recognition and less secure employment than the headline stars. In screen production, screenwriters, assistant directors, and crews worked under tight schedules; Korean writers' and crews' organizations campaigned for better pay, fixed hours, and a share of revenues from reuse and international distribution, and the creator of Squid Game publicly discussed the gap between a series' global success and its creators' rewards. Conditions varied widely by employer, genre, and individual status, and students should avoid treating every company or performer as having the same experience.

Chapter 89 discussed labor disputes arising within the earlier agency system. The conditions of the later period differed in scale and character. International touring, multinational contracts, streaming-platform commissioning, and constant online publicity created new pressures but also new opportunities: leading performers could earn very large incomes, negotiate greater independence, produce their own material, and reach audiences that earlier generations never could. Worldwide audiences also raised expectations about quality, frequency of releases, and public behavior. The costs and benefits of global success were therefore distributed unevenly, and the structural social problems of Korean society, examined in Chapter 91, also shaped the industry's working conditions.

Section 90.19

Cultural Representation, Global Fandom, Backlash, and Conflicting Interpretations

Korean cultural products were interpreted differently in different societies. Translation choices shaped how lyrics, jokes, and dialogue were understood; adaptations, such as American remakes of Korean series and formats, reshaped stories for local audiences; and viewers read characters and settings through their own experiences. Diaspora communities served as interpreters, sometimes explaining Korean references to non-Korean friends and sometimes criticizing portrayals as inauthentic. Global fan communities likewise explained, defended, and sometimes contested the content they loved.

Several controversies illustrate the tensions. Critics, including many Black and other audiences, pointed out cases in which Korean performers adopted hairstyles, fashion, or musical styles associated with African American culture, or in which performances included blackface, and several performers and companies apologized. These episodes raised questions about cultural appropriation and about the history of hip-hop and R&B in Korean pop, which had long borrowed from American Black music. Others debated gender representation, as idol images could reinforce narrow beauty standards, and racial representation, as Korean media sometimes depicted foreigners in stereotyped ways. Some Korean and foreign commentators debated the authenticity of internationally marketed pop, asking whether a style shaped for global markets, with songs in English and foreign production teams, was still distinctively Korean.

Political conflicts also shaped reception. After 2016 South Korea's deployment of an American missile-defense system led to informal restrictions on Korean entertainment in China, a case considered more fully in Chapter 94. In 2020, comments by BTS's leader about the shared history of Korean and American soldiers in the Korean War provoked a hostile reaction on Chinese social media and led companies to remove promotional material. In Japan, periods of political tension over history (Chapter 88) were accompanied by anti-Korean demonstrations and online hostility even as Korean pop remained popular with many Japanese fans. Fans themselves sometimes took political action: in 2020, fans' online activism, including coordinated ticket reservations and social-media campaigns, drew coverage in American media during a period of racial-justice protests and election campaigning.

Commercial pressure sometimes encouraged adaptation to foreign expectations, such as English-language songs, changes to concept, or avoidance of subjects that might offend large markets. Whether this was a pragmatic choice, a loss of identity, or a new hybrid is contested. Students should also distinguish legitimate cultural criticism, for example of appropriation or labor practices, from discrimination and hostility toward Korean people or culture. Anti-Asian hostility, including during the pandemic, affected Koreans and Korean Americans irrespective of the popularity of Korean entertainment.

The evidence suggests that international popularity does not automatically produce intercultural understanding. Many fans learned about Korea, studied the language, and developed lasting interest; others consumed products without much interest in the society behind them; and some reacted with resentment. Surveys and fan studies provide partial evidence, and they typically reflect those who chose to participate. A careful historian therefore treats popularity, understanding, and goodwill as three separate things.

Section 90.20

Global Korean Culture in 2026: Achievements, Limits, and Historical Significance

By November 2026, the trajectory from the viral breakthrough of 2012 to the present was clear in outline. A Korean-language pop song and video had become a worldwide curiosity. A series of Korean acts had built durable positions in music markets, with BTS returning to group activity in 2026 after a period of military service and solo projects, and BLACKPINK maintaining an international presence through group and solo work. Korean cinema had won the highest awards in French and American film institutions. Korean television had become a regular part of global streaming catalogs. Korean literature had received the Nobel Prize. Korean webtoons had become a source of adapted stories. An animated film involving Korean-diaspora creators and an American studio had won two Academy Awards. Korean cosmetics, food, and language courses had reached consumers in many countries.

The geographical reach of Korean cultural audiences had expanded considerably, though it remained uneven. Established audiences in Asia continued; large and active communities existed in North and South America and Europe; and interest had grown in parts of the Middle East and Africa. Regional differences in tastes, platform availability, regulation, and political context persisted, and available measurement, which often relies on surveys or platform statistics, is better for some regions than others.

One piece of recent evidence is a report released March 30, 2026, by South Korea’s Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism with the Korea Foundation for International Cultural Exchange, based on online survey fieldwork conducted November 13–December 12, 2025. It reported a favorability rating of 69.7 percent among 27,400 respondents aged 15 to 59 across thirty regions who had already experienced Korean cultural content. Respondents were recruited for an online survey among people with prior Korean-content experience, not drawn as a universal cross-section of residents worldwide. Thus 69.7 percent represents favorable responses within the surveyed eligible group; it is not a world-population estimate, nor by itself proof that the respondents volunteered because they had especially favorable views. The source window below explains these distinctions.

The year's events included BTS's return, with the March album Arirang, the March comeback performance, and the April opening of its world tour; BLACKPINK’s February release of Deadline and continued international activity; and the recognition of KPop Demon Hunters. Taken together they show continuity of earlier forces, such as organized fandom and corporate planning, and new forms, such as transnational production and global award recognition. Only dates of the BTS tour after the November 2026 endpoint are prospective; the tour’s April 2026 opening and any already completed dates are history, not forecasts.

Has South Korea become a durable global cultural producer? The evidence supports a qualified affirmative. The country has deep production institutions, large and varied audiences, and a record of creative achievement across media. But its position remains affected by foreign platforms that control distribution, domestic economic pressures including a shrinking and aging population (Chapter 91), the working conditions of creative laborers, competition from other countries' industries, and the unpredictability of tastes. Many successful products also depend on partners abroad. Influence, in short, depends on institutions and audiences as well as on individual successes, and it can be substantial without being permanent or universally welcomed.

What changed? The audience grew from a regional base to several continents; the range of products widened from music and drama to film, literature, animation, and consumer goods; the corporate structure globalized.

Chapter Summary

The one-billion-view milestone of December 2012 was a real and important event, but it was the visible peak of a much longer process. Gangnam Style demonstrated that Korean-language entertainment could reach global audiences through digital platforms. It did not start Hallyu, which had already built large regional audiences in the 1990s and 2000s, and it did not by itself create a sustainable international industry. Distinguishing a viral event from a durable industry is the chapter's first lesson.

Digital platforms, including YouTube, social media, music streaming, and video streaming, transformed distribution by reducing dependence on national broadcasters and foreign record companies and by providing subtitles, recommendations, and sharing tools. But platforms transmitted works and audiences that others created. Reach, engagement, and revenue are different things, and platforms created new dependencies as they removed older gatekeepers.

BTS, debuting in 2013 from a relatively small company, grew into an international act through music, narrative, constant online communication, a deliberate release strategy, and the work of a worldwide fan community. Its achievements, from the 2017 Billboard award to the United Nations appearance and the 2020 number-one debut of "Dynamite," were distinct from Psy's earlier peak. Its 2022 shift to solo activities, military service, and 2026 return with Arirang showed how global stardom was shaped by Korean law and by corporate and personal decisions.

ARMY exemplifies participatory fandom: fans translated, organized, raised funds, and interpreted. They were neither simply consumers nor simply company marketing; they were intermediaries whose labor was both voluntary and commercially valuable, and their influence is real but difficult to measure.

BLACKPINK followed a different route, emphasizing fashion, performance, and branding under an established agency, and it moved from guest act to Coachella headliner. Later groups, including TWICE, SEVENTEEN, Stray Kids, ATEEZ, aespa, IVE, LE SSERAFIM, and NewJeans, diversified the industry, and the world concert economy brought new revenue and new dependence on promoters, platforms, and ticketing. Charts, attendance, sales, and streaming measure different things.

Korean cinema reached beyond Asia through festivals, critics, distributors, and awards bodies. The Handmaiden, Train to Busan, and Burning represented different paths, and Parasite's Palme d'Or in 2019 and four Academy Awards in 2020, including the first Best Picture for a non-English-language film, were the culmination of decades of institutional development.

Television drama moved from regional sales to simultaneous global streaming, with Descendants of the Sun, Crash Landing on You, and Extraordinary Attorney Woo marking stages. Squid Game, released in 2021 and concluded in 2025, became the leading example, though its streaming figures must be read according to Netflix's methodology. Webtoons and literature, including Han Kang's 2024 Nobel Prize, show that Korean storytelling also travels through other institutions and audiences.

KPop Demon Hunters showed Korean cultural forms becoming material for multinational production, with an American studio, global streaming, and diaspora creators. Its 2026 Academy Awards illustrate that influence can exceed ownership. Meanwhile K-beauty, food, fashion, tourism, and language learning showed influence in consumer life, though not every commercial increase can be attributed to Hallyu, and reception varies widely by region.

Government agencies promoted the Korean Wave and described it as soft power, but they could not control which products audiences embraced. Entertainment conglomerates, platforms, and international partners restructured the industry, increasing both opportunity and concentration. Performers and creative workers bore costs including demanding schedules, contractual constraints, harassment, and unequal rewards, and global audiences interpreted Korean culture in conflicting ways, including through appropriation controversies, political boycotts, and hostility.

As of November 2026, South Korea had become a major participant in global cultural life, with an influence that rested on institutions and audiences as well as on individual hits. That influence was substantial without being permanent or universally welcome, and it remained dependent on foreign platforms, domestic pressures, creative labor, and changing tastes.

Communities of a people living outside their homeland, here Koreans and their descendants abroad.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that global cultural influence is built from many layers. A viral video, a number-one single, a Best Picture, or a streaming record each captures attention, but none explains by itself how a country becomes a durable participant in world culture. The longer story involves training systems, production companies, broadcasters, studios, public agencies, digital infrastructure, volunteer translators, diaspora networks, and audiences who chose to watch, listen, read, travel, and study.

The chapter also connects earlier parts of the textbook. The urbanized, educated, technologically advanced society described in Chapters 81 and 87, the post-1997 restructuring of Chapter 84, and the regional Hallyu of Chapter 89 form its foundations. Its developments bear on later chapters: the working conditions and demographic pressures of Chapter 91, the geopolitical relationships of Chapter 94, and the contemporary assessment of Chapter 96. Korean culture, like Korean industry and politics, must be read as part of a global system.

The chapter's themes are also continuities with the oldest question of this textbook. Chapter 1 asked what "Korea" means. In the cultural sphere, the answer has again changed: in 2026 "Korean" culture is made partly in Korea, partly by the diaspora, and partly by foreign companies and fans who adopt and reshape it. Students should be careful to distinguish achievements that belong to Korean institutions from those that belong to transnational collaborations.

Finally, the chapter invites humility about the present. Popularity can fade, platforms can change terms, and tastes can shift. The achievements are real and carefully documented, and the evidence supports a qualified judgment of durability. It does not support prediction. Historians of the next edition will know what happened after 2026; the task here is to explain how this moment was reached, and with what evidence.
